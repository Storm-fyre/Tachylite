> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> Between 1918 and 1951, the Indian freedom struggle experienced a seismic shift from the bottom up. Two global economic shocks—post-World War I inflation and the 1929 Great Depression price crash—squeezed rural cultivators and factory workers while landlords, millowners, and the British tax collector demanded fixed cash payments and unpaid forced labour. Refusing to wait for elite constitutional compromises, peasants formed militant unions (*Kisan Sabhas*), factory workers built strike committees, Socialists and Communists organized vanguard parties, subjects of the 562 Princely States rose up against royal autocracy, and radical writers, printmakers, and street-theatre troupes turned famine and class struggle into art.
> 
> This grassroots radicalization forced a two-front battle across late-colonial India. On one side, subaltern movements pushed the Indian National Congress to adopt pro-peasant and pro-worker manifestos and integrate the Princely States into the demand for complete independence. On the other side, the alarmed colonial state—and occasionally conservative Congress provincial ministries—fought back with anti-strike laws, Bolshevik conspiracy trials, armed police, and the 1935 constitutional design. By the late 1940s, armed sharecropper and tribal rebellions from Bengal and Maharashtra to Travancore and Telangana had permanently shattered the foundations of feudal landlordism and princely rule on the eve of independence.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. The Political Economy of Agrarian Radicalism and the Kisan Sabha Trajectory (1920–1939)

* **1. Post-WWI Rent-Squeeze, Taluqdari Feudalism, and Early Peasant Mobilizations (1920–1928):** When post-1918 inflation doubled living costs, Awadh’s **Taluqdars** (aristocratic landlords under the Oudh Rent Act of 1886) bypassed the 6.25% statutory rent-hike cap by subjecting seven-year leaseholders to **Bedakhli** (arbitrary eviction at lease-end) and auctioning plots for **Nazrana** (exorbitant upfront renewal premiums), alongside **Abwabs / Lag-Bag** (illegal feudal cesses like **Motorawan** [motorcar levy], **Hathiawan** [elephant tax], and **Murda-faroshi** [corpse-burning fee]) and **Hari / Begar** (unpaid forced ploughing and labour) on **Sir** (the landlord’s personal home-farm). Former Fiji **Girmitiya** (returned indentured labourer) Baba Ramchandra used *Ramcharitmanas* recitals and **Nai-Dhobi Bandh** (social boycotts denying barbers and washermen to landlords and their **Ziladars / Karindas** [estate agents]) to build the Oudh Kisan Sabha (Oct 1920) with Jawaharlal Nehru, while Madari Pasi led the 1921–22 **Eka** (Unity) Movement against **Thekadars** (rapacious revenue contractors), swearing a Ganga oath to pay only the **Parcha** (recorded statutory rent). Meanwhile, in southern and western India, N.G. Ranga organized peasants against **Ryotwari** (direct state-peasant taxation inflated by **Fasli Jasti** [extra irrigation surcharges]) vs. **Zamindari** (landlord-estate taxation imposing **Pullari** [forest-grazing fees]), establishing the Indian Peasant Institute at Nidubrolu (1933). In 1928, Vallabhbhai Patel defeated a 21.97% revenue hike in Bardoli by organizing 13 **Chhavanis** (workers’ camps), uniting upper-caste **Ujliparaj** ("fair-skinned" Patidar/Brahmin landowners) vs. indigenous **Kaliparaj / Raniparaj** ("black-skinned" / "forest-dwelling" Dubla tribals bound as **Halis** [hereditary debt-serfs]), and using mass resignations of **Patels / Talatis** (village headmen and accountants) plus women’s lock-ins against **Japti** (property-attachment seizures) until the Broomfield-Maxwell Committee slashed the hike to 6.03% and restored confiscated **Khalsa** (crown-forfeited) lands.
* **2. The Great Depression Price Crash, Zamindari Coercion, and Institutional Kisan Consolidation (1929–1939):** During the 1929–34 Great Depression, crop prices plummeted over 50%, yet the British kept the Rupee pegged at an overvalued **1s 6d Sterling ratio** (currency exchange rate protecting colonial remittances), doubling the real grain burden of fixed state taxes, **Mahajan / Sahukar** (moneylender) interest, and Bihar Zamindari produce-rents like **Danabandi / Bhaoli** (appraised standing-crop or threshed-grain levies). When bankrupt **Raiyats** (hereditary occupancy tenants) defaulted on rent, Bihar landlords used colonial courts to auction and convert their ancestral plots into **Bakasht** (landlord-resumed self-cultivated land), re-hiring the evicted owners as precarious **Bataidars** (unprotected sharecroppers paying 50% of the harvest). Swami Sahajanand Saraswati founded the Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS, 1929), allied with Congress Socialists, and—when the 1937 Bihar Congress Ministry compromised with landlords—led militant *Danda/Lathi* (bamboo staff) Satyagrahas with Karyanand Sharma (Barahiya Tal), Yadunandan Sharma (Reora), and Rahul Sankrityayan (Amwari) to forcibly harvest **Bakasht** crops against landlord **Lathiyals** (club-wielding private militias). This provincial surge culminated at Lucknow on April 11, 1936, in the **All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)** under Sahajanand and N.G. Ranga, which adopted the **Lal Jhanda** (Red Flag with hammer and sickle) and issued a manifesto demanding a 50% rent/revenue cut and the uncompensated abolition of **Zamindari, Taluqdari, Malguzari, Inamdari, and Jenmi** (regional feudal landlord systems across India), compelling the Congress to adopt its 1936 Faizpur Agrarian Programme.

### II. Late-Colonial Subaltern and Armed Agrarian Insurrections (1945–1951)

* **3. Sharecropper, Adivasi, and Coir-Worker Militancy on the Eve of Transfer of Power (1945–1947):** Between 1945 and 1947, Communist organizers shifted agrarian struggle to the poorest rural strata. In Maharashtra’s Thane district, Godavari and Shamrao Parulekar mobilized Warli Adivasis against absentee **Sawkars** (landlord-moneylenders) to abolish **Vethbigar** (compulsory unpaid farming and timber-hauling labour), **Lagin-Gadi** (marriage-debt slavery turning newlyweds into lifelong **Gadi** [bonded serfs] for a Rs 50 wedding loan), and **Khand** (usurious seed loans at 150–200% interest). Simultaneously, across 19 famine-scarred Bengal districts, the Kisan Sabha and peasant women’s **Nari Bahini** (militias armed with **Boti** [curved fish-blades] and **Gayen** [wooden rice-husking poles] under leaders like Ila Mitra and Bimala Maji) confronted rich **Jotedars** (intermediary landlords) on behalf of **Bargadars / Adhiars / Bhagchasis** (unrecorded sharecroppers who supplied all labour, bullocks, and ploughs). Rejecting the traditional **Adhiari** (nominal 50:50 split reduced to 25% by **Dera-bari / Karja** [usurious seed-interest] and illegal deductions inside the Jotedar’s **Khamar** [private threshing floor]) in favor of **Tebhaga** (the 1940 Floud Commission-backed two-thirds [67%] harvest share for the tiller), six million peasants enforced *"Nij Khamare Dhan Tolo"* (stacking paddy on the peasant’s own threshing floor). In Travancore (Oct 1946), Alappuzha coir workers and landless **Kudikidappukar** (homestead hutment tenants-at-will) rejected Dewan C.P. Ramaswami Iyer’s **"American Model" Constitution** (an autocratic scheme keeping an irremovable executive Dewan) and fought state troops with sharpened wooden **Areca-nut spears** in the bloody Punnapra-Vayalar Uprising.
* **4. The Telangana Peasant Armed Struggle in Hyderabad State (1946–1951):** In Nizam-ruled Hyderabad, land was divided into **Diwani / Khalsa** (state revenue tracts dominated by tax-farming **Deshmukhs / Deshpandes / Doras** ["Lords"] who falsified **Pahani** [survey records] with **Patwaris** [village accountants] to amass 40,000–150,000-acre estates) vs. **Jagirdari / Samsthanam** (autonomous noble fiefdoms) vs. **Sarf-e-Khas** (the Nizam’s personal crown lands). Landlords enforced **Vetti** (caste-based unpaid forced labour at the landlord’s **Gadhi** [fortified manor] and **Khudkasht** [home-farm]), **Bhagela** (generational tribal debt-serfdom), and **Adi-bapa** (keeping peasant girls as lifelong manor concubines). After young Communist leaders turned the Andhra Mahasabha into village **Sanghams** (peasant cells), the defence of washerwoman Chakali Ailamma’s harvest and the July 4, 1946 assassination of Doddi Komaraiah at Kadavendi ignited a full-scale revolution. Facing the Nizam’s troops and Qasim Razvi’s **Razakars** (fanatical paramilitary militia), peasants formed village militias armed with **Vadisela** (stone-slings) and **Bharamarams** (muzzle-loaders) alongside mobile **Dalams** (full-time guerrilla squads), liberating 3,000 villages where elected **Gram Rajyams** (village soviets) abolished **Vetti** and redistributed one million acres of land. After India’s **Operation Polo** (Sept 1948 military integration of Hyderabad), the CPI—following the ultra-left **B.T. Ranadive line** and **Andhra Thesis** (attempting urban insurrection and Maoist protracted guerrilla war against the Nehruvian state)—fought the Indian Army from Koya tribal forest sanctuaries until withdrawing the struggle in October 1951 to contest the First General Elections.

### III. Industrial Proletarianization, Trade Unionism, and the Colonial Statutory Containment Grid (1918–1939)

* **5. Post-WWI Inflation, the Madras Labour Union, and the Genesis of AITUC (1918–1926):** Post-WWI wartime profiteering and 70–100% food inflation devastated real wages for workers packed into urban **Chawls, Bustees, and Cheris** (tenements in Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras) and exploited by the **Jobber** (despotic shop-floor recruiter/creditor—called **Sardar** in Bengal, **Mukadam / Naikin** in Bombay, and **Maistry** in Madras—who extracted **Dasturi** [monthly wage bribes]). On April 27, 1918, B.P. Wadia founded India’s first modern trade union, the Madras Labour Union, at the Buckingham and Carnatic Mills; when Binny & Co. sued him in 1920, the Madras High Court imposed a Rs 75,000 injunction under the common-law **Tort of Malicious Conspiracy to Induce Breach of Contract** (ruling that organizing a strike was an illegal conspiracy). Driven by a nationwide strike wave and the 1919 creation of the International Labour Organization (ILO), nationalists and unionists founded the **All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC)** in Bombay on October 31, 1920, under President Lala Lajpat Rai, General Secretary Dewan Chaman Lal, and N.M. Joshi. To contain this surge, the British passed the **Trade Unions Act of 1926**, a dual-containment law reversing the Wadia ruling by granting registered unions civil and criminal immunity (Sections 17–18), while mandating that at least 50% of office-bearers be active industry workers and quarantining civic campaigns into separate, voluntary **Political Funds**.
* **6. The Militant Strike Wave, Girni Kamgar Union, and the Coercive Counter-Offensive (1928–1939):** When millowners pursued **Rationalization / Standardization** (forcing weavers to tend three or four looms instead of two with wage cuts), a six-month general strike of 150,000 Bombay textile workers in 1928 propelled the Communist-led **Girni Kamgar Union (GKU / Lal Bavta)** (Red Flag union led by S.A. Dange, Ben Bradley, and A.A. Alwe) to 54,000+ members, establishing elected shop-floor **Mill Committees** that bypassed Jobbers. Alarmed by the loss of 31.6 million man-days in 1928, Viceroy Lord Irwin enacted the **Trade Disputes Act of 1929**, which created non-binding conciliation boards, criminalized strikes without one month’s notice in **Public Utility Services** (railways, post, water, light, and power), and outlawed all **Sympathetic, General, and Political Strikes**. Simultaneously, Britain appointed the **Whitley Commission on Labour (1929–31)**; left-nationalist and Communist delegates at the 1929 Nagpur AITUC session boycotted the Commission, triggering splits into the moderate **Indian Trade Union Federation (ITUF/NTUF)** (N.M. Joshi, V.V. Giri) and the ultra-left **Red Trade Union Congress (RTUC, 1931)** (B.T. Ranadive, S.V. Deshpande) before Popular Front reunification in 1935–40. The Whitley Report exposed the Jobber debt-bribery nexus and Pathan moneylender **Sawai** (75–150% usurious interest), directly catalyzing the replacement of Jobbers with management **Labour Officers**, the **Factories Act of 1934** (capping perennial factories at a 54-hour week), and the **Payment of Wages Act of 1936** (curbing arbitrary wage fines and delayed pay).

### IV. The Communist Party, Workers and Peasants Parties, State Conspiracy Trials, and the Congress Socialist Party (1920–1940s)

* **7. The Comintern Nexus, Domestic Communist Genesis, Workers and Peasants Parties, and Bolshevik Conspiracy Cases (1920–1933):** At the Second Comintern Congress (1920), **Lenin’s Preliminary Theses** (advocating temporary alliances with bourgeois-democratic movements like the Congress) clashed with **M.N. Roy’s Supplementary Theses** (arguing that the colonial bourgeoisie would compromise with imperialism, requiring autonomous worker-peasant soviets). On October 17, 1920, at Tashkent, M.N. Roy, Evelyn Trent Roy, and Abani Mukherji founded the **Communist Party of India (CPI)**, recruiting anti-British **Hijrat Muhajirs** (pan-Islamic migrants trained at Tashkent and Moscow’s **KUTV** [Communist University of the Toilers of the East]). Inside India, autonomous Marxist circles—S.A. Dange in Bombay (*Gandhi vs. Lenin*, *The Socialist*), Muzaffar Ahmad and Kazi Nazrul Islam in Calcutta (*Navayug*, *Langal*), M. Singaravelu Chettiar in Madras (who hoisted the Red Flag on India’s first May Day, May 1, 1923), and Ghulam Hussain in Lahore (*Inqilab*)—united at the December 1925 Kanpur Communist Conference and built legal, dual-class **Workers and Peasants Parties (WPPs, 1925–28)** inside the Congress to bypass colonial bans. The British struck back under **Section 121-A IPC** (conspiracy to wage war against the King-Emperor) through three major trials: the **Peshawar Conspiracy Cases (1922–27)** against returning Pamir-corridor Muhajirs; the **Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case (1924)** jailing Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad, Shaukat Usmani, and Nalini Gupta; and the **Meerut Conspiracy Case (1929–33)** against 31 labour leaders (including British Communists Philip Spratt, Ben Bradley, and Lester Hutchinson), deliberately tried in a **mofussil** (provincial district) court with assessors to evade a Presidency High Court **Jury trial**. Although banned from 1934 to 1942, the CPI under General Secretary P.C. Joshi adopted the **Dutt-Bradley Thesis (1936)** (Comintern Popular Front unity with the Congress), later shifting during WWII from opposing the **"Imperialist War" (1939–41)** to supporting the **"People’s War" (1941–45)** after Hitler invaded the USSR.
* **8. The Congress Socialist Party (CSP, 1934) and the Dialectics of Left Unity:** Disillusioned by the suspension of Civil Disobedience and the right-wing drift toward council entry, young nationalists imprisoned in Nasik Jail (1932–33)—Jayaprakash Narayan, Minoo Masani, Achyut Patwardhan, Asoka Mehta, and N.G. Goray—joined Acharya Narendra Deva, Ram Manohar Lohia, Yusuf Meherally (who coined *"Simon Go Back"* and *"Quit India"*), and Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay at the Patna (May 1934) and Bombay (Oct 1934) conferences to found the **Congress Socialist Party (CSP)**. Operating strictly as an ideological caucus *inside* the Indian National Congress, the CSP synthesized Marxism, Fabian socialism, and Gandhian mass ethics to demand the collective affiliation of Kisan Sabhas and trade unions to Congress, total rejection of the 1935 Act, nationalization of key industries, and uncompensated abolition of landlordism. Under the **Meerut Thesis (Jan 1936)**, Jayaprakash Narayan opened CSP membership to cadres of the banned CPI to forge left unity; however, underground Communists (like E.M.S. Namboodiripad, P. Sundarayya, and P. Ramamurti) systematically captured the CSP’s southern provincial machinery in Kerala, Andhra, and Tamil Nadu, leading the CSP executive to formally expel all Communists at the March 1940 Ramgarh session before spearheading the 1942 Quit India underground.

### V. The Princely States Peoples’ Movement (Praja Mandals) and Socialist Realist Cultural Mobilization (1927–1947)

* **9. Paramountcy, the AISPC (1927), Congress Integration (Haripura to Ludhiana), and Regional Praja Mandal Struggles:** Shielded by British military **Paramountcy** (supreme Crown suzerainty declared non-transferable to an Indian Cabinet by the **1927 Butler Committee**), the rulers of 562 Princely States treated public revenue as their personal **Privy Purse** (private royal allowance) and crushed civil liberties. To coordinate over 700 local **Praja Mandals / Lok Parishads** (democratic states’ peoples’ associations), Balwantrai Mehta, Maniklal Kothari, and G.R. Abhyankar founded the **All India States Peoples’ Conference (AISPC)** in December 1927. Following the 1937 Congress provincial ministries, Congress shifted from organizational non-interference to the **1938 Haripura Resolution** (permitting individual Congress leaders to lead state struggles while keeping official Congress committees separate) vs. the **1939 Tripuri / Ludhiana Integration** (erasing all barriers between British and Princely India as Jawaharlal Nehru became AISPC President). Fierce regional struggles erupted: in **Rajkot (1938–39)**, Sardar Patel and Gandhi’s fast forced Chief Justice Maurice Gwyer’s arbitral award against Thakore Dharmendrasinhji; in **Mysore (1938)**, the Shivapura Flag Satyagraha and the **Vidurashwatha Massacre** (32 unarmed satyagrahis shot dead) forced Dewan Mirza Ismail to recognize the State Congress; in **Hyderabad (1938)**, Swami Ramanand Tirtha’s State Congress and Osmania University’s *Vande Mataram* student strike defied Nizam ban circular **Gashti Nishan 53**; in **Travancore (1938–47)**, Pattom Thanu Pillai’s State Congress and the Communist Youth League challenged Dewan C.P. Ramaswami Iyer; in Odisha’s 26 **Garjat States (1938–39)**, peasants resisting **Bethi** (unpaid palace/road labour), **Rasad** (free food provisioning for royal camps), **Magan** (forced royal wedding/car levies), and **Bheti** (compulsory gifts) saw 12-year-old **Banapua** (youth volunteer) boatman Baji Rout martyred in Dhenkanal, 26,000 peasants execute a protest **Hijrat** (mass exodus) from Talcher to Angul, and Ranpur crowds kill Political Agent Major Bazelgette; and in **Kashmir**, Sheikh Abdullah converted the Muslim Conference into the secular National Conference (1939), adopted the socialist **Naya Kashmir** manifesto (1944) for uncompensated land-to-the-tiller, and launched the **1946 Quit Kashmir** movement challenging the **1846 Treaty of Amritsar** (the "Sale Deed" selling Kashmir to Dogra rule for 75 lakh rupees).
* **10. Socialist Realism, Subaltern Visual Modernity, the Progressive Writers’ Association (1936), and IPTA (1943):** In visual art and literature, the 1930s–40s overthrew both colonial **Academic Naturalism** (salon oil portraiture) and late **Bengal School Revivalism** (nostalgic mythological wash watercolours) in favor of **Socialist Realism** (unflinching material depiction of subaltern struggle). Paris-trained Amrita Sher-Gil fused European **Post-Impressionism** (sculptural volume) with flat Ajanta and Pahari colour planes to paint gaunt, melancholic rural women and peasants (*Three Girls*, *South Indian Villagers Going to Market*). When wartime inflation and colonial **Rice Denial and Boat Denial Policies** (scorched-earth confiscations of boats and grain) caused the **1943 Great Bengal Famine** (3.5 million dead), Communist artists abandoned studio oils for reproducible graphic reportage: Chittaprosad carved high-contrast **Linocuts and Scraperboard** prints for *Hungry Bengal* (1943, burned by the British), Zainul Abedin drew skeletal *Famine Sketches* with dry-brush black ink on cheap packing paper, and Somnath Hore engraved his **Woodcut** *Tebhaga Diary* (1946–47). In literature, following the banned 1932 Urdu story collection **Angarey** (*Burning Coals*) and Sajjad Zaheer and Mulk Raj Anand’s 1934 London manifesto, the **Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA)** convened at Lucknow in April 1936 under Munshi Premchand (author of *Godaan*), who urged writers to act as a **Mashalchi** (vanguard torchbearer) redefining beauty around the sweat-soaked peasant woman—inspiring Anand (*Untouchable*, *Coolie*), Faiz Ahmad Faiz (*Naqsh-e-Faryadi*), Ismat Chughtai (deploying colloquial women’s **Begumati Zubaan** in *Lihaaf*), and Saadat Hasan Manto (*Toba Tek Singh*). To reach the 88% non-literate masses, the **Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA)** was founded in Bombay on May 25, 1943, leaving commercial proscenium stages to weaponize indigenous folk theatre—Bengal’s **Jatra / Kabigan**, Maharashtra’s **Tamasha / Powada** (Annabhau Sathe, Amar Shaikh), Andhra’s **Burrakatha** (*Maabhoomi*), Kerala’s **Ottamthullal**, and Gujarat’s **Bhavai**—alongside Bijon Bhattacharya’s landmark famine play **Nabanna** (*New Harvest*, Oct 1944), Balraj Sahni’s naturalist acting (*Dharti Ke Lal*, *Do Bigha Zamin*), and Salil Chowdhury’s **Ganasangeet** (polyphonic choral people’s songs like *He Samalo Dhan Ho*).
* **11. Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 14: From Subaltern Mass Radicalization to Imperial Constitutional Counter-Strategies, Wartime Rupture, and the Transfer of Power (1935–1947):** Confronted by this simultaneous consolidation of Kisan Sabhas, militant trade unions, the CPI and CSP Left, Princely Praja Mandals, and Socialist Realist cultural fronts, British Whitehall engineered its ultimate statutory containment grid: the **Government of India Act of 1935**. By pairing a proposed **All-India Federation** (conditioning central power on the entry of reactionary Princely States to neutralize the nationalist Left) with **Provincial Autonomy** (drawing Congress ministries into colonial law-and-order administration), the Raj sought to split the anti-imperialist front—setting the stage for the 1937–39 Congress ministries, theHaripura–Tripuri crisis, the 1942 Quit India **Prati Sarkars** (underground parallel governments), the INA offensive, and the post-war RIN Mutiny in Part 14.
