> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> No mass movement can fight a heavily armed empire in the streets forever without burning out. When Mahatma Gandhi abruptly called off the Non-Cooperation Movement in 1922 after violent clashes at Chauri Chaura, Indian nationalism entered a strategic breather that split the movement into two complementary jobs: one team put on suits and entered the British legislative councils to sabotage colonial budgets from the inside, while the other team stayed in the villages spinning cloth and training disciplined rural networks for the next big fight. By 1927, Britain accidentally reunited every Indian faction by appointing an all-white committee to decide India's political future, sparking a nationwide constitutional rebellion that culminated in the 1929 Lahore declaration of complete independence.
> 
> Just as this political deadline expired, the 1929 Great Depression crashed global crop prices by half while the colonial state refused to lower taxes and artificially manipulated the rupee to help British exporters. Gandhi masterfully harnessed this economic fury by targeting the most universal, everyday grievance in the land—the British government's monopoly on kitchen salt. His 241-mile march to Dandi ignited a massive pan-Indian wave of civil defiance—from coastal salt raids and tribal forest protests to frontier soldiers refusing to shoot unarmed crowds—forcing the British Viceroy to sit with Gandhi as an equal at the negotiating table and compelling the Congress to draft the social and economic blueprint of modern democratic India.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. The Post-Chauri Chaura Strategic Recalibration and the Swarajist Parliamentary Offensive (1922–1926)

* **1. The Strategic Mechanics of the Truce Phase and the 1922 Gaya Rupture:** Following the 1922 Bardoli withdrawal and Gandhi's imprisonment under **Section 124A** `(the colonial sedition statute criminalizing disaffection against the state)`, the nationalist movement shifted from an extra-parliamentary **War of Movement** `(high-intensity direct street confrontation)` to a prolonged **War of Position** `(slow ideological and institutional trench warfare during a truce phase)`. At the December 1922 Gaya Congress, delegates split over how to use this downtime: the **Pro-Changers / Swarajists** `(led by C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru, who wanted to enter colonial legislatures to sabotage them from within)` clashed with the **No-Changers** `(led by Vallabhbhai Patel and Rajendra Prasad, who opposed council entry as co-optation and insisted on grassroots village work)`. While the Pro-Changers aimed to wreck **Dyarchy** `(the 1919 dual-governance scheme that split provincial administration into British-controlled "Reserved" subjects and Indian-minister "Transferred" subjects)`, the No-Changers defeated their motion at Gaya to focus on **Constructive Work** `(grassroots rural mobilization using the Charkha [spinning wheel], Khadi [handspun cloth], and Vidyapiths [national schools])`, prompting Das and Nehru to form the **Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party** on January 1, 1923.
* **2. Institutionalization of the Swaraj Party, Constructive Work, and the 1923–1924 Accommodation:** Determined to avoid a repeat of the disastrous 1907 Surat Split, both factions compromised at the 1923 Delhi Special Session and formalized the 1924 Belgaum Pact after Britain cracked down on Bengal leaders under the **Bengal Criminal Law Amendment Ordinance** `(an emergency law permitting summary detention without trial)`. The Congress officially endorsed the Swaraj Party as its legislative wing while instituting a **Spinning Franchise** `(making the hand-spinning of 2,000 yards of yarn per month mandatory for Congress membership)`. Meanwhile, the No-Changers' rural **Ashrams** `(communal Gandhian training centers)` built deep economic and political counter-networks among peasants and Adivasis like the **Kaliparaj / Raniparaj** `(indigenous communities of South Gujarat, whom Gandhians upliftingly renamed from "black people" to "forest dwellers")`, creating the exact grassroots shock troops that later powered the 1928 Bardoli and 1930 Dandi campaigns.
* **3. Legislative Obstruction, Fiscal Audits, and the Responsivist Fragmentation:** Entering the councils in November 1923, the Swarajists forged a working majority in the Central Assembly, repeatedly vetoing the colonial **Finance Bill** `(annual tax and budget legislation)` and forcing the Viceroy to govern by naked autocracy using **Section 67B** `(the Viceroy's extraordinary certification power to enact rejected budgets by executive decree)`. After electing Vithalbhai Patel as the first Indian Speaker in 1925, the nationalist bloc famously defeated the 1928 **Public Safety Bill** `(a draconian measure to summarily deport foreign communist and labour organizers without trial)` via the Speaker's casting vote. However, following C.R. Das's death in 1925, the Swarajists fractured between **Non-Responsivists** `(Motilal Nehru's mainstream wing practicing uncompromising obstruction and staging a March 1926 walkout)` vs. **Responsivists** `(led by Lala Lajpat Rai, M.M. Malaviya, and N.C. Kelkar, who advocated "Responsive Cooperation"—accepting ministerial office and colonial patronage to protect Hindu communal and regional interests)`, severely weakening the party in the 1926 elections.

### II. Imperial Statutory Counter-Offensives and the Nationalist Constitutional Synthesis (1927–1929)

* **4. The Simon Commission Boycott and the Butler Committee on Princely Paramountcy (1927):** Invoking **Section 84A** `(the 1919 statutory clause mandating a ten-year constitutional review)` two years early to pre-empt a British Labour victory, Tory Secretary of State Lord Birkenhead appointed the all-white **Simon Commission** in November 1927, uniting Congress, Liberals, and Jinnah's Muslim League in a pan-Indian boycott marked by nationwide **Hartals** `(mass civic strikes and shutdowns)` and the fatal police lathi-charge on Lala Lajpat Rai. Simultaneously, Whitehall appointed the **Butler Committee (1927–29)** to erect an imperial firewall between British India and the 560+ Princely States. The Committee declared that **Paramountcy** `(the British Crown's supreme, undefined, and elastic prerogative over Indian Princes)` enabled British Residents to crush democratic **Praja Mandals** `(people's political associations inside Princely States)` and could never be transferred to an Indian nationalist cabinet without the Princes' explicit consent.
* **5. Constitutional Bargaining: From the Delhi Proposals to the Motilal Nehru Report and Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1927–1929):** Answering Birkenhead's challenge to draft an agreed Indian constitution, Jinnah's **Delhi Proposals (December 1927)** offered to trade **Separate Electorates** `(communal voting rolls where minorities vote only for co-religionist candidates)` for **Joint Electorates** `(unified common voter rolls with reserved minority seats)` if Muslims received one-third central seats, proportional majorities in Punjab and Bengal, and three new Muslim-majority provinces (Sindh, NWFP, Baluchistan). However, under Hindu Mahasabha pressure, the **Motilal Nehru Report (August 1928)**—which demanded immediate **Dominion Status** `(autonomous self-government within the British Commonwealth)`, 19 Fundamental Rights, and **Linguistic Provinces** `(redrawing provincial boundaries by spoken language)`—rejected reserved seats in Punjab and Bengal and vested **Residuary Powers** `(unenumerated legislative powers not assigned to either tier)` in a strong Centre. When the December 1928 Calcutta Convention voted down Jinnah's amendments, he promulgated **Jinnah's Fourteen Points (March 1929)**, demanding a loose federation with provincial residuary powers, retention of separate electorates, one-third central quotas, and a **Three-Fourths Communal Veto** `(blocking any legislative bill opposed by 75% of a community's members)`.
* **6. The Radical Youth Upsurge, Irwin's Diwali Declaration, and the 1929 Lahore Purna Swaraj Rupture:** Dissatisfied with the older leadership's quest for **Dominion Status** `(self-rule inside the British Empire)` vs. **Purna Swaraj** `(complete independence severing all British ties)`, radical youth leaders Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose founded the **Independence for India League (August 1928)** to push for complete independence and a socialist republic. After Viceroy Lord Irwin's vague **Diwali Declaration (October 1929)** failed to guarantee that the upcoming Round Table Conference would immediately draft a Dominion constitution as demanded by the nationalist **Delhi Manifesto**, the Congress one-year ultimatum expired. At midnight on December 31, 1929, at the **Lahore Congress**, President Jawaharlal Nehru declared the Nehru Report lapsed, unfurled the Tricolour for **Purna Swaraj**, repudiated the colonial public debt, and launched the **January 26, 1930 Independence Pledge** authorizing nationwide Civil Disobedience.

### III. The Political Economy of the Great Depression, the Salt Monopoly, and the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1931)

* **7. Agrarian Price Collapse, Monetary Orthodoxy, and Gandhi's Eleven Demands (January 1930):** The 1929 Great Depression triggered an agrarian **Scissors Crisis** `(agricultural crop prices plunged over 50% while colonial cash taxes and debts remained rigidly fixed, doubling the real tax burden)` across **Ryotwari** `(direct peasant-proprietor tax)`, **Mahalwari** `(village-estate tax)`, and **Zamindari** `(landlord-rent)` tracts, driving peasants deeper into debt to the **Sahukar** `(rural moneylender)`. Simultaneously, Britain clung to an overvalued **1s 6d vs. 1s 4d Rupee-Sterling Ratio** `(pegging the rupee at 1 shilling 6 pence—which gave British imports a 12.5% price subsidy—instead of the historical 1 shilling 4 pence rate demanded by Indian industrialists)` to remit **Home Charges** `(annual colonial tribute paid to London for debt, pensions, and military costs)`, forcing ruined Indian families into massive **Distress Gold Exports** `(selling ₹300+ crore of family jewelry to pay taxes)`. On January 31, 1930, Gandhi united capitalists, peasants, and subalterns by issuing his **Eleven Demands**—combining bourgeois demands (a 1s 4d rupee, textile tariffs, coastal shipping reservation), a 50% land revenue cut, total prohibition of **Abkari** `(colonial liquor/drug excise revenue)`, a 50% military/civil salary cut, and the abolition of the regressive **Salt Monopoly**.
* **8. The Dandi Salt March and the Spatial-Ideological Mechanics of Satyagraha (March–April 1930):** By targeting the **Indian Salt Act of 1882** `(which enforced a state salt monopoly and a 2,400% tax on production cost, hitting starving labourers hardest)`, Gandhi chose an issue of universal biological necessity that transcended internal class and caste divides. Between March 12 and April 6, 1930, Gandhi and 78 Ashramites marched 241 miles from Sabarmati to Dandi through Gujarat's pre-organized peasant heartland, amplified by underground **Congress Patrikas** `(cyclostyled nationalist news bulletins)` and prompting over 300 **Patels and Talatis** `(village headmen and revenue accountants)` to resign their colonial posts before Gandhi ritually broke the salt law at Dandi beach.
* **9. Pan-Indian Regional Mobilizations, Subaltern Insurgencies, and Working-Class Interventions:** Coastal salt marches erupted at Vedaranyam (led by C. Rajagopalachari) and Payyanur (led by K. Kelappan), while Gandhi's arrest under **Bombay Regulation XXV of 1827** `(an archaic statute permitting indefinite detention without trial)` was followed by Sarojini Naidu's May 21 non-violent raid on the **Dharasana Salt Works**, where police brutally beat unarmed **Satyagrahis** `(practitioners of non-violent civil resistance)` in view of the global press. Across the frontiers and hinterlands, the struggle took militant regional forms: in Peshawar, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's **Khudai Khidmatgars / Surkh Posh** `("Servants of God" or "Red Shirts"—a 50,000-strong non-violent Pathan movement)` faced armored-car massacres until Chandra Singh Garhwali's Hindu platoon refused orders to fire on the Muslim crowd; in Manipur and the Naga Hills, 16-year-old Rani Gaidinliu led the **Heraka** `("Pure"—an indigenous Zeliangrong Naga socio-religious uprising)` against colonial **Khajana** `(house tax)` and **Pothang / Begar** `(forced unpaid porterage labour)`; in Sholapur, 50,000 striking textile workers ran a parallel civic administration before Martial Law hangings; in Bihar and Bengal, landlocked peasants refused the **Chaukidari Tax** `(a hated rural levy funding village watchmen who acted as police informers)`; in Central India, Adivasis launched **Forest Satyagrahas** defying colonial **Pullari** `(cattle-grazing fees)`; in Gujarat, 80,000 peasants staged a mass **Hijrat** `(collective protest migration across the border into Baroda State to escape tax confiscations)`; and in Assam, students revolted against the **Cunningham Circular** `(a May 1930 directive forcing students and parents to sign political good-behavior bonds)`.

### IV. Imperial Constitutional Diplomacy, the Karachi Socio-Economic Charter, and the Round Table Impasse (1930–1934)

* **10. The First Round Table Conference and the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1930–1931):** Boycotted by the imprisoned Congress, the **First Round Table Conference (Nov 1930–Jan 1931)** saw British strategists, Liberals, and Indian Princes agree on an **All-India Federation** `(a constitutional scheme combining British Indian provinces with autocratic Princely nominees to permanently outvote the nationalist Congress at the Centre)`. Needing Congress participation to make this framework viable—and pressured by boycotts that halved British cloth imports—Viceroy Lord Irwin signed the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact / Delhi Pact (March 5, 1931)**, releasing non-violent political prisoners, remitting fines, restoring unsold forfeited lands, and legalizing coastal domestic salt-making and peaceful picketing in exchange for suspending Civil Disobedience, though Irwin refused to return lands already sold to third parties or commute the death sentences of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru.
* **11. The 1931 Karachi Congress Session: Constitutional Rights and Economic Dirigisme:** Meeting six days after Bhagat Singh's execution under President Vallabhbhai Patel, the **Karachi Congress (March 1931)** ratified the Delhi Pact while adopting the landmark **Resolution on Fundamental Rights and National Economic Programme**. Blending liberal democracy with socialist **Economic Dirigisme** `(direct state ownership or control over key industries, minerals, railways, and waterways)`, the Karachi Charter promised universal adult suffrage, secularism, free primary education, substantial rent/revenue cuts, tax exemptions for uneconomic holdings, living wages, trade union rights, and a ₹500/month civil salary cap—laying the direct foundation for Parts III and IV of independent India's 1950 Constitution.
* **12. The Second and Third Round Table Conferences, Coercive Martial Rule, and the 1934 Withdrawal:** Attending the **Second Round Table Conference (Sept–Dec 1931)** as the sole Congress delegate against a new hardline Tory-dominated British National Government, Gandhi faced a deadlocked Minorities Committee where sectional delegates signed a **Minorities Pact** demanding separate electorates—clashing sharply with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar over separate electorates for the **Depressed Classes** `(Dalits)` before Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald subsequently issued the unilateral **Communal Award (August 1932)**. Upon Gandhi's return, the new Viceroy Lord Willingdon launched a pre-planned blitzkrieg of **Civil Martial Law** `(four sweeping emergency ordinances outlawing every tier of the Congress, seizing its properties/funds, and gagging the press)`, arresting over 120,000 Satyagrahis during the second phase of Civil Disobedience (1932–34) while Britain imposed the **Ottawa Imperial Preference Agreement (1932)** `(locking Indian tariffs to favor British Empire imports)` and rubber-stamped its 1933 White Paper at the Congress-boycotted **Third Round Table Conference (Nov–Dec 1932)**. Recognizing mass organizational fatigue after years of property confiscations, Gandhi shifted toward his anti-untouchability (*Harijan*) campaign and Individual Satyagraha in 1933 before the AICC formally ratified the withdrawal of Civil Disobedience at Patna in **May 1934**.
* **13. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Mass Satyagraha Suspension to Left-Wing Radicalization and the 1935 Constitutional Arena:** Although the 1930–34 struggle ended without immediate independence, it established the Congress as India's parallel hegemonic state in waiting. However, disillusionment with Gandhian truce cycles amid ongoing Depression misery propelled a young radical cohort—led by Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Bose, Jayaprakash Narayan, and Swami Sahajanand—toward independent class mobilization, directly sparking the 1934 formation of the **Congress Socialist Party (CSP)** and the 1936 **All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)**.
