> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> When India gained independence in 1947 after two centuries of colonial extraction, its economy was trapped: citizens were too poor to save, giving businesses neither the capital nor the customer demand to build modern factories. To break this deadlock, India erected a centralized command state that poured scarce public money into giant steel mills and dams while wrapping the private sector in a suffocating web of licenses, size caps, and import bans. Instead of creating self-reliance, these rules shielded inefficient monopolies, kept small factories permanently tiny, and forced nationalized banks to fund ballooning government deficits.
> 
> By the 1980s, India only managed to speed up growth by borrowing heavily at home and abroad. When the Soviet Union collapsed and the 1990 Gulf War doubled oil prices, foreign lenders panicked and pulled their money out—leaving India in June 1991 with barely two weeks of cash for essential imports and forcing the government to airlift national gold reserves as collateral. Seizing this existential crisis, reformers dismantled the command apparatus in July 1991: they devalued and floated the Rupee, scrapped industrial licensing, slashed tax and tariff walls, and cleaned up bank balance sheets. Over the next three decades, India evolved from Soviet-style command planning to indicative market guidance, eventually replacing the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog in 2015 and adopting "Disciplined Swadeshi" by 2026—backing strategic domestic manufacturing with strict expiration dates and global export competition.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. Pre-Independence Planning Ideologies and the Institutional Architecture of the Command State

* **1. Competing Pre-1950 Blueprints: Technocratic Industrialism, Capitalist Corporatism, and Decentralist Agrarianism:** At independence, leaders agreed free markets alone could not break the **Ragnar Nurkse vicious circle of poverty** (a self-reinforcing trap where low income breeds low savings and weak buyer demand, keeping investment and income perpetually low), but disagreed over the **Incremental Capital-Output Ratio [ICOR]** (the extra units of capital investment needed to produce one extra unit of output; a lower ICOR means higher capital efficiency) and **wage goods** (everyday necessities like food and cloth bought by workers' wages). High-capital industrialization models included the **Visvesvaraya Plan (1934)** (shifting half the farm workforce into tariff-protected factories), the **National Planning Committee [NPC, 1938]** (mandating state ownership of heavy and key industries), and the **Bombay Plan (1944–45)** (where eight top industrialists endorsed state-funded heavy infrastructure, **created money / deficit financing** [printing new central bank money to cover budget gaps], and abolishing ***Zamindari* intermediaries** [colonial landlord tax-collectors] to de-risk private corporate expansion). Conversely, low-ICOR decentralist blueprints included the **Gandhian Plan (1944)** (championing ***Gram Swaraj*** [self-governing village republics] and ***Khadi*** [hand-spun cloth]), M.N. Roy’s Marxist **People’s Plan (1945)** (nationalizing land and capping factory profits at 3% to boost mass consumer purchasing power), and Jaiprakash Narayan’s **Sarvodaya Plan (1950)** (blending ***Bhoodan*** [voluntary land-gift movement] agrarian socialism with ***Loka-niti*** [decentralized people's politics]).
* **2. The Extra-Constitutional Planning Apparatus: The Planning Commission (1950) and National Development Council (1952):** Rather than passing a law in Parliament, the Cabinet created the **Planning Commission (March 15, 1950)** and the **National Development Council [NDC, August 6, 1952]** (the apex federal body of the PM, Union Cabinet, and State Chief Ministers that approved plans) via simple executive resolutions, making both **extra-constitutional and non-statutory bodies** (institutions neither mentioned in the Constitution nor created by an Act of Parliament) chaired **ex-officio** (automatically by virtue of holding another office) by the Prime Minister. This setup sideline-channeled the constitutional **Finance Commission [Article 280]** (a quasi-judicial body appointed every five years to share taxes under **Article 270** and statutory grants under **Article 275**) because the Planning Commission used **Article 282** (a miscellaneous spending clause) to hand out massive discretionary **Central Plan Assistance** and **Centrally Sponsored Schemes [CSS]** (central schemes implemented by states with tied rules). Furthermore, splitting state budgets into **Plan Expenditure** (spending on new Five-Year Plan projects) vs. **Non-Plan Expenditure** (routine spending to maintain existing assets, pay salaries, and service debt) created a severe **moral hazard** (a skewed incentive to take wasteful actions because someone else bears the cost), as states eagerly built new schools and canals with central "Plan" funds only to let them rot once maintenance shifted to starved "Non-Plan" budgets.

### II. The Mathematical Architecture of Early Indian Planning: Harrod-Domar to Mahalanobis (1951–1961)

* **3. The First Five-Year Plan (1951–1956): The Harrod-Domar Growth Identity and Wage-Goods Stabilization:** Facing Partition refugees and food shortages, economist K.N. Raj anchored the First Plan in the **Harrod-Domar growth model** (`g = s / ICOR`, where real GDP growth rate `g` equals the national savings rate `s` divided by ICOR `k`), which recognizes that investment simultaneously boosts buyer demand via the **Keynesian investment multiplier** (how ₹1 of new spending ripples through the economy to generate multiple rupees of income) and expands physical supply under a **fixed-coefficients production function** (an economic setup where abundant labor cannot substitute for scarce physical machinery). Because India's baseline **Average Propensity to Save [APS = S/Y]** (the fraction of total national income saved) was barely ~5.5%–9%, Raj drove a wedge between APS and the **Marginal Propensity to Save [MPS = ΔS/ΔY]** (the fraction of *new, incremental* income saved, set at 20%) to gradually raise national savings without starving current consumption. By channeling outlays into agriculture and giant river dams (Bhakra-Nangal, Hirakud, DVC) alongside good monsoons, realized ICOR dropped to an ultra-efficient `1.8 : 1`, beating the 2.1% growth target at **3.6%** and triggering **Wholesale Price Index [WPI] deflation** (a drop in bulk goods prices as farm harvests outpaced money supply).
* **4. The Second Five-Year Plan (1956–1961): The Mahalanobis Two-Sector/Four-Sector Strategy and Import-Substitution Industrialization (ISI):** Driven by **Export Pessimism / the Prebisch-Singer Thesis** (the structuralist belief that raw-commodity exporters face steadily worsening trade terms against manufactured imports and therefore cannot rely on trade to buy foreign machinery), P.C. Mahalanobis pivoted India toward **Import-Substitution Industrialization [ISI]** (replacing foreign imports with domestic manufacturing behind high tariff walls). His **Mahalanobis Two-Sector/Four-Sector Model** split investment between the **Capital Goods Sector [`K`-sector]** ("machines that make machines" like the Bhilai, Durgapur, and Rourkela steel plants, allocated investment fraction `λₖ = 0.33` with a low output-capital ratio `βₖ = 1/ICOR`) vs. the **Consumer Goods Sector [`C`-sector]** (allocated `λc = 0.67`, subdivided into **`C₁` factory consumer goods**, **`C₂` labor-intensive cottage/handloom goods**, and **`C₃` services**), accepting an **Intertemporal Crossover Trade-Off** (sacrificing short-term consumer output and jobs so compounding machine-making capacity yields steeper growth after a future crossover date `t*`). However, importing heavy equipment for steel mills triggered the **1957–58 Foreign Exchange Crisis**, while ignoring the rival **Vakil-Brahmananda Wage-Goods Model** (which warned that food and basic necessities, not steel, bottleneck growth in a country with **disguised unemployment**—surplus farm workers whose removal doesn't reduce crop output) caused 30% inflation as `K`-sector wages chased scarce food.
* **5. The Industrial Policy Resolution (IPR) of 1956: Statutory Tripartition and the "Commanding Heights":** To legally enforce the Mahalanobis allocation matrix and build a **"Socialistic Pattern of Society"** within a **Mixed Economy** (where public and private enterprises coexist), the **IPR 1956** ("The Economic Constitution of India") mandated that the State capture the **"Commanding Heights of the Economy"** (sovereign control over strategic basic industries and infrastructure). It divided all industry into three tiers: **Schedule A [17 industries]** (exclusive state monopoly for all new units, including arms, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy machinery, coal, oil, airlines, railways, telecom, and electricity) vs. **Schedule B [12 industries]** (mixed sector where the state progressively leads new ventures while private firms supplement, such as aluminum, machine tools, fertilizers, and basic drugs) vs. **Schedule C** (all remaining consumer and light industries, left to private initiative but trapped under strict central licensing and price controls).

### III. Trajectory of the Five-Year Plans (Third to Twelfth Plans) and Federal Transfer Formulas

* **6. Shocks, Plan Holidays, and Distributional Shifts: Third to Seventh Plans (1961–1990):** The **Third Plan (1961–66)** used the Chakravarty-Sandy **inter-industry input-output consistency model** (a mathematical matrix ensuring each sector's output matches the raw material needs of other sectors) to reach a **Rostowian take-off stage** (self-sustaining growth), but the 1962 China War, 1965 Pakistan War, and twin droughts crashed growth to 2.4%, forcing **"ship-to-mouth" dependence on US Public Law 480 [PL-480]** (concessional American wheat aid) and a **36.5% Rupee Devaluation (June 1966)** (officially lowering the Rupee's fixed value from ₹4.76 to ₹7.50 per USD). Suspending five-year planning for three annual **Plan Holidays (1966–69)**, India launched the **Green Revolution**—combining **High-Yielding Variety [HYV]** dwarf seeds, assured irrigation, chemical fertilizers, minimum price floors via the **Agricultural Prices Commission [APC/CACP, 1965]**, and public stockpiling via the **Food Corporation of India [FCI, 1965]**. After the **Fourth Plan (1969–74)** deepened state **dirigisme** (direct state control over banks, monopolies, and forex) amidst the 1971 War and 1973 OPEC Oil Shock, the **Fifth Plan (1974–78)** targeted poverty (*Garibi Hatao*) via the **Minimum Needs Programme [MNP]** (a national floor for eight basic rural/social services) before the Janata government terminated it a year early to introduce Gunnar Myrdal and D.T. Lakdawala’s **Rolling Plan (1978–80)** (a flexible 3-tier system revising 1-year, 5-year, and 15–20-year targets annually). Finally, the **Sixth (1980–85)** and **Seventh (1985–90)** Plans shattered Raj Krishna's **"Hindu Rate of Growth"** (India's 1950–1980 stagnation trap of ~3.5% GDP and ~1.3% per-capita growth) by hitting 5.7% and 6.01% through rural job schemes, incremental **broad-banding** (allowing factories to switch production between similar product lines under one license), and massive deficit borrowing.
* **7. The Gadgil-Mukherjee Formula and the Political Economy of Central Plan Assistance:** To replace political favoritism in Article 282 block grants with objective math, D.R. Gadgil (1969) and Pranab Mukherjee (1991) established the **Gadgil-Mukherjee Formula** for allocating **Normal Central Assistance [NCA]** across states. First, 30% of the pool was top-sliced for **Special Category States [SCS]** (11 hilly, tribal, and border states with non-viable tax bases, funded on a highly concessional **90% Grant : 10% Loan** mix) vs. the remaining 70% distributed among **General Category States** on a **30% Grant : 70% Loan** mix. A General state's share was calculated across four weights: **60% Population** (frozen at the **1971 Census** so states lowering fertility weren't penalized with smaller grants), **25% Per-Capita Income** (split between a **20% Deviation Method** reserved strictly for states below the national average income and a **5% Distance Method** rewarding all states based on their income gap from the richest state), **7.5% Fiscal/Developmental Performance** (tax effort, spending discipline, population control, female literacy, land reforms), and **7.5% Special Problems** (droughts, floods, slums).
* **8. Indicative Planning and Inclusive Sustainability: Eighth to Twelfth Plans (1992–2017):** After the 1991 reforms, the **Eighth Plan (1992–97)** shifted India from Soviet-style **Imperative Command Planning** (where the state dictates physical production quotas) to French-style **Indicative Planning** (where the state uses tax, price, and monetary signals to guide private investment while focusing public funds on human capital and infrastructure) using the **John W. Mellor model** (open-market growth powered by farm demand and human development). While the **Ninth Plan (1997–2002)** focused on seven **Basic Minimum Services [BMS]** amidst the Asian Crisis and Pokhran-II sanctions, the **Tenth Plan (2002–07)** pioneered quantifiable **Monitorable Targets** and state-wise growth breakdowns as private **Gross Fixed Capital Formation [GFCF]** (net spending on physical assets like factories and machinery) crossed 33% of GDP. The **Eleventh Plan (2007–12)** achieved a record **8.0% growth** under *"Faster and More Inclusive Growth,"* though unwithdrawn post-2008 crisis stimulus ignited inflation and the **Twin Balance Sheet [TBS] crisis** (simultaneous financial distress in over-indebted infrastructure firms and bad-loan-crippled public banks); finally, the **Twelfth Plan (2012–17)** added *"Sustainable Growth"* across three policy scenarios (*Strong Inclusive, Insufficient Action, Policy Logjam*) and 25 targets—including bringing the **Total Fertility Rate [TFR]** (average children born per woman) to the 2.1 replacement level—before five-year plans were retired in 2017.

### IV. The License-Permit-Quota Raj, Financial Repression, and Regulatory Strangulation

* **9. Industrial Licensing (IDRA 1951), Small-Scale Reservation, and Monopolies Regulation (MRTP Act 1969):** Microeconomic controls hardened into the **"License-Permit-Quota Raj"** (C. Rajagopalachari's term for the bureaucratic maze of permits needed to operate a business), which killed **Schumpeterian competition** (dynamic rivalry where innovative entrants displace stagnant incumbents) and pushed economy-wide ICOR above 5.5. Under the **Industries (Development and Regulation) Act [IDRA, 1951]**, firms needed prior central licenses to open a factory, make a new product variant, change location, or expand capacity by >25%; as exposed by the **Hazari (1967)** and **Dutt (1969)** Committees, big conglomerates gamed this via **pre-emptive capacity foreclosure** (applying for dozens of proxy licenses to exhaust the government's five-year industry quota and leaving them unbuilt to legally block competitors). Simultaneously, **Small-Scale Industry [SSI] Reservation** (banning medium/large firms from manufacturing 836+ everyday consumer goods) created a perverse **"Dwarfism" incentive trap and the "Missing Middle"** (an industrial structure split between millions of tiny informal sheds and a few giants, with no mid-sized 200–500 worker factories) because growing firms lost their reserved market, tax breaks, and cheap credit the moment they crossed tiny machinery caps—prompting owners to split production across fake ***benami*** (proxy-named) units. Worse, Chapter III of the **Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices [MRTP] Act, 1969** penalized sheer corporate asset size (`≥ ₹20 crore`, raised to `₹100 crore` in 1985) rather than cartel conduct, blocking Indian plants from reaching **Minimum Efficient Technical Scale [MES]** (the factory scale required to minimize unit production costs).
* **10. External Insulation (FERA 1973) and Financial Repression via Bank Nationalization (1969 & 1980):** Externally, **Section 29 of the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act [FERA, 1973]** capped foreign ownership in Indian subsidiaries at **40%** and turned currency violations into non-bailable crimes with presumed ***mens rea*** (reverse burden of proof where criminal intent is legally assumed and the accused must prove innocence), driving out IBM and Coca-Cola in 1977 and choking off non-debt **Foreign Direct Investment [FDI]** (long-term foreign investment in physical factories and equity). Domestically, because private corporate-owned banks directed <2.2% of credit to agriculture, Indira Gandhi executed **Bank Nationalization in two waves—14 banks (≥ ₹50 Cr deposits) on July 19, 1969, and 6 banks (≥ ₹200 Cr deposits) on April 15, 1980**—taking state control over 91%+ of banking. While the RBI’s **1:4 branch licensing rule** (requiring 4 new unbanked rural branches for every 1 urban branch), **Regional Rural Banks [RRBs, 1975]**, and mandatory **40% Priority Sector Lending [PSL]** (earmarking 40% of bank credit for farms and small enterprises) expanded rural savings and funded the Green Revolution, banking soon degenerated into textbook **Financial Repression (McKinnon-Shaw)** and **Fiscal Dominance** (where central bank policy is subordinated to financing cheap government deficits). By 1990–91, the State confiscated **53.5% to 63.5%** of bank **Net Demand and Time Liabilities [NDTL]** (total customer deposits) via a **38.5% Statutory Liquidity Ratio [SLR]** (forcing banks to buy low-interest government bonds) and a **15% Cash Reserve Ratio [CRR]** (plus a 10% incremental CRR locked as cash with the RBI), while political *Loan Melas* (mass rallies handing out unappraised loans) and lax accounting hid massive bank insolvency and crowded out private corporate borrowers at punitive 18%–20% interest rates.

### V. Anatomy of the 1991 Balance of Payments (BoP) Crisis: Twin Deficits and External Shocks

* **11. The 1980s Fiscal Profligacy, Twin Deficits, and Short-Term Debt Vulnerability:** India's 1980s growth spurt was fueled by unsustainable deficit spending that transmitted directly into an external payments crisis through the macroeconomic **Twin Deficits Identity** (`CAD ≡ (I - Sₚ) + (G - T)`, proving that when government deficit spending `[G - T]` plus private investment `[I]` exceeds private savings `[Sₚ]`, the excess domestic demand spills directly into an external **Current Account Deficit [CAD = Imports M - Exports X]**). As defence outlays, subsidies, and interest bills pushed the Centre's **Revenue Account into permanent Revenue Deficit** (borrowing merely to fund routine consumption and salaries rather than capital assets, hitting 3.3% of GDP) and the **Gross Fiscal Deficit `[G - T]` to 7.8%–8.4% of GDP** (9.4% combined with states), the government covered the gap via **Automatic Monetization through *ad hoc* Treasury Bills** (forcing the RBI to print fresh **Reserve Money `[M₀]`** to buy unlimited 91-day government IOUs at a frozen 4.6% rate). This money printing drove WPI inflation to **16.7%**, causing **Real Effective Exchange Rate [REER] overvaluation** (where high domestic inflation makes Indian exports artificially expensive abroad) and widening the CAD to **3.1% of GDP ($9.7 billion)** in FY 1990–91. Because FERA blocked FDI, India plugged this CAD with high-cost **External Commercial Borrowings [ECBs]**, short-term **Bankers' Acceptances** (<1-year trade credits), and volatile **Foreign Currency Non-Resident [FCNR] deposits**, pushing the **Debt-Service Ratio** (share of current export/invisible receipts consumed by foreign debt repayments) to **35.3%** and short-term debt to **382% of forex reserves**.
* **12. The Dual Geopolitical Shocks of 1990–1991: The Soviet Collapse, Gulf War, and the Gold Pledge:** In 1990–91, two external shocks triggered a classic emerging-market **sudden stop and liquidity run** (an abrupt freeze in foreign credit rollovers combined with panicked depositor flight). First, the collapse of the Soviet bloc shattered bilateral **Rupee-Ruble Clearing Agreements** (barter-like trade settled entirely in non-convertible Rupees), slashing India's eastern bloc exports by >50% and forcing hard-dollar spot purchases for oil and defense equipment. Second, Iraq's **August 1990 invasion of Kuwait (Gulf War)** doubled crude prices from $15 to >$35/barrel—hiking India's **POL [Petroleum, Oil, and Lubricants] import bill** by >60% to $6.0 billion—while wiping out Gulf worker **Invisibles / Private Transfers** (remittances) and requiring the costly airlift of 1,50,000 Indians. Amidst rapid prime ministerial turnover, rating agencies Moody's and S&P cut India to **Speculative [Junk] Grade**, prompting foreign banks to freeze short-term credit rollovers and NRIs to pull out over **$330 million/month** in FCNR deposits. As forex reserves crashed to **$1.1 billion** (barely **two weeks of import cover**), India averted its first-ever sovereign default through the secret **1991 Gold Pledge**: leasing 20 MT of confiscated gold via SBI to UBS Zurich ($200M) in May and physically airlifting **46.91 MT of RBI monetary gold** to the Bank of England and Bank of Japan ($405M) in July 1991.

### VI. The July 1991 Structural Adjustment Program and the Market-Led Regulatory State

* **13. Macroeconomic Stabilization, IMF Conditionalities, and the Exchange Rate Regime Transition:** Backed by an IMF Stand-By loan ($2.2B) and World Bank Structural Adjustment facility, PM P.V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh launched the **July 1991 New Economic Policy** along two tracks: **Short-Run Macroeconomic Stabilization** (compressing excess demand by cutting the fiscal deficit from 7.8% to 5.9% of GDP, abolishing export **Cash Compensatory Support [CCS]**, and signing the 1994/1997 RBI agreements that replaced automatic *ad hoc* T-Bill money printing with strictly capped, market-rate **Ways and Means Advances [WMA]**) vs. **Medium-Term Structural Adjustment** (dismantling supply-side licensing, tariff, and banking rigidities). To restore export competitiveness under the **Marshall-Lerner Condition** (the rule that currency depreciation improves the trade balance if combined export and import price elasticities exceed 1), the RBI executed a two-step **"Hop, Skip, and Jump" Rupee Devaluation of ~18–19% on July 1 & 3, 1991** (moving USD-INR from ₹21.14 to ₹25.80) and issued tradable **Exim Scrips** (import permits worth 30% of export earnings). Following the C. Rangarajan Committee blueprint, India transitioned in March 1992 to **LERMS [Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System]**—a **40:60 dual exchange rate** where 40% of forex receipts were surrendered at the official RBI rate for essential government imports (oil, fertilizer, defense) and 60% converted at market rates—before unifying into a **Market-Determined Managed Float on March 1, 1993** (where the market sets the rate and the RBI intervenes only to smooth extreme volatility) and accepting **IMF Article VIII Current Account Convertibility on August 20, 1994** (full freedom to convert currency for trade, travel, and remittances, later replacing criminal FERA with the civil **Foreign Exchange Management Act [FEMA, 1999]**).
* **14. The Industrial Policy Statement of July 24, 1991: Dismantling the Command Apparatus:** Tabled hours before the historic 1991 Budget, the **Industrial Policy Statement of July 24, 1991** dismantled the License Raj across four pillars. First, it executed sweeping **Delicensing**—abolishing IDRA industrial licenses for all except 18 sensitive sectors (pruned today to just **4 sectors**: aerospace/defense, industrial explosives, hazardous chemicals, and tobacco), requiring only an informational **Industrial Entrepreneur Memorandum [IEM]** while scrapping the **Mandatory Convertibility Clause** (which had let public lenders convert corporate loans into hostile equity takeovers). Second, it repealed **MRTP Chapter III pre-entry asset limits (₹100 Cr)** so conglomerates could expand and merge freely without central permission—paving the way for the **Competition Act, 2002** and the **Competition Commission of India [CCI]** (which police anti-competitive cartels and abuse of dominance rather than company size). Third, it slashed **Public Sector Reservation** from 17 Schedule A industries down to 8 (and today just **2 core sectors**: atomic energy and railway operations), cut off automatic bailouts by referring sick PSUs to the **Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction [BIFR]** backed by a **National Renewal Fund [NRF]** for worker **Voluntary Retirement Schemes [VRS]**, and initiated minority **Disinvestment** in profitable PSUs. Fourth, it broke the 40% FERA trap by introducing **Automatic Route FDI up to 51% foreign equity** in 34 priority industries alongside an empowered **Foreign Investment Promotion Board [FIPB]** for higher stakes.
* **15. Fiscal and Financial Sector Re-Engineering: The Raja Chelliah and Narasimham Committee Reforms:** Operating on the logic of the **Laffer Curve** (that slashing confiscatory tax rates—like India's 97.75% peak in 1974 and 56% in 1991—reduces evasion and boosts total revenue) and the **Lerner Symmetry Theorem** (that high import tariffs act as an implicit tax on exports by raising input costs and appreciating the currency), the **Raja Chelliah Tax Reforms Committee (1991–93)** compressed personal income tax into three slabs (20%, 30%, 40%, cut to **10%, 20%, 30% in 1997**), lowered corporate tax to 40%, slashed **Peak Non-Agri Customs Duties from 150% down toward 10%**, expanded input tax credits from **MODVAT to CENVAT** (manufacturing-stage **Value Added Taxes [VAT]** that prevent cascading tax-on-tax), and invoked the Union's **Entry 97 residuary power** to birth the **Service Tax (1994–95)**—laying the foundation for the **Goods and Services Tax [GST, 2017]**. Simultaneously, the **Narasimham Committee I (1991) and II (1998)** dismantled financial repression by slashing **SLR from 38.5% to 25%** (now 18%) and **CRR from 15% toward 3–5%** (now 4%), shifting government borrowing to market-rate G-Sec auctions, deregulating interest rates, licensing **New Generation Private Banks** (HDFC, ICICI, Axis), and allowing PSBs to raise up to 49% public equity. To expose hidden bad loans, Narasimham introduced **Basel Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio [CRAR]** (mandatory 8%–9% bank equity buffers against risky loans) and objective **IRACP [Income Recognition, Asset Classification, and Provisioning] norms**—enforcing a strict **90-day overdue rule** to classify defaulted loans into **Sub-Standard, Doubtful, and Loss Non-Performing Assets [NPAs]**—backed by **Debt Recovery Tribunals [DRTs, 1993]** and the **SARFAESI Act (2002)** (allowing banks to seize and auction pledged collateral without court delays, later reinforced by the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code [IBC, 2016]**).
* **16. From the Planning Commission to NITI Aayog (2015) and the Doctrine of "Disciplined Swadeshi" (2026):** Because private investment now drove >75% of capital formation and the **Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015)** raised untied state tax devolution from 32% to 42%, the Cabinet dissolved the Planning Commission and established **NITI Aayog on January 1, 2015** as a policy think-tank with **zero fund-allocation power** (while the **2017–18 Budget abolished the Plan vs. Non-Plan expenditure divide** in favor of **Revenue vs. Capital Expenditure**). NITI Aayog replaced Five-Year Plans with a 15-Year Vision (*Viksit Bharat @ 2047*), 7-Year Strategy, and 3-Year Action Agenda anchored in two pillars: **Cooperative Federalism** (bottom-up strategy with all State CMs and UT Lt. Governors sitting permanently inside the **Governing Council** and **Regional Councils**) vs. **Competitive Federalism** (spurring states and local bodies via public, real-time **delta-ranking outcome indices** under the **Aspirational Districts [ADP, 2018]** and **Aspirational Blocks [ABP, 2023]** Programmes). Culminating this 76-year evolution, the **Economic Survey 2025–26** codifies **"Disciplined Swadeshi"**—rejecting both pre-1991 unconditional **autarky** (permanent closed-economy protectionism without export accountability) and passive *laissez-faire* (leaving critical supply chains vulnerable) by restricting state support to strategic frontier sectors (**14 Production Linked Incentive [PLI] schemes, India Semiconductor Mission 2.0, Biopharma SHAKTI, Rare Earth Corridors**) governed by hard **5-to-6-year sunset clauses** (automatic expiration dates), audited ex-post **Domestic Value Addition [DVA]**, and **Global Value Chain [GVC]** export competition via **New-Generation Free Trade Agreements** (UAE, Australia, EFTA, UK).
