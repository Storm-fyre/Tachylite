> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> Every rupee in your pocket is caught in a constant tug-of-war between how fast buyers spend money and how fast farms and factories can actually produce goods. In India, prices rarely rise just because the economy is booming; instead, foreign crude oil spikes, erratic monsoon rains, and mandi middleman markups repeatedly shock food and fuel costs. Because India's old 2012 inflation and factory-output yardsticks were still measuring a bygone era of cassette tapes and 46% household food budgets, the government rolled out massive statistical overhauls in 2026—updating both the retail inflation basket (to base year 2024) and the industrial production index (to base year 2022–23) to reflect modern digital spending and green manufacturing.
> 
> Yet when retail inflation stays stubbornly high, it silently rewires household balance sheets. Rising prices eat away the real return on bank deposits, push workers into higher tax brackets without making them any richer, and make Indian exports uncompetitive abroad. To protect their wealth and maintain lifestyles, Indian households have shifted money out of bank deposits into physical real estate, stock-market SIPs, and swipe-now FinTech personal loans—squeezing net financial savings and forcing the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to clamp down on risky unsecured retail lending before shadow-bank defaults can infect the main banking system.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. Conceptual Taxonomy, Macroeconomic Phillips Curves, and Subcontinental Price Shocks

* **1. Structural Origins of Price Level Instability:** Under the **Quantity Theory of Money** (`M · V = P · Y`, where money supply `M` times circulation speed `V` equals price level `P` times real output `Y`), when spending outpaces real production capacity (`Y*`), prices (`P`) must rise. This occurs through three transmission loops: **Demand-Pull Inflation** (excess fiscal or credit spending creating a **Positive Output Gap** `Y - Y* > 0` that bids up prices) vs. **Cost-Push Inflation** (external supply shocks like crude oil spikes or crop failures shifting short-run supply leftward, alongside **Profit-Push / Markup Inflation** where cartels widen profit margins over marginal costs) vs. **Built-In Inflation / Wage-Price Spiral** (workers demanding higher nominal wages via government **Dearness Allowance [DA]** or rural **MGNREGA** wage indexation to offset past price rises, which firms pass right back into retail prices).
* **2. Headline vs. Core Inflation and Price Regimes:** Under its **Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT)** mandate (`4% ± 2%`), the RBI's **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** targets **Headline Inflation** (total retail price change across the full **CPI-Combined** basket, including volatile food and fuel) while tracking **Core Inflation** (CPI stripping out *Food & Beverages* and *Fuel & Light* to isolate persistent demand-side overheating). Across the diagnostic spectrum, policymakers distinguish **Disinflation** (prices still rising, but at a slower pace, `Δπ < 0`) vs. **Deflation** (an actual drop in the price level, `π < 0`, triggering the **Irving Fisher Debt-Deflation Spiral** where falling prices bloat the real burden of fixed nominal debt) vs. **Reflation** (deliberate fiscal and monetary stimulus to lift an economy out of deflation), alongside **Stagflation** (supply-shock inflation paired with contracting output and high unemployment), **Skewflation** (price spikes concentrated in a tiny cluster of items like vegetables while other prices stay flat), and **Shrinkflation** (FMCG firms quietly cutting packet weight/grammage while keeping the nominal **Maximum Retail Price [MRP]** sticker unchanged).
* **3. The Phillips Curve Architecture:** The **Short-Run Phillips Curve (SRPC)** models a temporary inverse trade-off between unemployment (`U`) and inflation (`π`), meaning central banks face a **Sacrifice Ratio** (the percentage-point loss of real GDP/employment required to permanently cut inflation by `1%`). However, under the **Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve** (`πₜ = πₜᵉ - β(Uₜ - U*) + νₜ`), attempting to push unemployment below the **NAIRU** (**Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment**, `U*`) causes workers to revise their inflation expectations (`πₜᵉ`) upward; this shifts the SRPC higher and renders the **Long-Run Phillips Curve (LRPC)** strictly vertical at `U*` with zero permanent employment gain.
* **4. Subcontinental Supply Drivers:** India's inflation is structurally vulnerable to imported crude (>85% imported) and edible oil (55–60% imported) shocks, extreme volatility in the **TOP Complex** (**Tomato, Onion, Potato**) and protein pulses, and **El Niño** (equatorial Pacific warming that suppresses Southwest Monsoon rains) or March heatwaves. These shocks are amplified by **Ezekiel’s Cobweb Phenomenon** (farmers basing sowing decisions on last season's mandi prices `Pₜ₋₁`, creating alternating cycles of gluts and shortages) and fragmented **APMC** (**Agricultural Produce Market Committee**) mandis where post-harvest wastage and *arhatiya* (middleman commission agent) markups prevent farm-gate price drops from reaching urban consumers.

### II. Retail and Wholesale Price Architectures: The February 2026 CPI Base-Year Revision and CPI–WPI Divergence

* **5. The February 2026 CPI Base-Year Revision (2012 → 2024):** On 12 February 2026, the **National Statistical Office (NSO)** under **MoSPI** updated the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** base year from `2012=100` to `2024=100` using the **Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) 2023–24** to eliminate **Laspeyres Substitution Bias** (the distortion where an outdated fixed-weight basket overstates inflation by ignoring how consumers shift toward newer or cheaper goods). Validating **Engel’s Law** (as household income rises, the percentage of budget spent on food falls), the weight of *Food and Beverages* was slashed by `9.11 percentage points` from `45.86%` to `36.75%`. The new series adopts **UN COICOP 2018** (**Classification of Individual Consumption According to Purpose**, replacing 6 broad groups and the opaque 28.32% *Miscellaneous* bucket with **12 granular Divisions**), expands the basket from `299 to 358 items` (purging VCRs/cassettes to add OTT streaming, pen-drives, PNG, and babysitters), includes **Rural Housing Rent** for the first time (making Division 4 *Housing & Utilities* the second-largest component at `17.67%`), and tracks **12 online e-commerce platforms** in cities above 25 lakh population.
* **6. Occupation-Specific CPIs:** For targeted wage indexation, the **Labour Bureau** (Ministry of Labour & Employment) compiles **CPI-IW** (**Industrial Workers**, base `2016=100` across 88 industrial centers and 7 formal sectors with a `39.17%` food weight, used to revise government **Dearness Allowance [DA]**) vs. **CPI-AL** and **CPI-RL** (**Agricultural and Rural Labourers**, base `1986–87=100` across 600 villages with massive `69.15%` and `66.77%` food weights, used to revise statutory farm minimum wages and **MGNREGA** wage rates).
* **7. WPI Architecture and CPI–WPI Divergence:** Compiled monthly by the **Office of the Economic Adviser (OEA), DPIIT** (Ministry of Commerce & Industry), the **Wholesale Price Index (WPI, base 2011–12=100)** tracks bulk factory-gate and primary mandi prices across `697 physical goods` (**Manufactured Products `64.23%`**, **Primary Articles `22.62%`**, and **Fuel & Power `13.15%`**, with a combined **WPI Food Index** of `24.38%`) while **excluding indirect taxes** and using a **Geometric Mean**. Because WPI contains **0% services**, is dominated by globally traded industrial metals/chemicals (`77.38%` Manufactured + Fuel), and excludes retail freight/tax wedges, global commodity slumps regularly plunge WPI into deflation even while retail CPI stays high—dragging down the **GDP Deflator** (`Nominal GDP / Real GDP × 100`), which artificially inflates measured Real GDP growth while shrinking Nominal GDP and fiscal tax buoyancy.

### III. Industrial Production Metrics: The May 2026 IIP Base-Year Revision, Eight Core Industries, and ASI

* **8. The May/June 2026 IIP Base-Year Revision (2011–12 → 2022–23):** Following the **TAC-IIP** report (chaired by Dr. Mridul Saggar, 25 May 2026), NSO launched the revised **Index of Industrial Production (IIP)**—a monthly **Laspeyres Volume Index** tracking physical factory output (`Q`)—with base year `2022–23=100` on 1 June 2026. Adopting **NIC-2025** (**National Industrial Classification**), it expands coverage from 3 to **4 broad sectors**: **Manufacturing (`76.062%`)**, **Mining & Quarrying (`11.053%`, adding Rare Earths from IREL and 9 minor minerals)**, **Electricity & Gas (`10.865%`, splitting Renewable vs. Non-Renewable power)**, and a newly added **Water Supply, Sewerage & Waste Management (`2.020%`)** sector across `1,042 products` in `463 item groups` (adding stents, vaccines, CCTV, and aircraft parts while dropping CFLs and kerosene). Crucially, it replaces WPI with the **Output Producer Price Index (Output PPI)** to deflate value-reported machinery (`36.02%` weight) and introduces **Dynamic Panel Maintenance** (replacing closed sample factories with active plants).
* **9. Use-Based IIP, Eight Core Industries (ICI), and ASI:** To pinpoint value-chain bottlenecks, IIP is cross-classified into **6 Use-Based Categories**: **Primary Goods** (largest weight at `34.05%` in 2011–12), **Intermediate Goods** (`17.22%`), **Consumer Non-Durables** (`15.33%`), **Consumer Durables** (`12.84%`), **Infrastructure/Construction Goods** (`12.34%`), and **Capital Goods** (`8.22%`, the premier gauge of private corporate **Gross Fixed Capital Formation [GFCF / Capex]**). Ahead of the monthly IIP, **OEA (DPIIT)** releases the **Index of Eight Core Industries (ICI)** (`40.27%` of IIP weight), ranked strictly in descending weight: **Refinery Products (`28.04%`) > Electricity (`19.85%`) > Steel (`17.92%`) > Coal (`10.33%`) > Crude Oil (`8.98%`) > Natural Gas (`6.88%`) > Cement (`5.37%`) > Fertilizers (`2.63%`)**. Structural manufacturing weights derive from NSO's **Annual Survey of Industries (ASI)** under the **Collection of Statistics Act, 2008**, covering formal factories registered under the **Factories Act, 1948 / OSHWC Code, 2020** (historically `≥10 workers` with power or `≥20` without power, split into a 100%-enumerated **Census Sector** for large units vs. a **Sample Sector** for smaller units), whereas informal manufacturing is tracked via **ASUSE** (**Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises**).

### IV. Macroeconomic Pathologies of Persistent Inflation and the Erosion of Household Financial Savings

* **10. Real Rate Suppression, Fiscal Drag, and REER Overvaluation:** Via the **Irving Fisher Equation** (`r ≈ i - π`), when inflation (`π`) exceeds after-tax nominal bank deposit rates (`r_post-tax = i(1 - τ) - π < 0`), savers suffer an implicit **Inflation Tax** that drives capital out of bank deposits into unproductive gold and land. Simultaneously, under unindexed progressive tax slabs, inflation triggers **Bracket Creep / Fiscal Drag** (pushing workers whose nominal wages `W` merely match inflation into higher marginal tax brackets, shrinking real disposable income `[Y - T]/P`). Externally, when domestic inflation exceeds foreign inflation while the RBI stabilizes the **NEER** (**Nominal Effective Exchange Rate**), India's **REER** (**Real Effective Exchange Rate** = `NEER × [P_India / P_Foreign]`) appreciates above `100`, eroding export competitiveness and widening the **Current Account Deficit (CAD)** until the Rupee undergoes nominal depreciation under **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)**.
* **11. Net Household Financial Savings (NHFS) Compression:** In national accounting, the household sector is the sole net lender to corporate and government borrowers via **Net Household Financial Savings (NHFS)** (`Gross Financial Assets Flows minus Annual Increase in Financial Liabilities/Borrowings`). After peaking at `11.5% of GDP` in FY21, NHFS plunged to a multi-decade trough of `5.1%–5.3% of GDP` in FY23–FY24 (standing at `7.0%` in FY25 and `6.2%` in FY26, below the `7.9%` historical average)—not because gross financial asset accumulation collapsed, but because household borrowing doubled (`~6% of GDP`). As households take mortgage loans (counted as a financial liability subtracting from NHFS) to buy real estate (counted under physical savings) and divert savings from bank deposits into **Mutual Fund SIPs** and equities (`13–18%` of financial savings), commercial banks suffer a tight **Credit-Deposit (CD) Ratio squeeze**.
* **12. Unsecured Retail Credit Boom and RBI Macroprudential Tightening:** High living costs and frictionless FinTech **Lending Service Providers (LSPs)** triggered a surge in **Unsecured Retail Consumer Credit** (personal loans, credit cards, and **Buy-Now-Pay-Later [BNPL]** lines), pushing India's **Household Debt-to-GDP Ratio** from `38.3%` to `45.5%–49.0%` by FY26 (with over half of borrowers taking their first loan/card before age 30). Because shadow-banking **NBFCs** funded these risky unsecured loans by borrowing heavily from **Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs)**, the RBI pre-empted systemic contagion via **Macroprudential Countercyclical Regulation**—hiking **Basel III Risk Weights** by `+25 percentage points` on consumer credit (`100% → 125%`, exempting housing, vehicle, education, gold, and SHG loans), credit card receivables (`125% → 150%` for banks; `100% → 125%` for NBFCs), and bank lending to NBFCs. By inflating **Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)** in the denominator of **CRAR** (**Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio** = `[Tier-1 + Tier-2 Capital] / RWA`), the RBI forced lenders to set aside `25%` more equity capital per rupee lent, raising interest spreads and cooling unsecured credit growth.
