> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> How do you build a republic with an elected President at the top without accidentally creating an autocrat who clashes with the Prime Minister? India’s constitutional framers solved this institutional tug-of-war by fusing a republican presidency into the British Westminster cabinet model: they chose *daily parliamentary accountability* over *fixed-term executive stability*. In this system, the President at the Centre and the Governor in the States reign as ceremonial symbols of statehood, while real executive power belongs to a Council of Ministers that must command a daily majority in the elected Lower House.
> 
> Yet, whenever the constitutional text grants these nominal heads powers to sign bills, issue emergency laws, pardon convicts, or oversee state governments, a fierce three-way tug-of-war erupts between the Union Executive, State Legislatures, and the Supreme Court. Across this chapter, you will watch how constitutional amendments and landmark Constitution Bench rulings systematically stripped away personal discretion and "pocket vetoes" from the President and Governors—binding them to Cabinet advice, outlawing executive lawmaking-by-stealth, subjecting clemency to the Rule of Law, and stopping unelected Governors from paralyzing elected State Assemblies.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. The Union Executive Architecture: The President, Electoral Calculus, and Westminster Aid and Advice (Articles 52–78)

* **1. Constitutional Status of the President, Electoral College Mechanics, and Impeachment (Articles 52–62, 71):** Choosing daily legislative accountability over American-style fixed-term separation, Articles 52–53 make the President the ceremonial **Head of the State** (symbolic representative of the nation) vs. the Prime Minister as the **Head of the Executive** (the actual ruler driving governance), while keeping Union executive power under Article 73 **co-extensive** (matching the exact boundaries of Parliament's lawmaking reach over the Union List). To prevent a directly elected President from claiming rival democratic legitimacy against the Prime Minister, Article 54 creates an indirect **Electoral College** comprising *only elected* MPs and State/UT MLAs (Delhi and Puducherry via the 70th Amendment, excluding all nominated members and Legislative Councils), where vacancies cannot invalidate a poll (Article 71(4); *In Re: Presidential Poll*, 1974). Article 55 balances **inter se** (among themselves) state demographic weight—using a population baseline frozen at the **1971 Census** until after 2026 (42nd and 84th Amendments) so states with lower fertility are not penalized—with federal parity between all MPs and all MLAs, electing the President via **Proportional Representation by Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV)** (ranked-choice secret ballot requiring an absolute majority quota of `⌊Valid Votes / 2⌋ + 1`) vs. **First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)** (plurality voting where the highest vote-getter wins even with under 50%). While the Supreme Court holds exclusive jurisdiction over election disputes (Article 71), removing a President for *"violation of the Constitution"* under Article 61 requires a **quasi-judicial impeachment** (a court-like legislative trial initiated with 14 days' notice by 25% of either House) passed by **Special Majority (Type 4)** (at least two-thirds of the *total membership* of both Houses separately)—creating a sharp asymmetry where nominated MPs vote in impeachment alongside elected MPs, whereas MLAs vote in elections but play zero role in impeachment.

* **2. The Vice-President of India: Constitutional Position, Election, and Removal (Articles 63–71):** Under Articles 63–65, the Vice-President serves a dual role: he draws his salary strictly as the **ex-officio** (by virtue of holding another office) Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, and steps in as Acting President for a maximum of six months if the presidency falls vacant (with the Chief Justice of India next in line under the 1969 Act). Because his primary routine job is presiding over the Upper House rather than representing the federal states, Article 66 excludes MLAs from his Electoral College while including **both elected and nominated MPs** of both Houses. Consequently, removing the Vice-President under Article 67(b) requires no specified constitutional ground or two-thirds trial—only a resolution initiated in the Rajya Sabha by an **Effective Majority** (>50% of total House strength minus vacancies) and agreed to by the Lok Sabha via a **Simple Majority** (>50% of members present and voting).

* **3. The Westminster Aid and Advice Dialectic: President, Prime Minister, and Council of Ministers (Articles 74–78):** To turn British convention into hard law, the **42nd Amendment (1976)** amended Article 74(1) to mandate that the President *shall* act on ministerial advice, while the **44th Amendment (1978)** added a single suspensive check allowing the President to return advice *once* for reconsideration. Even before these amendments, the Supreme Court cemented cabinet supremacy: *Ram Jawaya Kapur (1955)* established the **Residuary Theory of Executive Power** (the executive can formulate and execute welfare or administrative policies under Articles 73/162 without a prior enabling statute, provided it violates no Fundamental Right or existing law), and *Shamsher Singh (1974)* ruled that wherever the Constitution mentions the President's or Governor's "satisfaction," it means the *constitutional satisfaction* of the Council of Ministers, not personal discretion (while *S.R. Bommai*, 1994 clarified that Article 74(2) bars courts from probing *subjective* ministerial advice, but allows judicial scrutiny of the *objective material* behind it). Under Article 75, governance balances **Individual Responsibility / Doctrine of Pleasure** (Ministers hold office during the President's pleasure under Article 75(2), exercised strictly on the Prime Minister's advice to dismiss an errant colleague) vs. **Collective Responsibility** (the entire Council of Ministers *"swims and sinks together"* before the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3), facing removal via a Rule 198 **No-Confidence Motion**). Finally, the **91st Amendment (2003)** curbed coalition "jumbo cabinets" by capping ministry size at **15%** of the Lower House strength (minimum 12 in States under Article 164(1A); 10% in Delhi) and barring Tenth Schedule defectors from ministerial posts until re-elected, while *S.R. Chaudhuri (2001)* barred repeatedly re-appointing non-legislators beyond the six-month window (Articles 75(5)/164(4)).

### II. Presidential and Gubernatorial Legislative, Ordinance, and Clemency Powers (Articles 72, 111, 123, 161, 213)

* **4. The Veto Architecture over Union Legislation (Article 111 and the 24th Amendment):** Unlike the American President’s **Qualified Veto** (which Congress can override only with a two-thirds supermajority), Article 111 equips the Indian President with three distinct tools over Union Bills: **Absolute Veto** (withholding assent permanently, exercised on Cabinet advice over Private Members' Bills or when an outgoing government falls), **Suspensive Veto** (returning a non-Money Bill once for reconsideration, which Parliament can override by re-passing it with an ordinary simple majority), and **Pocket Veto** (keeping a Bill pending indefinitely because Article 111 sets no numerical deadline, famously used on the 1986 Post Office Bill). However, veto power varies by bill type: Money Bills (Article 110) cannot be returned via a Suspensive Veto, and the **24th Amendment (1971)** amended Article 368(2) (*"shall give his assent"*) to strip the President of *all* veto powers over Constitutional Amendment Bills.

* **5. Co-Extensive Legislative Power: The Ordinance-Making Regime and Judicial Safeguards (Articles 123 and 213):** Under Article 123 (President) and Article 213 (Governor), if at least one House of the legislature is in recess and urgent circumstances require immediate action, the executive (acting on Cabinet advice) can promulgate an **Ordinance** (an emergency executive law carrying the full force of an Act, strictly **co-extensive** with legislative competence, subject to Part III Fundamental Rights under Article 13(3)(a), and incapable of amending the Constitution). Any Ordinance issued when both Houses are in session is **ab initio** (from the very beginning) void, and every Ordinance expires **six weeks** after legislative reassembly—capping its maximum unapproved lifespan at **7.5 months** (six-month maximum session gap plus six weeks). To stop executives from bypassing legislatures, the Supreme Court held that executive satisfaction is reviewable for **mala fides** (bad faith or oblique motive) (*R.C. Cooper*, 1970; 44th Amendment), struck down successive **re-promulgation** of Ordinances as a *"fraud on the Constitution"* (*D.C. Wadhwa*, 1987), and ruled in *Krishna Kumar Singh (2017, 7-Judge)* that laying an Ordinance before the House is **mandatory** (legally compulsory) rather than **directory** (merely advisory), and that actions or rights created under a lapsed Ordinance do not automatically endure.

* **6. The Sovereign Prerogative of Clemency and Remission Jurisprudence (Articles 72 and 161):** Exercised strictly on Cabinet advice (*Maru Ram*, 1980), clemency under Article 72 (President) and Article 161 (Governor) operates through five modalities: **Pardon** (complete absolution from conviction, sentence, and guilt), **Commutation** (substituting a lighter nature of punishment, like death to life), **Remission** (reducing sentence duration without changing its character), **Respite** (awarding a lesser penalty due to special personal conditions like pregnancy or disability), and **Reprieve** (a temporary stay of execution). While only the President can pardon death sentences or **Court-Martial** (military court) sentences, Governors can remit or commute death sentences and cannot refer Article 161 Cabinet advice to the President (*A.G. Perarivalan*, 2022). Crucially, Indian law separates **statutory remission** (CrPC Sec. 432–435 / BNSS Sec. 473–477, which is bound by the 14-year floor under Sec. 433A and judicial *"fixed-term life sentences"* of 20–30 years under *V. Sriharan*, 2015) from **constitutional clemency** (Articles 72/161, an untouchable sovereign power that overrides statutory/judicial bars, yet remains subject to judicial review on six grounds like arbitrariness or non-application of mind under *Epuru Sudhakar*, 2006, and requires commuting death to life if mercy petitions suffer unexplained executive delay under *Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014). In *Bilkis Yakub Rasool / Bilkis Bano (2024)*, the Supreme Court quashed **ultra vires** (beyond legal authority) remission orders to restore the **status quo ante** (the previously existing legal state), ruling that only the State of *conviction and sentencing* (Maharashtra, not Gujarat) is the "appropriate government," and declaring an earlier court order obtained via **suppressio veri, suggestio falsi** (suppression of truth and suggestion of falsehood) to be **per incuriam** (decided through lack of care by ignoring binding precedent/statute) and a legal nullity.

### III. The State Executive and the Gubernatorial Crucible: Federal Friction, Discretion, and Legislative Assent (Articles 153–167, 200–201)

* **7. The Office of the Governor: Dual Constitutional Role, Appointment, and Tenure Security (Articles 153–159):** Adopting the Canadian model of presidential appointment (Article 155) to prevent provincial separatism, the Governor acts both as the State's nominal head and as a constitutional link to the Union, though *Hargovind Pant (1979)* established that the Governor holds an independent constitutional office and is *not* an employee or agent of the Central Government. Although Governors hold office during the *"pleasure of the President"* without impeachment protection (Article 156(1)), a 5-Judge Bench in *B.P. Singhal (2010)* ruled that this pleasure is not a license for arbitrary dismissal: Governors cannot be removed merely for ideological divergence from the ruling party at the Centre, and a **prima facie** (at first sight) case of capricious removal attracts judicial review—reinforcing the **Sarkaria (1988)**, **Venkatachaliah (2002)**, and **Punchhi (2010)** Commission blueprints advocating eminent non-local appointees, Chief Ministerial consultation, removal of university chancellor roles, and fixed-tenure impeachment safeguards.

* **8. Constitutional Discretion vs. Ministerial Aid and Advice in the States (Articles 163–167):** Unlike Article 74(1) for the President, Article 163(1)–(2) explicitly carves out discretionary power for the Governor and makes his decision on its scope textually final. However, in *Nabam Rebia (2016)*, a 5-Judge Bench pierced Article 163(2)’s finality clause, confining gubernatorial discretion strictly to three buckets: (1) **Express Discretion** (*"by the Constitution,"* such as Sixth Schedule mineral disputes or special state duties in Nagaland, Sikkim, and Arunachal Pradesh under Articles 371A/F/H); (2) **Implied Discretion** (*"under the Constitution"* due to conflict of interest, such as Article 356(1) President's Rule reports, Article 200 Bill reservation, or sanctioning prosecution of corrupt Ministers against Cabinet refusal per *M.P. Special Police Establishment*, 2004); and (3) **Situational Discretion** during hung Assemblies or loss of majority. While the Governor must ordinarily summon or prorogue the House on Cabinet advice (Article 174), *Shivraj Singh Chouhan (2020)* confirmed that the Governor can order a **Floor Test** (legislative majority vote) even during an ongoing Assembly if objective material shows the government lost its majority—though *Subhash Desai (2023)* barred using floor tests merely to resolve internal party splits.

* **9. The Article 200 & 201 Legislative Assent Jurisprudence (2023–2026 Baseline):** Under Article 200, when a State Bill arrives, the Governor exercises constitutional discretion across three options: assent, withhold assent, or reserve the Bill for the President (mandatory under the Second Proviso if it derogates from High Court powers), upon which the Governor becomes **functus officio** (having fulfilled his official role and retaining no further legal authority) and the President exercises an overriding federal veto under Article 201 (unbound even if the State Legislature re-passes the returned Bill within six months). Confronting Governors in opposition-ruled States sitting on Bills indefinitely, the Supreme Court dismantled the gubernatorial Pocket and Absolute Vetoes: *State of Punjab (2023)* ruled that withholding assent is intrinsically tied to the First Proviso, compelling the Governor to return a non-Money Bill *"as soon as possible"* with a message for reconsideration; and *State of Tamil Nadu (2025)* held that once the Assembly re-passes that returned Bill unamended, the Governor *must* grant assent and cannot subsequently reserve it for the President. Finally, in the **16th Presidential Reference under Article 143(1) (Nov 2025)**, a 5-Judge Bench balanced federal autonomy with separation of powers: while prolonged gubernatorial inaction (Pocket Veto) is unconstitutional and attracts limited judicial directions to act within a reasonable time, courts cannot judicially legislate fixed numerical timelines (e.g., 1–3 months) or invoke Article 142 (*"Complete Justice"*) to declare **"deemed assent"** at a stage **anterior** (prior in time) to actual executive enactment.
