> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> Every constitutional democracy faces a three-way institutional tug-of-war at the grassroots level: how do you keep local trial courts free from state political bosses, how do you stop over five crore pending cases from choking the system, and what happens when the government creates fast-track "tribunals" staffed by its own bureaucrats to bypass regular courts? This chapter traces how India’s Constitution solves the first problem by splitting power at the district level—letting the State Governor sign the formal appointment papers while giving the High Court total control over every trial judge's career, transfers, and discipline. To rescue these swamped trial courts, Parliament overhauled colonial criminal procedures in 2024 with strict forensic and digital deadlines, while building an alternative "multi-door" settlement system (mediation, lok adalats, and commercial arbitration) so citizens and businesses can resolve disputes with the force of a court decree without fighting decade-long trials.
>
> Yet the fiercest constitutional battle erupts when the Executive goes a step further and shifts core judicial power out of regular courts into specialized tribunals for service matters, taxes, corporate insolvency, and the environment. Originally introduced during the 1976 Emergency to strip High Courts of their oversight powers, this "tribunalization" regime triggered a landmark counter-revolution by the Supreme Court. First, the Court restored the High Courts' unshakeable power to review every tribunal order; since 2010, it has been locked in a high-stakes standoff with Parliament—repeatedly striking down government attempts to pack tribunals with short-term bureaucrats, even as Parliament re-enacts those very rules under the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. Constitutional Architecture of the Subordinate Judiciary (Part VI, Chapter VI: Articles 233–237)

* **1. The Dual-Control Federal Equilibrium (Articles 233–237):** To operationalize **Article 50** (the constitutional directive separating the judiciary from the executive) at the trial level, the Constitution splits formal appointment from real supervision. While the Governor formally appoints **District Judges** under **Article 233** and lower **Civil Judges** under **Article 234**, the High Court holds binding primacy in selection—Filling the District Judge cadre via 50% **merit-cum-seniority** promotion, 25% **Limited Departmental Competitive Examination** (accelerated promotion for serving civil judges), and 25% direct recruitment from practicing advocates with at least seven years at the Bar. Under the **Dheeraj Mor (2020) Continuous Practice Rule**, this 25% Bar quota is strictly reserved for lawyers who remain in continuous active practice right up to their application and appointment, barring serving civil judges from using prior legal practice to jump the queue (while **Article 233A** retrospectively validated pre-1966 irregular appointments). Once appointed, **Article 235** vests **plenary control** (complete, unqualified administrative and disciplinary authority) over all subordinate courts and integrated criminal Magistrates (**Article 237**) exclusively in the High Court, barring state vigilance agencies from investigating a judge without High Court permission and binding the Governor to the High Court's disciplinary recommendations.
* **2. The All-India Judicial Service (AIJS) under Article 312(3):** Inserted by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, **Article 312** empowers Parliament to create a centralized **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by two-thirds of members present and voting. While **Article 312(3)** sets a **constitutional floor** (barring the AIJS from including any post below a District Judge) and **Article 312(4)** provides a **non-obstante override** (allowing Parliament to amend Articles 233–237 by a simple legislative majority without triggering a formal **Article 368** constitutional amendment), the proposal remains deadlocked. State governments and High Courts resist it because centralized recruitment erodes High Court control under Articles 233 and 235, blocks promotional avenues for state civil judges, clashes with state reservation rosters, and ignores the **vernacular evidentiary imperative** (the necessity for trial judges to master local languages to evaluate oral witness depositions and village land records under **Section 307 BNSS**).
* **3. The Post-July 2024 Criminal and Civil Trial Hierarchy (BNSS, BNS, and BSA 2023):** Replacing colonial criminal codes on July 1, 2024, **Section 6 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** abolished colonial **Metropolitan Magistrates** and **Assistant Sessions Judges** to create a uniform pan-India trial hierarchy, while introducing **Community Service** (**Section 4 BNS**, a restorative non-custodial penalty for petty offences). The new regime enforces accountability across the investigative-trial pipeline: **Section 173** mandates **Zero FIR** (registering a **cognizable offence**—a serious crime allowing arrest without warrant—at any police station regardless of territorial jurisdiction) and **e-FIR** (electronic complaints signed within 3 days, with an optional 14-day preliminary inquiry to check if a **prima facie**—at first sight—case exists for offences punishable with 3 to 7 years). To prevent police fabrication and trial delays, the BNSS mandates crime-scene forensic visits for offences punishable with ≥7 years (**Section 176(3)**), compulsory audio-video recording of all searches and seizures (**Sections 105/185**), flexible **police custody / remand** (allowing the 15-day police interrogation window to be used in tranches across the first 40 or 60 days of detention under **Section 187(2)**), strict caps on charge-framing (60 days), adjournments (maximum 2), and judgments (30–45 days), alongside **trial in absentia** (trying an absconding **proclaimed offender** in their absence after 90 days under **Section 356**) and relaxed **undertrial bail** (releasing first-time offenders after serving one-third of their maximum potential sentence under **Section 479**).
* **4. Systemic Pathologies, Grassroots Access, and Institutional Infrastructure:** Real-time tracking on the **National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG)** reveals over 5.1 crore pending cases (87% in subordinate courts), driven by a low ratio of ~21 judges per million people (against the Law Commission’s 50-per-million target) and >20% vacancies—prompting the Supreme Court in *High Court Bar Association, Allahabad (2024)* to scrap automatic six-month stay vacations in favor of scientific case-flow management. At the village level, the **Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008** created mobile rural courts headed by a **Nyayadhikari** (a First-Class Judicial Magistrate) guided by **principles of natural justice** (fundamental fairness unbound by technical evidence rules), but non-mandatory *"may"* phrasing in **Section 3(1)** left most states inactive despite Supreme Court **mandamus** (a judicial writ commanding a public authority to perform a statutory duty) interventions. To modernize physical and digital capacity, **e-Courts Phase III** (₹7,210 crore) builds paperless cloud courts integrated with the **Inter-Operable Criminal Justice System (ICJS) 2.0**, while the proposed **National Judicial Infrastructure Authority of India (NJIAI)** seeks to replace the broken **Centrally Sponsored Scheme (CSS)** (where 60:40 central-state funds routinely lapse when states delay their 40% matching share) with an autonomous, non-lapsing judicial fund.

### II. The Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) Ecosystem and Commercial Justice Architecture

* **5. Statutory Foundations of Non-Adversarial Justice (Section 89 CPC, Article 39A, NALSA & Lok Adalats):** Fulfilling **Article 39A** (equal access to justice and free legal aid), **Section 89 CPC** empowers civil courts to divert suits after pleadings into four ADR tracks—where under the *Afcons Infrastructure (2010)* rule, the court needs **mutual party consent** to refer cases to **Arbitration** or **Conciliation** (since they permanently exit the civil court), but needs **no consent** for **Mediation** or **Lok Adalat** referral. Under the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (administered by the four-tier **NALSA** network), Lok Adalats split into two distinct models: **Regular Lok Adalats (Sections 19–21)** vs. **Permanent Lok Adalats (Sections 22A–22E)**. **Regular Lok Adalats** handle pending or pre-litigation cases (excluding **non-compoundable offences**—serious crimes that cannot be privately settled between parties) and are **purely conciliatory** with zero power to decide on merits (*Jalour Singh, 2008*), though a voluntary settlement becomes a final **deemed civil court decree** with a 100% court-fee refund and zero appeal (challengeable only via a narrow **Article 226/227 writ** for fraud). Conversely, **Permanent Lok Adalats (PLAs)** handle pre-litigation disputes over **Public Utility Services** up to ₹1 crore and exercise **hybrid concilio-adjudicatory power**: they must first conduct mandatory conciliation (*Canara Bank v. G.S. Jayarama, 2022*), and if settlement fails, they are statutorily empowered to adjudicate the dispute on merits (**Section 22C(8)**).
* **6. The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and Constitution Bench Jurisprudence (2023–2024):** Modeled on the UNCITRAL framework, the 1996 Act was tightened by the 2015–2021 Amendments to curb court interference: imposing a 12-month award deadline (**Section 29A**), restricting award challenges under **Section 34** to narrow **Public Policy** or domestic **Patent Illegality** (a glaring legal error on the face of the award, barring evidence re-appreciation), ending automatic stays on award enforcement (**Section 36**), and imposing **de jure ineligibility** (automatic legal disqualification under **Section 12(5) and the Seventh Schedule** for anyone with an employee or business link to a party). Between 2023 and 2024, three Constitution Benches reshaped this regime:
    1.  **In Re: Stamp Act Interplay (2023, 7-Judge Bench):** Overruling *N.N. Global II*, the Court distinguished **inadmissibility in evidence** (a curable tax defect under the Stamp Act) from **voidness ab initio** (legally dead from the beginning under the Contract Act). Invoking the **Doctrine of Separability** (treating the arbitration clause as an independent contract) and **Kompetenz-Kompetenz** (an arbitral tribunal’s authority under **Section 16** to rule on its own jurisdiction), the Court barred referral courts under **Sections 8 and 11** from impounding unstamped contracts, leaving stamp-duty enforcement to the arbitrator.
    2.  **Cox and Kings (2023, 5-Judge Bench):** Upheld the **Group of Companies Doctrine**, ruling that non-signatory corporate affiliates can be bound as veritable **"parties"** (**Section 2(1)(h)**)—rather than mere derivative claimants *"claiming through or under"* a party—when mutual intention is inferred from their direct involvement in negotiating, performing, or terminating a composite transaction.
    3.  **CORE II (2024, 5-Judge Bench):** Overruling *CORE I*, the Court fused **Article 14** constitutional equality with **Section 18** arbitral equal treatment, striking down public-sector contract clauses that allow a government entity to unilaterally appoint a sole arbitrator or force private contractors to pick their arbitrator from a narrow, government-curated panel.
* **7. The Mediation Act, 2023 and the Commercial Courts Act, 2015:** The **Commercial Courts Act, 2015** (as amended in 2018) fast-tracks business disputes whose **Specified Value** (minimum monetary threshold) is ≥₹3 lakh via a strict 120-day written-statement forfeiture rule, **Summary Judgment** (**Order XIII-A**, deciding claims without oral trial if a party has no real prospect of success), and **Section 12A Mandatory Pre-Institution Mediation**—where under *Patil Automation (2022)*, any commercial suit filed without exhausting mediation (unless seeking genuine urgent interim relief) must be rejected **suo motu** (on the court’s own motion) under **Order VII Rule 11 CPC**. Complementing this, the standalone **Mediation Act, 2023** keeps general pre-litigation mediation **voluntary** (**Section 5**), excludes a **First Schedule** negative list (criminal prosecutions, tax, NGT/CCI matters, and disputes **in rem**—affecting the legal status/rights of third parties and the public at large—as opposed to disputes **in personam**—binding only specific parties), caps mediation at 120+60 days under a **Mediation Council of India**, and makes a signed **Mediated Settlement Agreement (MSA)** directly enforceable as a civil court decree (**Section 27**), challengeable within 90+90 days strictly on fraud, corruption, impersonation, or subject-matter ineligibility.

### III. Administrative Adjudication and the Tribunalization Regime (Part XIV-A: Articles 323A & 323B)

* **8. Constitutional Dichotomy of Part XIV-A and Specialized Tribunals:** Inserted by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, **Part XIV-A** creates a sharp structural divide between **Article 323A** and **Article 323B**. **Article 323A** covers *only* recruitment and service conditions of public servants, vests **exclusive legislative competence in Parliament alone**, and permits only a **single-tier** structure (the **Central Administrative Tribunal - CAT** and **State Administrative Tribunals - SATs**). Conversely, **Article 323B** covers eight enumerated regulatory domains (tax, foreign exchange, labour, land reforms, urban property ceilings, elections, essential goods, and rent/tenancy), grants **concurrent competence to both Parliament and State Legislatures**, and explicitly permits a **multi-tier hierarchy** of original and appellate tribunals. Furthermore, in *Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010)*, the Supreme Court held that Part XIV-A is not exhaustive: Parliament holds **plenary legislative power** (complete, inherent lawmaking authority under **Articles 245–246** and **List III Entry 11A**: *"Administration of Justice"*) to create specialized tribunals outside Article 323B. These include the **NCLT and NCLAT** (corporate law and insolvency under the IBC), the **National Green Tribunal - NGT** (a **sui generis**—one-of-a-kind—environmental forum exercising *suo motu* powers under *Ankita Sinha, 2021* and applying the **Polluter Pays** and **Precautionary Principles** across seven scheduled environmental acts, excluding the Wildlife and Forest Rights Acts), and the **Armed Forces Tribunal - AFT** (where *Parashotam Dass, 2023* clarified that **Article 227(4)** only bars High Court administrative superintendence over military tribunals, leaving **Article 226** writ review fully intact).
* **9. Separation of Powers and the Basic Structure Counter-Revolution (Sampath Kumar to L. Chandra Kumar, 1997):** Originally, **Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d)** authorized **ouster clauses** (statutory provisions stripping regular courts of jurisdiction) that barred High Courts from reviewing tribunal rulings. In **S.P. Sampath Kumar (1987)**, a 5-Judge Bench initially saved this ouster by inventing the **Alternative Institutional Mechanism (Effective Substitute) Theory**—holding that Parliament could exclude High Court **Articles 226/227** review so long as the tribunal acted as an equally independent substitute. After a decade of bureaucratic dysfunction proved tribunals were no match for constitutional courts, a 7-Judge Bench in **L. Chandra Kumar (1997)** overruled *Sampath Kumar* and struck down the ouster clauses in Articles 323A(2)(d), 323B(3)(d), and Section 28 of the CAT Act as violations of the unamendable **Basic Structure**. The Court redefined tribunals as **supplemental** (not substitutional) **"Courts of First Instance"**: two-member tribunal benches (including at least one Judicial Member) can test the **vires** (constitutional or statutory validity) of any law *except* the **Parent Act** that created the tribunal itself, and litigants cannot file direct **Article 136 Special Leave Petitions (SLPs)** to the Supreme Court without first submitting the tribunal's order to writ scrutiny before a two-judge **Division Bench** of the jurisdictional High Court.
* **10. Executive Capture vs. Judicial Independence (R. Gandhi, Rojer Mathew, MBA I–IV, and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021):** Since 2010, the battlefield has shifted to stopping the Executive from packing tribunals with career bureaucrats. In **R. Gandhi (2010)** and **Madras Bar Association [MBA] I & II (2014–2015)**, the Supreme Court mandated that any tribunal replacing a High Court must mirror judicial independence—barring technical members from outnumbering judicial members, requiring a Selection Committee with a judicial casting vote and 5-to-7-year tenures, and striking down the entire **National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005** for transferring **"substantial questions of law"** to charter accountants and executive-controlled benches. When the government used **Section 184 of the Finance Act, 2017**—passed as an **Article 110 Money Bill** (a fiscal bill that bypasses the Rajya Sabha's veto)—to impose executive-dominated selection panels and 3-year tenures, a 5-Judge Bench in **Rojer Mathew (2019)** struck down the 2017 Rules and referred the Money Bill bypass to a 7-Judge Bench. After **MBA III (2020)** mandated 5-year tenures, eligibility for advocates with 10 years' standing, and a **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**, the Executive passed an April 2021 Ordinance imposing a **50-year minimum age bar** (effectively excluding younger advocates) and a **4-year tenure cap**. Although **MBA IV (July 2021)** struck down both limits under the **Doctrine of Unconstitutional Legislative Override** (holding that Parliament cannot pass a law simply to nullify a binding constitutional writ without curing the underlying defect in judicial independence), Parliament enacted the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** weeks later—abolishing nine appellate tribunals (including the **IPAB** and **FCAT**, returning their dockets to High Court **Intellectual Property Divisions**) while *verbatim re-enacting* the struck-down 50-year age bar, 4-year tenure cap, and a two-name selection panel for executive cherry-picking, locking the Judiciary and Executive in an ongoing constitutional standoff.
