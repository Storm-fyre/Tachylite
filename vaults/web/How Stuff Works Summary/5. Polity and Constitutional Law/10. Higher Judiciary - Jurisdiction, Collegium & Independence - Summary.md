> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> Unlike the United States, where state and federal courts operate on separate tracks, India built a single, unified judicial pyramid with the Supreme Court at the peak and 25 High Courts right below it to enforce both Union and State laws uniformly. Because this Higher Judiciary must routinely strike down unlawful actions by the Prime Minister’s Cabinet (which holds the sword) and Parliament (which holds the purse), the Constitution grants it sweeping powers: it acts as the sole referee in federal fights between the Centre and States, enforces citizen rights across the nation, punishes anyone who defies its authority, and even bends rigid procedural rules to deliver "complete justice."
> 
> However, immense judicial power triggered a half-century institutional tug-of-war over a single question: *who gets to appoint and discipline the judges?* After the Executive punished independent-minded judges through supersessions and transfers during the 1970s, the Supreme Court rewrote the rules of appointment in the 1990s—wresting power away from politicians and vesting it in a closed group of senior judges (the Collegium), and later striking down Parliament's 2014 constitutional amendment (the NJAC) that tried to take it back. Today, that tug-of-war plays out through executive delays ("pocket vetoes") on judicial appointments, debates over post-retirement government jobs for judges, and the delicate balance between internal judicial secrecy and the citizen's Right to Information.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. Unified Judicial Architecture and the Jurisdictional Matrix of the Higher Judiciary

* **1. Constitutional Foundations of the Integrated Pyramid (Articles 124–147 and Articles 214–231):** To prevent legal fragmentation across a diverse federation, India rejected the American dual-court model in favor of a single integrated judicial hierarchy administering both Union and State laws under **Part V, Chapter IV (Articles 124–147)** for the Supreme Court and **Part VI, Chapter V (Articles 214–231)** for the High Courts. In a deliberate structural asymmetry, **Parliament by law** fixes the Supreme Court's sanctioned strength (currently 34 judges: the CJI + 33), whereas the **President of India** alone determines each High Court's strength based on workload (**Article 216**). Furthermore, while there is no minimum age for appointment, the President can appoint an eminent legal scholar as a **distinguished jurist** (an academic/legal expert who hasn't necessarily been a judge or litigator) to the Supreme Court (**Article 124(3)**), a pathway absent for High Courts (**Article 217(2)**). To tackle backlogs or a lack of **quorum** (the minimum number of permanent judges needed to hold a court session), the Constitution allows deploying an **Ad Hoc Judge** (a sitting High Court judge temporarily called to sit on the Supreme Court bench under **Article 127**) vs. **Additional Judges** (temporary High Court judges appointed by the President for up to 2 years to clear arrears under **Article 224(1)**) vs. **Acting Judges** (temporary substitutes appointed when a permanent High Court judge is absent under **Article 224(2)**), alongside recalling retired judges (**Articles 128 and 224A**).
* **2. Exclusive Original Federal Jurisdiction (Article 131) and Constitutional Federal Litigation:** Whenever a legal clash erupts purely between sovereign governments—the Union vs. State(s) or State(s) vs. State(s)—the Supreme Court exercises **Original and Exclusive Jurisdiction** under **Article 131** (meaning the lawsuit begins directly in the Supreme Court and no other court, not even a High Court, can hear it), strictly barring private citizens or state-owned corporations (PSUs) from being parties. So long as a dispute raises a **justiciable** question (an issue capable of being decided by legal principles rather than pure politics) affecting a state's constitutional or legal rights, the suit is maintainable—unless carved out by specific constitutional ousters like pre-1950 princely treaties or a *sanad* (a colonial-era royal charter or grant), Finance Commission matters (**Article 280**), or inter-state river water tribunals (**Article 262**). Recently, states have pushed **Article 131** to its limits by filing original suits to challenge the constitutional validity of central statutes (a point awaiting a larger Constitution Bench resolution between *State of MP*, 2011 and *State of Jharkhand*, 2014) and to contest Union-imposed fiscal limits like the **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)** (a central cap restricting how much debt a state government can raise annually under **Article 293(3)**, referred to a 5-Judge Bench in *State of Kerala v. UOI*, 2024).
* **3. Extraordinary Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32 vs. Article 226) and the Appellate Pyramid (Articles 132–136):** When citizen rights are violated, both the Supreme Court (**Article 32**) and High Courts (**Article 226**) issue **Prerogative Writs** (extraordinary judicial commands—*Habeas Corpus*, *Mandamus*, *Prohibition*, *Certiorari*, and *Quo Warranto*), but with an inverse structural trade-off: **Article 32** is itself a guaranteed Fundamental Right (meaning the Supreme Court *cannot* refuse a petition showing a *prima facie*—at first sight—violation of Part III rights due to **laches**—unreasonable delay—or alternative remedies), yet its scope is narrower because it enforces *only* Fundamental Rights. Conversely, **Article 226** is a discretionary constitutional power (a High Court may decline relief if a statutory remedy exists, unless natural justice fails or a statute's *vires*—constitutional validity—is challenged), yet it is substantively wider because it enforces both Fundamental Rights **and "for any other purpose"** (ordinary statutory and legal rights), even reaching authorities outside the state if the **cause of action** (the bundle of facts giving rise to the legal claim) arose partly within its territory (**Article 226(2)**); however, once a High Court dismisses an **Article 226** petition on merits, **Res Judicata** (the rule that a matter finally judged cannot be re-litigated between the same parties) bars a fresh **Article 32** writ (except for *Habeas Corpus*). Beyond regular Constitutional (**Article 132**), Civil (**Article 133**, after the 30th Amendment removed the ₹20,000 **pecuniary valuation**—monetary threshold), and Criminal appeals (**Article 134**), which usually require an **Article 134A** High Court certificate granted *suo motu* (on the court's own motion) or on immediate oral plea, the Supreme Court wields a vast discretionary residual power via a **Special Leave Petition (SLP)** under **Article 136** to hear appeals against final or **interlocutory orders** (interim orders passed mid-trial) from any court or tribunal except Armed Forces tribunals. Because dismissing an SLP *in limine* (at the initial threshold without a speaking order) does not trigger the **Doctrine of Merger** (where a lower court's ruling is absorbed into the Supreme Court's binding decree), routine SLPs have flooded the Court, fueling the debate to establish a **National Court of Appeal (NCA)** with regional **Cassation Benches** (final appellate benches for routine civil/criminal errors) so the Supreme Court can function purely as a Constitutional Court.
* **4. Presidential Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143): Constitutional Mechanics and Binding Force:** Unlike the US and Australian apex courts, which refuse executive requests for legal advice, **Article 143** empowers the President (acting on Cabinet advice under **Article 74**) to refer major questions to a minimum 5-Judge Constitution Bench down two distinct tracks: under **Article 143(1)** (general questions of law or fact of public importance), the Court *"may"* report its opinion, making it **discretionary** (allowing the Court to decline vague, political, or hypothetical queries, as in the 1994 Ayodhya reference), whereas under **Article 143(2)** (disputes arising out of pre-1950 treaties or *sanads* excluded from Article 131), the Court *"shall"* report its opinion, making it **mandatory**. While an advisory opinion is not an executable decree, its legal reasoning carries high precedential authority across all courts—provided the Executive does not misuse **Article 143** as a backdoor appeal to overturn an already decided **lis** (a concrete legal dispute) settled *inter partes* (between specific contesting parties).

### II. Precedential Finality, Curative Remedies, Contempt Power, and Complete Justice

* **5. Court of Record and the Jurisprudence of Contempt (Articles 129, 215, and the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971):** Both the Supreme Court (**Article 129**) and High Courts (**Article 215**) are designated as a **Court of Record** (a superior court whose recorded proceedings carry perpetual, unquestionable evidentiary truth and which possesses an inherent constitutional power—*sui generis*, or in a class of its own—to punish for contempt that Parliament can regulate via the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** but can never abolish). The 1971 Act divides offenses into **Civil Contempt** (wilful disobedience to a court order or breach of an undertaking) vs. **Criminal Contempt** (acts or publications that scandalize the court, prejudice a pending judicial proceeding, or obstruct justice, against which *bona fide*—good-faith—truth in the public interest was added as a defense in 2006). While the Supreme Court's **Article 129** umbrella extends to punishing contempt committed against subordinate courts (*Delhi Judicial Service Assn.*, 1991), a 5-Judge Bench in *Supreme Court Bar Association v. UOI* (1998) ruled that neither **Article 129** nor **Article 142** allows the Court to usurp the Bar Council's statutory power by revoking an advocate's license to practice law as a contempt punishment.
* **6. Stare Decisis (Article 141), Review Petitions (Article 137), and Curative Jurisdiction:** **Article 141** codifies **Stare Decisis** ("to stand by things decided"), making the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India *except the Supreme Court itself* (*Bengal Immunity*, 1955), subject to strict bench-strength discipline where a smaller or co-equal bench can never dissent from a larger bench (*Dawoodi Bohra*, 2005). What strictly binds lower courts is the **Ratio Decidendi** (the core legal principle necessary to decide the dispute) vs. **Obiter Dicta** (incidental judicial observations that carry persuasive weight), whereas a precedent loses its binding force if rendered **Per Incuriam** (passed in ignorance of a relevant statute or larger-bench ruling) vs. **Sub Silentio** (where a particular legal point was neither perceived nor argued before the bench). To balance *Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium* (the public interest that litigation must reach finality) against *Actus curiae neminem gravabit* (an error of the court shall harm no one), a litigant has 30 days to file an **Article 137 Review Petition** (circulated in chambers without oral arguments to the same judges—except death penalty reviews, which get an open-court 3-judge hearing per *Mohd. Arif*, 2014) strictly to correct an **error apparent on the face of the record**, consider new evidence, or address an *ejusdem generis* ("of the same kind") analogous ground. If the Review Petition fails, **Article 32** writs cannot be filed against the Supreme Court's own judgment; instead, *Rupa Ashok Hurra* (2002) invoked inherent powers (*ex debito justitiae*—as a rightful debt of justice) under **Articles 129, 137, and 142** to create the last-resort **Curative Petition**, which requires a Senior Advocate's certificate, proof of either a violation of natural justice (no notice/hearing) or undisclosed judicial bias, and circulation to the **3 senior-most SC Judges (CJI + 2) plus the judges who passed the verdict**.
* **7. Plenary Equity and "Complete Justice" under Article 142: Scope and Constitutional Limits:** **Article 142(1)** grants the Supreme Court plenary equitable authority to pass any decree necessary for doing **"complete justice"** in a pending matter—allowing it to fill legislative vacuums with binding interim rules (like the *Vishaka Guidelines* on workplace sexual harassment) until Parliament legislates. However, under the **"Supplement, Not Supplant" Doctrine** (*Supreme Court Bar Association*, 1998), **Article 142** is strictly supplementary: it can never violate Fundamental Rights or override express substantive statutory prohibitions rooted in fundamental public policy (nor can it issue blanket judicial legislation automatically vacating all High Court stay orders after 6 months, per *HC Bar Assn. Allahabad*, 2024). Yet, as a 5-Judge Bench clarified in *Shilpa Sailesh* (2023), **Article 142** *can* relax procedural statutory timelines (such as waiving the 6-to-18-month cooling-off period in divorce) and directly dissolve marriages on the non-statutory equitable ground of **irretrievable breakdown of marriage** when a relationship is dead beyond salvage.

### III. Judicial Appointments, the Four Judges Cases, and the Collegium-Executive Standoff

* **8. Constitutional Text of Appointments (Articles 124(2) and 217) and the Evolution of the Four Judges Cases:** Although **Articles 124(2) and 217(1)** state that the President appoints Supreme Court and High Court judges after **"consultation"** with the CJI, executive supersessions of senior judges in 1973 and 1977 triggered a four-stage constitutional war over whether "consultation" means executive supremacy or judicial **concurrence** (binding agreement). In the **First Judges Case** (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981—which also expanded *locus standi*, or the right to approach the court via PIL), a 7-Judge Bench ruled 4:3 that consultation does *not* mean concurrence, giving ultimate primacy to the Union Executive under **Article 74(1)**. Twelve years later, the **Second Judges Case** (*SCARA*, 1993, 9-Judge Bench, 7:2) overruled *S.P. Gupta* using **Article 50** (separation of judiciary from executive) and the **Basic Structure Doctrine** (inviolable core features of the Constitution), ruling that consultation means **concurrence**, that the CJI must be appointed by strict seniority, and that appointments must be initiated by a judicial **Collegium** (Initially CJI + 2 senior-most judges). In the **Third Judges Case** (*In Re: Special Reference*, 1998, 9-Judge unanimous), the Court expanded the SC Collegium to **5 members (CJI + 4 senior-most Puisne Judges—associate judges ranking below the CJI)** for SC appointments, HC Chief Justices, and HC transfers (keeping CJI + 2 for HC Judge appointments), mandating written consultation with the **Conversant Judge** (the senior-most SC judge hailing from the candidate's parent High Court), blocking any name vetoed by **2 Collegium judges**, and making a **unanimous Collegium reiteration** strictly binding on the Executive. When Parliament unanimously passed the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** (**Articles 124A–124C**) and the **NJAC Act** to replace the Collegium with a 6-member **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)**—comprising 3 *ex officio* (by virtue of office) SC judges, the Union Law Minister, and 2 "eminent persons," where *any 2 members* could veto a recommendation—a 5-Judge Bench in the **Fourth Judges Case** (*SCARA*, 2015, 4:1 majority, Justice Chelameswar dissenting) applied the **Width and Identity Tests** (checking whether an amendment destroys a core constitutional identity) to strike down the 99th Amendment and NJAC *in toto* (entirely), reviving the Collegium on the ground that judicial primacy in appointments is the bedrock of judicial independence.
* **9. Operational Mechanics of the Collegium, Transfers (Article 222), and Executive Pocket-Veto Friction:** Since 2015, friction between the Collegium and the Union Executive has centered on revising the **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** (the administrative rulebook governing appointment vetting and timelines), with the Collegium rejecting an unchecked executive veto over candidates on "national security" grounds. In practice, despite the **Third Judges Case** mandate that unanimously reiterated names are binding, the Executive frequently exercises a *de facto* **Pocket Veto** (indefinitely sitting on files without issuing appointment warrants) and engages in **Selective Segregation** (splitting a single batch of recommended names to notify favored candidates early, which manipulates **inter-se seniority**—the relative rank among judges sworn in on different days, determining who later becomes Chief Justice). For inter-state transfers of High Court judges under **Article 222**, while the judge's personal consent is not required if done in the public interest rather than as punishment (*Sankalchand Sheth*, 1977), the proposal can only be initiated by the **5-Judge SC Collegium** after consulting both High Court Chief Justices and conversant SC judges, leaving the transfer immune to judicial review unless made without Collegium concurrence.

### IV. Institutional Independence Safeguards, Internal Governance, and Judicial Accountability

* **10. Structural Safeguards of Judicial Independence and Post-Retirement Vulnerabilities:** To shield judges from political coercion, they do not hold office during the **Pleasure of the President** (the rule allowing summary dismissal of civil servants under **Article 310**), enjoying fixed tenure until age **65** in the Supreme Court (**Article 124(2)**, age disputes settled via parliamentary law) and age **62** in High Courts (**Article 217(1)**, age disputes settled by the President in conformity with the CJI's advice under **Article 217(3)**). Their salaries and administrative expenses are non-votable **Charged Expenditure on the Consolidated Fund**—with a crucial federal split: Supreme Court salaries and pensions are both charged to the **Consolidated Fund of India** (**Article 112(3)(d)**), whereas High Court salaries are charged to the **Consolidated Fund of the State** (**Article 202(3)(d)**) while High Court **pensions** are charged to the **Consolidated Fund of India**. Furthermore, **Article 121** bans discussing a judge's conduct in Parliament (except during a formal removal motion), while **Article 211** imposes an **absolute, unconditional ban** on State Legislatures ever discussing a High Court or Supreme Court judge's conduct. After retirement, **Article 124(7)** imposes a **total lifetime practice bar** on retired Supreme Court judges pleading before any court or authority in India, whereas **Article 220** imposes only a **partial practice bar** on retired permanent High Court judges (barring them from lower courts and the High Court(s) where they served, but allowing them to practice in the Supreme Court and *other* High Courts)—yet neither article stops retired judges from accepting **executive sinecures** (post-retirement government appointments to tribunals, commissions, or Rajya Sabha seats), raising persistent conflict-of-interest concerns.
* **11. Removal Mechanics of Higher Judiciary Judges (Articles 124(4)–(5), 218, and the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968):** Both Supreme Court (**Article 124(4)**) and High Court judges (**Article 218**) can be removed solely on grounds of **"proved misbehaviour or incapacity"** through a rigorous 7-stage hybrid gauntlet under the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**: (1) a motion signed by **100 Lok Sabha MPs or 50 Rajya Sabha MPs**; (2) discretionary admission by the Speaker/Chairman; (3) investigation by a **3-member Statutory Inquiry Committee** (an SC Judge/CJI, an HC Chief Justice, and a distinguished jurist)—a statutory stage that does *not* lapse if the Lok Sabha dissolves (*Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability*, 1991); (4) formal framing of charges and trial; (5) a binding negative filter where a **"not guilty"** finding immediately kills the parliamentary motion; (6) if found guilty, passage by *both* Houses of Parliament in the **same session** via a **Dual-Criterion Special Majority** (a majority of the **total membership** of that House **PLUS ≥ 2/3rds** of members **present and voting**); and (7) a Presidential removal order. Because no judge has ever been fully impeached through all seven stages, the judiciary relies on an internal **In-House Peer Inquiry Procedure** (*C. Ravichandran Iyer*, 1995), where a 3-judge peer committee appointed by the CJI investigates misconduct, withdraws judicial work, and urges resignation before recommending impeachment.
* **12. Internal Administrative Prerogatives and Transparency: Master of the Roster and RTI Jurisprudence:** While the CJI is *primus inter pares* ("first among equals," holding a single vote like any other judge on the judicial bench), on the administrative side the CJI is the sole and exclusive **Master of the Roster** (*Shanti Bhushan*, 2018)—meaning the CJI individually, and *not* the 5-Judge Collegium, holds the prerogative to constitute benches and assign cases, rendering any *suo motu* order passed by a bench without CJI assignment void. Externally, in *CPIO, Supreme Court v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal* (2019), a 5-Judge Constitution Bench ruled that the Office of the CJI is an integral part of the Supreme Court and a **"Public Authority"** subject to the **Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005**. However, to protect judicial privacy (**Article 21**) and **institutional candour** (the fearless honesty of consultees giving confidential opinions on judicial candidates), the Court separated disclosable *outputs* (final Collegium resolutions and whether judges have declared their assets) from raw deliberative *inputs* (IB reports and candid assessments of rejected candidates), mandating a case-by-case **Proportionality Test** (weighing whether the larger public interest in disclosure overrides privacy and fiduciary confidentiality).
