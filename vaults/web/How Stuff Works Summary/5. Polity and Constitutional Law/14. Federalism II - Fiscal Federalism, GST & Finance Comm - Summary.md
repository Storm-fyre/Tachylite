> [!abstract]+ PART A: The "Zero-Jargon" Big-Picture Story
> India's Constitution was built with a deliberate financial mismatch: the Union Government collects the biggest, fastest-growing taxes to keep the national market unified, while State Governments bear the heaviest day-to-day costs of schools, hospitals, policing, and local infrastructure. To keep States from going broke—or from turning into vassals of New Delhi—the Constitution sets up a multi-layered tug-of-war over four big questions: **Who gets to tax what?** **How do the Union and States co-manage a unified national Goods and Services Tax (GST)?** **How does an independent referee (the Finance Commission) split central tax money fairly between rich industrial States and poorer populous States?** and **Can the Union cap how much money a State borrows?**
> 
> Across this chapter, you will watch this institutional tug-of-war swing in both directions. On the side of **State autonomy**, the Supreme Court has recently stepped in to protect State coffers—ruling in *Mohit Minerals* (2022) that the GST Council cannot boss around elected State Legislatures, and ruling in a landmark 9-Judge verdict (*MADA v. SAIL*, 2024) that States have an untouchable right to tax mineral-rich lands and mining rights because mining royalties are contractual lease payments, not central taxes. Yet on the side of **Central macroeconomic control**, the Union continues to shrink the shared tax pool using unshared cesses and surcharges, steers State budgets through conditional welfare schemes, and enforces strict **3% of GSDP** State borrowing caps—a power the Supreme Court refused to pause in *State of Kerala v. Union of India* (2024) before sending the showdown to a 5-Judge Constitution Bench.

## PART B: Section-by-Section Story Threads & Inline Decoder

### I. Constitutional Architecture of Fiscal Federalism and Taxing Sovereignty (Part XII, Articles 264–271)

* **1. The Rule of Law in Taxation (Article 265) and the Taxonomy of Public Exactions:** India's fiscal architecture in **Part XII** intentionally decouples high-yield Union taxes from heavy State welfare spending, creating both **vertical fiscal imbalance** (the revenue-to-expenditure mismatch between the Union and the States) and **horizontal fiscal imbalance** (inter-state gaps in wealth and public service costs). Under **Article 265**, the Executive cannot tax by mere notification; both the substantive **levy** (charging assessment) and procedural **collection** (recovery) require a valid legislative statute that obeys Fundamental Rights (*Moopil Nair*, 1961), the **Doctrine of Unjust Enrichment** (*Mafatlal*, 1997—barring refunds of illegal taxes if the payer already passed the burden to consumers), and **strict construction of fiscal statutes** (*Dilip Kumar*, 2018—ambiguous charging clauses favor the citizen, whereas ambiguous exemption clauses favor the State). To stop governments from disguising revenues, Supreme Court jurisprudence separates a **Tax** (a compulsory levy credited to the **Consolidated Fund** under **Article 266(1)** for general spending with zero ***quid pro quo***, meaning no specific return service, *Shirur Mutt*, 1954) vs. a **Fee** (a regulatory or compensatory charge requiring a "broad, general, and reasonable" *quid pro quo* correlation with a service rendered, *Sreenivasa Traders*, 1983) vs. a **Purpose-Specific Cess** (an earmarked tax-on-tax transferred via the Consolidated Fund to a dedicated reserve in the **Public Account** under **Article 266(2)**) vs. a **Union Surcharge** (**Article 271** unconditional tax-on-tax for general Union revenue, explicitly barred on GST)—with both cesses and surcharges excluded from the **Divisible Pool** (central taxes shared with States under **Article 270**).
* **2. Strict Separation of Taxing Entries and the Five-Category Revenue Distribution Matrix (Articles 268–271):** Under *M.P.V. Sundararamier* (1958), the **Seventh Schedule** enforces a rigid wall between general regulatory entries and specific taxing entries: the power to tax can never be implied as incidental to regulation, **List III (Concurrent List)** contains zero taxes, and **residuary taxing power** (**Article 248(2)** read with **Entry 97 List I** for unlisted taxes) rests exclusively with Parliament. To channel funds to the States, **Articles 268–271** create a five-tier revenue matrix: **Article 268** (Union-legislated stamp duties collected and retained 100% by States) vs. **Article 269** (Union-collected inter-state sales/consignment taxes on non-GST goods assigned 100% to States) vs. **Article 269A** (**Integrated GST / IGST** on inter-state supplies and imports apportioned between the Union and destination State) vs. **Article 270** (the **Single Comprehensive Divisible Pool** created by the **80th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000**, pooling all central taxes) vs. **Article 271** (100% Union-retained surcharges). Under **Article 279(1)**, the **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** issues a constitutionally final certificate ascertaining the **"Net Proceeds"** (~80–81% of **Gross Tax Revenue / GTR** after deducting cesses, surcharges, UT shares, and collection costs), of which **41%** is devolved to the States.

### II. Sub-Surface Fiscal Sovereignty: The Mineral Rights and Royalty Jurisprudence (MADA v. SAIL, 2024)

* **3. The 35-Year Doctrinal Conflict over Mineral Taxation (Entry 54 List I vs. Entries 49 & 50 List II):** Although States own onshore sub-surface minerals (**Articles 294–295**), their power to tax mineral rights (**Entry 50 List II**) is subject to Parliamentary limitations relating to mineral development (**Entry 54 List I**, under which Section 9 of the **MMDR Act, 1957** fixes mining **royalty**). Reversing *Hingir-Rampur* (1961), a 7-Judge Bench in *India Cement* (1989) erroneously declared that "royalty is a tax," stripping States of mineral taxation power—a ruling that *Kesoram Industries* (2004, 5-Judge Bench) called a "typographical error" (meaning "cess on royalty is a tax") but could not overrule due to ***stare decisis*** (**Article 141** binding judicial precedent). In July 2024, a **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in ***Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) v. SAIL* (2024)** (8:1 majority) overruled *India Cement*, holding that **royalty** is not a tax under **Article 265**, but an ***ex-contractu*** (arising from a lease contract) consideration paid to the lessor/owner for extracting exhaustible mineral capital, meaning the MMDR Act imposes no statutory limitation on State mineral taxes under **Entry 50 List II**, nor does Parliament have any power to tax minerals itself.
* **4. Sub-National Resource Autonomy and Fiscal Consequences of the MADA Doctrine:** The *MADA (2024)* majority further insulated **Entry 49 List II** (unconditioned State taxes on lands and buildings) from any Parliamentary curb by invoking the property maxim ***cujus est solum, ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos*** (whoever owns the soil owns everything up to the heavens and down to the depths, including sub-soil mineral strata). Separating the **subject of tax** (what is taxed in **pith and substance**, i.e., true core essence: the land) from the **measure of tax** (the economic yardstick used to quantify tax liability: mineral output or royalty), the Court held that States can validly use mineral value or royalty to measure land tax under **Entry 49**. Over Justice B.V. Nagarathna's dissent warning of cascading industrial inflation, the Court's August 14, 2024 consequential order rejected pure **prospective overruling** (applying a judgment only to the future) because pre-2024 State tax laws were never void ***ab initio*** (invalid from the very beginning), allowing States to recover past tax arrears from a **cut-off date of April 1, 2005** in **12 annual installments starting April 1, 2026**, while waiving all pre-July 2024 interest and penalties.

### III. Co-operative Fiscal Federalism and the Goods and Services Tax (GST) Regime (101st Amendment)

* **5. The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016) and Simultaneous Legislative Competence (Article 246A):** To eliminate **tax cascading** (tax-on-tax caused by blocked input tax credits between Central CENVAT and State VAT) and **origin-based taxation** (where producing States kept Central Sales Tax), the **101st Amendment (2016)** instituted a unified, **destination-based consumption tax** anchored on the taxable event of **"supply"** (**Article 366(12A)**). Bypassing the Seventh Schedule via a ***non-obstante* clause** ("notwithstanding" **Articles 246 and 254**), **Article 246A(1)** grants Parliament (**CGST**) and State Legislatures (**SGST**) simultaneous, equal competence over intra-state supplies without **Article 254 repugnancy** (automatic Union override), while **Article 246A(2)** and **Article 269A** give Parliament exclusive power over inter-state/import **IGST**. To protect State revenue autonomy, **alcoholic liquor for human consumption** is constitutionally excluded from GST (**Article 366(12A)**), **five petroleum products** (crude, diesel, petrol, natural gas, ATF) remain under pre-GST Central Excise and *ad valorem* (value-percentage) State VAT until the GST Council recommends their inclusion (**Article 279A(5)**), and **tobacco** faces dual GST plus Union Excise (**Entry 84 List I**).
* **6. The GST Council (Article 279A), Weighted Voting Mechanics, and the Mohit Minerals Doctrine:** To coordinate 32 taxing jurisdictions, **Article 279A** created the **GST Council** operating on a **75% (three-fourths) weighted majority** of members present and voting, where the Union holds **1/3rd (33.33%)** weight (an absolute unilateral Union veto) and all 31 States/UTs share **2/3rds (66.67%)** equally (~2.15% each, allowing any 12 States to exercise a collective blocking veto). In ***Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022)**, the Supreme Court ruled that GST Council recommendations carry only persuasive value and are **not constitutionally binding** on Parliament or State Legislatures when enacting primary laws under **Article 246A**—protecting **"uncooperative federalism"** (democratic state contestation against central hegemony)—though they remain a binding statutory ***sine qua non*** (indispensable condition) for delegated executive rate notifications under the CGST/SGST Acts. Meanwhile, the **GST Compensation Cess**—levied on luxury/sin goods to finance a **14% CAGR** (compounded annual growth rate) revenue guarantee to States (2017–2022) and extended until March 2026 to repay ₹2.69 lakh crore in pandemic **back-to-back loans**—completed its sunset in **2025–2026**, merging non-tobacco sin goods into a **40% special GST slab** (Sept 2025) and shifting tobacco/pan masala to Central Excise and a new security cess (Feb 2026).

### IV. The Finance Commission (Article 280), Vertical-Horizontal Equity, and Discretionary Transfers

* **7. Constitutional Mandate and Devolution Mechanics of the Finance Commission (Articles 280–281):** Every fifth year under **Article 280**, the President constitutes the **Finance Commission** (an independent **quasi-judicial** body with Civil Court powers) to recommend **vertical devolution** (the States' collective share of the divisible pool), **horizontal devolution** (the inter-se state allocation formula), **Article 275** grants-in-aid, augmentation of State funds for **Panchayats (Article 280(3)(bb))** and **Municipalities (Article 280(3)(c))** based on **State Finance Commissions (SFCs)**, and Presidential **Terms of Reference (ToR)**. After vertical devolution jumped from 32% to 42% under the **14th FC** (following the Planning Commission's abolition) and adjusted to **41%** under the **15th FC** (after J&K's reorganization into Union Territories), the **16th Finance Commission (2026–2031, chaired by Dr. Arvind Panagariya)** retained the **41%** vertical share. Crucially, the 16th FC shifted from entitlement to compliance by completely discontinuing **Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit (PDRD) Grants** (which bridged budget gaps but created moral hazard) and channeling its ₹9.47 lakh crore grants into strict condition-linked **Local Body Grants** (₹8.00 lakh crore, requiring timely SFCs and audited accounts) and **Disaster Management Grants** (₹2.04 lakh crore).
* **8. Horizontal Devolution Friction, the North-South Fiscal Debate, and Divisible Pool Erosion:** Horizontal sharing pits **Redistributive Equity** (supporting populous, lower-GSDP northern/eastern States via **Income Distance**, trimmed from 45% to **42.5%** by the 16th FC, and **2011 Census Population** at **17.5%**) against **Fiscal Efficiency** (rewarding southern/western States that achieved fertility stabilization via **Demographic Performance / inverse Total Fertility Rate** at **10%** and preserved forests via **Forest & Ecology** at **10%**). To defuse North-South friction over southern States receiving only ₹15–₹36 per ₹100 of tax contributed, the **16th FC (2026–31)** dropped the 2.5% Tax Effort criterion, trimmed **Area** to **10%**, and introduced a landmark **10% weight for "Contribution to GDP"**. Simultaneously, States protest that the Union's heavy reliance on non-shareable cesses and surcharges (~18–20% of GTR) erodes the nominal 41% devolution to an **effective ~32.8% of GTR**, while recycling those retained funds through **Article 282** discretionary **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)** (upheld in *Bhim Singh*, 2010)—which, unlike untied **Article 275** statutory grants, lock State budgets into **60:40 / 90:10 matching contributions** and rigid **Single Nodal Agency (SNA)** "just-in-time" treasury rules.

### V. Sub-National Debt Governance, Article 293 Borrowing Constraints, and Federal Litigation

* **9. Constitutional Regulation of Public Borrowing (Articles 292–293) and Fiscal Responsibility Regimes:** Sovereign borrowing is sharply asymmetrical: while **Article 292** empowers the Union to borrow domestically and externally without statutory ceilings, **Article 293(1)** confines State borrowing strictly within India, and **Article 293(3)–(4)** mandates that any State with an outstanding central loan or guarantee (all 28 States) must obtain conditional **Union consent** to raise any loan. Operating alongside State **FRBM Acts** (Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management laws anchoring State fiscal deficits at **3.0% of GSDP** under the 15th and 16th FCs), the Union uses **Article 293(3)–(4)** to enforce each State's annual **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)**. When States circumvented this 3% cap via **Off-Budget Borrowings (OBBs)** raised by **Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs)** and State PSEs (like Kerala's **KIIFB**), the Union directed in 2022 that any SPV loan serviced from the State budget or escrowed State taxes/cesses counts as direct State borrowing and is deducted from the State's open-market NBC.
* **10. The Article 293(3) Federal Litigation: State of Kerala v. Union of India (2024 Reference to a 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Challenging this deduction in an **Article 131 Original Federal Suit**, ***State of Kerala v. Union of India* (2024)** pitted Kerala's claim of plenary competence over *"Public debt of the State"* (**Entry 43 List II**) and a narrow creditor-risk reading of **Article 293(3)** against the Union's defense of national macroeconomic stability, sovereign credit ratings, and **lifting the corporate veil** (treating budget-serviced SPV debt as State debt in substance). On April 1, 2024, the Supreme Court rejected Kerala's plea for an interim ₹26,226 crore borrowing bailout under the **Three-Pronged Injunction Test**—ruling that **Prima Facie Case** (initial textual merit under Article 293), **Balance of Convenience** (weighing national macroeconomic harm against self-inflicted State fiscal hardship), and **Irreparable Injury** (since denied quotas can be adjusted in future years) all favored the Union—and referred four foundational questions on **Article 293** to a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** under **Article 145(3)**, setting up the transition to **Part 15's Emergency Provisions (Article 360 Financial Emergency)** and **Asymmetrical Federalism**.
