# Chapter 1: Oscillations, Resonance, Coupled Modes, String Waves & Dispersion

## 1. Simple Harmonic Motion and Phase-Space Geometry

Whenever any physical system—a vibrating crystal ion, a loaded beam, an $LC$ circuit, or a pendulum—sits near a smooth local minimum $x_0$ of its potential energy $U(x)$, small displacements probe only the local curvature of that valley. Expanding $U(x)$ in a Taylor series about equilibrium $x_0$ (where $U'(x_0) = 0$ and $U''(x_0) \equiv k > 0$) yields a parabolic potential well $U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \frac{1}{2}k(x - x_0)^2$.

This universal quadratic approximation explains why Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) serves as the linear backbone of classical mechanics, acoustics, optics, and quantum field theory. Setting $x_0 = 0$ and shifting the energy zero to $U(0) = 0$, the restoring force is strictly Hookean: $F(x) = -\frac{dU}{dx} = -kx$.

### 1.1 Governing Equation, Solution and Phase-Space Ellipses

**Physical Assumptions and Setup:** Consider a particle of constant inertial mass $m$ constrained to move along the $x$-axis under a conservative linear restoring force $F = -kx$ ($k > 0$) with zero friction. All quantities are expressed in SI units ($m$ in $\text{kg}$, $k$ in $\text{N m}^{-1}$, $x$ in $\text{m}$, $t$ in $\text{s}$).

Applying Newton's Second Law gives the homogeneous second-order linear ODE:

$$m\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + kx = 0 \implies \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad \omega_0 \equiv \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

Trying an exponential ansatz $x(t) = C e^{rt}$ yields the characteristic equation $r^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0$, giving purely imaginary roots $r = \pm i\omega_0$. Taking the general real superposition produces the displacement, velocity, and canonical momentum $p = m\dot{x}$:

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi), \qquad v(t) = \dot{x}(t) = -\omega_0 A\sin(\omega_0 t + \phi), \qquad p(t) = -m\omega_0 A\sin(\omega_0 t + \phi)$$

Here $A$ is the amplitude and $\phi$ is the initial phase constant, uniquely fixed by initial conditions $x(0) = x_0$ and $v(0) = v_0$:

$$A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\right)^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = -\frac{v_0}{\omega_0 x_0}$$

Eliminating the time parameter $(\omega_0 t + \phi)$ via $\cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) + \sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi) = 1$ yields the trajectory in the two-dimensional $(x, p)$ **phase space**:

$$\frac{x^2}{A^2} + \frac{p^2}{(m\omega_0 A)^2} = 1$$

Because total mechanical energy $E = T + U = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2 A^2$ is strictly conserved, every iso-energy orbit is a closed ellipse centered at the origin with semi-axes $a = A = \sqrt{2E/k}$ and $b = m\omega_0 A = \sqrt{2mE}$:

$$\boxed{\frac{x^2}{2E/k} + \frac{p^2}{2mE} = 1}$$

The phase point $(x(t), p(t))$ sweeps this ellipse **clockwise** (since $p > 0 \implies \dot{x} > 0$, moving right in the upper half-plane) with period $T_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = \frac{1}{\nu_0}$. Computing the enclosed phase-space area (the action integral $J$) gives:

$$J = \oint p\,dx = \pi a b = \pi\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}}\right)\left(\sqrt{2mE}\right) = 2\pi E\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = \frac{2\pi E}{\omega_0} = \frac{E}{\nu_0}$$

Because $J = E/\nu_0$ is an **adiabatic invariant**, slowly changing the spring stiffness $k$ or pendulum length $\ell$ changes the energy strictly in proportion to the frequency ($E \propto \nu_0$)—the classical precursor to Planck's quantization rule $E = n h \nu$.

Computing the cycle-averaged kinetic and potential energies over one period $T_0$:

$$\langle T \rangle = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2 A^2 \sin^2(\omega_0 t + \phi)\,dt = \frac{1}{4}kA^2 = \frac{E}{2}, \qquad \langle U \rangle = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \cos^2(\omega_0 t + \phi)\,dt = \frac{1}{4}kA^2 = \frac{E}{2}$$

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Checks:**
- $[\omega_0] = \sqrt{\frac{\text{N m}^{-1}}{\text{kg}}} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{kg m s}^{-2}\text{ m}^{-1}}{\text{kg}}} = \text{s}^{-1}$, consistent with $\omega_0 t$ being dimensionless.
- $[J] = [p][x] = \text{kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-1} = \text{J s}$, matching $[E/\nu_0] = \text{J}\cdot\text{s}$.
- In the limit $k \to 0$ ($\omega_0 \to 0$), the ellipse stretches horizontally into horizontal straight lines $p = \text{const}$ (free particle).

## 2. Damped Harmonic Oscillator

Real mechanical and electromagnetic oscillators never stay on a closed phase-space ellipse forever. Viscous fluid drag, eddy currents, or radiative losses continuously drain mechanical energy into disordered thermal motion, pulling the phase-space trajectory steadily inward toward the equilibrium point $(0, 0)$.

At low Reynolds numbers (Stokes drag) or in linear ohmic resistors, the dissipative force opposes instantaneous velocity linearly: $F_d = -b\dot{x}$, where $b > 0$ is the damping coefficient ($[b] = \text{N s m}^{-1} = \text{kg s}^{-1}$).

### 2.1 Governing ODE and Three Dynamical Regimes

**Physical Assumptions and Notation Lock:** A particle of mass $m$ experiences restoring force $-kx$ and linear viscous drag $-b\dot{x}$. Applying Newton's Second Law yields:

$$m\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0$$

Dividing through by $m$ gives the canonical form:

$$\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + 2\beta\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad 2\beta \equiv \gamma \equiv \frac{b}{m}, \qquad \omega_0 \equiv \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

Here $\beta = \frac{b}{2m}$ ($[\beta] = \text{s}^{-1}$) is the **amplitude damping constant** (half the energy decay rate $\gamma = b/m$). Substituting the trial solution $x(t) = C e^{rt}$ produces the auxiliary quadratic:

$$r^2 + 2\beta r + \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies r_{\pm} = -\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 - \omega_0^2}$$

The sign of the discriminant $\Delta = \beta^2 - \omega_0^2$ divides all damped dynamics into three physically distinct regimes.

#### Case I - Underdamped (Lightly Damped / Oscillatory) Motion ($\beta < \omega_0$)

When dissipation is weaker than restoring inertia, the radical becomes purely imaginary. Defining the **damped angular frequency**:

$$\omega_d \equiv \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2} < \omega_0$$

the roots are complex conjugates $r_{\pm} = -\beta \pm i\omega_d$. The general real solution is:

$$x(t) = e^{-\beta t}\left(C_1 e^{i\omega_d t} + C_2 e^{-i\omega_d t}\right) = e^{-\beta t}\left(B_1\cos\omega_d t + B_2\sin\omega_d t\right)$$

Combining the trigonometric terms into amplitude–phase form gives:

$$\boxed{x(t) = A_0 e^{-\beta t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi), \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2}}$$

Notice two subtle physical features. First, damping stretches the quasi-period $T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d} = \frac{T_0}{\sqrt{1 - (\beta/\omega_0)^2}} > T_0$; for weak damping ($\beta \ll \omega_0$), the frequency shift is second-order small: $\omega_d \approx \omega_0\left(1 - \frac{\beta^2}{2\omega_0^2}\right)$.

Second, the turning points ($\dot{x} = 0$) do **not** coincide with the envelope contact points $\cos(\omega_d t + \phi) = \pm 1$. Differentiating $x(t)$:

$$\dot{x}(t) = -A_0 e^{-\beta t}\left[\beta\cos(\omega_d t + \phi) + \omega_d\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)\right] = 0 \implies \tan(\omega_d t + \phi) = -\frac{\beta}{\omega_d}$$

Because $\tan\theta$ has period $\pi$, consecutive extrema still occur at exact uniform spacing $\Delta t = \frac{\pi}{\omega_d} = \frac{T_d}{2}$, and consecutive maxima on the same side are separated by the exact quasi-period $T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}$. In phase space, the trajectory spirals inward toward the origin.

#### Case II - Critically Damped Motion ($\beta = \omega_0$)

When $\beta = \omega_0$, the discriminant vanishes and the characteristic equation yields a single repeated root $r_1 = r_2 = -\omega_0$. A second linearly independent solution is obtained rigorously by taking the limit $\omega_d \to 0$ of the underdamped solution (or via reduction of order $x(t) = u(t)e^{-\omega_0 t}$):

$$\frac{d^2}{dt^2}(u e^{-\omega_0 t}) + 2\omega_0\frac{d}{dt}(u e^{-\omega_0 t}) + \omega_0^2 (u e^{-\omega_0 t}) = e^{-\omega_0 t}\frac{d^2 u}{dt^2} = 0 \implies u(t) = C_1 + C_2 t$$

Hence the general critically damped solution is:

$$\boxed{x(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t)\,e^{-\omega_0 t}}$$

Applying initial conditions $x(0) = x_0$ and $\dot{x}(0) = v_0$ gives $C_1 = x_0$ and $C_2 = v_0 + \omega_0 x_0$. The system cannot oscillate—at most it crosses zero once (if $v_0 < -\omega_0 x_0$ with $x_0 > 0$). Starting from rest ($v_0 = 0$), $x(t) = x_0(1 + \omega_0 t)e^{-\omega_0 t}$ decays monotonically to zero **faster than any overdamped system**, making critical damping the optimal design point for ballistic galvanometers, automobile shock absorbers, and seismic instruments.

#### Case III - Overdamped (Heavy / Dead-Beat) Motion ($\beta > \omega_0$)

When drag dominates, define the real positive quantity $\alpha \equiv \sqrt{\beta^2 - \omega_0^2} < \beta$. Both characteristic roots $r_1 = -(\beta - \alpha)$ and $r_2 = -(\beta + \alpha)$ are strictly real and negative, producing a non-oscillatory superposition of two decaying exponentials:

$$\boxed{x(t) = C_1 e^{-(\beta - \alpha)t} + C_2 e^{-(\beta + \alpha)t} = e^{-\beta t}\left(C_1 e^{\alpha t} + C_2 e^{-\alpha t}\right)}$$

For heavy damping ($\beta \gg \omega_0$), binomial expansion gives $\alpha = \beta\sqrt{1 - \omega_0^2/\beta^2} \approx \beta - \frac{\omega_0^2}{2\beta}$. The slow root approaches $-(\beta - \alpha) \approx -\frac{\omega_0^2}{2\beta} = -\frac{k}{b}$, while the fast root approaches $-2\beta = -\frac{b}{m}$. Because $\frac{\omega_0^2}{2\beta} \ll \omega_0$, an overdamped pendulum creeps back to equilibrium far more sluggishly than a critically damped one.

### 2.2 Logarithmic Decrement, Energy Decay, Relaxation Time and Quality Factor

To quantify dissipation experimentally in an underdamped oscillator, measure the ratio of two successive displacement maxima $x_n$ and $x_{n+1}$ separated by one full quasi-period $T_d = 2\pi/\omega_d$. Since $\cos[\omega_d(t + T_d) + \phi] = \cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$:

$$\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}} = \frac{A_0 e^{-\beta t_n}}{A_0 e^{-\beta(t_n + T_d)}} = e^{\beta T_d}$$

The **logarithmic decrement** $\Lambda$ is defined as the natural logarithm of this ratio:

$$\boxed{\Lambda \equiv \ln\left(\frac{x_n}{x_{n+1}}\right) = \beta T_d = \frac{2\pi\beta}{\omega_d} = \frac{2\pi\beta}{\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2}}}$$

Over $N$ complete oscillations, $\frac{x_0}{x_N} = \left(e^{\beta T_d}\right)^N = e^{N\Lambda}$, providing the precision laboratory formula:

$$\Lambda = \frac{1}{N}\ln\left(\frac{x_0}{x_N}\right)$$

Now compute the instantaneous mechanical energy $E(t) = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2$. Differentiating directly with respect to $t$ and using the exact equation of motion $m\ddot{x} + kx = -b\dot{x}$:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \dot{x}(m\ddot{x} + kx) = -b\dot{x}^2 \le 0$$

Energy never increases; dissipation pauses momentarily at each turning point ($\dot{x} = 0$) and peaks as the mass whips through $x = 0$. In the **lightly damped regime** ($\beta \ll \omega_0$, so $\omega_d \approx \omega_0$ and $\beta/\omega_0 \ll 1$), substitute $x(t) = A_0 e^{-\beta t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$ and $\dot{x}(t) \approx -\omega_0 A_0 e^{-\beta t}\sin(\omega_d t + \phi)$:

$$E(t) \approx \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2 A_0^2 e^{-2\beta t}\sin^2(\omega_d t + \phi) + \frac{1}{2}k A_0^2 e^{-2\beta t}\cos^2(\omega_d t + \phi) = \frac{1}{2}k A_0^2 e^{-2\beta t} = E_0 e^{-2\beta t}$$

Defining the **energy relaxation time** $\tau$ as the time required for mechanical energy to fall to $1/e$ of its initial value:

$$\boxed{E(t) = E_0 e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau \equiv \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{m}{b}}$$

Notice that amplitude decays as $e^{-t/(2\tau)}$, taking twice as long ($2\tau = 1/\beta$) to drop by $1/e$ as energy takes ($\tau = 1/(2\beta)$).

The **Quality Factor ($Q$)** measures how many radians of oscillation occur before the energy falls to $1/e$ of its initial value. Formally:

$$Q \equiv 2\pi \frac{\text{Energy stored in the oscillator}}{\text{Energy dissipated per cycle}} = 2\pi\frac{E}{|\Delta E|_{\text{cycle}}}$$

For weak damping, the energy lost over one period $T_d$ is $|\Delta E|_{\text{cycle}} \approx \left|\frac{dE}{dt}\right| T_d = \frac{E}{\tau}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}$. Substituting directly yields:

$$\boxed{Q = \omega_d \tau = \frac{\omega_d}{2\beta} \approx \frac{\omega_0}{2\beta} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b} = \frac{\sqrt{mk}}{b} \approx \frac{\pi}{\Lambda}}$$

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Checks:**
- $[\Lambda] = [\beta][T_d] = \text{s}^{-1}\cdot\text{s} = 1$ (dimensionless); $[Q] = [\omega_0][\tau] = \text{s}^{-1}\cdot\text{s} = 1$ (dimensionless).
- As $b \to 0$ ($\beta \to 0$): $\omega_d \to \omega_0$, $\Lambda \to 0$, $\tau \to \infty$, and $Q \to \infty$, recovering ideal conservative SHM.
- As $\beta \to \omega_0^-$: $\omega_d \to 0$, $T_d \to \infty$, $\Lambda \to \infty$, smoothly merging into critical damping.

| Regime | Damping Condition | Auxiliary Roots $r_{\pm}$ | General Displacement $x(t)$ | Phase-Space Trajectory | Representative System |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Undamped** | $\beta = 0$ ($Q = \infty$) | $\pm i\omega_0$ | $A_0\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$ | Closed clockwise ellipse | Ideal vacuum pendulum |
| **Underdamped** | $0 < \beta < \omega_0$ ($Q > 1/2$) | $-\beta \pm i\omega_d$ | $A_0 e^{-\beta t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)$ | Inward logarithmic spiral to $(0,0)$ | Tuning fork, quartz crystal ($Q \sim 10^5$) |
| **Critically Damped** | $\beta = \omega_0$ ($Q = 1/2$) | $-\omega_0, -\omega_0$ | $(C_1 + C_2 t)e^{-\omega_0 t}$ | Fastest non-spiraling approach to $(0,0)$ | Dead-beat galvanometer, car struts |
| **Overdamped** | $\beta > \omega_0$ ($Q < 1/2$) | $-\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 - \omega_0^2}$ | $C_1 e^{-(\beta-\alpha)t} + C_2 e^{-(\beta+\alpha)t}$ | Sluggish monotonic curve into $(0,0)$ | Hydraulic door closer, oil-damped bob |

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 1: Logarithmic Decrement, Damping Constant and $Q$-Factor ($m = 0.50\text{ kg}$, $k = 200\text{ N m}^{-1}$, amplitude $12.0\text{ cm} \to 3.0\text{ cm}$ in $15$ cycles)
> **Problem:** A spring–mass oscillator with $m = 0.50\text{ kg}$ and $k = 200\text{ N m}^{-1}$ executes underdamped oscillations in a viscous medium. Its amplitude drops from $12.0\text{ cm}$ to $3.0\text{ cm}$ over $15$ complete oscillations. Compute (i) the logarithmic decrement $\Lambda$, (ii) the damping constant $\beta$ and drag coefficient $b$, (iii) the damped angular frequency $\omega_d$, (iv) the energy relaxation time $\tau$, and (v) the quality factor $Q$.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Step 1 — Undamped natural angular frequency:**
> $$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{200\text{ N m}^{-1}}{0.50\text{ kg}}} = \sqrt{400\text{ s}^{-2}} = 20.0\text{ rad s}^{-1}$$
>
> **Step 2 — Logarithmic decrement $\Lambda$:**
> $$\Lambda = \frac{1}{N}\ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A_N}\right) = \frac{1}{15}\ln\left(\frac{12.0\text{ cm}}{3.0\text{ cm}}\right) = \frac{\ln 4}{15} = \frac{1.3863}{15} = \boxed{0.09242}$$
>
> **Step 3 — Exact damping constant $\beta$ and drag coefficient $b$:**
> From $\Lambda = \frac{2\pi\beta}{\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2}}$, squaring both sides yields:
> $$\Lambda^2(\omega_0^2 - \beta^2) = 4\pi^2\beta^2 \implies \beta = \frac{\Lambda\,\omega_0}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \Lambda^2}}$$
> Substituting $\Lambda = 0.09242$, $4\pi^2 = 39.4784$, $\Lambda^2 = 0.00854$:
> $$\beta = \frac{0.09242 \times 20.0}{\sqrt{39.4784 + 0.00854}} = \frac{1.8484}{6.2839} = \boxed{0.2942\text{ s}^{-1}}$$
> *(Note: the weak-damping estimate $\beta \approx \frac{\Lambda\omega_0}{2\pi} = 0.2942\text{ s}^{-1}$ agrees to four significant figures because $\Lambda \ll 2\pi$.)*
> $$b = 2m\beta = 2(0.50\text{ kg})(0.2942\text{ s}^{-1}) = \boxed{0.2942\text{ N s m}^{-1}}$$
>
> **Step 4 — Damped angular frequency $\omega_d$:**
> $$\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2} = \sqrt{400 - (0.2942)^2} = \sqrt{399.9135} = \boxed{19.998\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
>
> **Step 5 — Energy relaxation time $\tau$ and Quality Factor $Q$:**
> $$\tau = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{1}{2(0.2942\text{ s}^{-1})} = \boxed{1.700\text{ s}}$$
> $$Q = \omega_d \tau = (19.998\text{ rad s}^{-1})(1.700\text{ s}) = \boxed{34.0}$$
> *(Sanity check: $Q \approx \pi/\Lambda = 3.1416 / 0.09242 = 34.0$.)*

## 3. Forced Harmonic Oscillator, Resonance and Power Absorption

Left alone, any damped oscillator dies out. To sustain perpetual motion, an external periodic agent must pump energy into the system at a driving angular frequency $\omega$. When $\omega$ approaches the oscillator's internal frequency $\omega_0$, tiny periodic pushes arrive coherently in step with the velocity, building massive steady-state oscillations—the universal phenomenon of **resonance**.

### 3.1 Transient vs. Steady State via Complex Phasor Algebra

**Physical Assumptions and Setup:** Apply an external harmonic force $F(t) = F_0\cos(\omega t)$ (with real amplitude $F_0 > 0$) to a damped oscillator of parameters $(m, b, k)$. The inhomogeneous equation of motion is:

$$m\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = F_0\cos(\omega t)$$

Dividing by $m$ and setting $2\beta = b/m$, $\omega_0^2 = k/m$, and $f_0 = F_0/m$:

$$\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + 2\beta\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2 x = f_0\cos(\omega t)$$

By linearity, the complete general solution is the sum of the homogeneous (complementary) solution $x_h(t)$ and a particular steady-state integral $x_p(t)$:

$$x(t) = x_h(t) + x_p(t)$$

Because $x_h(t)$ carries the decaying envelope $e^{-\beta t}$ (Section 2), it represents the **transient response** controlled by initial conditions $(x_0, v_0)$. After several relaxation times ($t \gg \tau$), the transient vanishes and only the **steady-state response** $x_p(t)$ survives, locked strictly to the driving frequency $\omega$.

To solve for $x_p(t)$ cleanly without tedious trigonometric identities, promote the real ODE to the complex plane by letting $x_p(t) = \operatorname{Re}[z(t)]$, where $z(t)$ satisfies:

$$\frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} + 2\beta\frac{dz}{dt} + \omega_0^2 z = f_0 e^{i\omega t}$$

Try the steady-state phasor ansatz $z(t) = \tilde{A}\,e^{i\omega t}$, where $\tilde{A} = A(\omega)e^{-i\delta(\omega)}$ is a complex amplitude with real magnitude $A(\omega) > 0$ and phase lag $\delta(\omega)$. Substituting $\dot{z} = i\omega z$ and $\ddot{z} = -\omega^2 z$ cancels $e^{i\omega t}$:

$$\left(-\omega^2 + 2i\beta\omega + \omega_0^2\right)\tilde{A} = f_0 \implies \tilde{A} = \frac{f_0}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + i(2\beta\omega)}$$

Multiplying numerator and denominator by the complex conjugate $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) - i(2\beta\omega)$ isolates the modulus and phase:

$$\tilde{A} = \frac{f_0\left[(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) - i(2\beta\omega)\right]}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\beta\omega)^2} = A(\omega)\,e^{-i\delta(\omega)}$$

Taking the real part $x_p(t) = \operatorname{Re}\left[A(\omega)e^{i(\omega t - \delta)}\right]$ yields the exact steady-state displacement, amplitude, and phase lag:

$$\boxed{x_p(t) = A(\omega)\cos\big(\omega t - \delta(\omega)\big)}$$

$$\boxed{A(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\beta\omega)^2}} = \frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k - m\omega^2)^2 + (b\omega)^2}}}$$

$$\boxed{\tan\delta(\omega) = \frac{2\beta\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2} = \frac{b\omega}{k - m\omega^2}, \qquad \sin\delta = \frac{2\beta\omega}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\beta\omega)^2}} > 0}$$

### 3.2 Phase-Lag Flip ($0 \to \pi/2 \to \pi$) Across Resonance

Because $\sin\delta(\omega) > 0$ for all $\omega > 0$ and $\beta > 0$, the displacement **always lags** the driving force with $\delta(\omega) \in (0, \pi)$:

1. **Quasi-static / Stiffness-controlled limit ($\omega \ll \omega_0$):** The spring dominates; $A(\omega) \to F_0/k$ and $\tan\delta \to 0^+ \implies \delta \to 0$. The mass tracks the push in phase.
2. **Undamped natural frequency ($\omega = \omega_0$):** Inertia ($-m\omega^2 x$) and stiffness ($+kx$) cancel identically; $\cos\delta = 0, \sin\delta = 1 \implies \delta = \frac{\pi}{2}$. Displacement lags the force by $90^\circ$, meaning velocity is strictly **in phase** with the driving force.
3. **High-frequency / Inertia-controlled limit ($\omega \gg \omega_0$):** Mass inertia dominates; $A(\omega) \approx \frac{F_0}{m\omega^2} \to 0$ and $\cos\delta < 0, \sin\delta \to 0^+ \implies \delta \to \pi$. The oscillator moves completely out of phase (anti-phase) with the driver.

### 3.3 Amplitude Resonance vs. Velocity (Energy) Resonance

Examiners routinely ask candidates to prove why **amplitude resonance** and **velocity (energy) resonance** do not occur at the same driving frequency.

#### Amplitude Resonance ($\omega_A$)

The steady-state amplitude $A(\omega)$ is maximized when the radicand $D(\omega^2) \equiv (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2\omega^2$ reaches its minimum. Differentiating $D$ with respect to $\omega^2$ and setting the derivative to zero:

$$\frac{dD}{d(\omega^2)} = -2(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + 4\beta^2 = 0 \implies \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2$$

Since $\frac{d^2 D}{d(\omega^2)^2} = +2 > 0$, this is a strict minimum provided $\omega_0^2 > 2\beta^2$ (i.e., $Q > 1/\sqrt{2}$). Thus **amplitude resonance** occurs at:

$$\boxed{\omega_A = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2}}$$

Substituting $\omega = \omega_A$ back into $D(\omega_A^2) = (2\beta^2)^2 + 4\beta^2(\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2) = 4\beta^2(\omega_0^2 - \beta^2) = 4\beta^2\omega_d^2$ gives the exact maximum displacement amplitude:

$$\boxed{A_{\max} = \frac{F_0/m}{2\beta\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2}} = \frac{F_0}{b\,\omega_d}}$$

If damping is heavy enough that $\beta \ge \omega_0/\sqrt{2}$ ($Q \le 1/\sqrt{2}$), no peak exists at any $\omega > 0$; $A(\omega)$ decreases monotonically from $F_0/k$ at $\omega = 0$.

#### Velocity Resonance ($\omega_V$) and Mechanical Impedance

Differentiating the steady-state displacement $x_p(t) = A(\omega)\cos(\omega t - \delta)$ yields the steady-state velocity:

$$v_p(t) = -\omega A(\omega)\sin(\omega t - \delta) = V(\omega)\cos(\omega t - \delta_v), \qquad \delta_v \equiv \delta - \frac{\pi}{2}$$

Rewriting the velocity amplitude $V(\omega) = \omega A(\omega)$ by pulling $\omega$ inside the square root exposes the **complex mechanical impedance** $Z_m(\omega) = b + i\left(m\omega - \frac{k}{\omega}\right)$:

$$\boxed{V(\omega) = \frac{F_0}{\sqrt{b^2 + \left(m\omega - \frac{k}{\omega}\right)^2}} = \frac{F_0}{|Z_m(\omega)|}}$$

The denominator attains its global minimum **strictly** when the reactive term vanishes:

$$m\omega - \frac{k}{\omega} = 0 \implies \boxed{\omega_V = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \omega_0, \qquad V_{\max} = \frac{F_0}{b}}$$

Crucially, velocity resonance occurs at the **exact undamped natural frequency $\omega_0$**, completely independent of damping $b$! Comparing all three characteristic frequencies of a lightly damped oscillator gives the strict hierarchy:

$$\boxed{\omega_A = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2} \;<\; \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2} \;<\; \omega_V = \omega_0}$$

### 3.4 Power Absorption, Half-Power Bandwidth and Sharpness of Resonance

The instantaneous power delivered by the external driver is $P(t) = F(t)\,v_p(t) = F_0\cos(\omega t)\,V(\omega)\cos(\omega t - \delta_v)$. Expanding $\cos(\omega t - \delta_v) = \cos\omega t\cos\delta_v + \sin\omega t\sin\delta_v$ and averaging over one driving cycle $T = 2\pi/\omega$ (using $\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle = 1/2$ and $\langle\sin\omega t\cos\omega t\rangle = 0$):

$$\langle P(\omega)\rangle = \frac{1}{2} F_0 V(\omega)\cos\delta_v$$

From $\delta_v = \delta - \pi/2$, we have $\cos\delta_v = \sin\delta = \frac{b\omega}{\sqrt{(k - m\omega^2)^2 + (b\omega)^2}} = \frac{b}{|Z_m(\omega)|}$. Substituting $V(\omega) = F_0/|Z_m(\omega)|$ gives the exact cycle-averaged power absorbed (which equals the cycle-averaged power dissipated as heat $\langle b v_p^2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}b V^2(\omega)$):

$$\boxed{\langle P(\omega)\rangle = \frac{F_0^2\, b}{2\left[b^2 + \left(m\omega - \frac{k}{\omega}\right)^2\right]} = \frac{F_0^2}{2m}\frac{2\beta\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\beta\omega)^2}}$$

Maximum power absorption (also called **energy/power resonance**) occurs at $\omega_E = \omega_V = \omega_0$, where:

$$\langle P\rangle_{\max} = \frac{F_0^2}{2b} = \frac{F_0^2}{4m\beta}$$

To define the **half-power bandwidth** $\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1$, locate the two frequencies $\omega_1 < \omega_0 < \omega_2$ where $\langle P(\omega)\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\langle P\rangle_{\max}$:

$$\frac{F_0^2 b}{2\left[b^2 + \left(m\omega - \frac{k}{\omega}\right)^2\right]} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{F_0^2}{2b}\right) \iff \left|m\omega - \frac{k}{\omega}\right| = b$$

At these half-power points, $V(\omega) = V_{\max}/\sqrt{2}$ and $\delta_v = \pm\frac{\pi}{4}$ ($\delta = \frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{3\pi}{4}$). Solving both positive branches **without any weak-damping approximation**:

- Upper branch ($\omega_2 > \omega_0$): $m\omega_2^2 - b\omega_2 - k = 0 \implies \omega_2^2 - 2\beta\omega_2 - \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies \omega_2 = \beta + \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \beta^2}$.
- Lower branch ($\omega_1 < \omega_0$): $m\omega_1^2 + b\omega_1 - k = 0 \implies \omega_1^2 + 2\beta\omega_1 - \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies \omega_1 = -\beta + \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \beta^2}$.

Subtracting and multiplying $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ yield two exact universal identities valid for **all** values of damping:

$$\boxed{\Delta\omega \equiv \omega_2 - \omega_1 = 2\beta = \frac{b}{m}, \qquad \omega_1\omega_2 = \omega_0^2}$$

The **sharpness of resonance** is therefore measured directly by the Quality Factor $Q$:

$$\boxed{Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\beta} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b}}$$

Near resonance in a high-$Q$ oscillator ($|\omega - \omega_0| \ll \omega_0$), factoring $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \approx 2\omega_0(\omega_0 - \omega)$ reduces the power curve to a symmetric **Lorentzian** of Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) equal to $2\beta$:

$$\langle P(\omega)\rangle \approx \frac{\langle P\rangle_{\max}}{1 + \left(\frac{\omega - \omega_0}{\beta}\right)^2}$$

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Checks:**
- $[A(\omega)] = \frac{\text{N}}{\text{N m}^{-1}} = \text{m}$; $[\langle P\rangle] = \frac{\text{N}^2}{\text{N s m}^{-1}} = \text{N m s}^{-1} = \text{W}$.
- As $b \to 0$ ($Q \to \infty$): $\omega_A \to \omega_0$, $A(\omega_0) = Q(F_0/k) \to \infty$ (unbounded linear resonance growth $x(t) \propto t\sin\omega_0 t$), and $\Delta\omega \to 0$ (infinitely sharp spike).

| Feature | Amplitude Resonance | Velocity & Power Resonance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Resonant Frequency** | $\omega_A = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2}$ (shifts downward with damping) | $\omega_V = \omega_E = \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ (independent of damping) |
| **Existence Condition** | Requires $\beta < \omega_0/\sqrt{2}$ ($Q > 1/\sqrt{2}$) | Always exists for all $b > 0$ |
| **Peak Magnitude** | $A_{\max} = \dfrac{F_0}{b\,\omega_d} = \dfrac{F_0/m}{2\beta\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2}}$ | $V_{\max} = \dfrac{F_0}{b}$, $\quad\langle P\rangle_{\max} = \dfrac{F_0^2}{2b}$ |
| **Phase Lag $\delta$ (Displacement behind Force)** | $\tan\delta(\omega_A) = \dfrac{\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2}}{\beta}$ (slightly below $\pi/2$) | $\delta(\omega_0) = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ strictly ($\delta_v = 0$, force $\parallel$ velocity) |
| **Half-Power Bandwidth $\Delta\omega$** | Not symmetric around $\omega_A$ | Exact $\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1 = 2\beta = \omega_0/Q$ |

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2: Forced Oscillator Resonance Frequencies, Power Absorption and Exact Bandwidth ($m = 0.40\text{ kg}$, $k = 160\text{ N m}^{-1}$, $b = 1.6\text{ N s m}^{-1}$, $F = 8.0\cos\omega t\text{ N}$)
> **Problem:** A spring–mass oscillator with $m = 0.40\text{ kg}$, $k = 160\text{ N m}^{-1}$, and damping coefficient $b = 1.6\text{ N s m}^{-1}$ is driven by $F(t) = 8.0\cos(\omega t)\text{ N}$. Find (i) $\omega_0$, $\omega_d$, and amplitude resonance frequency $\omega_A$, (ii) steady-state amplitude at $\omega = \omega_0$ compared to exact $A_{\max}$ at $\omega_A$, (iii) maximum average power absorbed $\langle P\rangle_{\max}$, and (iv) exact half-power frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2$, bandwidth $\Delta\omega$, and $Q$-factor.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Step 1 — Characteristic angular frequencies:**
> $$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{160}{0.40}} = \sqrt{400} = \boxed{20.0\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
> $$2\beta = \frac{b}{m} = \frac{1.6}{0.40} = 4.0\text{ s}^{-1} \implies \beta = 2.0\text{ s}^{-1}$$
> $$\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \beta^2} = \sqrt{400 - 4} = \sqrt{396} = \boxed{19.90\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
> $$\omega_A = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2} = \sqrt{400 - 8} = \sqrt{392} = \boxed{19.80\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
>
> **Step 2 — Amplitude at $\omega_0$ vs. exact amplitude peak $A_{\max}$:**
> At velocity resonance $\omega = \omega_0$:
> $$A(\omega_0) = \frac{F_0}{b\omega_0} = \frac{8.0\text{ N}}{(1.6\text{ N s m}^{-1})(20.0\text{ rad s}^{-1})} = \boxed{0.2500\text{ m}}$$
> At exact amplitude resonance $\omega = \omega_A$:
> $$A_{\max} = \frac{F_0}{b\omega_d} = \frac{8.0\text{ N}}{(1.6\text{ N s m}^{-1})(19.90\text{ rad s}^{-1})} = \boxed{0.2513\text{ m}}$$
> *(Notice that $A_{\max}$ exceeds $A(\omega_0)$ by only $0.5\%$ because $Q = 5$ is already moderately large.)*
>
> **Step 3 — Maximum average power absorbed:**
> $$\langle P\rangle_{\max} = \frac{F_0^2}{2b} = \frac{(8.0\text{ N})^2}{2(1.6\text{ N s m}^{-1})} = \frac{64.0}{3.2} = \boxed{20.0\text{ W}}$$
>
> **Step 4 — Exact half-power frequencies, bandwidth and $Q$-factor:**
> $$\sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \beta^2} = \sqrt{400 + 4} = \sqrt{404} = 20.10\text{ rad s}^{-1}$$
> $$\omega_1 = -\beta + \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \beta^2} = -2.0 + 20.10 = \boxed{18.10\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
> $$\omega_2 = +\beta + \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \beta^2} = +2.0 + 20.10 = \boxed{22.10\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
> $$\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1 = 2\beta = \boxed{4.00\text{ rad s}^{-1}}, \qquad Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega} = \frac{20.0}{4.00} = \boxed{5.00}$$
> *(Geometric-mean check: $\omega_1\omega_2 = 18.10 \times 22.10 = 400.0 = \omega_0^2$.)*

## 4. Beats, Lissajous Figures and Coupled Oscillators

Linearity allows two or more harmonic motions to coexist simultaneously on the same particle or across interconnected masses. Depending on whether the superposed vibrations act along the same axis, along perpendicular axes, or across mechanically linked degrees of freedom, superposition creates rhythmic amplitude pulsing (**beats**), closed geometric loops (**Lissajous figures**), or collective eigenmodes (**coupled normal modes**).

### 4.1 Beats - Superposition of Collinear Near-Equal Frequencies

Consider two collinear harmonic waves of equal amplitude $a$ and slightly different angular frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2$ ($|\omega_1 - \omega_2| \ll \omega_1 + \omega_2$):

$$x_1(t) = a\cos(\omega_1 t), \qquad x_2(t) = a\cos(\omega_2 t)$$

Applying the sum-to-product identity $\cos\alpha + \cos\beta = 2\cos\left(\frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\alpha + \beta}{2}\right)$ yields:

$$\boxed{x(t) = x_1(t) + x_2(t) = \underbrace{\left[2a\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right)\right]}_{\text{slowly varying envelope } A_{\text{env}}(t)} \underbrace{\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}t\right)}_{\text{rapid carrier}}}$$

The rapid oscillation runs at the **carrier frequency** $\bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}$, while its amplitude is modulated slowly at $\omega_{\text{mod}} = \frac{|\omega_1 - \omega_2|}{2}$.

Why is the audible **beat frequency** equal to $|\nu_1 - \nu_2|$ rather than $\frac{1}{2}|\nu_1 - \nu_2|$? Acoustic intensity and energy depend on the **square** of the envelope amplitude:

$$I(t) \propto |A_{\text{env}}(t)|^2 = 4a^2\cos^2\left(\frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right) = 2a^2\Big[1 + \cos\big((\omega_1 - \omega_2)t\big)\Big]$$

Both positive and negative lobes of $\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right)$ produce an intensity maximum (waxing), with a $\pi$ phase slip of the carrier at each zero crossing (waning). Thus intensity waxes once every beat period $T_b = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega_1 - \omega_2|}$, giving:

$$\boxed{\nu_{\text{beat}} = \frac{1}{T_b} = |\nu_1 - \nu_2|}$$

**Unequal Amplitudes ($a_1 \ne a_2$):** Writing $x(t) = a_1\cos(\omega_1 t) + a_2\cos(\omega_2 t) = a_1\cos(\omega_1 t) + a_2\cos\big(\omega_1 t - \Delta\omega\, t\big)$ with $\Delta\omega = \omega_1 - \omega_2$, expand the second cosine and combine orthogonal components $\cos(\omega_1 t)$ and $\sin(\omega_1 t)$:

$$x(t) = \big(a_1 + a_2\cos\Delta\omega\,t\big)\cos(\omega_1 t) + \big(a_2\sin\Delta\omega\,t\big)\sin(\omega_1 t) = A(t)\cos\big(\omega_1 t - \theta(t)\big)$$

The slowly varying amplitude envelope is:

$$A(t) = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1 a_2\cos\big((\omega_1 - \omega_2)t\big)}$$

Thus amplitude oscillates between $A_{\max} = a_1 + a_2$ and $A_{\min} = |a_1 - a_2| > 0$, never dropping completely to zero. The beat frequency remains strictly $|\nu_1 - \nu_2|$, while the modulation visibility drops to $\mathcal{V} = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}} = \frac{2a_1 a_2}{a_1^2 + a_2^2}$.

### 4.2 Orthogonal Superposition - Lissajous Figures

When a particle simultaneously executes two mutually perpendicular SHMs along the $x$- and $y$-axes—as an electron beam does between orthogonal deflection plates in a Cathode Ray Oscilloscope (CRO)—the resulting two-dimensional curve is a **Lissajous figure**. The trajectory is closed and stationary if and only if the frequency ratio $\omega_x / \omega_y$ is a rational number $p/q$.

#### Case I - Equal Frequencies ($\omega_x = \omega_y = \omega$, Ratio $1:1$)

Let the orthogonal displacements be:

$$x = a\sin(\omega t + \phi), \qquad y = b\sin(\omega t)$$

From the $y$-equation, $\sin(\omega t) = \frac{y}{b}$ and $\cos(\omega t) = \pm\sqrt{1 - \frac{y^2}{b^2}}$. Expanding the $x$-equation:

$$\frac{x}{a} = \sin(\omega t)\cos\phi + \cos(\omega t)\sin\phi = \frac{y}{b}\cos\phi \pm \sqrt{1 - \frac{y^2}{b^2}}\sin\phi$$

Isolating the radical and squaring both sides:

$$\left(\frac{x}{a} - \frac{y}{b}\cos\phi\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{y^2}{b^2}\right)\sin^2\phi \implies \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\phi + \frac{y^2}{b^2}(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi) = \sin^2\phi$$

This yields the exact **general ellipse equation** inscribed in the rectangle $[-a, a] \times [-b, b]$:

$$\boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\phi = \sin^2\phi}$$

- **$\phi = 0$:** $\left(\frac{x}{a} - \frac{y}{b}\right)^2 = 0 \implies y = +\frac{b}{a}x$ (straight line in quadrants I and III).
- **$\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}$:** $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ (upright principal-axis ellipse; circle if $a = b$, traced clockwise since at $t=0$: $x = a, y = 0, \dot{y} = +b\omega$... wait: check direction! At $t = 0$, $x = a\sin(\pi/2) = +a$, $y = 0$, $\dot{y} = +b\omega > 0$, so the point moves **counter-clockwise** for $x = a\sin(\omega t + \pi/2), y = b\sin\omega t$!).
- **$\phi = \pi$:** $\left(\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b}\right)^2 = 0 \implies y = -\frac{b}{a}x$ (straight line in quadrants II and IV).
- **$\phi = \frac{3\pi}{2}$:** Same upright ellipse, traversed in the opposite (**clockwise**) sense.
- **Laboratory CRO Phase Measurement:** Setting $x = 0$ in the ellipse equation gives $y_0 = \pm b\sin\phi$, allowing direct readout of an unknown phase difference $\phi = \arcsin(y_0 / b)$ from the $y$-intercept $y_0$ and vertical semi-height $b$.

#### Case II - Frequency Ratio $2:1$ ($\omega_x = 2\omega, \omega_y = \omega$)

Take $x = a\sin(2\omega t + \phi)$ and $y = b\sin(\omega t)$. Expanding via double-angle identities $\sin(2\omega t) = 2\sin\omega t\cos\omega t$ and $\cos(2\omega t) = 1 - 2\sin^2\omega t$:

$$\frac{x}{a} = 2\frac{y}{b}\left(\pm\sqrt{1 - \frac{y^2}{b^2}}\right)\cos\phi + \left(1 - \frac{2y^2}{b^2}\right)\sin\phi$$

Moving the rational term to the left and squaring yields the quartic curve:

$$\boxed{\left[\frac{x}{a} - \left(1 - \frac{2y^2}{b^2}\right)\sin\phi\right]^2 = \frac{4y^2}{b^2}\left(1 - \frac{y^2}{b^2}\right)\cos^2\phi}$$

- **$\phi = 0$ or $\pi$:** $\frac{x^2}{a^2} = \frac{4y^2}{b^2}\left(1 - \frac{y^2}{b^2}\right)$, a symmetric **figure-of-eight** passing through the origin.
- **$\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}$:** $\frac{x}{a} = 1 - \frac{2y^2}{b^2}$, a **parabola** opening toward $-x$ (traced back and forth).

**General Tangency Rule for Unknown Frequency Ratios:** For any closed Lissajous figure bounded by the box $[-a, a] \times [-b, b]$ with $\omega_x / \omega_y = p/q$ (in lowest terms), let $N_y$ be the number of tangency points on a horizontal edge ($y = +b$) and $N_x$ be the number of tangency points on a vertical edge ($x = +a$), counting any sharp corner tangency as $1/2$. Then:

$$\frac{\omega_x}{\omega_y} = \frac{N_x \text{ (vertical-edge touches? Careful!)}}{\text{Verify directly!}}$$

Let us verify the edge-counting rule explicitly on $x = a\sin(2\omega t)$, $y = b\sin(\omega t)$ ($\omega_x/\omega_y = 2$):
- In one complete period $T = 2\pi/\omega$: $y$ reaches its upper maximum $+b$ once (at $\omega t = \pi/2$), giving $N_{\text{top}} = 1$ tangency on the horizontal line $y = +b$ (at $x = 0$).
- Meanwhile $x = a\sin(2\omega t)$ reaches its right maximum $+a$ twice (at $2\omega t = \pi/2, 5\pi/2$), giving $N_{\text{right}} = 2$ tangencies on the vertical line $x = +a$.
Therefore, the ratio is strictly:

$$\boxed{\frac{\omega_x}{\omega_y} = \frac{N_{\text{vertical edge}}}{N_{\text{horizontal edge}}} = \frac{\text{tangencies on } x = +a}{\text{tangencies on } y = +b}}$$

### 4.3 Two Coupled Oscillators, Normal Modes and Energy Sloshing

When two identical oscillators are linked by an interaction spring, neither mass executes pure sinusoidal motion on its own—pushing one continuously perturbs the other. Yet the combined system possesses two special collective patterns, called **normal modes**, in which **every** particle oscillates harmonically at the exact same frequency with constant relative phase.

**Physical Assumptions and Setup:** Consider two identical blocks of mass $m$ sliding on a frictionless track, each anchored to a rigid wall by an identical spring of stiffness $k$ and coupled to each other by a central spring of stiffness $k_c$. *(For two identical simple pendula of mass $m$ and length $\ell$ joined at their bobs by a horizontal spring $k_c$ under small oscillations, every equation below remains 100% identical under the exact substitution $k/m \leftrightarrow g/\ell$.)*

Let $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ denote the rightward displacements of masses $1$ and $2$ from equilibrium. The central spring stretches by $(x_2 - x_1)$, exerting $+k_c(x_2 - x_1)$ on mass $1$ and $-k_c(x_2 - x_1)$ on mass $2$. Newton's Second Law gives two coupled ODEs:

$$m\ddot{x}_1 = -kx_1 - k_c(x_1 - x_2) \implies m\ddot{x}_1 + (k + k_c)x_1 - k_c x_2 = 0 \qquad (1)$$

$$m\ddot{x}_2 = -kx_2 - k_c(x_2 - x_1) \implies m\ddot{x}_2 + (k + k_c)x_2 - k_c x_1 = 0 \qquad (2)$$

#### Method 1 - Secular Eigenvalue Determinant (General Matrix Method)

Seek normal-mode solutions where both masses vibrate synchronously at a single angular frequency $\omega$: $x_1(t) = C_1 e^{i\omega t}$, $x_2(t) = C_2 e^{i\omega t}$. Substituting into Eqs. (1)–(2):

$$\begin{pmatrix} k + k_c - m\omega^2 & -k_c \\ -k_c & k + k_c - m\omega^2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} C_1 \\ C_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

For non-trivial amplitudes $(C_1, C_2) \ne (0, 0)$, the determinant must vanish:

$$(k + k_c - m\omega^2)^2 - k_c^2 = 0 \implies k + k_c - m\omega^2 = \pm k_c$$

Solving both signs yields the two **normal-mode eigenfrequencies** and amplitude ratios:

1. **Symmetric (In-Phase) Mode ($+$ sign):**
   $$m\omega_1^2 = k \implies \boxed{\omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \quad \left(\text{pendula: } \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}\right)}, \qquad \frac{C_2}{C_1} = +1$$
   Both masses glide in unison ($x_1 = x_2$); the coupling spring neither stretches nor compresses, so $\omega_1$ is independent of $k_c$.

2. **Antisymmetric (Out-of-Phase) Mode ($-$ sign):**
   $$m\omega_2^2 = k + 2k_c \implies \boxed{\omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{k + 2k_c}{m}} \quad \left(\text{pendula: } \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell} + \frac{2k_c}{m}}\right)}, \qquad \frac{C_2}{C_1} = -1$$
   The masses move strictly opposite to each other ($x_1 = -x_2$); the midpoint of the coupling spring stays stationary, halving its effective length and doubling its effective stiffness to $2k_c$ on each mass.

```text
  Mode 1 (Symmetric / In-Phase, w1 = sqrt(k/m)):
  |~~k~~[ m1 ]~~~~kc~~~~[ m2 ]~~k~~|
           --->           --->          (kc unstretched)

  Mode 2 (Antisymmetric / Anti-Phase, w2 = sqrt((k + 2kc)/m)):
  |~~k~~[ m1 ]~~~~kc~~~~[ m2 ]~~k~~|
           --->           <---          (midpoint node; kc compressed)
```

#### Method 2 - Orthogonal Normal Coordinates and Mode-Energy Decoupling

Adding and subtracting Eqs. (1) and (2) and normalizing by $1/\sqrt{2}$ defines the **orthogonal normal coordinates**:

$$q_1 \equiv \frac{x_1 + x_2}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad q_2 \equiv \frac{x_1 - x_2}{\sqrt{2}} \iff x_1 = \frac{q_1 + q_2}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad x_2 = \frac{q_1 - q_2}{\sqrt{2}}$$

Substituting into Eqs. (1)–(2) cleanly decouples the system into two independent SHM oscillators:

$$\ddot{q}_1 + \omega_1^2 q_1 = 0, \qquad \ddot{q}_2 + \omega_2^2 q_2 = 0$$

Expressing the total mechanical energy $E = \frac{1}{2}m(\dot{x}_1^2 + \dot{x}_2^2) + \frac{1}{2}k(x_1^2 + x_2^2) + \frac{1}{2}k_c(x_1 - x_2)^2$ in normal coordinates removes every cross-term:

$$E = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}m\left(\dot{q}_1^2 + \omega_1^2 q_1^2\right)}_{E_1 = \text{const}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}m\left(\dot{q}_2^2 + \omega_2^2 q_2^2\right)}_{E_2 = \text{const}}$$

While individual oscillators exchange energy continuously, **normal modes never exchange energy with one another**.

#### Weak-Coupling Beats (Energy Sloshing)

Suppose mass $1$ is pulled to $x_1(0) = A$ while mass $2$ is held at equilibrium $x_2(0) = 0$, and both are released from rest ($\dot{x}_1(0) = \dot{x}_2(0) = 0$). Then $q_1(0) = q_2(0) = A/\sqrt{2}$ and $\dot{q}_1(0) = \dot{q}_2(0) = 0$, giving $q_1(t) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(\omega_1 t)$ and $q_2(t) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(\omega_2 t)$. Transforming back to physical coordinates:

$$x_1(t) = \frac{A}{2}\big(\cos\omega_1 t + \cos\omega_2 t\big) = A\cos\left(\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t\right)\cos\left(\frac{\omega_2 + \omega_1}{2}t\right)$$

$$x_2(t) = \frac{A}{2}\big(\cos\omega_1 t - \cos\omega_2 t\big) = A\sin\left(\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t\right)\sin\left(\frac{\omega_2 + \omega_1}{2}t\right)$$

Under weak coupling ($k_c \ll k$), $\Delta\omega \equiv \omega_2 - \omega_1 \approx \frac{k_c}{m\omega_1} \ll \bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}$. Treating the slow envelope as nearly constant over one fast carrier cycle, the instantaneous mechanical energies of the two oscillators slosh back and forth sinusoidally:

$$\boxed{E_1(t) \approx E_{\text{tot}}\cos^2\left(\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t\right), \qquad E_2(t) \approx E_{\text{tot}}\sin^2\left(\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t\right)}$$

Mass $1$ momentarily comes to a dead stop and transfers $100\%$ of its energy to mass $2$ at the **energy-transfer time**:

$$t_{\text{transfer}} = \frac{\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1} = \frac{T_b}{2}$$

Energy returns completely to mass $1$ after one full beat period $T_b = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1}$.

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Checks:**
- As $k_c \to 0$: $\omega_2 \to \omega_1$, so $t_{\text{transfer}} \to \infty$, $x_1(t) \to A\cos(\omega_1 t)$, and $x_2(t) \to 0$ (zero energy leaks to an uncoupled mass).
- As $k_c \to \infty$: $\omega_2 \to \infty$ (rigid bar connecting the masses), freezing out the antisymmetric mode ($x_1 = x_2$ always).

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 3: Coupled Simple Pendula Normal Modes and Energy Sloshing ($m = 0.50\text{ kg}$, $\ell = 1.00\text{ m}$, $k_c = 0.49\text{ N m}^{-1}$)
> **Problem:** Two identical simple pendula of bob mass $m = 0.50\text{ kg}$ and length $\ell = 1.00\text{ m}$ ($g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$) are coupled at their bobs by a light horizontal spring of force constant $k_c = 0.49\text{ N m}^{-1}$. Calculate (i) both normal-mode angular frequencies $\omega_1, \omega_2$ and linear frequencies $f_1, f_2$, (ii) the displacement equations $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ if bob $1$ is released from rest at $x_1(0) = 4.0\text{ cm}$ while bob $2$ starts at rest at $x_2(0) = 0$, and (iii) the time taken for the entire mechanical energy to transfer to bob $2$ for the first time.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Step 1 — Normal-mode frequencies:**
> For coupled simple pendula in the small-angle regime, the gravitational restoring stiffness is $k_{\text{eff}} = mg/\ell$, so $k_{\text{eff}}/m = g/\ell$:
> $$\omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}}{1.00\text{ m}}} = \boxed{3.130\text{ rad s}^{-1}}, \qquad f_1 = \frac{\omega_1}{2\pi} = \boxed{0.4982\text{ Hz}}$$
> $$\omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell} + \frac{2k_c}{m}} = \sqrt{9.80 + \frac{2(0.49)}{0.50}} = \sqrt{9.80 + 1.96} = \sqrt{11.76} = \boxed{3.429\text{ rad s}^{-1}}$$
> $$f_2 = \frac{\omega_2}{2\pi} = \boxed{0.5458\text{ Hz}}$$
>
> **Step 2 — Carrier and envelope modulation frequencies:**
> $$\bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2} = \frac{3.1305 + 3.4293}{2} = 3.280\text{ rad s}^{-1}$$
> $$\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2} = \frac{3.4293 - 3.1305}{2} = \frac{0.2988}{2} = 0.1494\text{ rad s}^{-1}$$
> With $A = 4.0\text{ cm} = 0.040\text{ m}$:
> $$\boxed{x_1(t) = 0.040\cos(0.1494\,t)\cos(3.280\,t)\text{ m}, \qquad x_2(t) = 0.040\sin(0.1494\,t)\sin(3.280\,t)\text{ m}}$$
>
> **Step 3 — First complete energy-transfer time:**
> Complete transfer to bob $2$ occurs when $\cos\left(\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t\right) = 0 \implies \frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t = \frac{\pi}{2}$:
> $$t_{\text{transfer}} = \frac{\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1} = \frac{3.1416}{0.2988\text{ rad s}^{-1}} = \boxed{10.51\text{ s}}$$
> *(One full cycle of energy transfer and return takes $T_b = 2t_{\text{transfer}} = 21.03\text{ s}$.)*

## 5. Stationary Waves, Boundary Impedance and Wave Packets

Passing from two coupled masses to an infinite chain of infinitesimal mass elements joined by continuous tension produces a one-dimensional elastic medium: the stretched string. Because every segment pulls on its immediate neighbors, a local disturbance does not stay localized—it propagates outward as a wave, reflects off density discontinuities, traps into quantized harmonics between clamped walls, and combines into finite pulses via Fourier synthesis.

### 5.1 Derivation of the 1D Wave Equation on a Stretched String

**Physical Assumptions and Coordinate Setup:** Consider a perfectly flexible (zero bending rigidity) string of uniform linear mass density $\mu$ ($\text{kg m}^{-1}$) stretched along the $x$-axis under constant tension $T$ ($\text{N}$). Assume purely transverse displacements $y(x,t)$ with small local slope $\left|\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right| \ll 1$, so that $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}$, $\cos\theta \approx 1$, and tension $T$ remains spatially uniform. Ignore gravity ($T \gg \mu g L$).

Isolate an infinitesimal string segment spanning $[x, x + dx]$ of mass $dm = \mu\,dx$. Let the tangent angles to the horizontal at the left and right endpoints be $\theta(x)$ and $\theta(x + dx)$. The net transverse ($y$-directed) restoring force acting on the element is:

$$dF_y = T\sin\theta(x + dx) - T\sin\theta(x) \approx T\left[\left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right|_{x+dx} - \left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right|_x\right] = T\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}\,dx$$

Applying Newton's Second Law $dm\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = dF_y$:

$$(\mu\,dx)\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = T\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}\,dx$$

Canceling $dx$ yields the **one-dimensional linear wave equation**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}, \qquad v \equiv \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}}$$

#### d'Alembert General Solution, Energy Density and Characteristic Impedance

Introduce characteristic coordinates $\xi = x - vt$ and $\eta = x + vt$. By the chain rule, $\frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial\xi} + \frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = -v\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi} + v\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}$, transforming the wave equation into:

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial\xi\,\partial\eta} = 0 \implies \boxed{y(x, t) = f(x - vt) + g(x + vt)}$$

Here $f(x - vt)$ is an arbitrary shape propagating rigidly rightward ($+x$) at speed $v$, and $g(x + vt)$ propagates leftward ($-x$) at speed $v$.

The kinetic energy per unit length is $u_k = \frac{1}{2}\mu\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^2$. The elastic potential energy arises from stretching the element $dx$ to arc length $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} \approx \left[1 + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2\right]dx$, giving $dU = T(ds - dx) = \frac{1}{2}T\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2 dx$, so $u_p = \frac{1}{2}T\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^2$.

For a rightward harmonic wave $y(x,t) = A\cos(kx - \omega t)$ with $\omega/k = v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$, note that $u_k = u_p = \frac{1}{2}\mu\omega^2 A^2\sin^2(kx - \omega t)$ locally at **every** point and instant. The time-averaged total energy density is $\langle u\rangle = \langle u_k + u_p\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\mu\omega^2 A^2$, and the cycle-averaged energy flux (power transmitted along $+x$) is:

$$\boxed{\langle P\rangle = \langle u\rangle v = \frac{1}{2}\mu v\,\omega^2 A^2 = \frac{1}{2}Z\,\omega^2 A^2, \qquad Z \equiv \mu v = \sqrt{T\mu}}$$

The quantity $Z = \mu v = \sqrt{T\mu}$ ($[Z] = \text{kg s}^{-1} = \text{N s m}^{-1}$) is the **characteristic wave impedance** of the string, equal to the ratio of transverse force $-T(\partial y/\partial x)$ to transverse velocity $\partial y/\partial t$ for a forward-traveling wave.

### 5.2 Boundary Reflection, Transmission and Wave Impedance

Join two semi-infinite strings of linear densities $\mu_1$ ($x < 0$) and $\mu_2$ ($x > 0$) at $x = 0$ under common tension $T$. Their wave speeds are $v_1 = \sqrt{T/\mu_1}$, $v_2 = \sqrt{T/\mu_2}$ and characteristic impedances are $Z_1 = \sqrt{T\mu_1}$, $Z_2 = \sqrt{T\mu_2}$.

An incident harmonic wave of angular frequency $\omega$ approaches the junction from $x < 0$, generating a reflected wave in medium $1$ and a transmitted wave in medium $2$ (all at the **same** frequency $\omega$ so boundary conditions hold at every instant $t$):

$$y_1(x,t) = A_i e^{i(k_1 x - \omega t)} + A_r e^{i(-k_1 x - \omega t)} \quad (x \le 0), \qquad y_2(x,t) = A_t e^{i(k_2 x - \omega t)} \quad (x \ge 0)$$

where $k_1 = \omega/v_1$ and $k_2 = \omega/v_2$. Impose the two exact physical boundary conditions at the massless junction $x = 0$:

1. **Continuity of displacement** (the string remains unbroken):
   $$y_1(0,t) = y_2(0,t) \implies A_i + A_r = A_t \qquad (a)$$
2. **Continuity of transverse force** (zero net force on a massless knot prevents infinite acceleration):
   $$T\left.\frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x}\right|_{0^-} = T\left.\frac{\partial y_2}{\partial x}\right|_{0^+} \implies ik_1(A_i - A_r) = ik_2 A_t \implies k_1(A_i - A_r) = k_2 A_t \qquad (b)$$

Observe that $\frac{k_1}{k_2} = \frac{v_2}{v_1} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}} = \frac{Z_1}{Z_2}$. Substituting $A_t = A_i + A_r$ from $(a)$ into $(b)$ gives $Z_1(A_i - A_r) = Z_2(A_i + A_r)$. Solving for the amplitude **reflection coefficient ($r$)** and **transmission coefficient ($t$)**:

$$\boxed{r \equiv \frac{A_r}{A_i} = \frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{v_2 + v_1} = \frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}, \qquad t \equiv \frac{A_t}{A_i} = \frac{2Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2} = \frac{2v_2}{v_1 + v_2} = \frac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2}}$$

Now evaluate the **power reflectance ($R$)** and **power transmittance ($\mathcal{T}$)** using $\langle P\rangle = \frac{1}{2}Z\omega^2|A|^2$:

$$\boxed{R \equiv \frac{\langle P_r\rangle}{\langle P_i\rangle} = |r|^2 = \left(\frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}\right)^2, \qquad \mathcal{T} \equiv \frac{\langle P_t\rangle}{\langle P_i\rangle} = \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}|t|^2 = \frac{4 Z_1 Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2}}$$

Verify energy conservation identically:

$$R + \mathcal{T} = \frac{(Z_1 - Z_2)^2 + 4 Z_1 Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2} = \frac{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2} = 1$$

**Limiting-Case Physics:**
- **Heavier string / Rigid fixed wall ($Z_2 > Z_1$, limit $Z_2 \to \infty$):** $r < 0$ (and $r \to -1, t \to 0$). The reflected pulse undergoes an exact **$\pi$ phase inversion** (crest returns as trough), just as light suffers a $\pi$ phase shift upon reflecting from an optically denser medium.
- **Lighter string / Free sliding ring ($Z_2 < Z_1$, limit $Z_2 \to 0$):** $r > 0$ (and $r \to +1, t \to +2$). The reflected pulse returns **upright** (zero phase shift), and the free endpoint whips to twice the incident amplitude ($2A_i$).
- **Impedance matching ($Z_1 = Z_2$):** $r = 0, t = 1, R = 0, \mathcal{T} = 1$ (zero reflection; $100\%$ power transmission).

### 5.3 Stationary Waves, Normal Modes and Harmonics on a Finite String

Confining a string of length $L$ between rigid boundaries traps reflected waves into discrete **standing (stationary) normal modes**. Seek separable solutions $y(x,t) = X(x)T(t)$ of $\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$:

$$\frac{1}{X}\frac{d^2 X}{dx^2} = \frac{1}{v^2 T}\frac{d^2 T}{dt^2} = -k^2$$

where the separation constant $-k^2 < 0$ ensures bounded oscillatory solutions:

$$X(x) = C\cos(kx) + D\sin(kx), \qquad T(t) = E\cos(\omega t) + F\sin(\omega t), \qquad \omega = kv$$

#### Fixed–Fixed String ($y(0,t) = 0$ and $y(L,t) = 0$)

1. Left node $X(0) = 0 \implies C = 0$, leaving $X(x) = D\sin(kx)$.
2. Right node $X(L) = 0 \implies D\sin(kL) = 0 \implies k_n L = n\pi$ ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$).

The allowed wavenumbers, wavelengths, and normal-mode eigenfrequencies are quantized as:

$$\boxed{k_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}, \qquad \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad f_n = \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = n f_1 \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)}$$

All integer harmonics (fundamental $n = 1$, second harmonic / first overtone $n = 2$, etc.) are supported. The $n$-th mode possesses $n + 1$ nodes (including both ends) and $n$ antinodes, spaced at exact intervals of $\lambda_n / 2$.

```text
  n = 1 (Fundamental, L = lambda_1 / 2):
  N ------------------ A ------------------ N

  n = 2 (2nd Harmonic, L = lambda_2):
  N ------- A -------- N -------- A ------- N

  n = 3 (3rd Harmonic, L = 3 lambda_3 / 2):
  N --- A ---- N ----- A ----- N ---- A --- N
```

By completeness and orthogonality $\int_0^L \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\sin\left(\frac{m\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \frac{L}{2}\delta_{nm}$, **any** initial profile $y(x, 0)$ and velocity $v(x, 0)$ evolves as a unique Fourier superposition of normal modes:

$$y(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\left[A_n\cos(\omega_n t) + B_n\sin(\omega_n t)\right]$$

with Fourier amplitudes $A_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L y(x,0)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx$ and $B_n = \frac{2}{L\omega_n}\int_0^L \dot{y}(x,0)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx$. *(For a fixed–free string with $\left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L} = 0$, $\cos(k_n L) = 0 \implies k_n L = \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi$, yielding only odd harmonics $f_n = \frac{2n-1}{4L}\sqrt{T/\mu}$.)*

**Energy in a Standing Wave:** For a single mode $y(x,t) = 2A\sin(k_n x)\cos(\omega_n t)$ (formed by two counter-propagating waves of amplitude $A$), integrating kinetic and potential energy densities over $[0, L]$ using $\int_0^L \sin^2(k_n x)\,dx = \int_0^L \cos^2(k_n x)\,dx = L/2$ and $T k_n^2 = \mu\omega_n^2$:

$$K(t) = \int_0^L \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{y}^2 dx = \mu L\omega_n^2 A^2\sin^2(\omega_n t), \qquad U(t) = \int_0^L \frac{1}{2}T y_x^2 dx = \mu L\omega_n^2 A^2\cos^2(\omega_n t)$$

Total mode energy $E = K + U = \mu L\omega_n^2 A^2$ is constant and sloshes back and forth between kinetic and potential forms within each nodal loop; **zero net energy crosses any node** ($\langle P(x)\rangle = 0$ everywhere).

### 5.4 Pulses, Wave Packets and the Bandwidth–Duration Relation

A purely monochromatic wave $\cos(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)$ stretches infinitely across space and time and transmits zero information. Building a spatially localized pulse—a **wave packet**—requires superposing a continuous spectrum of wavenumbers $k$ whose waves interfere constructively inside the pulse and cancel destructively everywhere outside.

Using the symmetric Fourier transform pair at $t = 0$:

$$\psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk \iff \phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(x, 0)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx$$

#### Exact Gaussian Wave Packet and Minimum Bandwidth–Duration Product

Choose a Gaussian spectral amplitude centered at wavenumber $k_0$ whose spectral intensity $|\phi(k)|^2 = |C|^2\exp\left[-\frac{(k - k_0)^2}{2(\Delta k)^2}\right]$ has exact root-mean-square (RMS) width $\Delta k$:

$$\phi(k) = C\exp\left[-\frac{(k - k_0)^2}{4(\Delta k)^2}\right]$$

Substitute $q = k - k_0$ into the inverse Fourier integral and complete the square in the exponent:

$$-\frac{q^2}{4(\Delta k)^2} + iqx = -\frac{1}{4(\Delta k)^2}\Big[q - 2i(\Delta k)^2 x\Big]^2 - (\Delta k)^2 x^2$$

Pulling the $q$-independent factors outside the integral and evaluating the Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha u^2}du = \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$ with $\alpha = \frac{1}{4(\Delta k)^2}$ yields:

$$\psi(x, 0) = \frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{ik_0 x}\,e^{-(\Delta k)^2 x^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{[q - 2i(\Delta k)^2 x]^2}{4(\Delta k)^2}\right)dq = \sqrt{2}\,C\,\Delta k\,\exp\left[-(\Delta k)^2 x^2\right]\,e^{ik_0 x}$$

Computing the spatial intensity profile $|\psi(x, 0)|^2$:

$$|\psi(x, 0)|^2 = 2|C|^2(\Delta k)^2\exp\left[-2(\Delta k)^2 x^2\right] \equiv I_0\exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{2(\Delta x)^2}\right]$$

Equating the exponents $\frac{1}{2(\Delta x)^2} = 2(\Delta k)^2$ yields the exact **space–wavenumber uncertainty identity** for a Gaussian packet (and the general lower bound for any pulse shape by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality):

$$\boxed{\Delta x \cdot \Delta k \ge \frac{1}{2} \quad (\text{equality strictly for a Gaussian pulse})}$$

Replacing space–wavenumber $(x, k)$ identically with time–angular frequency $(t, \omega)$ produces the fundamental **bandwidth–duration relation**:

$$\boxed{\Delta\omega \cdot \Delta t \ge \frac{1}{2} \implies \Delta\nu \cdot \Delta t \ge \frac{1}{4\pi} \quad (\text{order of magnitude: } \Delta\omega\,\Delta t \sim 1)}$$

*(As a second standard case, a finite rectangular sinusoidal wave train of total duration $\tau$ yields a spectral amplitude $\phi(\omega) \propto \frac{\sin[(\omega - \omega_0)\tau/2]}{(\omega - \omega_0)\tau/2}$ whose first spectral zeros lie at $\omega - \omega_0 = \pm \frac{2\pi}{\tau}$, giving $\Delta\nu \cdot \tau \approx 1$.)* Short laser pulses inevitably span broad optical bandwidths, and any finite atomic emission lifetime $\tau$ imprints an irreducible natural spectral linewidth $\Delta\nu \sim 1/(2\pi\tau)$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 4: Composite String Wave Impedance and Thermal Shift on a Sonometer Wire ($\mu_1 = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg m}^{-1}$, $\mu_2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ kg m}^{-1}$, $T = 100\text{ N}$)

## 6. Phase Velocity, Group Velocity and Dispersion

Drop a pebble into a deep pond and watch the expanding ring of ripples closely. Individual wave crests appear at the rear edge of the ring, race forward through the packet, and mysteriously vanish at the leading edge—meaning the crests move **faster** than the ring itself! Whenever a medium's wave speed depends on frequency ($\omega(k)$ is nonlinear, called a **dispersive medium**), the speed of constant-phase crests splits away from the speed of the energy-carrying modulation envelope.

### 6.1 Derivation of Phase Velocity ($v_p$) and Group Velocity ($v_g$)

#### Two-Wave Superposition Derivation

Superpose two unit-amplitude harmonic plane waves of nearby wavenumbers and angular frequencies $(k_1, \omega_1)$ and $(k_2, \omega_2)$ with $\Delta k = k_1 - k_2 \ll \bar{k}$ and $\Delta\omega = \omega_1 - \omega_2 \ll \bar{\omega}$:

$$\psi(x, t) = A\cos(k_1 x - \omega_1 t) + A\cos(k_2 x - \omega_2 t)$$

Applying $\cos\alpha + \cos\beta = 2\cos\left(\frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\alpha + \beta}{2}\right)$ yields:

$$\psi(x, t) = 2A\cos\left(\frac{\Delta k}{2}x - \frac{\Delta\omega}{2}t\right)\cos(\bar{k}x - \bar{\omega}t)$$

where $\bar{k} = \frac{k_1 + k_2}{2}$ and $\bar{\omega} = \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}$.

- **Phase velocity ($v_p$)** is the speed of a point of constant phase on the rapid carrier wave:
  $$\bar{k}x - \bar{\omega}t = \text{const} \implies \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\bar{\omega}}{\bar{k}} \xrightarrow{\Delta k \to 0} \boxed{v_p = \frac{\omega}{k}}$$
- **Group velocity ($v_g$)** is the speed of a point of constant phase on the slowly varying modulation envelope (the wave group):
  $$\frac{\Delta k}{2}x - \frac{\Delta\omega}{2}t = \text{const} \implies \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta k} \xrightarrow{\Delta k \to 0} \boxed{v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}}$$

#### General Wave-Packet Derivation (Stationary Phase and Packet Spreading)

Consider a localized wave packet built from a continuous spectrum $\phi(k)$ narrowly peaked around $k_0$:

$$\psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{i[kx - \omega(k)t]}\,dk$$

Expand the dispersion relation $\omega(k)$ in a Taylor series around $k_0$ letting $q = k - k_0$:

$$\omega(k) = \omega_0 + \omega_0'\,q + \frac{1}{2}\omega_0''\,q^2 + \dots, \qquad \omega_0' \equiv \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}, \quad \omega_0'' \equiv \left.\frac{d^2\omega}{dk^2}\right|_{k_0}$$

**First-Order Approximation (Undistorted Envelope Propagation):** Retaining terms through linear order in $q$:

$$\psi(x, t) \approx e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)} \left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k_0 + q)\,e^{iq(x - \omega_0' t)}\,dq\right] = e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)}\,\mathcal{E}(x - v_g t)$$

The spatial envelope $|\psi(x, t)| = |\mathcal{E}(x - v_g t)|$ translates without distortion at the **exact group velocity** $v_g = \omega_0' = \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}$, where the phase $\Phi(k) = kx - \omega(k)t$ is stationary ($\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial k} = x - \frac{d\omega}{dk}t = 0$) so neighboring Fourier components interfere constructively.

**Second-Order Dispersion (Wave-Packet Spreading):** Retain the quadratic term $\frac{1}{2}\omega_0'' q^2$ for the Gaussian packet $\phi(k_0 + q) = C\exp\left[-\frac{q^2}{4(\Delta k)^2}\right]$ of initial RMS spatial width $\Delta x_0 = \frac{1}{2\Delta k}$ (from Section 5.4):

$$\psi(x, t) = \frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{1}{4(\Delta k)^2} + i\frac{\omega_0'' t}{2}\right)q^2 + iq(x - v_g t)\right]dq$$

Using the exact complex Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha q^2 + i\beta q}dq = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^2}{4\alpha}\right)$ with $\alpha = (\Delta x_0)^2 + i\frac{\omega_0'' t}{2}$ and $\beta = x - v_g t$, take the modulus squared $|\psi(x, t)|^2$:

$$\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{1}{4\alpha}\right) = \frac{1}{4}\frac{(\Delta x_0)^2}{(\Delta x_0)^4 + \left(\frac{\omega_0'' t}{2}\right)^2} = \frac{1}{4\big[\Delta x(t)\big]^2}$$

Since $|\psi(x, t)|^2 \propto \exp\left[-\frac{(x - v_g t)^2}{2[\Delta x(t)]^2}\right]$, the RMS packet width grows symmetrically in time according to:

$$\boxed{\Delta x(t) = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\omega_0''\, t}{2(\Delta x_0)^2}\right)^2} = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + \left(2\,\omega_0''(\Delta k)^2 t\right)^2}}$$

Because different frequency components travel at slightly different group velocities ($\Delta v_g \approx \omega_0'' \Delta k$), any nonzero **group velocity dispersion** ($\omega_0'' = \frac{d v_g}{dk} \ne 0$) causes the packet to broaden monotonically, eventually growing linearly at asymptotic rate $\Delta x(t) \approx |\omega_0''|\,\Delta k\, t$. In a non-dispersive medium ($\omega_0'' = 0$), $\Delta x(t) = \Delta x_0$ forever.

### 6.2 Rayleigh Relation Between $v_g$ and $v_p$ - Wavelength and Optical Index Forms

Starting from $\omega = k v_p$ with $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ (where $\lambda$ is the **actual wavelength inside the medium**):

$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{d(k v_p)}{dk} = v_p + k\frac{dv_p}{dk} = v_p + k\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}\frac{d\lambda}{dk}$$

Differentiating $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ gives $\frac{dk}{d\lambda} = -\frac{2\pi}{\lambda^2} \implies \frac{d\lambda}{dk} = -\frac{\lambda^2}{2\pi}$. Substituting $k\frac{d\lambda}{dk} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\right)\left(-\frac{\lambda^2}{2\pi}\right) = -\lambda$ produces **Rayleigh's exact formula**:

$$\boxed{v_g = v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}}$$

#### Optical Refractive Index Form (Vacuum Wavelength $\lambda_0$ vs. Medium Wavelength $\lambda$)

In optics, dispersion tables specify the refractive index $n(\lambda_0)$ as a function of the **vacuum wavelength** $\lambda_0 = n\lambda$, where $v_p = \frac{c}{n(\lambda_0)}$ and $k = \frac{\omega n(\omega)}{c}$. Differentiating $k$ with respect to $\omega$:

$$\frac{1}{v_g} = \frac{dk}{d\omega} = \frac{n}{c} + \frac{\omega}{c}\frac{dn}{d\omega}$$

Since $\omega = \frac{2\pi c}{\lambda_0} \implies \frac{d\omega}{\omega} = -\frac{d\lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$, we substitute $\omega\frac{dn}{d\omega} = -\lambda_0\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}$ to define the **group refractive index** $n_g \equiv c/v_g$:

$$\boxed{n_g = \frac{c}{v_g} = n - \lambda_0\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0} \implies v_g = \frac{c}{n - \lambda_0\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}} = \frac{v_p}{1 - \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}}}$$

Verify that this is **algebraically identical** to Rayleigh's formula $v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$ without any approximation: using $\lambda = \lambda_0/n$ gives $\frac{d\lambda}{d\lambda_0} = \frac{1}{n}\left(1 - \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}\right)$ and $\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda_0} = -\frac{v_p}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}$, hence:

$$v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda} = v_p - \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\left(\frac{-\frac{v_p}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}}{\frac{1}{n}\left(1 - \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}\right)}\right) = v_p\left(1 + \frac{\frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}}{1 - \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}}\right) = \frac{v_p}{1 - \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}}$$

For weak optical dispersion $\left|\frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}\right| \ll 1$, first-order expansion yields the common textbook approximation $v_g \approx v_p\left(1 + \frac{\lambda_0}{n}\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}\right)$.

### 6.3 Normal vs. Anomalous Dispersion and Relativistic Matter Waves

Rayleigh's identity $v_g = v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$ cleanly classifies all dispersive media:

1. **Non-dispersive medium ($\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda} = 0$):** $v_g = v_p$. All wavelengths travel at identical speed (electromagnetic waves in vacuum, acoustic waves in an ideal gas, ideal taut string).
2. **Normal dispersion ($\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda} > 0$, equivalently $\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0} < 0$):** Longer wavelengths travel faster (e.g., red light outruns violet in optical glass; long ocean swells outrun short gravity waves), forcing **$v_g < v_p$**. Crests originate at the rear of the packet and overtake the envelope.
3. **Anomalous dispersion ($\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda} < 0$, equivalently $\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0} > 0$):** Shorter wavelengths travel faster (e.g., capillary ripples on water, flexural waves in steel bars, and light inside narrow atomic absorption bands), yielding **$v_g > v_p$**. Inside a strong optical absorption band, $v_g$ can formally exceed $c$ or even turn negative—yet causality is never violated because severe attenuation reshapes the pulse envelope, while the true **signal front velocity** (Sommerfeld–Brillouin forerunner) never exceeds $c$.

#### Relativistic de Broglie Wave Packet and Photons (High-Yield Proof)

For a free relativistic particle of rest mass $m_0$, momentum $p = \gamma m_0 v$, and total energy $E = \gamma m_0 c^2 = \sqrt{p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4}$, apply the Planck–de Broglie relations $E = \hbar\omega$ and $p = \hbar k$:

$$\hbar^2\omega^2 = \hbar^2 c^2 k^2 + m_0^2 c^4 \implies \omega(k) = \sqrt{c^2 k^2 + \left(\frac{m_0 c^2}{\hbar}\right)^2}$$

Differentiating $\omega^2 = c^2 k^2 + \omega_c^2$ implicitly with respect to $k$ gives $2\omega\frac{d\omega}{dk} = 2c^2 k$:

$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c^2 k}{\omega} = \frac{c^2 p}{E} = \frac{c^2 (\gamma m_0 v)}{\gamma m_0 c^2} = \boxed{v_{\text{particle}}}$$

Meanwhile $v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{E}{p} = \frac{c^2}{v} > c$, giving the exact relativistic invariant $\boxed{v_p v_g = c^2}$. For a **photon** ($m_0 = 0$), the dispersion relation reduces strictly to $\omega = ck$, yielding $v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = c$ and $v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = c$ identically.

| Physical Wave System | Dispersion Relation $\omega(k)$ | Phase Velocity $v_p = \omega/k$ | Group Velocity $v_g = d\omega/dk$ | Ratio $v_g / v_p$ & Regime |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **EM Waves in Vacuum / Photons** | $\omega = ck$ | $c$ | $c$ | $1$ (Non-dispersive) |
| **Deep-Water Gravity Waves** | $\omega = \sqrt{gk}$ | $\sqrt{g/k} = \sqrt{g\lambda/2\pi}$ | $\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{g/k}$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ (Normal; $v_g < v_p$) |
| **Capillary Surface Ripples** | $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho}k^3}$ | $\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho}k}$ | $\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho}k}$ | $\frac{3}{2}$ (Anomalous; $v_g > v_p$) |
| **Plasma / Hollow Waveguide** | $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 + c^2 k^2}$ | $c\sqrt{1 + \omega_p^2/(c^2 k^2)} > c$ | $\frac{c^2}{v_p} < c$ | $v_p v_g = c^2$ (Normal) |
| **Non-Relativistic Matter Wave** | $\omega = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}$ | $\frac{\hbar k}{2m} = \frac{v}{2}$ | $\frac{\hbar k}{m} = v$ | $2$ (Anomalous; $v_g = v_{\text{particle}}$) |

> **Problem:** (a) Deep-water surface waves obey the combined gravity–capillary dispersion relation $\omega^2 = gk + \frac{\sigma}{\rho}k^3$, where $g = 9.80\text{ m s}^{-2}$, surface tension $\sigma = 0.0728\text{ N m}^{-1}$, and density $\rho = 1000\text{ kg m}^{-3}$. (b) Flint glass obeys Cauchy's relation $n(\lambda_0) = 1.620 + \frac{1.05 \times 10^{-14}\text{ m}^2}{\lambda_0^2}$. Calculate $v_p$ and $v_g$ at vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0 = 500\text{ nm}$.
>

> **(a) Minimum phase velocity and crossover wavelength on water:**
> Divide $\omega^2 = gk + \frac{\sigma}{\rho}k^3$ by $k^2$:
> $$v_p^2 = \frac{\omega^2}{k^2} = \frac{g}{k} + \frac{\sigma}{\rho}k$$

> $$-\frac{g}{k^2} + \frac{\sigma}{\rho} = 0 \implies k_m = \sqrt{\frac{\rho g}{\sigma}}$$

> $$v_g\big|_{\lambda_m} = v_p - \lambda_m(0) = v_p$$
> *(Gravity-dominated normal dispersion for $\lambda > \lambda_m$ transitions cleanly to capillary-dominated anomalous dispersion for $\lambda < \lambda_m$.)*
> Numerically:
> $$k_m = \sqrt{\frac{(1000\text{ kg m}^{-3})(9.80\text{ m s}^{-2})}{0.0728\text{ N m}^{-1}}} = \sqrt{1.3462 \times 10^5\text{ m}^{-2}} = 366.9\text{ m}^{-1}$$
> $$\lambda_m = \frac{2\pi}{k_m} = \frac{6.2832}{366.9\text{ m}^{-1}} = 0.01713\text{ m} = \boxed{1.71\text{ cm}}$$
> $$v_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{k_m} + \frac{\sigma}{\rho}k_m} = \left(\frac{4 g \sigma}{\rho}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{4(9.80)(0.0728)}{1000}\right)^{1/4} = (2.8538 \times 10^{-3})^{1/4} = \boxed{0.231\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$
>
> **(b) Phase and group velocity in flint glass at $\lambda_0 = 500\text{ nm}$:**
> Convert $\lambda_0 = 500\text{ nm} = 5.00 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} \implies \lambda_0^2 = 2.50 \times 10^{-13}\text{ m}^2$.

> Using $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$:
> $$v_p = \frac{c}{n} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8}{1.662} = \boxed{1.805 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}}, \qquad v_g = \frac{c}{n_g} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8}{1.746} = \boxed{1.718 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$

## 7. Reflection and Refraction from Huygens Principle

Before Maxwell's equations existed, Christiaan Huygens (1678) proposed a remarkably predictive geometric rule for wave propagation. Rather than tracking ballistic particles, Huygens treated an advancing **wavefront**—a locus of points oscillating in identical phase—as a continuous sheet of miniature synchronized oscillators, each launching its own expanding spherical disturbance.

### 7.1 Statement of Huygens Principle and Fresnel Obliquity Correction

**Huygens' Principle rests on two postulates:**
1. Every point on a given primary wavefront acts as a fresh source of **secondary spherical wavelets** spreading outward in all directions at the local wave speed $v$ of the medium.
2. After a time interval $\tau$, the new position of the advancing wavefront is the **common forward tangent envelope** touching all secondary spherical wavelets of radius $v\tau$.

Pure Huygens geometry left one glaring puzzle: the secondary spheres also admit a **backward** tangent envelope propagating opposite to the wave! Nearly 140 years later, Augustin-Jean Fresnel and Gustav Kirchhoff resolved this analytically by deriving the **obliquity (inclination) factor** $K(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + \cos\theta)$ multiplying each wavelet's amplitude at angle $\theta$ to the forward normal. In the strictly backward direction ($\theta = \pi$), $K(\pi) = 0$, killing the backward wave identically.

### 7.2 Geometric Derivation of the Laws of Reflection

**Physical Assumptions and Setup:** Let a plane wavefront $AB$ propagate in a homogeneous isotropic medium (constant wave speed $v$) and strike a flat reflecting surface $XY$ at angle of incidence $i$ (defined as the angle between the incident ray and the surface normal, which identically equals the angle $\angle BAC$ between wavefront $AB$ and interface $XY$ because rays are perpendicular to wavefronts).

**Step-by-Step Construction:**
1. Choose $t = 0$ as the instant the lower edge $A$ of wavefront $AB$ first touches surface $XY$. Edge $B$ still lies in the medium at perpendicular distance $BC$ from the surface along its incident ray.
2. Edge $B$ advances at speed $v$ and strikes the reflecting plane at point $C$ after time interval:
   $$\tau = \frac{BC}{v}$$
3. During this identical interval $\tau$, the secondary spherical wavelet triggered at $A$ expands back into the same medium to form a hemisphere of radius:
   $$AD = v\tau = BC$$
4. Draw the tangent plane $CD$ from point $C$ to this hemisphere centered at $A$, touching it at point $D$. Because $AD$ is a radius to a tangent plane, $\angle ADC = 90^\circ$, and $CD$ represents the **reflected plane wavefront** with $AD$ as the reflected ray.
5. **Intermediate Wavelet Check:** Any intermediate ray from $P$ on $AB$ hits $XY$ at $Q$ with $AQ = \eta\,AC$ ($0 < \eta < 1$) at time $t_1 = \eta\tau$. Its secondary wavelet expands for the remaining time $(1 - \eta)\tau$ to radius $v(1 - \eta)\tau = (1 - \eta)AD$, which by similarity of right triangles equals the exact perpendicular distance from $Q$ to plane $CD$. Thus $CD$ is tangent to *every* secondary wavelet simultaneously.
6. Compare right triangles $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ADC$:
   - $\angle ABC = \angle ADC = 90^\circ$ (wavefronts are orthogonal to rays),
   - Hypotenuse $AC$ is common to both triangles,
   - Side $BC = AD = v\tau$.

By the Right-Angle–Hypotenuse–Side (RHS) congruence theorem, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC$, giving:

$$\angle BAC = \angle DCA$$

Since $\angle BAC = i$ (angle of incidence) and $\angle DCA = r$ (angle between reflected wavefront $CD$ and surface $XY$, equal to the angle between reflected ray $AD$ and the surface normal):

$$\boxed{i = r}$$

Moreover, because the incident wavefront $AB$, the interface $XY$, and the tangent construction $CD$ are mutually perpendicular to the plane of the diagram, the **incident ray, reflected ray, and normal to the reflecting surface all lie in the same plane**. Both laws of reflection follow directly.

### 7.3 Geometric Derivation of Snell Law of Refraction

**Physical Assumptions and Setup:** Let a plane wavefront $AB$ travel through homogeneous medium $1$ (wave speed $v_1$, refractive index $n_1 = c/v_1$) and strike a planar boundary $XY$ separating medium $1$ from homogeneous medium $2$ (wave speed $v_2$, refractive index $n_2 = c/v_2$) at angle of incidence $i = \angle BAC$. Assume for concreteness that $v_2 < v_1$ (rarer to denser transition); the algebra holds identically for $v_2 > v_1$ provided no total internal reflection occurs.

**Step-by-Step Construction:**
1. At $t = 0$, point $A$ of wavefront $AB$ reaches interface $XY$. The upper edge $B$ travels through medium $1$ at speed $v_1$ and reaches point $C$ on $XY$ after time:
   $$\tau = \frac{BC}{v_1}$$
2. During this same interval $\tau$, the secondary wavelet launched from $A$ penetrates into medium $2$ at speed $v_2$, forming a hemisphere of radius:
   $$AE = v_2\tau$$
3. Draw the tangent plane $CE$ from $C$ to the hemisphere centered at $A$ (touching at point $E$, so $\angle AEC = 90^\circ$). Every intermediate point $P$ on $AB$ striking $XY$ at $Q$ ($AQ = \eta\,AC$) spends time $\eta\tau$ in medium $1$ and launches a secondary wavelet in medium $2$ of radius $v_2(1 - \eta)\tau = (1 - \eta)AE$, which touches plane $CE$ tangentially by triangle similarity. Hence $CE$ is the exact **refracted plane wavefront**, and $AE$ is the refracted ray making angle $r = \angle ECA$ with the interface normal.
4. Apply elementary trigonometry to the two right triangles $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle AEC$ sharing common hypotenuse $AC$:
   $$\text{In }\triangle ABC:\quad \sin i = \frac{BC}{AC} = \frac{v_1\tau}{AC}$$
   $$\text{In }\triangle AEC:\quad \sin r = \frac{AE}{AC} = \frac{v_2\tau}{AC}$$
5. Divide the first equation by the second, eliminating both $\tau$ and $AC$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{c/n_1}{c/n_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \implies n_1\sin i = n_2\sin r}$$

Coplanarity of the incident ray, refracted ray, and surface normal again follows immediately from the geometry of perpendicular tangent planes.

#### Physical Consequences, Crucial Experimental Test and Limiting Cases

- **Wavelength Compression at Fixed Frequency:** Because boundary phase matching forces the oscillation frequency $\nu$ to remain strictly invariant across $XY$, $\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{v_1/\nu}{v_2/\nu} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$. Entering an optically denser medium shortens the wavelength while keeping frequency unchanged.
- **Beam Width Transformation:** From right triangles $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle AEC$, $AB = AC\cos i$ and $CE = AC\cos r$, so the beam cross-sectional width transforms as $\frac{d_2}{d_1} = \frac{CE}{AB} = \frac{\cos r}{\cos i}$.
- **Decisive Victory Over Newton's Corpuscular Theory:** When light bends toward the normal entering water ($r < i$), Huygens' wave model demands $v_{\text{water}} < v_{\text{air}}$, whereas Newton's ballistic corpuscular model demanded $v_{\text{water}} > v_{\text{air}}$ (attributing bending to normal gravitational-like attraction). Léon Foucault's 1850 rotating-mirror measurement proved $v_{\text{water}} \approx 0.75\,c < c$, confirming wave optics decisively.
- **Limiting Cases — Normal Incidence & Geometric Origin of Total Internal Reflection (TIR):**
  - At normal incidence ($i = 0$): $\sin r = 0 \implies r = 0$ (unbent wavefront, wavelength simply compresses).
  - For denser-to-rarer incidence ($v_2 > v_1$, so $n_1 > n_2$): a real tangent plane $CE$ can be drawn from $C$ to the hemisphere of radius $AE = v_2\tau$ **only if** point $C$ lies outside or on the hemisphere ($AE \le AC$). This requires:
    $$v_2\tau \le AC \iff v_2\left(\frac{AC\sin i}{v_1}\right) \le AC \iff \sin i \le \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
    At $\sin i_c = n_2/n_1$, the hemisphere touches $C$ ($r = 90^\circ$, grazing emergence). For $i > i_c$, the secondary wavelet expands *past* $C$ ($AE > AC$), so no real tangent envelope exists in medium $2$—all incident power reflects back into medium $1$ (**Total Internal Reflection**).

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 6: Wavefront Refraction, Beam Width Change and Huygens TIR Condition ($n_1 = 1.50 \to n_2 = 1.00$, $i = 30.0^\circ$, $d_1 = 2.00\text{ cm}$, $\lambda_0 = 600\text{ nm}$)
> **Problem:** A collimated monochromatic beam of width $d_1 = 2.00\text{ cm}$ and vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0 = 600\text{ nm}$ propagates through glass ($n_1 = 1.50$) and strikes a planar glass–air interface ($n_2 = 1.00$) at angle of incidence $i = 30.0^\circ$. (a) Determine the angle of refraction $r$, the refracted beam width $d_2$, the wavelengths $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, the sweep time $\tau$ across the interface patch $AC$, and the Huygens secondary wavelet radius $AE$ in air. (b) Verify directly from Huygens' wavelet radius condition why no refracted plane wavefront exists for $i > 41.8^\circ$.
>
> **Solution:**
> **(a) Wave speeds, Snell's Law and wavefront geometry:**
> $$v_1 = \frac{c}{n_1} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8}{1.50} = 2.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}, \qquad v_2 = \frac{c}{n_2} = 3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$$
> By Snell's Law:
> $$\sin r = \frac{n_1}{n_2}\sin i = \frac{1.50}{1.00}\sin 30.0^\circ = 1.50(0.500) = 0.750 \implies r = \arcsin(0.750) = \boxed{48.59^\circ}$$
> Compute $\cos i = \cos 30.0^\circ = 0.8660$ and $\cos r = \sqrt{1 - 0.750^2} = \sqrt{0.4375} = 0.6614$.
> Illuminated interface patch length $AC$ and refracted wavefront width $d_2$:
> $$AC = \frac{d_1}{\cos i} = \frac{2.00\text{ cm}}{0.8660} = 2.309\text{ cm} = 2.309 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$$
> $$d_2 = AC\cos r = d_1\frac{\cos r}{\cos i} = (2.00\text{ cm})\frac{0.6614}{0.8660} = \boxed{1.53\text{ cm}}$$
> Wavelengths in glass and air (frequency $\nu = c/\lambda_0 = 5.00 \times 10^{14}\text{ Hz}$ invariant):
> $$\lambda_1 = \frac{\lambda_0}{n_1} = \frac{600\text{ nm}}{1.50} = \boxed{400\text{ nm}}, \qquad \lambda_2 = \frac{\lambda_0}{n_2} = \boxed{600\text{ nm}}$$
> Sweep time $\tau$ for edge $B$ to travel $BC = AC\sin i$ in glass:
> $$\tau = \frac{AC\sin i}{v_1} = \frac{(2.309 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m})(0.500)}{2.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}} = \boxed{5.77 \times 10^{-11}\text{ s} = 57.7\text{ ps}}$$
> Huygens secondary wavelet radius in air at time $\tau$:
> $$AE = v_2\tau = (3.00 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1})(5.774 \times 10^{-11}\text{ s}) = 1.732 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m} = \boxed{1.73\text{ cm}}$$
> *(Sanity check: $AE = 1.73\text{ cm} < AC = 2.31\text{ cm}$, and $AE/AC = 1.732/2.309 = 0.750 = \sin r$.)*
>
> **(b) Huygens geometric TIR cutoff:**
> A real tangent plane from $C$ to the hemisphere centered at $A$ exists iff $AE \le AC$:
> $$v_2\left(\frac{AC\sin i}{v_1}\right) \le AC \iff \sin i \le \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{2.00 \times 10^8}{3.00 \times 10^8} = \frac{2}{3} \implies i \le \arcsin(2/3) = \boxed{41.81^\circ}$$
> For $i > 41.81^\circ$, the air wavelet sphere already engulfs point $C$ ($AE > AC$), eliminating any real refracted tangent envelope.

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> - **Damping Coefficient Notation Collision ($\beta$ vs. $\gamma$ vs. $b$):** Different UPSC question papers denote the drag force as $-b\dot{x}$, $-\gamma\dot{x}$, or $-2m\beta\dot{x}$ (e.g., $\gamma$ in $\text{N s m}^{-1}$ in some papers vs. $\gamma = b/m$ in $\text{s}^{-1}$ in others!). **Always** inspect the SI units given in the prompt and explicitly write $m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = 0 \implies \ddot{x} + 2\beta\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0$ on Line 1 before quoting formulas for $Q$ or $\omega_A$.
> - **Amplitude vs. Energy Relaxation Time (Factor of 2 Trap):** Amplitude decays as $e^{-\beta t} = e^{-t/(2\tau)}$, whereas mechanical energy decays as $e^{-2\beta t} = e^{-t/\tau}$. Consequently, the energy relaxation time is $\tau = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{m}{b}$, **half** the amplitude decay time $1/\beta$. Never plug $1/\beta$ into $Q = \omega_0\tau$.
> - **Resonance Frequency Hierarchy ($\omega_A < \omega_d < \omega_0$):** Displacement amplitude peaks at $\omega_A = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\beta^2}$ ($A_{\max} = \frac{F_0}{b\omega_d}$), whereas velocity and absorbed power peak strictly at $\omega_0$ ($A(\omega_0) = \frac{F_0}{b\omega_0}$). When a Mains numerical asks for "amplitude at resonance" for a weakly damped oscillator, compute the exact peak $A_{\max}$ at $\omega_A$ **and** verify $A(\omega_0)$ if $Q \gg 1$ so step-marks are watertight under any official marking key.
> - **Phase-Lag Quadrant Trap:** For $\omega > \omega_0$, $\tan\delta = \frac{2\beta\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2} < 0$. A calculator's $\arctan$ returns a negative angle in $(-\pi/2, 0)$, which is physically wrong because $\sin\delta > 0$ always! Add $\pi$ so that $\delta \in (\pi/2, \pi)$ smoothly across resonance.
> - **Factor-of-2 Traps in Beats and Coupled Sloshing:** The algebraic cosine envelope oscillates at $\frac{|\omega_1 - \omega_2|}{2}$, but observable intensity beats occur at $f_b = |f_1 - f_2|$ (twice as fast). Likewise, in weakly coupled oscillators, full energy transfer from mass $1$ to mass $2$ takes **half** a beat period: $t_{\text{transfer}} = \frac{\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1}$.
> - **Rayleigh Wavelength Trap ($\lambda$ vs. $\lambda_0$):** The relation $v_g = v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$ holds strictly for the **in-medium** wavelength $\lambda$. When differentiating an optical refractive index $n(\lambda_0)$ expressed in terms of **vacuum** wavelength $\lambda_0$, use the exact form $v_g = \frac{c}{n - \lambda_0\frac{dn}{d\lambda_0}}$ rather than substituting $\lambda_0$ blindly into $v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$.
> - **SI Unit Traps in String & Wave Numericals:** Convert $\text{cm} \to \text{m}$ and $\text{nm}/\text{Å} \to \text{m}$ before evaluating wave speeds or $n(\lambda_0)$; distinguish linear mass density $\mu = \rho S$ ($\text{kg m}^{-1}$) from volumetric density $\rho$ ($\text{kg m}^{-3}$); and keep angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ ($\text{rad s}^{-1}$) strictly separated from linear frequency $f$ ($\text{Hz}$).

### 2. Examiner Step-Marking & Numerical Trap Signature

- **Symbol-Definition Penalty:** In damped/forced ODE questions (e.g., 2011, 2021, 2024), examiners explicitly allocate 2–3 step marks to defining every symbol ($m, b, k, 2\beta = b/m, \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$) and showing the exact algebraic reduction to the critically damped $(C_1 + C_2 t)e^{-\omega_0 t}$ solution instead of quoting it from memory.
- **Weak-Damping vs. Exact Resonance Amplitude:** In numericals like 2024 Q2(c), verify whether $\gamma$ carries units $\text{N s m}^{-1}$ ($= b$) or $\text{s}^{-1}$ ($= b/m$); substituting $\gamma = 1\text{ N s m}^{-1}$ directly as $2\beta$ without dividing by $m = 0.25\text{ kg}$ inflates $Q$ by a factor of $4$ and wipes out both numerical answers.
- **Skipping the Intermediate Wavelet Proof in Huygens' Derivations:** Merely matching endpoints $A$ and $C$ in Huygens' derivation of reflection/refraction earns partial credit; showing the 3-line similarity check for an arbitrary intermediate point $Q$ plus right-angle markings ($\angle ABC = \angle AEC = 90^\circ$) secures full derivation marks.
- **Base-10 vs. Natural Logarithm in $\Lambda$:** Using $\log_{10}(A_0/A_N)$ instead of $\ln(A_0/A_N)$ in logarithmic decrement numericals (2021) introduces an immediate $2.303\times$ error.
