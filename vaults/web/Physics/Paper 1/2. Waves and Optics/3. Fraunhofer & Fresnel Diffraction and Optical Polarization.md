# Chapter 3: Fraunhofer & Fresnel Diffraction and Optical Polarization

## 1. Diffraction Fundamentals - Fresnel vs. Fraunhofer Regimes

When light encounters an aperture or sharp obstacle comparable in scale to its wavelength $\lambda$, it spills around the geometric shadow boundary and sculpts intricate fringes of light and dark. Physically, diffraction obeys no separate force law from interference: while two-beam interference sums waves from a few discrete slits, **diffraction** integrates the continuous superposition of infinitely many coherent Huygens–Fresnel secondary wavelets emitted across the unobstructed portion of a wavefront.

The mathematical character of this integral depends entirely on whether the wavefront curvature matters across the aperture of half-width $a$ as viewed from distance $L$. This ratio is governed by the dimensionless **Fresnel Number** $N_F \equiv \frac{a^2}{\lambda L}$:

- When $N_F \gtrsim 1$ ($L \lesssim a^2/\lambda$), the path difference across the aperture retains **quadratic** phase terms ($\propto x^2/L$), producing **Fresnel (Near-Field) Diffraction**.
- When $N_F \ll 1$ ($L \gg a^2/\lambda$), quadratic curvature vanishes and the phase varies **strictly linearly** across the aperture ($\propto x\sin\theta$), producing **Fraunhofer (Far-Field) Diffraction**—whose amplitude distribution equals the exact two-dimensional spatial Fourier transform of the aperture transmission function.

| Feature | Fraunhofer (Far-Field) Diffraction | Fresnel (Near-Field) Diffraction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fresnel Number $N_F = a^2/(\lambda L)$** | $N_F \ll 1$ ($L \gg a^2/\lambda$) | $N_F \gtrsim 1$ ($L \lesssim a^2/\lambda$) |
| **Source & Screen Distances** | Effectively infinite (realized at finite lab distances via collimating + focusing lenses) | Finite source and/or screen distances; no focusing lenses required |
| **Incident & Diffracted Wavefronts** | Strictly **plane** wavefronts | **Spherical** or **cylindrical** wavefronts |
| **Aperture Phase Variation** | Linear in aperture coordinate ($\phi \propto x\sin\theta$) | Quadratic in aperture coordinate ($\phi \propto x^2/L$) |
| **Pattern Evolution with Distance $L$** | Shape remains invariant; scales purely linearly with $L$ (angular pattern fixed) | Pattern morphs qualitatively as $L$ changes (axial center alternates bright/dark) |
| **Mathematical Tool** | Elementary Fourier integrals & complex geometric sums | Fresnel Half-Period Zones, Fresnel Integrals $C(v), S(v)$, Cornu's Spiral |

## 2. Fraunhofer (Far-Field) Diffraction

In the laboratory, placing a monochromatic source at the focus of a collimating lens delivers a plane wavefront normally onto an aperture, and a second converging lens of focal length $f$ collects all parallel diffracted rays leaving at angle $\theta$ onto a single screen point $y = f\tan\theta \approx f\theta$.

### 2.1 Single Slit - Full Integral Derivation and Secondary Maxima

**Physical Assumptions and Setup:** Consider a long vertical slit of width $a$ spanning $x \in [-a/2, +a/2]$, illuminated normally by a monochromatic plane wave of wavelength $\lambda$ and wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Treat every horizontal strip $dx$ at coordinate $x$ as a coherent secondary source radiating in phase with uniform linear amplitude density $\mathcal{C}$.

Relative to the ray from the slit center ($x = 0$), a ray leaving position $x$ at diffraction angle $\theta$ travels an extra optical path $\Delta(x) = x\sin\theta$, carrying phase shift $\phi(x) = k x\sin\theta$. Integrating all elementary contributions across the slit gives the complex diffracted electric field:

$$E(\theta) = \mathcal{C}\int_{-a/2}^{+a/2} e^{ikx\sin\theta}\,dx = \mathcal{C}\left[\frac{e^{ikx\sin\theta}}{ik\sin\theta}\right]_{-a/2}^{+a/2} = \mathcal{C}\,\frac{e^{i\frac{ka\sin\theta}{2}} - e^{-i\frac{ka\sin\theta}{2}}}{ik\sin\theta}$$

Define the **single-slit diffraction phase parameter** $\beta$ (equal to **half** the total phase difference between the two outer edges of the slit):

$$\beta \equiv \frac{k a\sin\theta}{2} = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$$

Factoring out $a$ and recognizing $\sin\beta = \frac{e^{i\beta} - e^{-i\beta}}{2i}$ yields the field amplitude and intensity $I(\theta) = |E(\theta)|^2$ (with central peak amplitude $E_0 = \mathcal{C}a$ and peak intensity $I_0 = |E_0|^2$):

$$E(\theta) = E_0\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta} \implies \boxed{I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}}$$

#### Positions of Principal Maximum, Zero Minima and Secondary Maxima

1. **Central Principal Maximum ($\theta = 0, \beta = 0$):** Using $\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta} = 1$, $I(0) = I_0$.
2. **Exact Zero Minima:** $I(\theta) = 0$ whenever $\sin\beta = 0$ with $\beta \ne 0$, i.e., $\beta = \pm m\pi$ ($m = 1, 2, 3, \dots$):
   $$\boxed{a\sin\theta_m = \pm m\lambda \qquad (m = 1, 2, 3, \dots)}$$
   The angular width of the central maximum between the two first minima ($m = \pm 1$) is $\Delta\theta_{\text{central}} = \frac{2\lambda}{a}$ (linear width $W_0 = \frac{2\lambda f}{a}$ on the focal screen)—strictly **twice** the width $\frac{\lambda}{a}$ of every outer secondary lobe.
3. **Secondary Maxima (Exact Transcendental Condition & Intensity Ratios):** Differentiate $I(\beta) = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}$ with respect to $\beta$ and set to zero:
   $$\frac{dI}{d\beta} = 2I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)\left(\frac{\beta\cos\beta - \sin\beta}{\beta^2}\right) = 0$$
   Excluding the minima ($\sin\beta = 0$), every secondary maximum strictly satisfies:
   $$\boxed{\tan\beta = \beta}$$
   Intersecting $y = \tan\beta$ and $y = \beta$ graphically yields non-zero roots lying slightly **below** the odd half-multiples $\left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi$:
   - $\beta_1 = 1.4303\pi = 4.4934\text{ rad}$ (vs. $3\pi/2 = 4.7124\text{ rad}$),
   - $\beta_2 = 2.4590\pi = 7.7253\text{ rad}$ (vs. $5\pi/2 = 7.8540\text{ rad}$),
   - $\beta_3 = 3.4709\pi = 10.9041\text{ rad}$ (vs. $7\pi/2 = 10.9956\text{ rad}$).

   Substituting $\tan\beta = \beta$ directly into $\sin^2\beta = \frac{\tan^2\beta}{1 + \tan^2\beta} = \frac{\beta^2}{1 + \beta^2}$ yields the exact secondary peak intensities:
   $$\frac{I_m}{I_0} = \frac{1}{1 + \beta_m^2} \implies \frac{I_1}{I_0} = 4.72\%, \quad \frac{I_2}{I_0} = 1.65\%, \quad \frac{I_3}{I_0} = 0.83\%$$
   In the common textbook approximation $\beta_m \approx \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi$ ($\sin\beta_m \approx \pm 1$), successive maxima fall cleanly in the ratio:
   $$I_0 : I_1 : I_2 : I_3 \approx 1 : \frac{4}{9\pi^2} : \frac{4}{25\pi^2} : \frac{4}{49\pi^2} \approx 1 : \frac{1}{22.2} : \frac{1}{61.7} : \frac{1}{120.9}$$

**Dimensional & Limiting-Case Checks:**
- $[\beta] = \frac{\text{m}}{\text{m}} = 1$ (dimensionless).
- As $a/\lambda \to \infty$ (geometrical optics limit): $\theta_1 = \lambda/a \to 0$, collapsing all light into a sharp central beam.
- As $a \le \lambda$: $\sin\theta_1 = \lambda/a \ge 1$ admits no real minima; the slit radiates as a broad cylindrical line source.

### 2.2 Double Slit - Modulation Envelope and Missing Orders

**Setup:** Consider two identical parallel vertical slits, each of width $a$, separated by an opaque strip of width $b$, giving center-to-center spacing $d = a + b$. Positioning the slit centers at $x = -d/2$ and $x = +d/2$, integrate over both open intervals:

$$E(\theta) = \mathcal{C}\int_{-d/2 - a/2}^{-d/2 + a/2} e^{ikx\sin\theta}\,dx + \mathcal{C}\int_{+d/2 - a/2}^{+d/2 + a/2} e^{ikx\sin\theta}\,dx$$

Shifting the integration variable in each integral to the respective slit center ($x = \mp d/2 + u$, $u \in [-a/2, +a/2]$) factors out the exact single-slit integral:

$$E(\theta) = \left(e^{-i\frac{kd\sin\theta}{2}} + e^{+i\frac{kd\sin\theta}{2}}\right)\mathcal{C}\int_{-a/2}^{+a/2} e^{iku\sin\theta}\,du = 2 E_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)\cos\gamma$$

where $\beta = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$ and the **inter-slit interference phase parameter** is $\gamma \equiv \frac{k d\sin\theta}{2} = \frac{\pi(a + b)\sin\theta}{\lambda}$. Squaring produces the double-slit intensity distribution:

$$\boxed{I(\theta) = 4 I_0\underbrace{\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2}_{\substack{\text{Single-Slit}\\\text{Diffraction Envelope}}}\,\underbrace{\cos^2\gamma}_{\substack{\text{Double-Slit}\\\text{Interference Fringes}}}}$$

- **Interference Maxima** ($\cos^2\gamma = 1$): $\gamma = n\pi \implies (a + b)\sin\theta = n\lambda$ ($n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$).
- **Interference Minima** ($\cos^2\gamma = 0$): $\gamma = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi \implies (a + b)\sin\theta = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$.
- **Diffraction Envelope Zeros:** $\beta = p\pi \implies a\sin\theta = p\lambda$ ($p = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$).

#### Missing Orders Condition

Whenever an interference maximum of order $n$ falls at the **exact same angle $\theta$** as a single-slit diffraction minimum of order $p$, the zero diffraction envelope annihilates that interference peak completely! Dividing the two conditions eliminates $\sin\theta$ and $\lambda$:

$$\frac{(a + b)\sin\theta}{a\sin\theta} = \frac{n\lambda}{p\lambda} \implies \boxed{\frac{n}{p} = \frac{d}{a} = \frac{a + b}{a} = 1 + \frac{b}{a}}$$

- If $b = a$ ($d = 2a$): $n = 2p \implies$ interference orders $\pm 2, \pm 4, \pm 6, \dots$ are **missing**, leaving exactly **3** interference maxima ($n = -1, 0, +1$) inside the central diffraction peak!
- If $b = 2a$ ($d = 3a$): $n = 3p \implies$ orders $\pm 3, \pm 6, \pm 9, \dots$ are missing, leaving **5** peaks ($n = -2, -1, 0, +1, +2$) inside the central envelope.
- In general, for integer ratio $k = d/a$, orders $n = \pm k, \pm 2k, \dots$ vanish, enclosing exactly $2k - 1$ bright interference fringes inside the central diffraction maximum.

  I / (4 I0)

### 2.3 $N$-Slit Diffraction Grating - Complex Phasor Sum, Dispersion and Resolving Power

**Unabridged Complex Phasor Derivation:** A plane transmission diffraction grating consists of $N$ identical parallel slits of individual width $a$ ruled at uniform period (grating element) $d = a + b$. Relative to slit $0$, the $j^{\text{th}}$ slit ($j = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1$) contributes the single-slit amplitude $E_0\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}$ delayed in phase by $j(2\gamma)$, where $2\gamma = \frac{2\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}$.

Summing all $N$ coherent complex phasors forms a finite geometric series with common ratio $r = e^{i2\gamma}$:

$$E(\theta) = E_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{i2j\gamma} = E_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)\frac{1 - e^{i2N\gamma}}{1 - e^{i2\gamma}}$$

Factoring out symmetric half-phases $e^{iN\gamma}$ in the numerator and $e^{i\gamma}$ in the denominator converts the exponentials cleanly into sines:

$$E(\theta) = E_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)\frac{e^{iN\gamma}\left(e^{-iN\gamma} - e^{iN\gamma}\right)}{e^{i\gamma}\left(e^{-i\gamma} - e^{i\gamma}\right)} = E_0\,e^{i(N-1)\gamma}\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\right)$$

Computing $I(\theta) = E(\theta)E^*(\theta)$ cancels $|e^{i(N-1)\gamma}|^2 = 1$ and yields the exact **$N$-Slit Fraunhofer Intensity Formula**:

$$\boxed{I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}, \quad \gamma = \frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}}$$

#### Principal Maxima, Minima, Secondary Maxima and Peak Sharpness

1. **Principal Maxima:** Occur where $\sin\gamma = 0 \implies \gamma = m\pi$ ($m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$), making $\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma} \to \frac{0}{0}$. By L'Hôpital's rule:
   $$\lim_{\gamma\to m\pi}\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma} = \lim_{\gamma\to m\pi}\frac{N\cos N\gamma}{\cos\gamma} = \pm N \implies I_{\text{principal}} = N^2 I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2$$
   This yields the **Grating Equation** (for normal incidence; for oblique incidence at angle $i$, $d(\sin i \pm \sin\theta) = m\lambda$):
   $$\boxed{d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda, \qquad m_{\max} = \left\lfloor\frac{d}{\lambda}\right\rfloor}$$
2. **Interference Minima:** Occur where $\sin(N\gamma) = 0$ while $\sin\gamma \ne 0 \implies N\gamma = q\pi$ ($q$ integer, $q \ne 0, \pm N, \pm 2N, \dots$). Thus **$(N - 1)$ zero minima** sit between any two consecutive principal maxima!
3. **Secondary Maxima:** Between those $(N - 1)$ minima lie **$(N - 2)$ weak secondary maxima**. Differentiating $\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\right)^2$ with respect to $\gamma$ gives:
   $$N\cos(N\gamma)\sin\gamma - \sin(N\gamma)\cos\gamma = 0 \implies \boxed{\tan(N\gamma) = N\tan\gamma}$$
   Using $\sin^2(N\gamma) = \frac{N^2\tan^2\gamma}{1 + N^2\tan^2\gamma} = \frac{N^2\sin^2\gamma}{1 + (N^2 - 1)\sin^2\gamma}$ yields the exact secondary peak height:
   $$\frac{I_{\text{sec}}}{I_{\text{principal}}} = \frac{1}{1 + (N^2 - 1)\sin^2\gamma}$$
   As $N$ grows large ($N \sim 10^4$), secondary maxima become vanishingly faint ($\propto 1/N^2$) while principal peaks shoot up as $N^2$ and narrow in angular half-width as $1/N$:
   $$\Delta\gamma_{\text{half}} = \frac{\pi}{N} \implies \Delta\theta_{\text{half}} = \frac{\lambda}{N d\cos\theta}$$

#### Angular Dispersion, Overlapping Orders and Chromatic Resolving Power

- **Angular Dispersion ($D$):** Differentiating $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ at fixed order $m$:
  $$\boxed{D \equiv \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d\cos\theta} = \frac{\tan\theta}{\lambda}}$$
  Notice $D$ depends on order $m$ and line spacing $d$, **NOT** on the total number of slits $N$!
- **Overlapping of Spectral Orders:** Wavelength $\lambda_1$ in order $m_1$ coincides at identical angle $\theta$ with wavelength $\lambda_2$ in order $m_2$ whenever:
  $$\boxed{m_1\lambda_1 = m_2\lambda_2}$$
  For white light ($400\text{–}700\text{ nm}$), the first order ($m = 1$) never overlaps higher orders ($1\times 700 < 2\times 400$), whereas the red end of $m = 2$ ($1400\text{ nm}$) overlaps violet and green of $m = 3$ ($1200\text{–}1500\text{ nm}$).
- **Chromatic Resolving Power ($\mathcal{R}$) via Rayleigh Criterion:** Two close wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ are just resolved in order $m$ when the $m^{\text{th}}$ principal maximum of $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ falls directly onto the **first adjacent zero minimum** of $\lambda$:
  - Principal maximum of $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$: $N d\sin\theta = N m(\lambda + \Delta\lambda)$.
  - First adjacent minimum of $\lambda$: $N d\sin\theta = (N m + 1)\lambda$.

  Equating the right-hand sides cancels $N m\lambda$:
  $$N m\,\Delta\lambda = \lambda \implies \boxed{\mathcal{R} \equiv \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = m N = \frac{W\sin\theta}{\lambda}}$$
  where $W = N d$ is the **total ruled width** of the grating! Crucially, since $|\sin\theta| < 1$, the maximum attainable resolving power of any grating is bounded strictly by its total width in wavelengths ($\mathcal{R}_{\max} < W/\lambda$), independent of how tightly the grooves are ruled!

### 2.4 Circular Aperture, Airy Pattern and Rayleigh Criterion for Telescopes & Microscopes

**Integral Derivation of the Airy Pattern:** Illuminate a circular opening of diameter $D = 2a$ normally with plane wave $\lambda$. Using polar coordinates $(\rho, \phi)$ in the aperture plane ($dA = \rho\,d\rho\,d\phi$) with $\phi$ measured from the direction of observation angle $\theta$, the optical path difference from element $(\rho, \phi)$ relative to the center is $\rho\sin\theta\cos\phi$:

$$E(\theta) = \mathcal{C}\int_0^a \rho\,d\rho \int_0^{2\pi} e^{ik\rho\sin\theta\cos\phi}\,d\phi$$

Apply the Bessel integral identity $J_0(u) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{iu\cos\phi}\,d\phi$ (with $u = k\rho\sin\theta$) and the recurrence integral $\int_0^x u J_0(u)\,du = x J_1(x)$ (with $x \equiv k a\sin\theta = \frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda}$):

$$E(\theta) = 2\pi\mathcal{C}\int_0^a \rho\,J_0(k\rho\sin\theta)\,d\rho = \frac{2\pi\mathcal{C}}{(k\sin\theta)^2}\int_0^x u J_0(u)\,du = \pi a^2\mathcal{C}\left[\frac{2 J_1(x)}{x}\right]$$

Squaring gives the circular **Airy Diffraction Distribution** (with central peak $I_0 = |\pi a^2\mathcal{C}|^2$ since $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2 J_1(x)}{x} = 1$):

$$\boxed{I(\theta) = I_0\left[\frac{2 J_1(x)}{x}\right]^2, \qquad x = \frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda}}$$

The first zero of the first-order Bessel function $J_1(x)$ occurs at $x_1 = 3.8317 = 1.220\pi$, setting the angular radius of the central **Airy Disk** (which contains $83.8\%$ of total transmitted energy; the first bright surrounding ring peaks at just $1.75\%$ of $I_0$):

$$\frac{\pi D\sin\theta_1}{\lambda} = 1.22\pi \implies \boxed{\sin\theta_1 \approx \theta_1 = \frac{1.22\lambda}{D}}$$

#### Rayleigh Criterion for Optical Instruments

Two equally bright incoherent point sources are **just resolved** when the central Airy maximum of one coincides with the **first dark Airy ring** of the other (producing a clearly detectable $26.5\%$ central intensity dip between the two peaks):

1. **Astronomical Telescope (Objective Diameter $D$):**
   $$\text{Limit of Resolution: }\;\theta_{\min} = \frac{1.22\lambda}{D}, \qquad \boxed{\text{Resolving Power} = \frac{1}{\theta_{\min}} = \frac{D}{1.22\lambda}}$$
2. **Optical Microscope (Self-Luminous Objects, Immersion Index $n$, Objective Half-Angle $\alpha$):**
   Defining the **Numerical Aperture** $\text{NA} \equiv n\sin\alpha$, two point objects separated by distance $d_{\min}$ are just resolved when:
   $$\boxed{d_{\min} = \frac{1.22\lambda_0}{2 n\sin\alpha} = \frac{0.61\lambda_0}{\text{NA}}, \qquad \text{Resolving Power} = \frac{1}{d_{\min}} = \frac{2 n\sin\alpha}{1.22\lambda_0}}$$
   Switching from visible light ($\lambda_0 \approx 550\text{ nm}$) to **ultraviolet light** ($\lambda_0 \approx 250\text{ nm}$) more than **doubles** a microscope's resolving power because $\text{RP} \propto 1/\lambda_0$, while oil immersion further boosts $n$ from $1.00$ to $\approx 1.52$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 1: Plane Transmission Grating (Orders, Missing Spectra, Dispersion, Resolution) & Telescope Limit
> **Problem:** (a) A plane transmission grating of total ruled width $W = 2.00\text{ cm}$ has $5000\text{ lines cm}^{-1}$ and is illuminated normally by sodium light ($\lambda = 589.3\text{ nm}$). Find (i) the grating element $d$ and highest observable order $m_{\max}$, (ii) which spectral orders are missing if the opaque spacing $b$ is three times the transparent slit width $a$, (iii) the angular dispersion $D = d\theta/d\lambda$ in the second order, and (iv) whether the sodium doublet ($\Delta\lambda = 0.60\text{ nm}$) is resolved in the first order. (b) Two stars $10\text{ light-years}$ away ($1\text{ ly} = 9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$) are just resolved by a telescope of objective diameter $D = 1.22\text{ m}$ at $\lambda = 550\text{ nm}$. Find their angular and linear separation.
>
> **Solution:**
> **(a) Diffraction grating analysis:**
> **(i) Grating element & highest order:**
> $$d = \frac{1\text{ cm}}{5000} = 2.00 \times 10^{-4}\text{ cm} = \boxed{2.00 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m} = 2000\text{ nm}}$$
> $$m_{\max} = \left\lfloor\frac{d}{\lambda}\right\rfloor = \left\lfloor\frac{2000\text{ nm}}{589.3\text{ nm}}\right\rfloor = \lfloor 3.394 \rfloor = \boxed{3}$$
> **(ii) Missing orders:**
> Given $b = 3a \implies d = a + b = 4a \implies \frac{n}{p} = \frac{d}{a} = 4$. Thus orders $n = 4, 8, 12, \dots$ are theoretically missing; since $m_{\max} = 3$, **no observable orders are absent** (orders $1, 2, 3$ all appear).
> **(iii) Second-order angular dispersion ($m = 2$):**
> $$\sin\theta_2 = \frac{2\lambda}{d} = \frac{2(589.3\text{ nm})}{2000\text{ nm}} = 0.5893 \implies \cos\theta_2 = \sqrt{1 - (0.5893)^2} = 0.8079$$
> $$D = \frac{m}{d\cos\theta_2} = \frac{2}{(2.00 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m})(0.8079)} = \boxed{1.238 \times 10^6\text{ rad m}^{-1} = 1.238 \times 10^{-3}\text{ rad nm}^{-1}}$$
> **(iv) First-order resolution of sodium doublet:**
> Total number of slits $N = W \times (5000\text{ cm}^{-1}) = 2.00 \times 5000 = 10{,}000$.
> Available resolving power in $m = 1$: $\mathcal{R}_1 = m N = 10{,}000$.
> Required resolving power: $\mathcal{R}_{\text{req}} = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{589.3\text{ nm}}{0.60\text{ nm}} = 982.2$.
> Since $\mathcal{R}_1 = 10{,}000 \gg 982.2$, the doublet is **cleanly resolved** (only $983$ lines are needed).
>
> **(b) Telescope Rayleigh limit:**
> $$\theta_{\min} = \frac{1.22\lambda}{D} = \frac{1.22(5.50 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m})}{1.22\text{ m}} = \boxed{5.50 \times 10^{-7}\text{ rad} \approx 0.113''}$$
> $$s = L\,\theta_{\min} = (10 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m})(5.50 \times 10^{-7}) = \boxed{5.20 \times 10^{10}\text{ m} \approx 0.35\text{ AU}}$$

## 3. Fresnel (Near-Field) Diffraction and Cornu Spiral

Without collimating and focusing lenses, wavefronts retain finite curvature as they pass an obstacle. Augustin-Jean Fresnel conquered near-field diffraction with two masterstrokes: partitioning circularly symmetric wavefronts into discrete **Half-Period Zones** (exposing multi-focus zone plates and Poisson's bright spot), and plotting rectangular/edge integrals on a universal spiral curve—**Cornu's Spiral**.

### 3.1 Fresnel Half-Period Zones (HPZs) and Rectilinear Propagation

**Geometric Construction:** Consider a plane monochromatic wavefront $W$ (wavelength $\lambda$) advancing toward an axial observation point $P$ located at perpendicular distance $b$ from pole $O$. Draw concentric spheres centered at $P$ of radii $b + \frac{\lambda}{2}, b + \frac{2\lambda}{2}, \dots, b + \frac{n\lambda}{2}$ intersecting plane $W$ in circles of radii $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$.

The annular region between circles $r_{n-1}$ and $r_n$ is the **$n^{\text{th}}$ Fresnel Half-Period Zone**—every successive zone is farther from $P$ by $\lambda/2$, meaning wavelets from consecutive zones arrive **strictly out of phase by $\pi$**.

1. **Radius of the $n^{\text{th}}$ zone:** From right triangle $O P r_n$:
   $$r_n^2 + b^2 = \left(b + \frac{n\lambda}{2}\right)^2 = b^2 + n b\lambda + \frac{n^2\lambda^2}{4} \implies \boxed{r_n = \sqrt{n b\lambda + \frac{n^2\lambda^2}{4}} \approx \sqrt{n b\lambda}} \quad (n\lambda \ll b)$$
   *(For a spherical wavefront from a point source $S$ at distance $a$ from pole $O$, matching sagitta paths $\frac{r_n^2}{2a} + \frac{r_n^2}{2b} = \frac{n\lambda}{2}$ replaces $b$ by $\frac{ab}{a+b}$, giving $r_n = \sqrt{\frac{n a b\lambda}{a + b}}$.)*
2. **Area of the $n^{\text{th}}$ zone:**
   $$\boxed{A_n = \pi\left(r_n^2 - r_{n-1}^2\right) = \pi b\lambda + \frac{(2n - 1)\pi\lambda^2}{4} \approx \pi b\lambda}$$
   To leading order, all half-period zones share **identical area** $\pi b\lambda$, independent of $n$!
3. **Resultant Amplitude via Schuster's Grouping Method:** The amplitude $R_n$ contributed by the $n^{\text{th}}$ zone is proportional to $\frac{A_n}{d_n}K(\theta_n)$, where mean distance $d_n = b + \frac{(2n-1)\lambda}{4}$ grows slowly and Fresnel's obliquity factor $K(\theta_n) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + \cos\theta_n)$ falls gradually toward zero. Thus $R_1 > R_2 > R_3 > \dots \to 0$. Summing alternating-sign contributions from $N$ zones:
   $$R_P = R_1 - R_2 + R_3 - R_4 + \dots + (-1)^{N-1}R_N$$
   Regroup terms into symmetric triads:
   $$R_P = \frac{R_1}{2} + \left(\frac{R_1}{2} - R_2 + \frac{R_3}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{R_3}{2} - R_4 + \frac{R_5}{2}\right) + \dots \pm \frac{R_N}{2}$$
   Because $R_n$ declines almost linearly, $R_{2k} \approx \frac{R_{2k-1} + R_{2k+1}}{2}$, annihilating every bracketed triad and leaving:
   $$\boxed{R_P = \frac{R_1}{2} \pm \frac{R_N}{2} \quad (+ \text{ for odd } N,\; - \text{ for even } N)}$$
   For a completely unobstructed wavefront ($N \to \infty, R_N \to 0$):
   $$\boxed{R_{\infty} = \frac{R_1}{2} \implies I_{\infty} = \frac{I_1}{4}}$$
   **Resolution of Rectilinear Propagation:** Even an obstacle merely a few millimeters wide blocks hundreds of HPZs (since $r_1 = \sqrt{b\lambda} \sim 0.7\text{ mm}$ at $b = 1\text{ m}$), leaving only high-obliquity distant zones whose sum is negligible—explaining why short-wavelength light casts crisp shadows!

### 3.2 Fresnel Zone Plate - Multi-Focal Lengths, Phase-Reversal Plate and Lens Comparison

A **Fresnel Zone Plate** is fabricated by drawing concentric circles of radii $r_n = r_1\sqrt{n}$ on a transparent sheet and blackening alternate zones (e.g., all even zones opaque, all odd zones clear).

1. **Primary Focus ($f_1$):** When illuminated by a plane wave $\lambda$, at axial distance $b = f_1$ satisfying $r_1^2 = f_1\lambda$, every transparent odd zone contributes strictly **in phase**:
   $$R_{\text{ZP}} = R_1 + R_3 + R_5 + \dots \approx N_{\text{odd}} R_1 \gg \frac{R_1}{2}$$
   producing a dazzling real image point at:
   $$\boxed{f_1 = \frac{r_1^2}{\lambda}}$$
   For a finite point source at distance $a = -u$ and focus at $b = +v$, $r_n^2\left(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}\right) = n\lambda$ immediately yields the exact thin-lens imaging equation $\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f_1}$!
2. **Multiple Higher-Order Foci ($f_p$):** Move the screen closer to $b = f_1 / (2p - 1)$ ($p = 1, 2, 3, \dots$). At $b = f_1/3$, each clear ring of the plate now encloses **three** fresh HPZs ($R_1' - R_2' + R_3' \approx R_1' > 0$), forming a weaker secondary focus! At even submultiples ($b = f_1/2$), each clear ring encloses **two** fresh HPZs ($R_1' - R_2' \approx 0$), yielding dark nulls. Hence a zone plate exhibits an infinite ladder of **odd-denominator foci** (plus symmetric virtual diverging foci at $-f_p$):
   $$\boxed{f_p = \frac{r_1^2}{(2p - 1)\lambda} = \frac{f_1}{2p - 1} \qquad (p = 1, 2, 3, \dots)}$$
3. **Wood's Phase-Reversal Zone Plate:** Rather than absorbing half the energy in opaque even rings, deposit or etch a dielectric step of thickness $t = \frac{\lambda}{2(\mu - 1)}$ onto the even rings to flip their phase by $\pi$. Now **every** zone interferes constructively ($R_{\text{rev}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots \approx 2 R_{\text{ZP}}$), quadrupling focal **intensity** ($I_{\text{rev}} = 4 I_{\text{ZP}}$)!
4. **Zone Plate vs. Convex Lens:**
   - **Mechanism:** Diffraction vs. refraction.
   - **Foci:** Infinite odd-series real + virtual foci $f_p = \pm f_1/(2p - 1)$ vs. single focal length $f$.
   - **Chromatic Aberration (Opposite Sign!):** In a zone plate $f_1 = r_1^2/\lambda \propto 1/\lambda$, so **red focuses closer than violet ($f_R < f_V$)**; in a normal glass convex lens, **$f_V < f_R$**.
   - **Optical Path Arrival:** Successive open zones arrive delayed by $1\lambda$ vs. strictly zero optical path difference across a lens.

### 3.3 Circular Aperture and Circular Obstacle (Poisson Bright Spot)

1. **Circular Aperture of Radius $a$:** From axial distance $b$, the opening exposes $N = \frac{a^2}{b\lambda}$ half-period zones (plane wave). By $R_P = \frac{R_1}{2} \pm \frac{R_N}{2}$:
   - **$N$ odd** ($b = \frac{a^2}{(2m+1)\lambda}$): $R_P \approx R_1 \implies I_P \approx 4 I_\infty$ (**bright axial center**!).
   - **$N$ even** ($b = \frac{a^2}{2m\lambda}$): $R_P = \frac{R_1 - R_N}{2} \approx 0$ (**dark axial center**!).
2. **Circular Opaque Disc of Radius $a$ (Arago–Poisson Spot):** The disc blocks the first $m = \frac{a^2}{b\lambda}$ zones, exposing zones $m + 1, m + 2, \dots, \infty$. By Schuster's grouping:
   $$R_P = R_{m+1} - R_{m+2} + R_{m+3} - \dots = \frac{R_{m+1}}{2} \ne 0$$
   Therefore, the exact geometric center of the shadow behind **any** smooth circular disc always contains a **bright spot** (Poisson's/Arago's spot) of intensity $\approx I_\infty$!

### 3.4 Fresnel Integrals and Cornu Spiral - Straight Edge and Long Narrow Slit

#### Derivation of Fresnel Integrals $C(v)$ and $S(v)$

**Setup:** A long narrow slit source $S$ parallel to the $z$-axis emits a cylindrical wavefront of radius $a$ toward a screen plane at distance $b$ beyond the aperture plane ($SP = a + b$ perpendicular to the planes). Let $s$ measure arc length along the cylindrical wavefront from the pole $O$ (intersection with line $SP$).

By the sagitta approximation for $s \ll a, b$, a point on the wavefront at coordinate $s$ has extra optical path to $P$ relative to the axial ray $OP$:

$$\Delta(s) = \frac{s^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}\right) = \frac{a + b}{2 a b}s^2$$

The corresponding quadratic phase lag is $\phi(s) = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta(s) = \frac{\pi(a + b)}{a b\lambda}s^2$. Introducing the **dimensionless Fresnel variable $v$**:

$$\boxed{v \equiv s\sqrt{\frac{2(a + b)}{a b\lambda}} \implies \phi(v) = \frac{\pi}{2}v^2, \qquad ds = \sqrt{\frac{a b\lambda}{2(a + b)}}\,dv}$$

Summing elementary strips across the unobstructed wavefront interval $[v_1, v_2]$ yields the complex diffracted amplitude:

$$E_P = \mathcal{K}\int_{v_1}^{v_2} e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}v^2}\,dv = \mathcal{K}\left[\int_{v_1}^{v_2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi u^2}{2}\right)du - i\int_{v_1}^{v_2}\sin\left(\frac{\pi u^2}{2}\right)du\right]$$

Define the two standard **Fresnel Integrals** (the Cartesian coordinates $X = C(v)$ and $Y = S(v)$ of **Cornu's Spiral**):

$$\boxed{C(v) \equiv \int_0^v \cos\left(\frac{\pi u^2}{2}\right)du, \qquad S(v) \equiv \int_0^v \sin\left(\frac{\pi u^2}{2}\right)du}$$

**Five Exact Geometric Laws of Cornu's Spiral:**
1. **Symmetry:** $C(-v) = -C(v)$ and $S(-v) = -S(v)$ (symmetric through the origin $(0,0)$).
2. **Arc Length Equals $v$:** $d\ell = \sqrt{(dC)^2 + (dS)^2} = \sqrt{\cos^2(\frac{\pi v^2}{2}) + \sin^2(\frac{\pi v^2}{2})}\,dv = dv \implies \ell(v) = v$.
3. **Tangent Angle Equals Phase:** $\tan\psi = \frac{dS}{dC} = \tan\left(\frac{\pi v^2}{2}\right) \implies \psi = \frac{\pi v^2}{2}$ (horizontal at $v = 0$, vertical at $v = \pm 1$).
4. **Linearly Growing Curvature:** $\kappa = \frac{d\psi}{dv} = \pi v$, so radius of curvature $\rho = \frac{1}{\pi v} \to 0$ as $|v| \to \infty$.
5. **Asymptotic Focal Eyes $J_\pm\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}, \pm\frac{1}{2}\right)$:** Using $\int_0^\infty \cos(\frac{\pi u^2}{2})\,du = \int_0^\infty \sin(\frac{\pi u^2}{2})\,du = \frac{1}{2}$, an unobstructed wavefront ($v \in (-\infty, +\infty)$) corresponds to chord $J_- J_+$ of length $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$, giving unobstructed intensity $I_0 = 2|\mathcal{K}|^2$. Thus for **any** exposed interval $[v_1, v_2]$, the intensity equals half $I_0$ times the **squared chord length** between points $v_1$ and $v_2$ on the spiral:
   $$\boxed{I(v_1, v_2) = \frac{I_0}{2}\Big[\big(C(v_2) - C(v_1)\big)^2 + \big(S(v_2) - S(v_1)\big)^2\Big]}$$

#### Application 1 - Fresnel Diffraction at a Straight Edge

Let a semi-infinite opaque straight edge block the wavefront for $v < v_0$, leaving $[v_0, +\infty)$ exposed (so one chord endpoint stays fixed at $J_+(+\frac{1}{2}, +\frac{1}{2})$ while the lower endpoint $v_0$ slides along the spiral as $P$ moves across the screen):

1. **Exact Geometrical Shadow Boundary ($v_0 = 0$):** Chord runs from $(0,0)$ to $J_+(1/2, 1/2)$, squared length $= (1/2)^2 + (1/2)^2 = 1/2$:
   $$\boxed{I_{\text{edge}} = \frac{I_0}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{I_0}{4}}$$
2. **Inside the Geometrical Shadow ($v_0 > 0$):** As $v_0$ winds from $0 \to +\infty$ into the upper eye $J_+$, the chord length shrinks **monotonically** to zero—light leaks slightly into the shadow with **zero oscillations/fringes**!
3. **Outside the Shadow in the Illuminated Region ($v_0 < 0$):** As $v_0$ winds around the lower eye $J_-$, the chord distance to $J_+$ oscillates with decreasing amplitude around $\sqrt{2}$, producing **asymmetric bright and dark fringes** crowding closer together as $x_n \propto \sqrt{n}$:
   - Extremal chords occur when the spiral tangent ($\psi = \frac{\pi v^2}{2}$) is perpendicular to the $45^\circ$ diagonal $J_- J_+$ (i.e., $\psi = \frac{3\pi}{4} + m\pi$):
     - **Bright Maxima:** $|v_{\max, n}| \approx \sqrt{4n - \frac{5}{2}}$ ($n = 1 \implies |v| = 1.225$, peaking at **$I_{\max, 1} \approx 1.37\,I_0$**; $n = 2 \implies |v| = 2.345$, $I_{\max, 2} \approx 1.20\,I_0$).
     - **Dark Minima:** $|v_{\min, n}| \approx \sqrt{4n - \frac{1}{2}}$ ($n = 1 \implies |v| = 1.871$, dipping to **$I_{\min, 1} \approx 0.78\,I_0$**—never strictly zero!; $n = 2 \implies |v| = 2.739$, $I_{\min, 2} \approx 0.84\,I_0$).
   - Far outside ($v_0 \to -\infty$), the chord settles to $J_- J_+ = \sqrt{2}$, recovering uniform illumination $I_0$.

#### Application 2 - Fresnel Diffraction by a Long Narrow Slit of Width $w$

A long slit of width $w$ exposes a **constant arc length** $\Delta v = w\sqrt{\frac{2(a + b)}{a b\lambda}}$ sliding along Cornu's spiral ($v_2 - v_1 = \Delta v$ fixed):
- **Axial Center ($v_1 = -\Delta v/2, v_2 = +\Delta v/2$):** Symmetric chord through the origin; varying slit width $\Delta v$ toggles the central axis between intense brightness ($\Delta v \approx 2.4$, spanning outer loops!) and deep dimness ($\Delta v \approx 3.7$, endpoints facing across inner coils!).
- **Narrow-Slit / Fraunhofer Limit ($\Delta v \ll 1$):** Sliding the short arc around the tight coils winds $\Delta v$ into full circles whose chord periodically hits **zero**, seamlessly reproducing the Fraunhofer single-slit $\text{sinc}^2$ zeros!
- **Wide-Slit Limit ($\Delta v \gg 1$):** One endpoint rests tightly inside $J_+$ while the other sweeps past the origin, splitting the pattern into two independent straight-edge fringe systems bordering a bright uniform interior plateau $I_0$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2: Zone Plate Multi-Foci & Cornu Straight-Edge Fringe Coordinates
> **Problem:** (a) A Fresnel zone plate has central zone radius $r_1 = 0.60\text{ mm}$ and is illuminated normally by a plane wave of wavelength $\lambda = 600\text{ nm}$. Find (i) its first three real focal lengths $f_1, f_2, f_3$, (ii) its primary focal length when illuminated by blue light $\lambda' = 450\text{ nm}$, and (iii) the primary real image distance for a point object at $u = -90.0\text{ cm}$ ($\lambda = 600\text{ nm}$). (b) A plane wave ($\lambda = 500\text{ nm}$) strikes a straight edge; diffraction is viewed on a screen $b = 2.00\text{ m}$ behind the edge. Locate the distances $x_{\max, 1}$ and $x_{\min, 1}$ of the first bright maximum and first dark minimum from the geometric shadow edge.
>
> **Solution:**
> **(a) Zone plate optics:**
> Convert $r_1 = 6.00 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} \implies r_1^2 = 3.60 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2$.
> **(i) First three foci at $\lambda = 6.00 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$:**
> $$f_1 = \frac{r_1^2}{\lambda} = \frac{3.60 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2}{6.00 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} = \boxed{0.600\text{ m} = 60.0\text{ cm}}$$
> $$f_2 = \frac{f_1}{3} = \boxed{20.0\text{ cm}}, \qquad f_3 = \frac{f_1}{5} = \boxed{12.0\text{ cm}}$$
> **(ii) Primary focal length at $\lambda' = 450\text{ nm} = 4.50 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}$:**
> $$f_1' = \frac{r_1^2}{\lambda'} = \frac{3.60 \times 10^{-7}}{4.50 \times 10^{-7}} = \boxed{0.800\text{ m} = 80.0\text{ cm}}$$

## 4. Polarization of Light and Uniaxial Crystal Optics

Interference and diffraction confirm that light behaves as a wave, yet longitudinal sound waves interfere and diffract just as easily. **Polarization** is the decisive signature proving that electromagnetic light waves are strictly **transverse**: their electric field vector $\mathbf{E}$ oscillates exclusively perpendicular to the propagation direction $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$, granting two orthogonal transverse degrees of freedom whose relative amplitude and phase sculpt linear, circular, and elliptical wave states.

### 4.1 Polarization States, Malus Law, Brewster Angle and Complete Detection Protocol

Consider a monochromatic plane wave traveling along $+z$ with orthogonal field components:

$$E_x(z, t) = E_{0x}\cos(kz - \omega t), \qquad E_y(z, t) = E_{0y}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta)$$

Eliminating $(kz - \omega t)$ identically as in $1:1$ Lissajous figures (Chapter 1) yields the **polarization ellipse**:

$$\boxed{\frac{E_x^2}{E_{0x}^2} + \frac{E_y^2}{E_{0y}^2} - \frac{2 E_x E_y}{E_{0x} E_{0y}}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta}$$

1. **Linearly Polarized Light (LPL):** $\delta = m\pi$ ($m = 0, \pm 1, \dots$) $\implies E_y = \pm\frac{E_{0y}}{E_{0x}}E_x$ (fixed line of vibration).
2. **Circularly Polarized Light (CPL):** $E_{0x} = E_{0y} = E_0$ **and** $\delta = \pm\frac{\pi}{2} + 2m\pi \implies E_x^2 + E_y^2 = E_0^2$.
   - **Standard Optics Handedness Convention** (observer looking **into** the oncoming beam toward $-z$, $+x$ right, $+y$ up):
     - **Right Circularly Polarized (RCP):** $\delta = -\frac{\pi}{2} \implies$ at $z = 0$, $E_x = E_0\cos\omega t$, $E_y = -E_0\sin\omega t$ ($\mathbf{E}$ rotates **clockwise**).
     - **Left Circularly Polarized (LCP):** $\delta = +\frac{\pi}{2} \implies$ at $z = 0$, $E_x = E_0\cos\omega t$, $E_y = +E_0\sin\omega t$ ($\mathbf{E}$ rotates **counter-clockwise**).
3. **Elliptically Polarized Light (EPL):** General combination of amplitudes and phase $\delta$ (tilted ellipse if $\delta \ne \pm\pi/2$; upright ellipse if $\delta = \pm\pi/2$ and $E_{0x} \ne E_{0y}$).

#### Malus's Law & Brewster's Law

- **Malus's Law:** When LPL of intensity $I_0$ traverses an ideal analyzer oriented at angle $\theta$ to $\mathbf{E}$, the transmitted field is $E_0\cos\theta$, giving transmitted intensity:
  $$\boxed{I(\theta) = I_0\cos^2\theta}$$
  *(Unpolarized light of intensity $I_{\text{unpol}}$ loses exactly half its intensity through an ideal polarizer: $I_1 = I_{\text{unpol}}\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle = \frac{1}{2}I_{\text{unpol}}$.)*
- **Brewster's Law (Polarization by Reflection):** At the Brewster (polarizing) incidence angle $i_B$ where reflected and refracted rays meet at $90^\circ$ ($i_B + r_B = 90^\circ$), atomic dipoles driven in the plane of incidence ($p$-polarization) oscillate **collinearly with the reflected ray**—and since an electric dipole radiates **zero** power along its axis, the reflected $p$-component vanishes identically, leaving pure $s$-polarized reflected light ($\mathbf{E} \perp$ plane of incidence):
  $$n_1\sin i_B = n_2\sin(90^\circ - i_B) = n_2\cos i_B \implies \boxed{\tan i_B = \frac{n_2}{n_1}}$$

#### Systematic Detection Protocol (Distinguishing All Five Light States)

Pass an unknown beam through a rotatable **Analyzer (Nicol prism / Polaroid)** and observe intensity over a $360^\circ$ rotation:

1. **Intensity drops cleanly to zero ($I_{\min} = 0$) twice per turn** $\implies$ **Linearly Polarized (LPL)**.
2. **Intensity varies between $I_{\max}$ and $I_{\min} > 0$ (never zero)** $\implies$ either **Elliptically Polarized (EPL)** OR **Partially Linearly Polarized**. Insert a **Quarter-Wave Plate (QWP)** ahead of the analyzer with its optic axis aligned to the $I_{\max}$ orientation:
   - Rotating the analyzer now produces **complete extinction ($I_{\min} = 0$)** $\implies$ **EPL** (converted to LPL by QWP).
   - Intensity still never vanishes $\implies$ **Partially Linearly Polarized**.
3. **Intensity stays strictly uniform across $360^\circ$** $\implies$ either **Circularly Polarized (CPL)** OR **Unpolarized**. Insert a **Quarter-Wave Plate (QWP)** ahead of the analyzer:
   - Rotating the analyzer now yields **complete extinction** twice per turn $\implies$ **CPL** (converted to LPL at $\pm 45^\circ$).
   - Intensity stays strictly constant $\implies$ **Unpolarized Light**.

### 4.2 Double Refraction (Birefringence), Huygens Wavefronts and Nicol Prism

When unpolarized light enters an optically anisotropic **uniaxial crystal** (such as calcite $\text{CaCO}_3$ or quartz $\text{SiO}_2$), asymmetric crystal binding forces along one unique symmetry direction—the **Optic Axis**—split the beam into two orthogonally linearly polarized refracted rays:

1. **Ordinary Ray ($o$-ray):** Electric vector vibrates strictly **perpendicular** to the **principal section** (plane containing the ray and the optic axis). Travels at uniform speed $v_o = c/\mu_o$ in every direction, obeys Snell's law strictly, and generates a **spherical** Huygens wavefront.
2. **Extraordinary Ray ($e$-ray):** Electric vector lies **within** the principal section. Its speed varies continuously with direction—equaling $v_o$ along the optic axis (zero birefringence!) and reaching its extremum $v_e = c/\mu_e$ perpendicular to the optic axis. Generally disobeys Snell's law and generates an **ellipsoid of revolution** Huygens wavefront touching the $o$-sphere along the optic axis!

- **Negative Uniaxial Crystals ($\mu_o > \mu_e \iff v_e > v_o$):** Example: **Calcite** ($\mu_o = 1.6584, \mu_e = 1.4864$ at $589.3\text{ nm}$), Tourmaline. The $o$-sphere sits completely **inside** the oblate $e$-ellipsoid.
- **Positive Uniaxial Crystals ($\mu_e > \mu_o \iff v_o > v_e$):** Example: **Quartz** ($\mu_o = 1.5442, \mu_e = 1.5533$ at $589.3\text{ nm}$), Ice, Rutile. The prolate $e$-ellipsoid sits completely **inside** the $o$-sphere.

#### Nicol Prism - Construction and Total Internal Reflection Filtering

To isolate pure linearly polarized light from calcite's two refracted beams, William Nicol (1828) engineered a crystal geometry that selectively eliminates the $o$-ray by **Total Internal Reflection (TIR)**:
- Take a clear calcite rhombohedron roughly $3\times$ longer than wide (natural blunt face angle $71^\circ$) and grind the end faces down to **$68^\circ$** in the principal section.
- Slice the crystal diagonally along a plane perpendicular to both the principal section and the polished $68^\circ$ end faces (cut runs at $22^\circ$ to the long edges), polish, and cement the halves together using **Canada Balsam** ($\mu_{\text{CB}} \approx 1.55$, strategically straddling calcite's $\mu_e = 1.486$ and $\mu_o = 1.658$!).
- **Action:** An unpolarized ray entering parallel to the long side splits into the $o$-ray ($\mu_o = 1.658 > \mu_{\text{CB}} = 1.55$) and the $e$-ray (effective $\mu_e \approx 1.51 < \mu_{\text{CB}} = 1.55$). The $o$-ray hits the rarer Canada balsam layer at an incidence angle exceeding the critical angle $i_c = \arcsin(1.55/1.658) \approx 69.2^\circ$ and undergoes **TIR** into the blackened side casing, whereas the $e$-ray enters an optically denser cement layer and transmits cleanly as $100\%$ **linearly polarized light** vibrating strictly in the principal section! *(Valid within an angular aperture cone of $\approx 28^\circ$.)*

### 4.3 Quarter-Wave Plate ($\lambda/4$) and Half-Wave Plate ($\lambda/2$)

Cut a thin uniaxial crystal plate of uniform thickness $d$ with its **optic axis parallel to its flat faces**. Under normal incidence, both $o$- and $e$-rays travel collinearly along the same thickness $d$ with orthogonal polarizations ($\mathbf{E}_e \parallel \text{optic axis}$, $\mathbf{E}_o \perp \text{optic axis}$), accumulating optical path difference $\Delta = |\mu_e - \mu_o|d$ and phase retardation:

$$\boxed{\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}|\mu_e - \mu_o|d}$$

*(In negative calcite, $\mu_e < \mu_o$, so the optic axis is the **fast axis**; in positive quartz, $\mu_o < \mu_e$, so the optic axis is the **slow axis**.)*

1. **Quarter-Wave Plate (QWP, $\lambda/4$):** Introduces phase lag $\delta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ (general multi-order: $\delta = (2m + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}$):
   $$\boxed{d_{\lambda/4, \min} = \frac{\lambda_0}{4|\mu_e - \mu_o|}, \qquad d_{\text{multi}} = \frac{(2m + 1)\lambda_0}{4|\mu_e - \mu_o|} \quad (m = 0, 1, 2, \dots)}$$
   Converts LPL incident at $\pm 45^\circ$ to the optic axis into **CPL** (and inversely converts **CPL $\to$ LPL** at $\pm 45^\circ$), and converts LPL at any other angle $\theta \ne 0^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ$ into upright **EPL**.
2. **Half-Wave Plate (HWP, $\lambda/2$):** Introduces phase lag $\delta = \pi$ (general multi-order: $\delta = (2m + 1)\pi$):
   $$\boxed{d_{\lambda/2, \min} = \frac{\lambda_0}{2|\mu_e - \mu_o|}}$$
   Reversing the sign of one orthogonal field component reflects an incoming LPL vector at angle $\theta$ across the optic axis to $-\theta$, cleanly **rotating the plane of linear polarization by $2\theta$**, and converts **RCP $\leftrightarrow$ LCP**!

| **Half-Wave Plate ($\lambda/2$)** | $\Delta = \dfrac{\lambda_0}{2}$, $\;\delta = \pi$ | $\dfrac{\lambda_0}{2|\mu_e - \mu_o|}$ | Remains **LPL**, rotated cleanly by **$2\theta$** | Reverses handedness (**RCP $\leftrightarrow$ LCP**) |

| **Nicol Prism** | TIR of $o$-ray at Canada balsam ($\mu_{\text{CB}} = 1.55$) | Calcite rhombohedron ($68^\circ$ ends) | Transmits $I_0\cos^2\theta$ (Malus's law) | Transmits constant $I_0/2$ as LPL |

### 4.4 Optical Activity - Fresnel Circular Birefringence, Specific Rotation and Laurent Polarimeter

#### Phenomenon, Molecular Origin and Specific Rotation

Certain transparent crystals (quartz along its optic axis) and liquids/solutions (sucrose, glucose, turpentine, tartaric acid) continuously rotate the plane of vibration of transmitted LPL by an angle $\theta$: **dextrorotatory ($+$)** rotates clockwise looking into the oncoming beam; **levorotatory ($-$)** rotates counter-clockwise.

**Why optical rotation in liquids has a molecular (chiral) origin:** When crystalline quartz is melted into amorphous fused silica, its optical activity vanishes completely—proving quartz's activity stems from helical arrangement of its crystal lattice. By contrast, sugar, turpentine, and camphor remain optically active in pure liquid, dilute solution, and even **gaseous vapor** phases where all intermolecular lattice structure is destroyed—proving the optical rotation originates inside individual **chiral (asymmetric) molecules** lacking mirror or inversion symmetry!

By **Biot's Laws**, at constant wavelength $\lambda$ and temperature $T$, $\theta$ scales linearly with path length $\ell$ and solution concentration $c$, defining **Specific Rotation ($S$)**:

$$\boxed{S \equiv \frac{\theta}{\ell\,c} \qquad (\text{standard polarimetry units: }\ell\text{ in dm} = 0.1\text{ m},\; c\text{ in g cm}^{-3} = 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3})}$$

#### Fresnel's Quantitative Derivation via Circular Birefringence

Fresnel recognized that any vertically polarized LPL wave entering an optically active medium at $z = 0$ decomposes identically into two equal-amplitude counter-rotating circular waves—one **RCP** and one **LCP**:

$$\mathbf{E}_{\text{RCP}}(0, t) = \big(A\cos\omega t,\; -A\sin\omega t\big), \qquad \mathbf{E}_{\text{LCP}}(0, t) = \big(A\cos\omega t,\; +A\sin\omega t\big)$$

Summing gives $E_x(0, t) = 2A\cos\omega t$ and $E_y(0, t) = 0$. Inside a chiral medium, RCP and LCP waves experience slightly different refractive indices $\mu_R \ne \mu_L$ (speeds $v_R = c/\mu_R$, $v_L = c/\mu_L$). After traversing thickness $d$, they acquire distinct phases $\phi_R = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\mu_R d$ and $\phi_L = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\mu_L d$:

$$E_x(d, t) = A\cos(\omega t - \phi_R) + A\cos(\omega t - \phi_L) = 2A\cos\left(\frac{\phi_R - \phi_L}{2}\right)\cos\left(\omega t - \frac{\phi_R + \phi_L}{2}\right)$$

$$E_y(d, t) = -A\sin(\omega t - \phi_R) + A\sin(\omega t - \phi_L) = 2A\sin\left(\frac{\phi_R - \phi_L}{2}\right)\cos\left(\omega t - \frac{\phi_R + \phi_L}{2}\right)$$

Because $E_x(d, t)$ and $E_y(d, t)$ share the **exact same harmonic time factor**, the emergent beam remains strictly **linearly polarized**, rotated from the $+x$-axis by angle $\theta$:

$$\frac{E_y(d, t)}{E_x(d, t)} = \tan\left(\frac{\phi_R - \phi_L}{2}\right) \implies \boxed{\theta = \frac{\phi_R - \phi_L}{2} = \frac{\pi d}{\lambda_0}(\mu_R - \mu_L)}$$

**Handedness Check:** If $\mu_L > \mu_R$ ($v_R > v_L$, RCP travels faster), then $\theta < 0$, tilting $\mathbf{E}$ from $+x$ toward $-y$—**clockwise** looking into the beam (**dextrorotatory**)! Conversely, $\mu_R > \mu_L$ ($v_L > v_R$) rotates counter-clockwise (**levorotatory**). Fresnel verified this tiny circular birefringence ($\Delta\mu \sim 7 \times 10^{-5}$ in quartz) directly using a compound prism train of alternating $d$- and $l$-quartz prisms that spatially split the two circularly polarized beams!

#### Laurent Half-Shade Polarimeter

Direct extinction between crossed Nicol prisms suffers low accuracy because $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ flattens out near $90^\circ$. The **Laurent Half-Shade Polarimeter** exploits the human eye's exquisite precision in matching equal illumination across two adjacent semicircles:
- **Layout:** Monochromatic sodium lamp $\to$ collimating lens $\to$ polarizing Nicol $P$ $\to$ **Laurent Half-Shade Plate** (one half clear glass; the other half a quartz **half-wave plate** cut parallel to its optic axis, tilted at small angle $\alpha$ to $P$'s vibration axis $OP$) $\to$ glass sample tube of length $\ell$ $\to$ rotatable analyzing Nicol $A$ mounted on a graduated Vernier circle $\to$ eyepiece telescope.
- **Working:** Light through the glass half retains vibration along $OP$, while light through the quartz HWP half is reflected across the optic axis to $OQ$ (forming angle $2\alpha$ with $OP$). Setting analyzer $A$'s transmission axis perpendicular to the optic axis (bisecting the obtuse angle between $OP$ and $OQ$) yields **equal dim half-shade brightness** $I_0\sin^2\alpha$ across both halves—the slightest misalignment makes one half noticeably brighter and the other darker! Introducing an optically active solution rotates both $OP$ and $OQ$ rigidly by $\theta$; rotating analyzer $A$ by the exact same angle $\theta$ restores uniform half-shade matching, reading $\theta$ to $\sim 0.01^\circ$ and yielding unknown concentration $c = \frac{\theta}{S\ell}$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 3: Multi-Order Calcite Plate Polarization State & Sucrose Circular Birefringence
> **Problem:** (a) A calcite plate ($\mu_o = 1.6584$, $\mu_e = 1.4864$ at $\lambda_0 = 589.3\text{ nm}$) of thickness $d = 4.283\,\mu\text{m}$ has its optic axis parallel to the faces. If **right circularly polarized (RCP)** light of wavelength $589.3\text{ nm}$ falls normally on it, find the exact emergent polarization state and orientation relative to the optic axis. (b) A $20.0\text{ cm}$ polarimeter tube filled with aqueous sucrose solution ($S = +66.5^\circ\text{ dm}^{-1}(\text{g cm}^{-3})^{-1}$ at $\lambda_0 = 589.3\text{ nm}$) rotates plane-polarized sodium light clockwise by $19.95^\circ$. Calculate (i) the solution concentration $c$ and (ii) the circular birefringence $|\mu_L - \mu_R|$.
>
> **Solution:**
> **(a) Multi-order calcite wave-plate transformation:**
> Birefringence: $|\mu_o - \mu_e| = 1.6584 - 1.4864 = 0.1720$.
> Phase retardation introduced by thickness $d = 4.283 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$:
> $$\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}(\mu_o - \mu_e)d = \frac{2\pi(0.1720)(4.283 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m})}{5.893 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}} = 2\pi(1.250) = \frac{5\pi}{2} = 2\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}$$
> Because $\delta \equiv \frac{\pi}{2}\pmod{2\pi}$, the plate acts as a **multi-order Quarter-Wave Plate**.
> Align the calcite optic axis along $+x$ (**fast axis**, since $\mu_e < \mu_o$) and perpendicular $+y$ (**slow axis**, $\mu_o > \mu_e$).
> Incoming RCP at $z = 0$: $E_x = E_0\cos(\omega t)$, $E_y = -E_0\sin(\omega t) = E_0\cos\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
> Passing through the plate, the slow $y$-component ($o$-ray) lags the fast $x$-component ($e$-ray) by an additional phase $\delta = \frac{5\pi}{2}$:
> $$E_x^{\text{out}} = E_0\cos(\omega t - \phi_x)$$
> $$E_y^{\text{out}} = E_0\cos\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2} - \phi_x - \frac{5\pi}{2}\right) = E_0\cos(\omega t - \phi_x - 2\pi) = +E_0\cos(\omega t - \phi_x) = +E_x^{\text{out}}$$
> Hence the emergent beam is **Linearly Polarized Light (LPL) at $+45^\circ$ to the calcite optic axis**.
>
> **(b) Polarimeter concentration & circular birefringence:**
> Convert tube length $\ell = 20.0\text{ cm} = 2.00\text{ dm} = 0.200\text{ m}$.
> **(i) Concentration $c$:**
> $$c = \frac{\theta}{S\ell} = \frac{19.95^\circ}{(66.5^\circ\text{ dm}^{-1}\text{g}^{-1}\text{ cm}^3)(2.00\text{ dm})} = \frac{19.95}{133.0} = \boxed{0.150\text{ g cm}^{-3} = 150\text{ kg m}^{-3}}$$
> **(ii) Circular birefringence $|\mu_L - \mu_R|$:**
> Convert $\theta = 19.95^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} = 0.3482\text{ rad}$. For dextrorotatory rotation ($\mu_L > \mu_R$):
> $$\mu_L - \mu_R = \frac{\theta\,\lambda_0}{\pi d} = \frac{(0.3482)(5.893 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m})}{\pi(0.200\text{ m})} = \frac{2.052 \times 10^{-7}}{0.6283} = \boxed{3.27 \times 10^{-7}}$$

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> - **Single-Slit Half-Phase Parameter $\beta$ Trap:** Always define $\beta = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$ explicitly as **half** the total phase difference across the slit, so minima sit at $\beta = m\pi$ ($a\sin\theta = m\lambda$)—and never write $\frac{2\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$ inside $\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}$!
> - **Missing-Order & $m_{\max}$ Double Trap:** In double-slit and grating numericals (e.g., UPSC 2011 Q3(c)), the opaque width $b$ is **not** the grating period—always compute **$d = a + b$** first so missing orders satisfy $\frac{n}{p} = \frac{a + b}{a} = 1 + \frac{b}{a}$! Then immediately check $m_{\max} = \lfloor d/\lambda \rfloor$ so you do not list missing orders beyond the physical horizon $|\sin\theta| \le 1$.
> - **Dispersive Power vs. Resolving Power Confusion:** Angular dispersion $D = \frac{m}{d\cos\theta}$ depends on groove spacing $d$ (NOT total rulings $N$), whereas chromatic resolving power $\mathcal{R} = m N = \frac{W\sin\theta}{\lambda}$ depends on total illuminated rulings $N$ (and total ruled width $W = N d$). Never swap them!
> - **Zone Plate Even-Denominator & Chromatic Traps:** A zone plate has **NO** real foci at $f_1/2, f_1/4, \dots$—only odd submultiples $f_p = \frac{r_1^2}{(2p - 1)\lambda}$! Furthermore, its chromatic dispersion runs **opposite** to a glass convex lens ($f_{\text{red}} < f_{\text{violet}}$).
> - **Cornu Spiral Intensity Normalization Trap:** Unobstructed illumination ($J_- J_+ = \sqrt{2}$) has squared chord length $2$, so intensity is $I = \frac{I_0}{2}\times(\text{chord})^2$—forgetting the factor of $1/2$ falsely gives $I_{\text{edge}} = I_0/2$ instead of the exact $I_0/4$!
> - **Multi-Order Wave-Plate & Fast/Slow Axis Traps (UPSC 2011 Q4(a)):** A crystal plate thicker than $d_{\lambda/4, \min}$ does **not** automatically fail as a wave plate! Compute exact $\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}|\mu_e - \mu_o|d$ modulo $2\pi$: odd multiples of $\pi/2$ act as QWP (converting CPL $\to$ LPL), odd multiples of $\pi$ act as HWP (flipping LCP $\leftrightarrow$ RCP), and integer multiples of $2\pi$ leave CPL unchanged! Always check whether the crystal is **negative** (calcite: optic axis = fast) or **positive** (quartz: optic axis = slow) before assigning the sign of the emergent azimuth!
> - **Polarimetry Decimeter Trap:** Specific rotation $S = \frac{\theta}{\ell c}$ uses path length $\ell$ strictly in **decimeters** ($1\text{ dm} = 10\text{ cm} = 0.1\text{ m}$) and concentration $c$ in $\text{g cm}^{-3}$. Plugging $\ell = 20\text{ cm}$ directly as $20$ instead of $2.0\text{ dm}$ introduces a fatal $10\times$ error!
