# Chapter 1: Electrostatics, Boundary-Value Problems, Multipoles & Dielectric Media

## 1. Electrostatic Fields, Potentials, Poisson-Laplace Equations and Uniqueness

Electrostatics governs stationary charge distributions where all time derivatives vanish. Locally, every electrostatic field $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ is uniquely fixed by two differential constraints: its divergence is sourced strictly by charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r})$, and its curl vanishes identically because Coulomb forces are central and conservative.

Rather than wrestling directly with three coupled vector components of $\mathbf{E}$, the curl-free condition permits collapsing the entire field into a single scalar potential $V(\mathbf{r})$. Boundary-value electrostatics then reduces to solving a linear elliptic partial differential equation—Poisson's or Laplace's equation—subject to physical boundary conditions on conductors and at infinity.

### 1.1 Gauss's Law, Scalar Potential, and Poisson-Laplace Equations

**Physical Assumptions & Setup:** Consider a stationary charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ in vacuum (permittivity $\epsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$). The fundamental SI differential equations of electrostatics are:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}$$

By the Helmholtz theorem on a simply connected domain, $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}$ implies that $\mathbf{E}$ is the negative gradient of a scalar potential $V(\mathbf{r})$, defined relative to a reference point $\mathcal{O}$ (typically infinity for localized charge distributions):

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = -\nabla V(\mathbf{r}), \qquad V(\mathbf{r}) = -\int_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathbf{r}} \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}') \cdot d\mathbf{l}'$$

Substituting $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ directly into the differential form of Gauss's law gives **Poisson's equation**:

$$\nabla \cdot (-\nabla V) = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} \implies \boxed{\nabla^2 V(\mathbf{r}) = -\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r})}{\epsilon_0}}$$

In any charge-free region ($\rho = 0$), this simplifies to **Laplace's equation**:

$$\boxed{\nabla^2 V(\mathbf{r}) = 0}$$

**Harmonic Properties of Laplace Solutions:** Functions satisfying $\nabla^2 V = 0$ are *harmonic* and exhibit two powerful physical properties:
1. **Mean-Value Theorem:** The value of $V$ at any point $\mathbf{r}$ equals the spherical average of $V$ over any sphere of radius $R$ centered at $\mathbf{r}$ that encloses no charge:
   $$V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi R^2}\oint_{\text{sphere}} V\,da$$
2. **Absence of Local Extrema (Earnshaw's Theorem):** Because $V(\mathbf{r})$ is the average of its immediate neighbors, $V$ cannot possess a local maximum or minimum in free space; all extrema occur strictly on the boundary of the charge-free domain. Consequently, no charged particle can be held in stable static equilibrium by electrostatic forces alone.

**Dimensional & Limiting Checks:** $[\nabla^2 V] = \text{V}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}$, while $[\rho/\epsilon_0] = (\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-3})/(\text{C}\cdot\text{V}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}) = \text{V}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}$. In 1D with $\rho = 0$, $d^2 V/dx^2 = 0$ yields linear $V(x) = ax + b$, recovering a uniform field $E_x = -a$.

### 1.2 Uniqueness Theorems I and II

In practical electrostatics, we rarely know $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ everywhere in advance—free charges on conductors rearrange themselves to keep each conductor equipotential. Uniqueness theorems guarantee that *any* method (separation of variables, method of images, conformal mapping, or inspired guessing) that satisfies Poisson's equation and the boundary conditions yields the **one and only** true physical solution.

#### First Uniqueness Theorem (Dirichlet Boundary Condition)

**Theorem Statement:** The solution to Poisson's equation $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ in a volume $\mathcal{V}$ is uniquely determined if $V$ is specified on the entire closed boundary surface $\mathcal{S}$ bounding $\mathcal{V}$ (including the sphere at infinity if $\mathcal{V}$ is unbounded).

**Step-by-Step Proof:**
1. Suppose two potentials $V_1(\mathbf{r})$ and $V_2(\mathbf{r})$ both satisfy Poisson's equation inside $\mathcal{V}$ with identical boundary values on $\mathcal{S}$:
   $$\nabla^2 V_1 = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \quad \nabla^2 V_2 = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} \quad \text{in }\mathcal{V}; \qquad V_1\big|_{\mathcal{S}} = V_2\big|_{\mathcal{S}}$$
2. Define the difference potential $V_3 \equiv V_1 - V_2$. By linearity of the Laplacian:
   $$\nabla^2 V_3 = \nabla^2 V_1 - \nabla^2 V_2 = 0 \quad \text{in }\mathcal{V}, \qquad V_3\big|_{\mathcal{S}} = 0$$
3. Apply the vector product rule $\nabla \cdot (V_3 \nabla V_3) = |\nabla V_3|^2 + V_3 \nabla^2 V_3$ and integrate over $\mathcal{V}$ using the Divergence Theorem (Green's first identity):
   $$\int_{\mathcal{V}} \left(|\nabla V_3|^2 + V_3 \nabla^2 V_3\right) d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} V_3 (\nabla V_3) \cdot d\mathbf{a}$$
4. Inside $\mathcal{V}$, $\nabla^2 V_3 = 0$; on $\mathcal{S}$, $V_3 = 0$. Hence the surface integral vanishes identically:
   $$\int_{\mathcal{V}} |\nabla V_3|^2 \, d\tau = 0$$
5. Since $|\nabla V_3|^2 \ge 0$ everywhere in $\mathcal{V}$, the integrand must vanish identically:
   $$\nabla V_3 = \mathbf{0} \implies V_3(\mathbf{r}) = \text{const} \quad \text{throughout }\mathcal{V}$$
6. Continuity up to $\mathcal{S}$, where $V_3 = 0$, fixes the constant to zero:

$$\boxed{V_3(\mathbf{r}) = 0 \implies V_1(\mathbf{r}) = V_2(\mathbf{r}) \quad \forall\,\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{V}}$$

*(Neumann Remark: If instead the normal derivative $\partial V/\partial n = -\mathbf{E}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is specified everywhere on $\mathcal{S}$, the surface integral $\oint_{\mathcal{S}} V_3 (\partial V_3/\partial n)\,da$ again vanishes, giving $\nabla V_3 = \mathbf{0}$, so $V$ is unique up to an irrelevant additive constant and $\mathbf{E}$ is strictly unique.)*

#### Second Uniqueness Theorem (Conductors with Specified Total Charges)

**Theorem Statement:** In a volume $\mathcal{V}$ containing a specified charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ and $N$ conductors with surfaces $\mathcal{S}_1, \mathcal{S}_2, \dots, \mathcal{S}_N$ (plus an outer boundary $\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}$), the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is uniquely determined if the **total charge** $Q_i$ on each conductor is specified (even when the potentials $V_i$ of the conductors are unknown in advance).

**Step-by-Step Proof:**
1. Suppose two fields $\mathbf{E}_1 = -\nabla V_1$ and $\mathbf{E}_2 = -\nabla V_2$ satisfy $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}_1 = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}_2 = \rho/\epsilon_0$ inside $\mathcal{V}$ and yield the same total charge on every conductor surface $\mathcal{S}_i$:
   $$\oint_{\mathcal{S}_i} \mathbf{E}_1 \cdot d\mathbf{a}_i = \oint_{\mathcal{S}_i} \mathbf{E}_2 \cdot d\mathbf{a}_i = \frac{Q_i}{\epsilon_0} \qquad (i = 1, 2, \dots, N)$$
   Here $d\mathbf{a}_i$ points outward from the $i$-th conductor (i.e., *inward* into $\mathcal{V}$).
2. Define $\mathbf{E}_3 \equiv \mathbf{E}_1 - \mathbf{E}_2 = -\nabla V_3$, where $V_3 = V_1 - V_2$. Inside $\mathcal{V}$, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}_3 = 0$. On each conductor surface $\mathcal{S}_i$, $V_1$ and $V_2$ are separately constant (equipotential conductors), so $V_3\big|_{\mathcal{S}_i} = C_i = \text{const}$.
3. Apply the Divergence Theorem to $V_3 \mathbf{E}_3$ over $\mathcal{V}$, noting that the complete boundary $\mathcal{S}$ consists of the outer surface $\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}$ (outward normal) and the inner conductor surfaces $\mathcal{S}_i$ (whose outward normal from $\mathcal{V}$ is $-d\mathbf{a}_i$):
   $$\int_{\mathcal{V}} \nabla \cdot (V_3 \mathbf{E}_3)\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}} V_3 \mathbf{E}_3 \cdot d\mathbf{a}_{\text{out}} - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \oint_{\mathcal{S}_i} V_3 \mathbf{E}_3 \cdot d\mathbf{a}_i$$
4. Expand the LHS: $\nabla \cdot (V_3 \mathbf{E}_3) = (\nabla V_3)\cdot\mathbf{E}_3 + V_3(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}_3) = -E_3^2 + 0 = -E_3^2$.
5. On the RHS, the constant $V_3 = C_i$ pulls out of each conductor integral:
   $$\oint_{\mathcal{S}_i} V_3 \mathbf{E}_3 \cdot d\mathbf{a}_i = C_i \oint_{\mathcal{S}_i} (\mathbf{E}_1 - \mathbf{E}_2)\cdot d\mathbf{a}_i = C_i\left(\frac{Q_i}{\epsilon_0} - \frac{Q_i}{\epsilon_0}\right) = 0$$
   Similarly, on $\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}$, either $V$ is fixed ($V_3 = 0$), total charge is fixed, or $\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}} \to \infty$ where $V_3 \sim 1/r$, $E_3 \sim 1/r^2$, $da \sim r^2$, so the integral vanishes as $1/r \to 0$.
6. Hence:
   $$-\int_{\mathcal{V}} E_3^2\,d\tau = 0 \implies \boxed{\mathbf{E}_3 = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{E}_1(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{E}_2(\mathbf{r}) \quad \forall\,\mathbf{r} \in \mathcal{V}}$$

### 1.3 Electrostatic Energy of Discrete and Continuous Distributions

Work performed by an external agent in assembling charges quasistatically against mutual Coulomb repulsion is stored reversibly as electrostatic potential energy $W$. Remarkably, this energy can be re-expressed entirely as an integral over the electric field filling space—a first hint that electromagnetic fields carry real physical energy.

#### Energy of Discrete Point Charges
Assemble $N$ point charges $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N$ from infinity to positions $\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N$ one by one:
- Bringing $q_1$: $W_1 = 0$.
- Bringing $q_2$ in the field of $q_1$: $W_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$.
- Bringing $q_i$ in the field of $q_1, \dots, q_{i-1}$: $W_i = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}}$.

Summing over all $i = 1, \dots, N$ counts every unordered pair $(i,j)$ once. Summing freely over all $i \neq j$ counts each pair twice, introducing the famous factor of $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$W_{\text{discrete}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\substack{j=1 \\ j\neq i}}^{N}\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} = \boxed{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i V_{\text{other}}(\mathbf{r}_i)}$$

where $V_{\text{other}}(\mathbf{r}_i) = \sum_{j \neq i}\frac{q_j}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{ij}}$ explicitly excludes the infinite self-potential of $q_i$ at its own location.

#### Energy of a Continuous Charge Distribution & Field-Energy Equivalence
For a smooth volume charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ bounded inside a finite region $\mathcal{V}$, the self-potential of an infinitesimal element $dq = \rho\,d\tau$ vanishes as $d\tau^{2/3} \to 0$, so replacing the sum by an integral yields:

$$W = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \rho(\mathbf{r}) V(\mathbf{r})\,d\tau$$

**Unabridged Derivation of the Field-Energy Form:**
1. Extend the integration volume to a large sphere $\mathcal{V}_R$ of radius $R$ enclosing all charges (since $\rho = 0$ outside $\mathcal{V}$) and substitute Gauss's law $\rho = \epsilon_0 \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}$:
   $$W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}_R} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}) V \, d\tau$$
2. Apply the vector product rule $\nabla \cdot (V\mathbf{E}) = (\nabla V)\cdot\mathbf{E} + V(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})$, which rearranges using $\nabla V = -\mathbf{E}$ to:
   $$V(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}) = \nabla\cdot(V\mathbf{E}) + E^2$$
3. Substitute into $W$ and apply the Divergence Theorem on the spherical surface $\mathcal{S}_R$ of radius $R$:
   $$W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}_R} E^2 \, d\tau + \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\oint_{\mathcal{S}_R} V \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{a}$$
4. Take the limit $R \to \infty$. For any localized charge distribution, $V \sim 1/R$, $|\mathbf{E}| \sim 1/R^2$, and the surface area scales as $4\pi R^2$. Thus the surface integral vanishes as:
   $$\left|\oint_{\mathcal{S}_R} V\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{a}\right| \sim \frac{1}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{R^2}\cdot R^2 = \frac{1}{R} \xrightarrow{R\to\infty} 0$$
5. Only the strictly non-negative volume integral over all space survives:

$$\boxed{W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}} E^2 \, d\tau, \qquad u_e \equiv \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0 E^2}$$

**Crucial Physical Distinction:** $W_{\text{field}} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2 d\tau$ is always $\ge 0$, whereas $W_{\text{discrete}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j} \frac{q_i q_j}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{ij}}$ can be negative (e.g., an opposite-sign pair $+q, -q$). There is no contradiction: expanding $|\mathbf{E}_1 + \mathbf{E}_2|^2 = E_1^2 + E_2^2 + 2\mathbf{E}_1\cdot\mathbf{E}_2$ reveals that the cross-term $\epsilon_0\int \mathbf{E}_1\cdot\mathbf{E}_2\,d\tau$ reproduces the mutual interaction energy $\frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}}$, while $\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int (E_1^2 + E_2^2)\,d\tau$ carries the infinite self-energies of ideal mathematical point charges that $W_{\text{discrete}}$ discarded.

**Dimensional Check:** $[u_e] = [\epsilon_0][E]^2 = (\text{C}^2\cdot\text{N}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-2})(\text{N}^2\cdot\text{C}^{-2}) = \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^{-2} = \text{J}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: 1D Poisson Equation & Self-Energy of a Uniformly Charged Sphere
> **Problem:**
> *(a)* In a 1D region $0 \le x \le L$ with $L = 5\text{ m}$, the charge density is $\rho(x) = \rho_0 x/L$, where $\rho_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-9}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$. Given $E_x(0) = 0$ and $V(L) = 0$, determine $E_x(x)$ and $V(x)$ analytically, and evaluate $E_x(5\text{ m})$ and $V(0)$.
> *(b)* Compute the electrostatic self-energy $W$ of a uniformly charged solid non-conducting sphere of radius $R = 3.0\text{ cm}$ and total charge $Q = 1.0\,\mu\text{C}$ by **both** $\frac{1}{2}\int\rho V\,d\tau$ and $\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$, verifying exact agreement.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part (a):** In 1D, Poisson's equation reduces to:
> $$\frac{d^2 V}{dx^2} = -\frac{\rho(x)}{\epsilon_0} = -\frac{\rho_0}{\epsilon_0 L}x$$
> Integrating once with respect to $x$:
> $$\frac{dV}{dx} = -\frac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0 L}x^2 + C_1 \implies E_x(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx} = \frac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0 L}x^2 - C_1$$
> Applying $E_x(0) = 0$ gives $C_1 = 0$, so:
> $$E_x(x) = \frac{\rho_0 x^2}{2\epsilon_0 L}$$
> Integrating a second time:
> $$V(x) = -\frac{\rho_0 x^3}{6\epsilon_0 L} + C_2$$
> Applying $V(L) = 0$ gives $C_2 = \frac{\rho_0 L^2}{6\epsilon_0}$, yielding:
> $$V(x) = \frac{\rho_0}{6\epsilon_0 L}\left(L^3 - x^3\right)$$
> Substituting $\frac{\rho_0}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{8.854\times 10^{-9}}{8.854\times 10^{-12}} = 1.000 \times 10^3\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}$ and $L = 5\text{ m}$:
> $$E_x(5\text{ m}) = \frac{10^3 \times 5^2}{2 \times 5} = \boxed{2.50 \times 10^3\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}}, \qquad V(0) = \frac{10^3 \times 5^3}{6 \times 5} = \boxed{4.17 \times 10^3\text{ V}}$$
>
> **Part (b):** For a uniform sphere of charge density $\rho = \frac{3Q}{4\pi R^3}$, Gauss's law gives:
> $$E_{\text{in}}(r) = \frac{Qr}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3}\quad(r \le R), \qquad E_{\text{out}}(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\quad(r > R)$$
> Integrating $V(r) = -\int_\infty^r E\,dr'$ yields $V_{\text{out}}(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$ and:
> $$V_{\text{in}}(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} + \int_r^R \frac{Qr'}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3}\,dr' = \frac{Q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left(3 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$$
> - **Method 1 ($\frac{1}{2}\int\rho V\,d\tau$):**
>   $$W = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3Q}{4\pi R^3}\right)\int_0^R \frac{Q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left(3 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{3Q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 R^4}\left[r^3 - \frac{r^5}{5R^2}\right]_0^R = \frac{3Q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left(\frac{4}{5}\right) = \frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0 R}$$
> - **Method 2 ($\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$):**
>   $$W_{\text{in}} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_0^R \left(\frac{Qr}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3}\right)^2 4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R^6}\cdot\frac{R^5}{5} = \frac{Q^2}{40\pi\epsilon_0 R}$$
>   $$W_{\text{out}} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_R^\infty \left(\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\right)^2 4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R} = \frac{5Q^2}{40\pi\epsilon_0 R}$$
>   $$W = W_{\text{in}} + W_{\text{out}} = \frac{6Q^2}{40\pi\epsilon_0 R} = \frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0 R}$$
>   Both methods match identically (notice $5/6$ of the energy resides outside the sphere!).
> - **Numeric Evaluation:** With $Q = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}$, $R = 3.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$, $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 8.988 \times 10^9\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\text{C}^{-2}$:
>   $$W = \frac{3}{5}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right)\frac{Q^2}{R} = 0.6 \times 8.988 \times 10^9 \times \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-12}}{3.0 \times 10^{-2}} = \boxed{0.180\text{ J}}$$

## 2. Multipole Expansion of Scalar Potential and Electric Dipole Physics

Viewed from a distance $r$ much larger than the physical extent $a$ of a localized charge cloud, fine geometric details blur away. At leading order, the distribution looks like a single point charge ($1/r$). If the net charge is zero, the dominant surviving signature is the separation between positive and negative charge centers—a dipole ($1/r^2$)—followed by planar or linear stretching encoded in the quadrupole ($1/r^3$).

The multipole expansion systematically orders these geometric fingerprints as a power series in $1/r$ using Legendre polynomials.

### 2.1 Exact Legendre-Polynomial Multipole Expansion

**Coordinate Setup:** Let a localized charge distribution $\rho(\mathbf{r}')$ lie entirely within a sphere of radius $a$ centered at the origin, and evaluate $V(\mathbf{r})$ at an exterior field point $r > a$. Let $\alpha$ be the angle between the field vector $\mathbf{r}$ and the source vector $\mathbf{r}'$, so $\cos\alpha = \hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}'$.

The exact electrostatic potential is:

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}'} \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|}\,d\tau'$$

By the law of cosines, express the separation distance $|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|$ as:

$$|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'| = \sqrt{r^2 + r'^2 - 2rr'\cos\alpha} = r\sqrt{1 - 2\left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)\cos\alpha + \left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^2}$$

Set $u \equiv \frac{r'}{r} < 1$ and $x \equiv \cos\alpha$. Expand $(1 - 2ux + u^2)^{-1/2}$ via the binomial series $(1 + \varepsilon)^{-1/2} = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon + \frac{3}{8}\varepsilon^2 - \dots$ with $\varepsilon = u^2 - 2ux$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2ux + u^2}} &= 1 - \frac{1}{2}(u^2 - 2ux) + \frac{3}{8}(u^2 - 2ux)^2 + \mathcal{O}(u^3) \\
&= 1 + ux + \left(-\frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{2}x^2\right)u^2 + \mathcal{O}(u^3) \\
&= 1 + x\,u + \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1)u^2 + \mathcal{O}(u^3)
\end{aligned}$$

The coefficients of $u^l$ are precisely the **Legendre polynomials** $P_l(x)$ generated by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2ux + u^2}} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} u^l P_l(x)$:
- $P_0(x) = 1$
- $P_1(x) = x$
- $P_2(x) = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1)$
- $P_3(x) = \frac{1}{2}(5x^3 - 3x)$

Therefore, for all $r > r'$:

$$\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^l P_l(\cos\alpha)$$

Substituting this uniformly convergent series into the potential integral yields the **exact multipole expansion**:

$$\boxed{V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{l+1}}\int_{\mathcal{V}'} (r')^l P_l(\cos\alpha)\,\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau'}$$

Unpacking the first three physically dominant terms explicitly:

1. **Monopole Term ($l = 0$, scales as $1/r$):**
   $$V_{\text{mono}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}\int \rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau' = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$
   where $Q = \int \rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau'$ is the total net charge.

2. **Dipole Term ($l = 1$, scales as $1/r^2$):** Since $r' P_1(\cos\alpha) = r'\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}' = \hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r}'$:
   $$V_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\int \mathbf{r}'\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau' = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2}$$
   where the **electric dipole moment** is defined as:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{p} \equiv \int_{\mathcal{V}'} \mathbf{r}'\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau' \quad \text{(discrete: }\mathbf{p} = \sum_i q_i \mathbf{r}'_i\text{)}}$$

3. **Quadrupole Term ($l = 2$, scales as $1/r^3$):** Using $r'^2 P_2(\cos\alpha) = \frac{1}{2}\left[3(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r}')^2 - r'^2\right]$ and writing $(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r}')^2 = \sum_{i,j} \hat{r}_i \hat{r}_j x'_i x'_j$ and $r'^2 = r'^2\sum_{i,j}\hat{r}_i\hat{r}_j\delta_{ij}$:
   $$V_{\text{quad}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{2r^3}\sum_{i,j=1}^{3} \hat{r}_i \hat{r}_j Q_{ij}$$
   where the **traceless symmetric electric quadrupole moment tensor** is:
   $$\boxed{Q_{ij} \equiv \int_{\mathcal{V}'} \left(3x'_i x'_j - r'^2\delta_{ij}\right)\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau'}$$
   Notice $Q_{ij} = Q_{ji}$ and $\text{Tr}(Q) = \sum_i Q_{ii} = \int (3r'^2 - 3r'^2)\rho\,d\tau' = 0$, leaving only 5 independent components (matching the $2l+1 = 5$ spherical harmonics for $l=2$).

**Origin-Dependence Theorem:** Shift the coordinate origin by a constant vector $\mathbf{a}$ so every source coordinate transforms as $\bar{\mathbf{r}}' = \mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{a}$. Then the dipole moment transforms as:

$$\bar{\mathbf{p}} = \int (\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{a})\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau' = \mathbf{p} - Q\mathbf{a}$$

Thus $\mathbf{p}$ is independent of the choice of origin if and only if the net charge $Q = 0$. In general, **the lowest non-vanishing multipole moment of any charge distribution is strictly independent of the origin**.

### 2.2 Potential and Electric Field of an Electric Dipole

Consider a physical dipole consisting of $+q$ at $+\mathbf{d}/2$ and $-q$ at $-\mathbf{d}/2$, with $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$. Taking the point-dipole limit $d \to 0$, $q \to \infty$ with $\mathbf{p}$ held constant eliminates all higher multipoles ($l \ge 2$), making the dipole potential exact everywhere outside the origin:

$$V_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2} = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$$

where $\mathbf{p}$ is aligned along the $+z$-axis and $\theta$ is the polar angle.

#### Derivation in Spherical Coordinates
Apply the spherical gradient $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right)$:

$$E_r = -\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\right) = \frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}$$

$$E_\theta = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\right) = \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}$$

$$E_\phi = 0$$

Combining components gives the spherical field expression and magnitude:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{E}_{\text{dip}}(r,\theta) = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\left(2\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right), \qquad |\mathbf{E}_{\text{dip}}| = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\sqrt{3\cos^2\theta + 1}}$$

#### Coordinate-Free Vector Derivation
Write $V_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}}{r^3}$ and differentiate directly using $\nabla(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{p}$ (since $\mathbf{p}$ is constant) and $\nabla(r^{-3}) = -3r^{-4}\hat{\mathbf{r}} = -3\mathbf{r}/r^5$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{E}_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) &= -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{\nabla(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r})}{r^3} + (\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r})\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r^3}\right)\right] \\
&= -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{\mathbf{p}}{r^3} - \frac{3(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r})\mathbf{r}}{r^5}\right]
\end{aligned}$$

Factoring out $r^{-3}$ gives the compact coordinate-free form:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{E}_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\left[3(\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \mathbf{p}\right]}$$

**Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
- **Axial (End-On) Position ($\theta = 0$, $\hat{\mathbf{r}} \parallel \mathbf{p}$):** $\mathbf{E}_{\text{axial}} = +\frac{2\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}$ (parallel to $\mathbf{p}$).
- **Equatorial (Broadside) Position ($\theta = \pi/2$, $\hat{\mathbf{r}} \perp \mathbf{p}$):** $\mathbf{E}_{\text{eq}} = -\frac{\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}$ (antiparallel to $\mathbf{p}$, exactly half the axial magnitude).
- **Dimensional Check:** $[p/(\epsilon_0 r^3)] = (\text{C}\cdot\text{m})/[(\text{C}^2\cdot\text{N}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-2})\cdot\text{m}^3] = \text{N}\cdot\text{C}^{-1}$.

### 2.3 Force, Torque, and Potential Energy of a Dipole in an External Field

Place a small physical dipole ($-q$ at $\mathbf{r}$, $+q$ at $\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{d}$, with $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$) in a smooth external electrostatic field $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ satisfying $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}$.

#### 1. Net Force on the Dipole
The net force is the vector sum of Coulomb forces on the two ends:

$$\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{d}) - q\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$$

Taylor-expand each Cartesian component $E_i(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{d}) = E_i(\mathbf{r}) + (\mathbf{d}\cdot\nabla)E_i(\mathbf{r}) + \mathcal{O}(d^2)$ and take the point-dipole limit $d \to 0$ with $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$ fixed:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{F} = (\mathbf{p} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{E}}$$

*(Equivalently, since $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{p}$ is constant, the vector identity $\nabla(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}) = (\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{p}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})$ immediately yields $\mathbf{F} = \nabla(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E})$!)*
- **Uniform Field Limit:** If $\mathbf{E}$ is spatially uniform, every derivative vanishes and $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$.
- **Inhomogeneous Field:** A dipole aligned parallel to $\mathbf{E}$ is pulled toward regions of *stronger* field magnitude (this explains why a charged comb attracts neutral paper bits via induced dipoles).

#### 2. Torque on the Dipole
Computing torque about the midpoint $\mathbf{r}_c = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{d}/2$, the lever arms of $+q$ and $-q$ are $+\mathbf{d}/2$ and $-\mathbf{d}/2$:

$$\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{center}} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{d}}{2}\right) \times q\mathbf{E}\left(\mathbf{r}_c + \frac{\mathbf{d}}{2}\right) + \left(-\frac{\mathbf{d}}{2}\right) \times (-q)\mathbf{E}\left(\mathbf{r}_c - \frac{\mathbf{d}}{2}\right)$$

Expanding $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}_c \pm \mathbf{d}/2) = \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}_c) \pm \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{d}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E} + \mathcal{O}(d^2)$, the first-order gradient terms cancel identically, leaving:

$$\boxed{\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}}$$

*(About an arbitrary origin located at displacement $-\mathbf{r}_c$ from the dipole center, the total torque includes the orbital term $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{origin}} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{r}_c \times \mathbf{F}$.)*

#### 3. Potential Energy of the Dipole
The electrostatic potential energy of the pair in the external potential $V(\mathbf{r})$ is:

$$U = q V(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{d}) - q V(\mathbf{r}) = q\left[V(\mathbf{r}) + \mathbf{d}\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf{r}) + \mathcal{O}(d^2) - V(\mathbf{r})\right] = (q\mathbf{d})\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf{r})$$

Using $\nabla V = -\mathbf{E}$:

$$\boxed{U = -\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{E} = -pE\cos\theta}$$

**Consistency & Stability Checks:**
- Taking the negative gradient recovers the force: $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = \nabla(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}) = (\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}$.
- Taking the negative angular derivative recovers the restoring torque: $\tau_\theta = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\theta} = -pE\sin\theta$, matching $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{E}$ (tending to decrease $\theta$).
- **Equilibria:** $\theta = 0$ ($\mathbf{p} \parallel \mathbf{E}$) has minimum energy $U_{\min} = -pE$ (**stable equilibrium**; small angular oscillations of a dipole of moment of inertia $I$ execute SHM with frequency $\omega = \sqrt{pE/I}$). $\theta = \pi$ ($\mathbf{p}$ antiparallel to $\mathbf{E}$) has maximum energy $U_{\max} = +pE$ (**unstable equilibrium**).
- **Dipole-Dipole Interaction Energy:** Substituting $\mathbf{E}_1(\mathbf{r})$ of dipole $\mathbf{p}_1$ into $U_{12} = -\mathbf{p}_2 \cdot \mathbf{E}_1(\mathbf{r})$ immediately gives:
  $$U_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\left[\mathbf{p}_1\cdot\mathbf{p}_2 - 3(\mathbf{p}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})(\mathbf{p}_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\right]$$

## 3. Method of Images and Classical Applications

When a point charge is brought near a conductor, mobile electrons redistribute across the conductor surface in a non-uniform layer $\sigma$ whose exact form is unknown beforehand. Computing $V$ by direct integration over $\sigma$ is impossible until $\sigma$ is found.

The **First Uniqueness Theorem** breaks this circularity completely. If we remove the conductor and place one or more fictitious **image charges** strictly *outside* the physical region of interest $\mathcal{V}$ such that:
1. All real charges inside $\mathcal{V}$ remain untouched (so $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ holds identically in $\mathcal{V}$), and
2. The resulting potential reproduces the exact boundary conditions on the conductor surface $\mathcal{S}$ and at infinity,

then the image potential is **guaranteed** to equal the true electrostatic potential everywhere inside $\mathcal{V}$ (and *only* inside $\mathcal{V}$—it is physically meaningless inside the conductor!).

### 3.1 Point Charge Near an Infinite Grounded Conducting Plane

**Coordinate Setup:** Let an infinite grounded conducting plane occupy the $xy$-plane ($z = 0$, $V = 0$), and place a real point charge $+q$ at $(0, 0, d)$ in the upper half-space $\mathcal{V} = \{z > 0\}$.

```
            z
            ^
            |
            *  +q at (0, 0, +d)     <-- Physical region V (z > 0)
            |
    --------+--------  z = 0 (Grounded Conducting Plane, V = 0)
            |
            o  q' = -q at (0, 0, -d) <-- Fictitious Image Region (z < 0)
```

1. **Image Construction & Uniqueness Verification:** Place a single image charge $q' = -q$ at the mirror point $(0, 0, -d)$ inside the excluded region $z < 0$. The trial potential for $z \ge 0$ is:
   $$\boxed{V(x,y,z) = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2}}\right] \qquad (z \ge 0)}$$
   Check all uniqueness criteria in $z > 0$:
   - Only $+q$ lies in $z > 0$, so $\nabla^2 V = -\frac{q}{\epsilon_0}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{r} - d\hat{\mathbf{z}})$ holds everywhere in $z > 0$.
   - On the plane $z = 0$: $V(x, y, 0) = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+d^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+d^2}}\right] = 0$.
   - As $r \to \infty$ in $z \ge 0$: $V \to 0$.
   By the First Uniqueness Theorem, this is the exact potential for $z \ge 0$ (while $V = 0$ identically for $z \le 0$ inside the grounded conductor).

2. **Induced Surface Charge Density $\sigma$:** At the conductor surface ($z = 0^+$, outward normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = +\hat{\mathbf{z}}$), Gauss's pillbox gives $\sigma = \epsilon_0 E_z\big|_{z=0^+} = -\epsilon_0\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0^+}$. Differentiating term-by-term:
   $$\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{-(z - d)}{\left(x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2\right)^{3/2}} + \frac{(z + d)}{\left(x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2\right)^{3/2}}\right]$$
   Evaluating at $z = 0$ with radial cylindrical coordinate $r_\perp = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$:
   $$\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0^+} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2d}{\left(r_\perp^2 + d^2\right)^{3/2}} \implies \boxed{\sigma(r_\perp) = -\frac{qd}{2\pi\left(r_\perp^2 + d^2\right)^{3/2}}}$$

3. **Total Induced Charge Verification:** Integrating over the entire $xy$-plane using $da = 2\pi r_\perp\,dr_\perp$:
   $$Q_{\text{ind}} = \int_0^\infty \left[-\frac{qd}{2\pi(r_\perp^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}\right] 2\pi r_\perp\,dr_\perp = -qd\left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_\perp^2 + d^2}}\right]_0^\infty = -qd\left(0 - \left(-\frac{1}{d}\right)\right) = -q$$
   All electric field lines emanating from $+q$ terminate on the infinite grounded plane.

4. **Force & Interaction Energy (Critical Factor-of-1/2 Check):** Since the field acting on $+q$ from the induced surface charges equals the field produced by the image charge $q' = -q$ at distance $2d$:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{F} = -\frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 d^2}\hat{\mathbf{z}}}$$
   To find the electrostatic interaction energy $W$, compute the work done by an external agent ($\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = -\mathbf{F}$) bringing $+q$ from $z = \infty$ to $z = d$:
   $$W = \int_\infty^d \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\cdot d\mathbf{z}' = \int_\infty^d \frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 z'^2}\,dz' = \left[-\frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 z'}\right]_\infty^d = \boxed{-\frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 d}}$$
   *Why is $W$ half of the two-charge energy $-\frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 d}$?* Because the physical electric field occupies only the half-space $z > 0$ ($\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{z>0} E^2 d\tau = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}} E_{\text{pair}}^2 d\tau$). Equivalently, the external agent moves only the real charge $+q$ while the grounded conductor holds its induced charges at $V = 0$ with zero mechanical work.

### 3.2 Point Charge Near a Grounded Conducting Sphere

**Coordinate Setup:** Center a grounded conducting sphere of radius $R$ at the origin ($V(R,\theta) = 0$). Place a point charge $+q$ on the $+z$-axis at distance $d > R$ from the origin. The physical domain is the exterior $\mathcal{V} = \{r > R\}$.

```
                  .---.
               .-'     '-.
              /     |     \
             |   O  * q'   |-----------* +q
              \  0  b  R  /            d     --> +z
               '-.     .-'
                  '---'
         b = R^2 / d,   q' = -q (R / d)
```

1. **Exact Derivation of Image Charge $q'$ and Position $b$:** By azimuthal symmetry around the $z$-axis, try placing a single image charge $q'$ inside the sphere ($0 < b < R$) on the $+z$-axis at $(0, 0, b)$. At any point $\mathbf{r} = (r,\theta)$ outside the sphere, the trial potential is:
   $$V(r,\theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac{q}{r_1} + \frac{q'}{r_2}\right)$$
   where by the law of cosines:
   $$r_1 = \sqrt{r^2 + d^2 - 2rd\cos\theta}, \qquad r_2 = \sqrt{r^2 + b^2 - 2rb\cos\theta}$$
   Impose the Dirichlet boundary condition $V(R,\theta) = 0$ for **all** polar angles $\theta \in [0,\pi]$:
   $$\frac{q}{\sqrt{R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta}} + \frac{q'}{\sqrt{R^2 + b^2 - 2Rb\cos\theta}} = 0 \implies q'\sqrt{R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta} = -q\sqrt{R^2 + b^2 - 2Rb\cos\theta}$$
   Factor $d/R$ out of the first square root so the coefficient of $\cos\theta$ inside both radicals can be matched term-by-term:
   $$\sqrt{R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta} = \frac{d}{R}\sqrt{\frac{R^4}{d^2} + R^2 - 2R\left(\frac{R^2}{d}\right)\cos\theta}$$
   Comparing the radicands term-by-term for arbitrary $\cos\theta$ uniquely forces:
   $$\boxed{b = \frac{R^2}{d}, \qquad q' = -\frac{R}{d}q}$$
   Check: Since $d > R$, we have $b = R(R/d) < R$, confirming $q'$ lies strictly *inside* the sphere (outside $\mathcal{V}$), and $V \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$. By Uniqueness Theorem I, the exact exterior potential is:
   $$\boxed{V(r,\theta) = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + d^2 - 2rd\cos\theta}} - \frac{R/d}{\sqrt{r^2 + (R^2/d)^2 - 2r(R^2/d)\cos\theta}}\right] \quad (r \ge R)}$$

2. **Induced Surface Charge Density $\sigma(\theta)$:** Compute $\sigma(\theta) = -\epsilon_0\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R}$ explicitly:
   $$\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[-\frac{r - d\cos\theta}{\left(r^2 + d^2 - 2rd\cos\theta\right)^{3/2}} + \frac{R}{d}\frac{r - b\cos\theta}{\left(r^2 + b^2 - 2rb\cos\theta\right)^{3/2}}\right]$$
   At $r = R$, substitute $b = R^2/d$ and use the identity derived above:
   $$\sqrt{R^2 + b^2 - 2Rb\cos\theta} = \frac{R}{d}\sqrt{R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta}$$
   Let $D(\theta) \equiv \sqrt{R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta}$. Then at $r = R$:
   $$\begin{aligned}
   \left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R} &= \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[-\frac{R - d\cos\theta}{D(\theta)^3} + \frac{R}{d}\frac{R - (R^2/d)\cos\theta}{(R/d)^3 D(\theta)^3}\right] \\
   &= \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 D(\theta)^3}\left[-(R - d\cos\theta) + \frac{d^2}{R^2}\left(R - \frac{R^2}{d}\cos\theta\right)\right] \\
   &= \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 D(\theta)^3}\left[-R + d\cos\theta + \frac{d^2}{R} - d\cos\theta\right] = \frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R\,D(\theta)^3}
   \end{aligned}$$
   The $\cos\theta$ terms in the numerator cancel cleanly! Multiplying by $-\epsilon_0$ yields:
   $$\boxed{\sigma(\theta) = -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4\pi R\left(R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta\right)^{3/2}}}$$

3. **Total Induced Charge Verification:** Integrate over the sphere ($da = 2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta$). Let $u = R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta$, so $du = 2Rd\sin\theta\,d\theta$, with $u(0) = (d-R)^2$ and $u(\pi) = (d+R)^2$:
   $$\begin{aligned}
   Q_{\text{ind}} &= -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4\pi R}\int_0^\pi \frac{2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta}{\left(R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta\right)^{3/2}} = -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4d}\int_{(d-R)^2}^{(d+R)^2} u^{-3/2}\,du \\
   &= -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4d}\left[-\frac{2}{\sqrt{u}}\right]_{(d-R)^2}^{(d+R)^2} = -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{2d}\left(\frac{1}{d - R} - \frac{1}{d + R}\right) = -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{2d}\cdot\frac{2R}{d^2 - R^2} = -\frac{R}{d}q = q'
   \end{aligned}$$
   *(This also follows immediately from Gauss's law on a sphere of radius $R^+$, since the exterior field matches the field of $q'$ inside!)*

4. **Force on $+q$:** Distance from $q'$ at $b = R^2/d$ to $q$ at $d$ is $d - b = \frac{d^2 - R^2}{d}$. Therefore:
   $$\mathbf{F} = \frac{q q'}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (d - b)^2}\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \frac{q(-qR/d)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (d^2 - R^2)^2 / d^2}\hat{\mathbf{z}} \implies \boxed{\mathbf{F}_{\text{gnd}} = -\frac{q^2 R d}{4\pi\epsilon_0 \left(d^2 - R^2\right)^2}\hat{\mathbf{z}}}$$

5. **Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
   - **Infinite-Plane Limit ($d = R + h$, $h \ll R$, $R \to \infty$):** $b = \frac{R^2}{R+h} \approx R - h$ (distance $h$ behind surface), $q' = -\frac{R}{R+h}q \approx -q$, $d^2 - R^2 = h(2R + h) \approx 2Rh$, so:
     $$F_{\text{gnd}} \approx -\frac{q^2 R^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (2Rh)^2} = -\frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 h^2}$$
     exact recovery of the grounded plane!
   - **Far-Field Limit ($d \gg R$):** $F_{\text{gnd}} \approx -\frac{q^2 R}{4\pi\epsilon_0 d^3}$ (attractive $1/d^3$ force from monopole-induced-charge interaction).

### 3.3 Extension to an Insulated (Isolated) Conducting Sphere

Suppose the conducting sphere is **insulated** rather than grounded, carrying a fixed total net charge $Q$ (for a neutral insulated sphere, $Q = 0$). Now the surface must remain an **equipotential** $V(R,\theta) = V_0 = \text{const}$ (not zero!), and the total surface charge must equal $Q$.

**Superposition Strategy (Licensed by Uniqueness Theorem II):**
1. Keep the first image charge $q' = -qR/d$ at $b = R^2/d$. Together with $+q$, this already makes $V = 0$ everywhere on $r = R$, inducing charge $q'$.
2. Place a **second image charge** $q''$ right at the **center of the sphere** ($r = 0$). A point charge at the origin contributes a strictly angle-independent constant potential $\frac{q''}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R}$ over the sphere surface $r = R$, preserving equipotentiality!
3. By Gauss's law just outside $r = R$, the total charge on the sphere equals the sum of all image charges enclosed inside $r = R$:
   $$Q = q' + q'' \implies \boxed{q'' = Q - q' = Q + \frac{R}{d}q \quad \text{at } r = 0}$$
   *(Alternatively, if the sphere is held at a fixed battery potential $V_0$, set $\frac{q''}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} = V_0 \implies q'' = 4\pi\epsilon_0 R V_0$.)*

**Exact Potential, Equipotential Value, Surface Charge & Force:**
- **Sphere potential:** $V(R) = \frac{q''}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} + \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 d}$ (for a neutral sphere $Q = 0$, the sphere floats at the exact potential produced by $+q$ at the sphere's center!).
- **Surface charge density:** Adding the uniform density $\frac{q''}{4\pi R^2}$ from $q''$ to the grounded density gives:
  $$\sigma_{\text{ins}}(\theta) = \frac{Q + qR/d}{4\pi R^2} - \frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4\pi R\left(R^2 + d^2 - 2Rd\cos\theta\right)^{3/2}}$$
- **Force on $+q$:** Superposing the forces from $q'$ at $b = R^2/d$ and $q''$ at $r = 0$:
  $$\boxed{\mathbf{F}_{\text{ins}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{Q + qR/d}{d^2} - \frac{q R d}{\left(d^2 - R^2\right)^2}\right]\hat{\mathbf{z}}}$$
- **Neutral Insulated Sphere ($Q = 0$):** Combining fractions gives a strictly **attractive** force decaying as $1/d^5$ at large distances (induced dipole attraction!):
  $$F_{\text{neut}} = -\frac{q^2 R^3\left(2d^2 - R^2\right)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 d^3\left(d^2 - R^2\right)^2} \xrightarrow{d \gg R} -\frac{2q^2 R^3}{4\pi\epsilon_0 d^5}$$
- **Short-Range Attraction Paradox ($qQ > 0$):** Even when $q$ and $Q$ have the **same sign**, as $d \to R^+$ the second term $-\frac{q^2 Rd}{(d^2 - R^2)^2}$ diverges negatively as $-(d-R)^{-2}$, overpowering the finite repulsion $+\frac{q(Q+q)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}$! Induced polarization near the closest cap always creates net attraction at sufficiently small gap distance.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Vertical Dipole Above a Grounded Plane & Grounded vs. Insulated Sphere
> **Problem:**
> *(a)* A vertically oriented point electric dipole $\mathbf{p} = p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ ($p = 2.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}$) is held at height $h = 5.0\text{ cm}$ above an infinite grounded conducting plate at $z = 0$. Using the method of images, derive the exact force between the dipole and the plate and compute its numerical value.
> *(b)* A point charge $q = +2.0\,\mu\text{C}$ is placed $d = 30\text{ cm}$ from the center of a conducting sphere of radius $R = 10\text{ cm}$. Calculate the image charges, maximum and minimum induced surface charge density (grounded case), and the force on $q$ when the sphere is (i) grounded and (ii) insulated and electrically neutral.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part (a) (Vertical Dipole + Grounded Plane):**
> 1. Model the real dipole as $+q$ at $z = h + \delta/2$ and $-q$ at $z = h - \delta/2$, with $p = q\delta$ as $\delta \to 0$.
> 2. Reflect each charge across $z = 0$ with opposite sign:
>    - Image of $+q$ at $+(h + \delta/2)$ is $-q$ at $-(h + \delta/2)$.
>    - Image of $-q$ at $+(h - \delta/2)$ is $+q$ at $-(h - \delta/2)$.
>    In the image dipole, $+q$ sits *above* $-q$ (higher $z$), so the image dipole points **parallel** to the real dipole: $\mathbf{p}' = +p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ at $(0, 0, -h)$.
>    *(Check $V(z=0)$: On $z = 0$, the radial vector from real dipole has $z$-component $-h$ while from image dipole has $+h$; equal distances yield $V \propto (-ph + ph) = 0$ identically.)*
> 3. Field of the image dipole $\mathbf{p}' = p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ along the $+z$-axis at coordinate $z > 0$ (axial distance $z + h$):
>    $$E'_z(z) = \frac{2p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (z + h)^3}$$
> 4. Force on real dipole $\mathbf{p} = p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ at $z = h$:
>    $$F_z = (\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla)E'_z\Big|_{z=h} = p\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\frac{2p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (z + h)^3}\right]\right|_{z=h} = p\left[\frac{-6p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (z + h)^4}\right]_{z=h} = -\frac{6p^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (2h)^4}$$
>    Simplifying $4 \times 16 = 64$ and $6/64 = 3/32$:
>    $$\boxed{\mathbf{F} = -\frac{3p^2}{32\pi\epsilon_0 h^4}\hat{\mathbf{z}} \quad \text{(attractive toward the plate)}}$$
> 5. **Numeric Substitution:** With $p = 2.0\times 10^{-8}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}$, $h = 5.0\times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$, $\frac{3}{32\pi\epsilon_0} = \frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right)$:
>    $$|F_z| = \frac{3}{8}\left(8.988\times 10^9\right)\frac{(2.0\times 10^{-8})^2}{(5.0\times 10^{-2})^4} = \frac{3}{8}(8.988\times 10^9)\frac{4.0\times 10^{-16}}{6.25\times 10^{-6}} = \boxed{0.216\text{ N (downward)}}$$
>
> **Part (b) (Conducting Sphere, $R = 0.10\text{ m}$, $d = 0.30\text{ m}$, $q = 2.0\times 10^{-6}\text{ C}$):**
> 1. **Grounded Sphere:**
>    $$b = \frac{R^2}{d} = \frac{(0.10)^2}{0.30} = \boxed{3.33\text{ cm}}, \qquad q' = -\frac{R}{d}q = -\frac{1}{3}(2.0\,\mu\text{C}) = \boxed{-0.667\,\mu\text{C}}$$
>    Force on $q$:
>    $$F_{\text{gnd}} = -\frac{q^2 R d}{4\pi\epsilon_0(d^2 - R^2)^2} = -8.988\times 10^9 \times \frac{(2.0\times 10^{-6})^2(0.10)(0.30)}{(0.09 - 0.01)^2} = \boxed{-0.169\text{ N (attractive)}}$$
>    Induced surface charge density extrema (nearest cap $\theta = 0$, $D(0) = d - R = 0.20\text{ m}$; farthest cap $\theta = \pi$, $D(\pi) = d + R = 0.40\text{ m}$):
>    $$\sigma(0) = -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4\pi R(d - R)^3} = -\frac{(2.0\times 10^{-6})(0.08)}{4\pi(0.10)(0.20)^3} = \boxed{-15.9\,\mu\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}}$$
>    $$\sigma(\pi) = -\frac{q(d^2 - R^2)}{4\pi R(d + R)^3} = \frac{\sigma(0)}{8} = \boxed{-1.99\,\mu\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}}$$
> 2. **Insulated Neutral Sphere ($Q = 0$):**
>    Add second image $q'' = -q' = \boxed{+0.667\,\mu\text{C}}$ at $r = 0$. Its repulsive contribution at $d = 0.30\text{ m}$ is:
>    $$F'' = +\frac{q q''}{4\pi\epsilon_0 d^2} = +8.988\times 10^9 \times \frac{(2.0\times 10^{-6})(0.6667\times 10^{-6})}{(0.30)^2} = +0.1332\text{ N}$$
>    Net force:
>    $$F_{\text{neut}} = F_{\text{gnd}} + F'' = -0.1685 + 0.1332 = \boxed{-0.0354\text{ N (attractive)}}$$

## 4. Dielectrics, Polarization, Electric Displacement and Clausius-Mossotti

Unlike conductors, dielectrics possess no freely mobile charge carriers; electrons remain tethered to parent nuclei or molecules. When subjected to an electric field, however, every molecule undergoes tiny local charge displacement—either stretching electron clouds relative to nuclei (**electronic/induced polarization**) or twisting permanent molecular dipoles into partial alignment (**orientational polarization**).

Macroscopically, these billions of microscopic dipoles are smeared into a smooth vector field: the **Polarization** $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r})$, defined as electric dipole moment per unit volume (SI unit: $\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}$):

$$\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}) \equiv \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{d\tau}$$

### 4.1 Bound Volume and Surface Charges

**Unabridged Vector-Calculus Derivation:** Consider a polarized dielectric body of volume $\mathcal{V}$ bounded by closed surface $\mathcal{S}$. An infinitesimal volume element $d\tau'$ at $\mathbf{r}'$ carries dipole moment $d\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}')\,d\tau'$. Letting $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}} \equiv \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'$ and $\mathcal{R} = |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|$, the electrostatic potential at an arbitrary field point $\mathbf{r}$ is:

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}') \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}{\mathcal{R}^2}\,d\tau'$$

Note the gradient with respect to **source coordinates** $\mathbf{r}'$:

$$\nabla'\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\right) = \nabla'\left[\left((x-x')^2 + (y-y')^2 + (z-z')^2\right)^{-1/2}\right] = +\frac{\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'}{\mathcal{R}^3} = +\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}{\mathcal{R}^2}$$

Substitute this identity into the integral and apply the vector product rule $\nabla'\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbf{P}}{\mathcal{R}}\right) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}(\nabla'\cdot\mathbf{P}) + \mathbf{P}\cdot\nabla'\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\right)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
V(\mathbf{r}) &= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}') \cdot \nabla'\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}}\right) d\tau' \\
&= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \nabla'\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}')}{\mathcal{R}}\right) d\tau' - \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{\nabla'\cdot\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}')}{\mathcal{R}}\,d\tau'
\end{aligned}$$

Convert the first volume integral to a surface integral via the Divergence Theorem ($\hat{\mathbf{n}}'$ is the outward unit normal to $\mathcal{S}$):

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\oint_{\mathcal{S}} \frac{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}')\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}'}{\mathcal{R}}\,da' + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{-\nabla'\cdot\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}')}{\mathcal{R}}\,d\tau'$$

This potential is mathematically indistinguishable from the potential produced in vacuum by an effective **bound surface charge density** $\sigma_b$ and **bound volume charge density** $\rho_b$:

$$\boxed{\sigma_b = \mathbf{P} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}, \qquad \rho_b = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{P}}$$

**Physical Interpretation & Conservation Check:**
- At the outer boundary, the positive heads (or negative tails) of aligned dipole chains stick out uncanceled, leaving surface charge $\sigma_b = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$.
- Inside the bulk, whenever $\mathbf{P}$ is non-uniform, more charge flows *out* of a small box than *in* if $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P} > 0$, leaving a net deficit $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$.
- **Total Bound Charge Conservation:** Since the dielectric started electrically neutral, total bound charge must vanish identically. Applying the Divergence Theorem confirms this immediately:
  $$Q_b = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \sigma_b\,da + \int_{\mathcal{V}} \rho_b\,d\tau = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{P}\cdot d\mathbf{a} - \int_{\mathcal{V}} (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P})\,d\tau = 0$$

### 4.2 Electric Displacement $\mathbf{D}$, Linear Dielectrics, and Boundary Conditions

Inside a dielectric, the total charge density splits into external **free charge** $\rho_f$ (controllable charges deposited on plates or embedded ions) and material-response **bound charge** $\rho_b$:

$$\rho = \rho_f + \rho_b = \rho_f - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$$

Substitute into Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho/\epsilon_0$:

$$\epsilon_0 \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho_f - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{P} \implies \nabla\cdot\left(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}\right) = \rho_f$$

Define the **Electric Displacement Field** $\mathbf{D}$ (SI unit: $\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}$):

$$\boxed{\mathbf{D} \equiv \epsilon_0 \mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}, \qquad \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_f, \qquad \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{a} = Q_{f,\text{enc}}}$$

**Critical Conceptual Warning:** While $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f$ mirrors Gauss's law for free charge, the curl of $\mathbf{D}$ does **not** generally vanish:
$$\nabla \times \mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) + \nabla\times\mathbf{P} = \nabla\times\mathbf{P} \neq \mathbf{0}$$
Therefore, there is *no* general "Coulomb's law for $\mathbf{D}$" determined purely by $\rho_f$! $\mathbf{D}$ can be extracted directly from $Q_{f,\text{enc}}$ via symmetry only when the geometry forces $\nabla\times\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{0}$ (e.g., spherical, cylindrical, or infinite planar symmetry where boundaries align with equipotentials).

#### Linear, Isotropic, Homogeneous (LIH) Dielectrics
For modest fields in isotropic non-ferroelectric media, polarization is directly proportional to the total macroscopic field $\mathbf{E}$:

$$\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \mathbf{E}$$

where $\chi_e$ is the dimensionless **electric susceptibility**. Consequently:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)\mathbf{E} = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r \mathbf{E} = \epsilon \mathbf{E}}$$

where $\epsilon_r \equiv 1 + \chi_e$ is the **relative permittivity** (dielectric constant, often written $K$) and $\epsilon = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r$ is the absolute permittivity. Note that inside a homogeneous LIH dielectric, $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}) = -\frac{\chi_e}{1+\chi_e}\rho_f$: in any free-charge-free bulk ($\rho_f = 0$), **$\rho_b = 0$ identically**, so bound charge resides exclusively on boundaries! The electrostatic energy density in a linear dielectric generalizes to $u_e = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon E^2$.

#### Electrostatic Boundary Conditions Across a Dielectric Interface
Consider an interface separating linear dielectric Medium 1 ($\epsilon_1$) and Medium 2 ($\epsilon_2$), carrying free surface charge density $\sigma_f$, with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ pointing **from 1 into 2**:

1. **Normal Component of $\mathbf{D}$ (Gaussian Pillbox):** Construct a wafer-thin pillbox of face area $\Delta A$ and height $h \to 0$ straddling the interface. Applying $\oint \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{a} = Q_{f,\text{enc}}$ gives $(D_{2\perp} - D_{1\perp})\Delta A = \sigma_f\Delta A$:
   $$\boxed{D_{2\perp} - D_{1\perp} = \sigma_f \iff \epsilon_2 E_{2\perp} - \epsilon_1 E_{1\perp} = \sigma_f \iff \epsilon_2\frac{\partial V_2}{\partial n} - \epsilon_1\frac{\partial V_1}{\partial n} = -\sigma_f}$$
   *(In terms of total charge, $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{a}$ gives $E_{2\perp} - E_{1\perp} = \frac{\sigma_f + \sigma_b}{\epsilon_0}$.)*

2. **Tangential Component of $\mathbf{E}$ (Rectangular Loop):** Construct a narrow rectangular loop of length $L$ parallel to the interface and width $h \to 0$ across it. Applying $\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ gives $(E_{2\parallel} - E_{1\parallel})L = 0$:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{E}_{2\parallel} - \mathbf{E}_{1\parallel} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad V_2\big|_{\text{interface}} = V_1\big|_{\text{interface}}}$$
   *(Equivalently, $\frac{\mathbf{D}_{2\parallel}}{\epsilon_2} = \frac{\mathbf{D}_{1\parallel}}{\epsilon_1}$ — tangential $\mathbf{D}$ is discontinuous across a dielectric boundary!)*

3. **Law of Refraction of Electric Field Lines ($\sigma_f = 0$):** Let $\mathbf{E}_1$ and $\mathbf{E}_2$ make angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ with the interface normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$. Dividing $E_1\sin\theta_1 = E_2\sin\theta_2$ by $\epsilon_1 E_1\cos\theta_1 = \epsilon_2 E_2\cos\theta_2$ yields:
   $$\boxed{\frac{\tan\theta_1}{\tan\theta_2} = \frac{\epsilon_1}{\epsilon_2}}$$
   Field lines bend *away* from the normal upon entering a higher-permittivity medium ($\epsilon_2 > \epsilon_1 \implies \theta_2 > \theta_1$).

### 4.3 Lorentz Local Field and the Clausius-Mossotti Relation

The macroscopic field $\mathbf{E}$ inside a dielectric is a spatial average over volumes containing thousands of molecules. An individual molecule at a lattice site does *not* experience $\mathbf{E}$ directly, because the molecule cannot polarize itself—we must exclude its own singular dipole field while retaining the fields of all neighboring dipoles.

**Physical Assumptions & Lorentz Virtual Cavity Setup:** Consider a non-polar isotropic dielectric (or cubic crystal) with uniform macroscopic field $\mathbf{E}$ and uniform polarization $\mathbf{P} = P\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Around a chosen target molecule at origin $O$, imagine carving out a fictitious **mesoscopic Lorentz sphere** of radius $a$ much larger than the interatomic spacing ($a \gg d_{\text{atom}}$) yet much smaller than macroscopic sample dimensions ($a \ll L$).

```
         E_macro = E (uniform upward along +z)
               .-------.
            .-' - - - - '-.   <-- Upper inner wall: n_out = -r_hat
           /  -         -  \      sigma_b = P . (-r_hat) = -P cos(theta) < 0
          |                 |
          |        O        |  <-- Field at O from cavity wall:
          |    (molecule)   |      E_cav = +P / (3 epsilon_0)  (upward!)
           \  +         +  /
            '-. + + + + .-'   <-- Lower inner wall: sigma_b > 0
               '-------'
```

Decompose the actual **local field** $\mathbf{E}_{\text{loc}}$ acting on the target molecule into four physical pieces:

$$\mathbf{E}_{\text{loc}} = \mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}} + \mathbf{E}_{\text{outer}} + \mathbf{E}_{\text{cav}} + \mathbf{E}_{\text{near}}$$

1. **Macroscopic Field ($\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}} + \mathbf{E}_{\text{outer}}$):** The sum of the applied external field $\mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}}$ and the depolarizing field $\mathbf{E}_{\text{outer}}$ from bound charges on the *outer* boundary of the sample equals the macroscopic field $\mathbf{E}$ everywhere inside the continuum—*except* that carving out the Lorentz sphere removes the continuum inside $r < a$ and exposes bound charge on the inner cavity surface!
   *(Equivalently: $\mathbf{E}_{\text{macro}} = \mathbf{E}_{\text{outside cavity}} + \mathbf{E}_{\text{inside continuum sphere}}$, so removing the continuum sphere adds $-\mathbf{E}_{\text{sphere}} = +\frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\epsilon_0}$, which equals the surface-charge field $\mathbf{E}_{\text{cav}}$ calculated directly below.)*

2. **Lorentz Cavity Surface Field ($\mathbf{E}_{\text{cav}}$):** On the inner spherical wall of radius $a$, the outward normal of the *dielectric* points *inward* toward the center $O$: $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Hence the bound surface charge density on the cavity wall is:
   $$\sigma_b(\theta) = \mathbf{P}\cdot(-\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = -P\cos\theta$$
   Negative bound charge coats the upper hemisphere ($\theta < \pi/2$), and positive bound charge coats the lower hemisphere ($\theta > \pi/2$), producing an upward ($+\hat{\mathbf{z}}$) field at center $O$. By azimuthal symmetry, horizontal components cancel and $E_{\text{cav},z}$ integrates cleanly over spherical rings of area $da = 2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta$:
   $$\begin{aligned}
   E_{\text{cav},z} &= \int_0^\pi \frac{(-\sigma_b(\theta))\,da}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a^2}\cos\theta = \int_0^\pi \frac{(P\cos\theta)(2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta)}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a^2}\cos\theta \\
   &= \frac{P}{2\epsilon_0}\int_0^\pi \cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{P}{2\epsilon_0}\left[-\frac{\cos^3\theta}{3}\right]_0^\pi = \frac{P}{2\epsilon_0}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{P}{3\epsilon_0}
   \end{aligned}$$
   In vector form:
   $$\mathbf{E}_{\text{cav}} = +\frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\epsilon_0}$$

3. **Near-Dipole Field ($\mathbf{E}_{\text{near}}$):** We must restore the discrete individual dipoles located inside the Lorentz sphere (excluding the molecule at $O$ itself). For dipole $\mathbf{p}_k = p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ at position $(x_k, y_k, z_k)$ relative to $O$, its $z$-field at $O$ is $E_{z,k} = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3z_k^2 - r_k^2}{r_k^5}$. Summing over all sites in a **simple cubic, BCC, or FCC lattice** (or averaging over a completely random isotropic gas/liquid), cubic symmetry dictates:
   $$\sum_k \frac{x_k^2}{r_k^5} = \sum_k \frac{y_k^2}{r_k^5} = \sum_k \frac{z_k^2}{r_k^5} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_k \frac{x_k^2 + y_k^2 + z_k^2}{r_k^5} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_k \frac{r_k^2}{r_k^5}$$
   Consequently:
   $$E_{\text{near},z} = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_k \frac{3z_k^2 - r_k^2}{r_k^5} = 0 \implies \mathbf{E}_{\text{near}} = \mathbf{0}$$

Combining all contributions produces the **Lorentz Local Field**:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{E}_{\text{loc}} = \mathbf{E} + \frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\epsilon_0}}$$

#### Derivation of the Clausius-Mossotti Equation
Let $N$ be the number of molecules per unit volume, each acquiring an induced dipole moment $\mathbf{p} = \alpha \mathbf{E}_{\text{loc}}$, where $\alpha$ is the **molecular polarizability** (SI unit: $\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\text{V}^{-1} = \text{F}\cdot\text{m}^2$). Then:

$$\mathbf{P} = N\mathbf{p} = N\alpha \mathbf{E}_{\text{loc}} = N\alpha\left(\mathbf{E} + \frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\epsilon_0}\right)$$

Solve algebraically for $\mathbf{P}$ in terms of $\mathbf{E}$:

$$\mathbf{P}\left(1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}\right) = N\alpha \mathbf{E} \implies \mathbf{P} = \frac{N\alpha}{1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}}\mathbf{E}$$

Match this against the macroscopic constitutive relation $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0(\epsilon_r - 1)\mathbf{E}$:

$$\epsilon_r - 1 = \frac{N\alpha/\epsilon_0}{1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}}$$

Now add $3$ to both sides (or invert algebraically) to isolate $\frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}$:

$$\epsilon_r + 2 = 3 + \frac{N\alpha/\epsilon_0}{1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}} = \frac{3\left(1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}\right) + \frac{N\alpha}{\epsilon_0}}{1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}} = \frac{3}{1 - \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}}$$

Dividing $\epsilon_r - 1$ by $\epsilon_r + 2$ cancels the denominator cleanly, giving the **Clausius-Mossotti Equation**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2} = \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}}$$

*(Notation Check: Many textbooks define polarizability volume $\alpha_{\text{vol}} \equiv \frac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$ with units of $\text{m}^3$, in which case $\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2} = \frac{4\pi}{3}N\alpha_{\text{vol}}$. Always state your SI definition of $\alpha$ explicitly!)*

**Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
- **Dilute Gas Limit ($N\alpha/\epsilon_0 \ll 1$):** $\epsilon_r \approx 1$, so $\epsilon_r + 2 \approx 3$ and $\epsilon_r - 1 \approx \frac{N\alpha}{\epsilon_0}$ (local-field correction disappears when molecules are far apart).
- **Polarization Catastrophe ($\frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0} \to 1^-$):** $\epsilon_r \to \infty$, signaling spontaneous polarization even at zero external field—the microscopic seed of ferroelectricity.
- **Optical Frequencies ($\epsilon_r = n^2$):** Replacing $\epsilon_r$ by the squared refractive index $n^2$ converts Clausius-Mossotti directly into the **Lorentz-Lorenz equation**: $\frac{n^2 - 1}{n^2 + 2} = \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Molecular Polarizability via Clausius-Mossotti
> **Problem:** Liquid carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$, nonpolar) at $20^\circ\text{C}$ has relative permittivity $\epsilon_r = 2.24$, mass density $\rho_m = 1.594 \times 10^3\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$, and molar mass $M = 153.82\text{ g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$. Using the Clausius-Mossotti relation, calculate (a) the SI molecular polarizability $\alpha$ and (b) the polarizability volume $\alpha_{\text{vol}} = \alpha/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ in $\text{\AA}^3$.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Molecular Number Density $N$:** Convert $M = 0.15382\text{ kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ and use $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}$:
>    $$N = \frac{\rho_m N_A}{M} = \frac{(1.594 \times 10^3\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3})(6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1})}{0.15382\text{ kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = 6.241 \times 10^{27}\text{ m}^{-3}$$
> 2. **Clausius-Mossotti Factor:**
>    $$\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2} = \frac{2.24 - 1}{2.24 + 2} = \frac{1.24}{4.24} = 0.2925$$
> 3. **SI Molecular Polarizability $\alpha$:**
>    $$\alpha = \frac{3\epsilon_0}{N}\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right) = \frac{3(8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F}\cdot\text{m}^{-1})}{6.241 \times 10^{27}\text{ m}^{-3}}(0.2925) = \boxed{1.24 \times 10^{-39}\text{ F}\cdot\text{m}^2}$$

>    $$\alpha_{\text{vol}} = \frac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = \frac{1.245 \times 10^{-39}}{1.1127 \times 10^{-10}} = 1.12 \times 10^{-29}\text{ m}^3 = \boxed{11.2\,\text{\AA}^3}$$
>    *(Sanity check: typical small-molecule polarizability volumes lie in the $1\text{–}15\,\text{\AA}^3$ range, matching the physical molecular volume of $\text{CCl}_4$.)*

## 5. Classical Spherical Boundary-Value Problems

When a spherical object—conducting or dielectric—is immersed in a uniform external electric field $\mathbf{E}_0 = E_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, rotational symmetry around the $z$-axis guarantees that the potential $V(r,\theta)$ is independent of the azimuthal angle $\phi$. In every charge-free region ($\rho_f = 0$ and homogeneous medium), $V$ obeys Laplace's equation $\nabla^2 V = 0$.

### 5.1 General Azimuthally Symmetric Solution of Laplace's Equation

In spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$ with $\frac{\partial V}{\partial \phi} = 0$, Laplace's equation reads:

$$\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\right) = 0$$

Assume a separable product solution $V(r,\theta) = R(r)\Theta(\theta)$. Substituting into Laplace's equation and multiplying by $\frac{r^2}{R(r)\Theta(\theta)}$ separates the variables cleanly:

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = -\frac{1}{\Theta\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) = \lambda$$

where $\lambda$ is a separation constant. For regular solutions across the entire polar interval $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ (specifically avoiding logarithmic blow-up at the north and south poles $\theta = 0,\pi$), $\lambda$ must take the quantized eigenvalues $\lambda = l(l + 1)$ for non-negative integers $l = 0, 1, 2, \dots$:

1. **Radial Euler-Cauchy Equation:**
   $$r^2\frac{d^2 R}{dr^2} + 2r\frac{dR}{dr} - l(l+1)R = 0$$
   Trying $R(r) = r^k$ yields $k(k-1) + 2k - l(l+1) = (k - l)(k + l + 1) = 0 \implies k = l$ or $k = -(l+1)$. Thus:
   $$R_l(r) = A_l r^l + \frac{B_l}{r^{l+1}}$$

2. **Angular Legendre Equation:** Substituting $x = \cos\theta$ ($\sin\theta\frac{d}{d\theta} = -(1-x^2)\frac{d}{dx}$):
   $$\frac{d}{dx}\left[(1 - x^2)\frac{d\Theta}{dx}\right] + l(l+1)\Theta = 0$$
   The linearly independent solutions are the Legendre polynomials $P_l(\cos\theta)$ and the second-kind functions $Q_l(\cos\theta)$. Since $Q_l(\cos\theta)$ diverges logarithmically at $\theta = 0,\pi$, physical finiteness along the $z$-axis forces us to discard $Q_l$ completely.

By linearity and completeness of $\{P_l(\cos\theta)\}$, the **most general azimuthally symmetric solution** to $\nabla^2 V = 0$ is:

$$\boxed{V(r,\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left(A_l r^l + \frac{B_l}{r^{l+1}}\right)P_l(\cos\theta)}$$

Coefficients $A_l, B_l$ are extracted uniquely via **Legendre polynomial orthogonality**:

$$\int_{-1}^{1} P_l(x) P_{l'}(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} P_l(\cos\theta) P_{l'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2}{2l + 1}\delta_{ll'}$$

### 5.2 BVP 1 - Conducting Sphere of Radius $R$ in a Uniform Electric Field $\mathbf{E}_0$

**Physical Story:** Place an uncharged (or grounded) conducting sphere of radius $R$ at the origin inside an initially uniform field $\mathbf{E}_0 = E_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Free electrons drift toward the $-z$ hemisphere, exposing positive ionic charge on the $+z$ hemisphere. Charge migration stops the instant the induced surface dipole field cancels $\mathbf{E}_0$ completely inside the conductor ($\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = \mathbf{0}$), turning the sphere into an equipotential whose exterior field lines must strike the surface at $90^\circ$.

**Boundary Conditions for the Exterior Region ($r \ge R$):**
1. **Conductor Equipotential Surface ($r = R$):** For a grounded sphere, $V(R,\theta) = 0$. *(For an isolated neutral sphere, odd reflection symmetry $z \to -z$ requires the induced charge to be antisymmetric around the equator, forcing the sphere's constant potential $V_0$ to equal the unperturbed potential at the origin, $V_0 = -E_0(0) = 0$ as well!)* Thus:
   $$V(R, \theta) = 0 \quad \forall\,\theta \in [0,\pi]$$
2. **Asymptotic Matching ($r \to \infty$):** Far from the sphere, the induced disturbance fades out and $\mathbf{E} \to E_0\hat{\mathbf{z}} = -\nabla(-E_0 z)$, so:
   $$V(r,\theta) \xrightarrow{r \to \infty} -E_0 z = -E_0 r\cos\theta = -E_0 r P_1(\cos\theta)$$

**Unabridged Coefficient Determination (Zero Skipped Steps):**
Start with the general expansion outside the sphere ($r \ge R$):

$$V(r,\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left(A_l r^l + \frac{B_l}{r^{l+1}}\right)P_l(\cos\theta)$$

1. **Apply Asymptotic Condition ($r \to \infty$):** As $r \to \infty$, all $B_l/r^{l+1}$ terms vanish, leaving $\sum_{l=0}^\infty A_l r^l P_l(\cos\theta) \to -E_0 r P_1(\cos\theta)$. Since powers $r^l$ and orthogonal polynomials $P_l(\cos\theta)$ are linearly independent:
   - For $l \ge 2$: $A_l = 0$ (otherwise $V$ grows faster than $r$).
   - For $l = 1$: $A_1 = -E_0$.
   - For $l = 0$: $A_0 = 0$ (fixes the zero of potential at the equatorial plane $z = 0$).

   The potential narrows to:
   $$V(r,\theta) = -E_0 r P_1(\cos\theta) + \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_l}{r^{l+1}}P_l(\cos\theta)$$

2. **Apply Boundary Condition at $r = R$:** Setting $V(R,\theta) = 0$ yields:
   $$\left(-E_0 R + \frac{B_1}{R^2}\right)P_1(\cos\theta) + \sum_{\substack{l=0 \\ l \neq 1}}^{\infty}\frac{B_l}{R^{l+1}}P_l(\cos\theta) = 0 \qquad \forall\,\theta \in [0,\pi]$$
   Multiply both sides by $P_{l'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta$ and integrate from $\theta = 0$ to $\pi$. Using $\int_0^\pi P_l(\cos\theta)P_{l'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2}{2l'+1}\delta_{ll'}$ isolates each harmonic independently:
   - For every $l' \neq 1$: $\frac{B_{l'}}{R^{l'+1}}\cdot\frac{2}{2l'+1} = 0 \implies B_{l'} = 0$.
   - For $l' = 1$: $\left(-E_0 R + \frac{B_1}{R^2}\right)\cdot\frac{2}{3} = 0 \implies B_1 = E_0 R^3$.

3. **Exact Exterior Potential ($r \ge R$):** Substituting $A_1 = -E_0$ and $B_1 = E_0 R^3$:
   $$\boxed{V_{\text{out}}(r,\theta) = -E_0 r\left(1 - \frac{R^3}{r^3}\right)\cos\theta = -E_0 r\cos\theta + \frac{E_0 R^3\cos\theta}{r^2}}$$
   *(Inside the conductor, $r \le R$, $V_{\text{in}} = 0$ and $\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = \mathbf{0}$.)*

4. **Induced Dipole Moment & Electric Field $\mathbf{E}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{r})$:** Compare the second term $\frac{E_0 R^3\cos\theta}{r^2}$ directly with the point-dipole potential $\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$. The sphere acts **exactly** as an ideal induced point dipole at the origin of moment:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{p}_{\text{ind}} = 4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 \mathbf{E}_0}$$
   Computing $\mathbf{E}_{\text{out}} = -\nabla V_{\text{out}}$ in spherical coordinates:
   $$E_r = -\frac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial r} = E_0\left(1 + \frac{2R^3}{r^3}\right)\cos\theta, \qquad E_\theta = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial\theta} = -E_0\left(1 - \frac{R^3}{r^3}\right)\sin\theta, \qquad E_\phi = 0$$
   In coordinate-free vector form (evaluated at any exterior point $\mathbf{r}$):
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{E}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{E}_0 + \frac{R^3}{r^3}\left[3(\mathbf{E}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \mathbf{E}_0\right]}$$

5. **Induced Surface Charge Density $\sigma(\theta)$:** At $r = R$, $E_\theta(R,\theta) = 0$ (strictly normal field, verifying the boundary condition!), while $E_r(R,\theta) = 3E_0\cos\theta$. Thus:
   $$\sigma(\theta) = -\epsilon_0\left.\frac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R} = \epsilon_0 E_r(R,\theta) \implies \boxed{\sigma(\theta) = 3\epsilon_0 E_0 \cos\theta}$$

**Sanity Checks:**
- **Total induced charge:** $Q_{\text{ind}} = \int_0^\pi (3\epsilon_0 E_0\cos\theta)(2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta) = 6\pi\epsilon_0 E_0 R^2\left[-\frac{\cos^2\theta}{2}\right]_0^\pi = 0$.
- **Direct dipole-moment verification:** Integrating $z\sigma(\theta)$ over the sphere surface:
  $$p_z = \int_0^\pi (R\cos\theta)(3\epsilon_0 E_0\cos\theta)(2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta) = 6\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 E_0\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = 4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 E_0$$
  exact agreement!
- **Field Enhancement at Poles:** At $\theta = 0, \pi$, $|\mathbf{E}_{\text{out}}(R)| = 3E_0$ (a conducting sphere amplifies the local electric field by a factor of $3$ at its poles, critical in dielectric breakdown analysis).
- **Extension to Insulated Charged Sphere (Net Charge $Q$):** Simply superpose the $l = 0$ monopole solution $+\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$ onto $V_{\text{out}}$ (giving $V_{\text{sphere}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R}$ and $\sigma(\theta) = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2} + 3\epsilon_0 E_0\cos\theta$).

### 5.3 BVP 2 - Linear Dielectric Sphere in a Uniform Electric Field $\mathbf{E}_0$

**Physical Story:** Now replace the metal sphere by a solid linear, isotropic, homogeneous dielectric sphere of radius $R$ and relative permittivity $\epsilon_r$ ($\epsilon = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r$) placed in vacuum ($\epsilon_0$) inside a uniform field $\mathbf{E}_0 = E_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. Molecular dipoles align with the field, creating positive bound charge on the $+z$ cap and negative bound charge on the $-z$ cap. These bound charges generate an opposing *depolarizing field* inside the sphere—weakening, but **not** extinguishing, the internal field!

```
               E_0 = E_0 z_hat (uniform outside at infinity)
                      ^         ^
                      |   + +   |
                   .-'  +     +  '-.      Outside (r > R):
                  /     E_in (up)   \     E_0 + pure dipole field
                 |   ^    ^    ^     |    Inside (r < R):
                 |   |    |    |     |    E_in = [3 / (eps_r + 2)] E_0
                  \     E_in (up)   /     (Strictly UNIFORM!)
                   '-.  -     -  .-'
                      |   - -   |         sigma_b = P cos(theta)
                      ^         ^
```

Since there is no free charge anywhere ($\rho_f = 0$, $\sigma_f = 0$) and the dielectric is homogeneous inside $r < R$, Laplace's equation $\nabla^2 V = 0$ holds separately in both regions ($r < R$ and $r > R$).

**Complete Set of Four Boundary Conditions:**
1. **Regularity at the Origin ($r \to 0$):** $V_{\text{in}}(r,\theta)$ must remain finite at $r = 0$, forcing $B_l^{\text{in}} = 0$ for all $l \ge 0$:
   $$V_{\text{in}}(r,\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} A_l r^l P_l(\cos\theta) \qquad (r \le R)$$
2. **Asymptotic Matching ($r \to \infty$):** $V_{\text{out}}(r,\theta) \to -E_0 r P_1(\cos\theta)$, forcing $A_1^{\text{out}} = -E_0$ and $A_l^{\text{out}} = 0$ for all $l \neq 1$:
   $$V_{\text{out}}(r,\theta) = -E_0 r P_1(\cos\theta) + \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \frac{D_l}{r^{l+1}} P_l(\cos\theta) \qquad (r \ge R)$$
3. **Continuity of Potential at $r = R$** (equivalent to tangential $E_\theta$ continuity):
   $$V_{\text{in}}(R,\theta) = V_{\text{out}}(R,\theta) \qquad \forall\,\theta \in [0,\pi]$$
4. **Continuity of Normal Displacement $D_r$ at $r = R$** ($\sigma_f = 0$, so $\epsilon_0\epsilon_r E_{\text{in},r} = \epsilon_0 E_{\text{out},r}$):
   $$\epsilon_r \left.\frac{\partial V_{\text{in}}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R} = \left.\frac{\partial V_{\text{out}}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=R} \qquad \forall\,\theta \in [0,\pi]$$

**Unabridged Legendre-Orthogonality Derivation:**
Substitute $V_{\text{in}}$ and $V_{\text{out}}$ into Boundary Conditions (3) and (4):

- From Condition (3) at $r = R$:
  $$\sum_{l=0}^{\infty} A_l R^l P_l(\cos\theta) = -E_0 R P_1(\cos\theta) + \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \frac{D_l}{R^{l+1}} P_l(\cos\theta)$$
- From Condition (4) at $r = R$:
  $$\sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \epsilon_r l A_l R^{l-1} P_l(\cos\theta) = -E_0 P_1(\cos\theta) - \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \frac{(l+1)D_l}{R^{l+2}} P_l(\cos\theta)$$

Multiply each equation by $P_{l'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta$ and integrate over $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ to match each harmonic $l$ individually:

- **Case 1: All harmonics $l \neq 1$:**
  $$A_l R^l - \frac{D_l}{R^{l+1}} = 0 \implies D_l = A_l R^{2l+1}$$
  $$\epsilon_r l A_l R^{l-1} + \frac{(l+1)D_l}{R^{l+2}} = 0$$
  Substituting $D_l = A_l R^{2l+1}$ into the second equation gives:
  $$\left[\epsilon_r l + (l + 1)\right] A_l R^{l-1} = 0$$
  Since $\epsilon_r \ge 1$ and $l \ge 0$, the bracket $\epsilon_r l + l + 1 \ge 1$ is strictly positive!
  - For $l \ge 2$: $A_l = 0$ and $D_l = 0$ uniquely.
  - For $l = 0$: the second equation gives $D_0/R^2 = 0 \implies D_0 = 0$, and the first gives $A_0 = D_0/R = 0$.
  Hence **every single coefficient with $l \neq 1$ vanishes identically!**

- **Case 2: Dipole harmonic $l = 1$:**
  - Equation (i) from $V$ continuity:
    $$A_1 R = -E_0 R + \frac{D_1}{R^2} \implies A_1 = -E_0 + \frac{D_1}{R^3}$$
  - Equation (ii) from $D_r$ continuity:
    $$\epsilon_r A_1 = -E_0 - \frac{2 D_1}{R^3}$$
  Eliminate $D_1/R^3$: multiply Equation (i) by $2$ and add to Equation (ii):
  $$2 A_1 + \epsilon_r A_1 = -2 E_0 - E_0 \implies (\epsilon_r + 2)A_1 = -3 E_0 \implies \boxed{A_1 = -\frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}E_0}$$
  Substitute $A_1$ back into Equation (i) to find $D_1$:
  $$\frac{D_1}{R^3} = A_1 + E_0 = \left(1 - \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)E_0 = \left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)E_0 \implies \boxed{D_1 = \left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)R^3 E_0}$$

**Complete Exact Solutions Inside and Outside the Dielectric Sphere:**

1. **Inside the Sphere ($r \le R$):**
   $$\boxed{V_{\text{in}}(r,\theta) = -\frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}E_0 r\cos\theta = -\frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}E_0 z}$$
   Taking $-\nabla V_{\text{in}}$ yields the celebrated result that the **internal electric field and polarization are strictly uniform** everywhere inside the sphere:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}\mathbf{E}_0, \qquad \mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0(\epsilon_r - 1)\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = 3\epsilon_0\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)\mathbf{E}_0}$$
   *(Internal displacement field: $\mathbf{D}_{\text{in}} = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r \mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = \frac{3\epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r + 2}\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}_0$.)*

2. **Outside the Sphere ($r \ge R$):**
   $$\boxed{V_{\text{out}}(r,\theta) = -E_0 r\cos\theta + \left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)\frac{R^3 E_0}{r^2}\cos\theta}$$
   The exterior disturbance is a **pure dipole field** with total induced dipole moment:
   $$\boxed{\mathbf{p}_{\text{ind}} = 4\pi\epsilon_0\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)R^3\mathbf{E}_0 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \mathbf{P}}$$
   The exterior field components are:
   $$E_{\text{out},r} = E_0\left[1 + 2\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)\frac{R^3}{r^3}\right]\cos\theta, \qquad E_{\text{out},\theta} = -E_0\left[1 - \left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)\frac{R^3}{r^3}\right]\sin\theta$$

3. **Bound Volume & Surface Charges:**
   $$\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P} = 0 \quad (r < R), \qquad \boxed{\sigma_b(\theta) = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = 3\epsilon_0\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)E_0\cos\theta}$$

**Four Ironclad Sanity Checks:**
1. **Vacuum Limit ($\epsilon_r \to 1$):** $\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} \to \mathbf{E}_0$, $\mathbf{P} \to \mathbf{0}$, $D_1 \to 0$, $\sigma_b \to 0$. Unperturbed uniform field!
2. **Conducting Sphere Limit ($\epsilon_r \to \infty$):**
   $$\lim_{\epsilon_r\to\infty}\frac{3}{\epsilon_r+2} = 0 \implies \mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} \to \mathbf{0}; \qquad \lim_{\epsilon_r\to\infty}\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2} = 1 \implies \begin{cases} V_{\text{out}} \to -E_0 r(1 - R^3/r^3)\cos\theta \\ \mathbf{p}_{\text{ind}} \to 4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3\mathbf{E}_0 \\ \sigma_b \to 3\epsilon_0 E_0\cos\theta \end{cases}$$
   **Exact 100% recovery of the conducting sphere BVP!**
3. **Self-Consistent Depolarizing Field Check:** A uniformly polarized sphere of polarization $\mathbf{P}$ in vacuum produces an internal self-field $\mathbf{E}_{\text{depol}} = -\frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\epsilon_0}$ (precisely the opposite of the Lorentz cavity field!). Adding this to $\mathbf{E}_0$:
   $$\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = \mathbf{E}_0 - \frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\epsilon_0} = \mathbf{E}_0 - \left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)\mathbf{E}_0 = \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}\mathbf{E}_0$$
   Exact self-consistency!
4. **Generalization to Dielectric Sphere ($\epsilon_1$) in Ambient Dielectric ($\epsilon_2$):** Replacing $\epsilon_r \to \epsilon_1/\epsilon_2$ everywhere immediately gives $\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} = \frac{3\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_1 + 2\epsilon_2}\mathbf{E}_0$. Setting $\epsilon_1 = \epsilon_0$ and $\epsilon_2 = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r$ yields the field inside a **spherical vacuum cavity carved in a uniform dielectric**: $\mathbf{E}_{\text{cav}} = \frac{3\epsilon_r}{2\epsilon_r + 1}\mathbf{E}_0 > \mathbf{E}_0$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Dielectric Sphere vs. Conducting Sphere in a Uniform Field
> **Problem:** A solid glass sphere of radius $R = 5.0\text{ cm}$ and dielectric constant $\epsilon_r = 4.0$ is placed in a uniform external electric field $E_0 = 1.0 \times 10^4\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$ in free space. Calculate: (a) the internal electric field $E_{\text{in}}$, (b) the uniform polarization $P$, (c) the maximum bound surface charge density $\sigma_{b,\max}$, (d) the total induced dipole moment $p_{\text{diel}}$, and (e) the electric field just outside the north pole ($r = R^+, \theta = 0$). Compare each result with an identical-radius uncharged conducting sphere ($\epsilon_r \to \infty$).
>
> **Solution:**
> Given $R = 5.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$ ($R^3 = 1.25 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3$), $E_0 = 1.0 \times 10^4\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$, $\epsilon_r = 4.0$, $\epsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ F}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$, $4\pi\epsilon_0 = 1.1127 \times 10^{-10}\text{ F}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$.
>
> 1. **Dielectric Sphere ($\epsilon_r = 4.0$):**
>    - **Internal field:**
>      $$E_{\text{in}} = \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2}E_0 = \frac{3}{4.0 + 2}(1.0 \times 10^4) = \boxed{5.00 \times 10^3\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}}$$
>    - **Polarization:**
>      $$P = \epsilon_0(\epsilon_r - 1)E_{\text{in}} = (8.854 \times 10^{-12})(3.0)(5.00 \times 10^3) = \boxed{1.33 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}}$$
>    - **Maximum bound surface charge density** (at north pole $\theta = 0$):
>      $$\sigma_{b,\max} = P\cos 0 = \boxed{0.133\,\mu\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}}$$
>    - **Total induced dipole moment** (note $\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2} = \frac{3}{6} = 0.50$):
>      $$p_{\text{diel}} = 4\pi\epsilon_0\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)R^3 E_0 = (1.1127 \times 10^{-10})(0.50)(1.25 \times 10^{-4})(1.0 \times 10^4) = \boxed{6.95 \times 10^{-11}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}}$$
>    - **Field just outside north pole ($r = R^+, \theta = 0$):**
>      $$E_{\text{out}}(R^+, 0) = E_0\left[1 + 2\left(\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right)\right] = E_0\left(\frac{3\epsilon_r}{\epsilon_r + 2}\right) = (1.0 \times 10^4)\left(\frac{12}{6}\right) = \boxed{2.00 \times 10^4\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}}$$
>      *(Normal $D$ boundary check: $D_{\text{in}} = \epsilon_0(4.0)(5.0 \times 10^3) = 2.0 \times 10^4\epsilon_0 = \epsilon_0 E_{\text{out}} = D_{\text{out}}$ — exact!)*
>
> 2. **Conducting Sphere Comparison ($\epsilon_r \to \infty$):**
>    - $E_{\text{in}} = \boxed{0}$ (complete shielding).
>    - $\sigma_{\max} = 3\epsilon_0 E_0 = \boxed{0.266\,\mu\text{C}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}}$ (exactly $2\times$ the $\epsilon_r = 4$ sphere).
>    - $p_{\text{cond}} = 4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 E_0 = \boxed{1.39 \times 10^{-10}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}}$ (exactly $2\times$).
>    - $E_{\text{out}}(R^+, 0) = 3E_0 = \boxed{3.00 \times 10^4\text{ V}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}}$ ($1.5\times$ the glass pole field).

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> 1. **Image-Energy Factor-of-2 Blunder:** For a point charge $+q$ at distance $d$ above a grounded plane, the interaction energy is $W = -\frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 d}$, **NOT** $-\frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 d}$. Never plug $q$ and $q'$ into the two-point-charge formula $\frac{qq'}{4\pi\epsilon_0(2d)}$—the physical field exists only in the half-space $z > 0$, halving the stored energy!
> 2. **Image-Dipole Orientation Trap:** A dipole *perpendicular* to a grounded conducting plane ($\mathbf{p} = p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$) produces a **parallel** image dipole ($\mathbf{p}' = +p\hat{\mathbf{z}}$), whereas a dipole *parallel* to the plane ($\mathbf{p} = p\hat{\mathbf{x}}$) produces an **antiparallel** image dipole ($\mathbf{p}' = -p\hat{\mathbf{x}}$). Always reflect individual $+q$ and $-q$ endpoints first—never guess the arrow!
> 3. **Missing Legendre Orthogonality in Spherical BVPs:** Writing "by inspection, only $l = 1$ survives" in UPSC Mains costs step-marks. Explicitly multiply the boundary equations by $P_{l'}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta$, integrate from $0$ to $\pi$, invoke $\int_{-1}^1 P_l P_{l'}\,dx = \frac{2}{2l+1}\delta_{ll'}$, and show that the determinant $[\epsilon_r l + (l+1)] \neq 0$ forces $A_l = D_l = 0$ for every $l \neq 1$.
> 4. **Lorentz Cavity Normal Sign:** On the inner surface of the Lorentz spherical cavity, the outward normal of the dielectric points **radially inward** ($\hat{\mathbf{n}} = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}$), so $\sigma_b = -\mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = -P\cos\theta$. Reversing this sign flips $\mathbf{E}_{\text{cav}}$ to $-\mathbf{P}/(3\epsilon_0)$ and produces the wrong denominator $(\epsilon_r - 2)$ in Clausius-Mossotti!
> 5. **SI vs. CGS Polarizability & $\epsilon_0$ Leakage:** In SI, $\mathbf{p} = \alpha\mathbf{E}_{\text{loc}}$ gives $\frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2} = \frac{N\alpha}{3\epsilon_0}$ ($[\alpha] = \text{F}\cdot\text{m}^2$). Never write $\frac{4\pi}{3}N\alpha$ without explicitly declaring $\alpha_{\text{vol}} = \alpha/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ in $\text{m}^3$. Similarly, never drop $\epsilon_0$ in $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$ or $\sigma = -\epsilon_0\frac{\partial V}{\partial n}$.
> 6. **Blindly Assuming $\oint \mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{a}$ Solves Asymmetric Dielectrics:** $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f$ determines $\mathbf{D}$ directly *only* when symmetry forces $\nabla\times\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{0}$. For a finite dielectric slab or sphere in a uniform field, $\nabla\times\mathbf{P} \neq \mathbf{0}$ at the boundary, so you **must** solve Laplace's equation with boundary conditions.

### 2. Examiner Step-Marking & Numerical Trap Signature

- **Skipping Legendre Orthogonality & Regularity Justifications:** In 15- to 20-mark spherical BVP questions (such as CSE 2025), candidates lose 3–5 marks by jumping straight to $V = (A_1 r + B_1/r^2)\cos\theta$ without first writing the full $\sum_l (A_l r^l + B_l r^{-l-1})P_l(\cos\theta)$ expansion, invoking finiteness at $r = 0$ (dielectric case), and proving $A_l = B_l = 0$ for $l \neq 1$ via orthogonality.
- **Coordinate-Free Vector Field Conversion:** Examiners increasingly ask for $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ in terms of radius vector $\mathbf{r}$ (explicitly tested in CSE 2025) rather than stopping at $(E_r, E_\theta)$. Always show both spherical components *and* the coordinate-free form $\mathbf{E}_0 + \frac{a^3}{r^3}[3(\mathbf{E}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \mathbf{E}_0]$.
- **Uniqueness Invocation & Surface-Normal Sign in Method of Images:** Always state the First Uniqueness Theorem explicitly before writing the image potential, verify all boundary conditions on the conductor and at infinity, and watch the outward normal direction when evaluating $\sigma = -\epsilon_0\frac{\partial V}{\partial n}$ so the induced charge carries the correct negative sign.
- **Partial-Derivative Order in Dipole Force $(\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}$:** Candidates frequently substitute $x = y = 0$ *before* differentiating $\mathbf{E}(x,y,z)$, killing genuine transverse derivatives $\partial E_x/\partial x$ and violating Newton's third law. Differentiate first, evaluate at the dipole coordinates second.
