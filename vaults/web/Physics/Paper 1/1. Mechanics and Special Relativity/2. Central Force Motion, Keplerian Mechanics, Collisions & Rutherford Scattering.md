# Chapter 2: Central Force Motion, Keplerian Mechanics, Collisions & Rutherford Scattering

Two isolated bodies interacting solely along the line joining them—whether a planet orbiting the Sun, a binary star system, an electron bound to a proton, or an $\alpha$-particle recoiling off a gold nucleus—obey a single mathematical architecture. Six coupled second-order differential equations in three-dimensional space collapse cleanly into one trivial free-particle motion of the Center of Mass plus a **one-dimensional radial problem** governed by an effective potential $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$.

This chapter develops the complete analytical machinery of two-body central dynamics and scattering geometry in strict SI units: reduced-mass decoupling, Binet's exact orbit equation, Kepler's laws, the classical Virial Theorem, circular-orbit stability and radial oscillations, geostationary and Hohmann orbital maneuvers, Laboratory–CM collision velocity triangles (including maximum deflection limits and neutron moderation), and the full derivation of Rutherford's hyperbolic orbit and $\csc^4(\theta/2)$ differential scattering cross-section.

## 1. Two-Body Reduction, Reduced Mass & Planar Central-Force Kinematics

### 1.1 Decoupling the Two-Body Problem via Reduced Mass

Consider two isolated particles of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ located at inertial position vectors $\mathbf{r}_1$ and $\mathbf{r}_2$, interacting exclusively through a mutual central potential $V(|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|)$. By Newton's Third Law, the force on $m_1$ due to $m_2$ and the force on $m_2$ due to $m_1$ are equal, opposite, and directed along the relative separation vector $\mathbf{r} \equiv \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2$:

$$m_1 \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_1 = \mathbf{F}_{12}(\mathbf{r}) = f(r)\hat{r}, \qquad m_2 \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_2 = \mathbf{F}_{21}(\mathbf{r}) = -\mathbf{F}_{12}(\mathbf{r}) = -f(r)\hat{r}$$

where $r = |\mathbf{r}|$ and $\hat{r} = \mathbf{r}/r$. Rather than solving six coupled Cartesian equations, transform from $(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2)$ to the **Center-of-Mass coordinate** $\mathbf{R}$ and the **relative coordinate** $\mathbf{r}$:

$$\mathbf{R} = \frac{m_1 \mathbf{r}_1 + m_2 \mathbf{r}_2}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2$$

Inverting this linear map expresses the individual particle positions directly in terms of $(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{r})$ (letting $M \equiv m_1 + m_2$):

$$\mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{R} + \frac{m_2}{M}\mathbf{r}, \qquad \mathbf{r}_2 = \mathbf{R} - \frac{m_1}{M}\mathbf{r}$$

Adding the two equations of motion cancels internal forces identically, proving uniform straight-line motion of the Center of Mass:

$$(m_1 + m_2)\ddot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{21} = \mathbf{0} \implies \dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} = \text{const}$$

Next, divide the $m_1$-equation by $m_1$, divide the $m_2$-equation by $m_2$, and subtract the second from the first:

$$\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_1 - \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_2 = \frac{\mathbf{F}_{12}}{m_1} - \frac{-\mathbf{F}_{12}}{m_2} = \left(\frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}\right)\mathbf{F}_{12} = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_1 m_2}\mathbf{F}_{12}$$

Defining the **Reduced Mass** $\mu$ decouples the internal dynamics completely into an **equivalent one-body problem** of a fictitious particle of mass $\mu$ moving in a fixed force field $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})$ anchored at the origin:

$$\boxed{\mu\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = f(r)\hat{r}, \qquad \mu \equiv \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \left(\frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}\right)^{-1}}$$

#### Kinetic Energy & Angular Momentum Decoupling

Substituting $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_1 = \dot{\mathbf{R}} + \frac{m_2}{M}\dot{\mathbf{r}}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2 = \dot{\mathbf{R}} - \frac{m_1}{M}\dot{\mathbf{r}}$ into total kinetic energy $T$ and total angular momentum $\mathbf{L}$ cancels all cross-terms identically:

$$T = \frac{1}{2}m_1\dot{\mathbf{r}}_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2^2 = \frac{1}{2}M\dot{\mathbf{R}}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{m_1 m_2(m_2 + m_1)}{M^2}\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 = \boxed{\frac{1}{2}M\dot{\mathbf{R}}^2 + \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}^2}$$

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}_1\times m_1\dot{\mathbf{r}}_1 + \mathbf{r}_2\times m_2\dot{\mathbf{r}}_2 = \mathbf{R}\times M\dot{\mathbf{R}} + \mathbf{r}\times\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \boxed{\mathbf{L}_{\text{CM}} + \mathbf{L}_{\text{rel}}}$$

**Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
- **Infinite Target Mass ($m_2 \gg m_1$):** $\mu = \frac{m_1}{1 + m_1/m_2} \to m_1$, and $\mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{r}_2$ (Sun–planet limit, or infinitely heavy gold nucleus in idealized Rutherford scattering).
- **Equal Masses ($m_1 = m_2 = m$):** $\mu = \frac{m}{2}$ (e.g., positronium $e^+ e^-$ has half the reduced mass of hydrogen, halving all its Rydberg transition energies; equal-mass binary stars orbit their midpoint with $\mu = m/2$).
- **Strict Inequality:** $\mu < \min(m_1, m_2)$ always—just like two resistors in parallel.

### 1.2 Planar Confinement, Angular Momentum Conservation & Areal Velocity

Having stepped into the inertial Center-of-Mass frame ($\dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{0}$), the entire two-body problem reduces to a particle of mass $\mu$ at $\mathbf{r}(t)$ under $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = f(r)\hat{r}$.

#### Proof of Angular Momentum Conservation & Planar Confinement

Compute the time derivative of the relative angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}$:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}\times\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}} + \mathbf{r}\times\mu\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{r}\times f(r)\hat{r} = \frac{f(r)}{r}(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{r}) \equiv \mathbf{0}$$

Therefore, **$\mathbf{L}$ is a strict constant vector in both magnitude and direction**. Taking the scalar dot product of the instantaneous position vector $\mathbf{r}(t)$ with constant $\mathbf{L}$:

$$\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mu\dot{\mathbf{r}}) \equiv 0$$

Every point on the trajectory lies permanently orthogonal to the fixed vector $\mathbf{L}$ through the force center. Thus, **every central-force trajectory is strictly confined to a fixed plane** (assuming $\mathbf{L} \neq \mathbf{0}$; if $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{0}$, motion is strictly 1D radial).

#### Plane Polar Coordinates & Constancy of Areal Velocity (Kepler's Second Law)

Align the $z$-axis along $\mathbf{L}$ so the orbit lies in the $xy$-plane with polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ and orthonormal rotating basis $\hat{r} = \cos\theta\,\hat{i} + \sin\theta\,\hat{j}$, $\hat{\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\hat{i} + \cos\theta\,\hat{j}$ (satisfying $\dot{\hat{r}} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\theta}$ and $\dot{\hat{\theta}} = -\dot{\theta}\hat{r}$):

$$\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \dot{r}\hat{r} + r\dot{\theta}\hat{\theta}, \qquad \ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \left(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2\right)\hat{r} + \left(r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}\right)\hat{\theta}$$

Newton's Second Law $\mu\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = f(r)\hat{r}$ splits into radial and azimuthal components:

$$\mu\left(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2\right) = f(r), \qquad \mu\left(r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}\right) = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mu r^2\dot{\theta}\right) = 0$$

Hence the conserved angular momentum magnitude is $L = \mu r^2\dot{\theta} = \text{const}$. During infinitesimal time $dt$, the radius vector sweeps an infinitesimal triangular area element $d\mathbf{A} = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{r}\times d\mathbf{r} = \frac{1}{2}(r\hat{r})\times(dr\,\hat{r} + r\,d\theta\,\hat{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2}r^2 d\theta\,\hat{k}$. Dividing by $dt$ yields the **areal velocity**:

$$\boxed{\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}r^2\dot{\theta} = \frac{L}{2\mu} = \text{const}}$$

Note the profound generality: **Kepler's Second Law holds for ANY central force $f(r)$ whatsoever**—inverse-square, harmonic, Yukawa, or logarithmic—because it relies purely on isotropy (zero torque), not on the radial form of $f(r)$.

## 2. Effective Potential Landscape, Binet Orbit Equation & Keplerian Mechanics

### 2.1 Reduction to 1D Radial Dynamics & the Effective Potential $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$

Because every central force $f(r) = -\frac{dV}{dr}$ is strictly conservative ($\nabla\times[f(r)\hat{r}] \equiv \mathbf{0}$), total mechanical energy $E$ in the CM frame is conserved:

$$E = \frac{1}{2}\mu\left(\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2\right) + V(r)$$

Eliminating the cyclic angular velocity via $\dot{\theta} = \frac{L}{\mu r^2}$ collapses the 2D orbital problem into an exact **1D radial energy equation**:

$$\boxed{E = \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + V_{\text{eff}}(r), \qquad V_{\text{eff}}(r) \equiv V(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}}$$

Equivalently, differentiating with respect to $t$ (or substituting $\dot{\theta} = L/(\mu r^2)$ into the radial equation of motion) gives $\mu\ddot{r} = -\frac{dV_{\text{eff}}}{dr} = f(r) + \frac{L^2}{\mu r^3}$. The extra repulsive term $V_{\text{cf}}(r) = +\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}$ is the **centrifugal potential energy barrier**—representing the angular kinetic energy required to preserve angular momentum $L$ as the particle approaches the origin.

For an attractive inverse-square potential $V(r) = -\frac{k}{r}$ ($k > 0$) with $L \neq 0$, the repulsive $+L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ barrier dominates as $r \to 0$ (preventing a head-on plunge into the center), while the attractive $-k/r$ well dominates as $r \to \infty$. Their competition creates a unique global minimum at:

$$\frac{dV_{\text{eff}}}{dr}\bigg|_{r_0} = \frac{k}{r_0^2} - \frac{L^2}{\mu r_0^3} = 0 \implies r_0 = \frac{L^2}{\mu k}, \qquad V_{\min} = V_{\text{eff}}(r_0) = -\frac{\mu k^2}{2 L^2}$$

```text
  V_eff(r)  (Attractive Inverse-Square Central Force, L > 0)
     ^
     | \      Centrifugal Barrier: +L^2 / (2 mu r^2)
     |  \
 E>0 +---\---------------------------------------------> Hyperbola (Unbound, 1 turning pt)
     |    \
 E=0 +=====\===========================================> Parabola  (Escape threshold, r_max -> inf)
     |      \                V_eff(r) = -k/r + L^2/(2 mu r^2)
     |       \                           ....--------'' (asymptote 0-)
 E<0 +--------*.........................*--------------> Ellipse   (Bound: r_min <= r <= r_max)
     |         \ r_min                 r_max
     |          \        .-----.
Vmin +-----------\------*-------\----------------------> Circle    (r = r_0 = L^2 / mu k)
     |            '.   r_0       '
     |              '-.   Attractive Well: -k/r
     |                 '
     +------------------+------------------------------> r
     0                 r_0
```

Reading directly off the $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ landscape without solving a single differential equation classifies every possible Keplerian trajectory:
1. **$E > 0$:** Single inner turning point $r_{\min}$; particle escapes to $r \to \infty$ with nonzero speed $\implies$ **Hyperbola**.
2. **$E = 0$:** Single inner turning point $r_{\min} = r_0/2$; particle barely reaches $r \to \infty$ at zero speed $\implies$ **Parabola** (exact escape boundary).
3. **$V_{\min} < E < 0$:** Trapped between two finite radial turning points (periapsis $r_{\min}$ and apoapsis $r_{\max}$ where $\dot{r} = 0$) $\implies$ **Bound Ellipse**.
4. **$E = V_{\min} = -\frac{\mu k^2}{2L^2}$:** Permanently fixed at the potential minimum $r = r_0$ ($\dot{r} \equiv 0$) $\implies$ **Circular Orbit**.

### 2.2 Exact Derivation of Binet's Orbit Equation ($u = 1/r$) & Conic Solutions

To find the explicit geometric shape $r(\theta)$ of the orbit rather than $r(t)$, eliminate time $t$ in favor of polar angle $\theta$ via the **Binet substitution** $u(\theta) \equiv \frac{1}{r(\theta)}$.

#### Unabridged Derivation of Binet's Differential Equation

**(i) Setup & Differential Operator:** From angular momentum conservation, $\dot{\theta} = \frac{L}{\mu r^2} = \frac{L}{\mu}u^2$. By the chain rule, the time-derivative operator transforms as:

$$\frac{d}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta} = \frac{L}{\mu}u^2\frac{d}{d\theta}$$

**(ii) Step-by-Step Transformation of $\dot{r}$ and $\ddot{r}$:**
- First radial derivative:
  $$\dot{r} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{u}\right) = -\frac{1}{u^2}\frac{du}{dt} = -\frac{1}{u^2}\left(\frac{L}{\mu}u^2\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = -\frac{L}{\mu}\frac{du}{d\theta}$$
- Second radial derivative:
  $$\ddot{r} = \frac{d\dot{r}}{dt} = \frac{L}{\mu}u^2\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(-\frac{L}{\mu}\frac{du}{d\theta}\right) = -\frac{L^2}{\mu^2}u^2\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2}$$
- Substitute $\ddot{r}$ and $r\dot{\theta}^2 = \frac{1}{u}\left(\frac{L}{\mu}u^2\right)^2 = \frac{L^2}{\mu^2}u^3$ into the radial equation $\mu(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2) = f(r)$:
  $$\mu\left(-\frac{L^2}{\mu^2}u^2\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} - \frac{L^2}{\mu^2}u^3\right) = f\!\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)$$
- Dividing through by $-\frac{L^2 u^2}{\mu}$ gives **Binet's Differential Orbit Equation**:

$$\boxed{\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{\mu}{L^2 u^2}f\!\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)}$$

*(Dimensional Check: $[u] = \text{m}^{-1}$; $[\mu f / (L^2 u^2)] = (\text{kg}\cdot\text{kg m s}^{-2}) / (\text{kg}^2\text{ m}^4\text{ s}^{-2}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}) = \text{m}^{-1}$. Exact!)*

#### Solving the Attractive Inverse-Square Force Law ($f(r) = -k/r^2 = -ku^2$, $k > 0$)

Substituting $f(1/u) = -k u^2$ collapses the right-hand side of Binet's equation into a **pure constant**—explaining why the inverse-square force is linearly integrable into simple harmonic motion in $u(\theta)$:

$$\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}$$

The general solution is the sum of the constant particular integral $u_p = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}$ and the homogeneous harmonic solution $A\cos(\theta - \theta_0)$ (choosing $A \ge 0$ and rotating axes so $\theta_0 = 0$ places periapsis at $\theta = 0$):

$$u(\theta) = \frac{1}{r(\theta)} = \frac{\mu k}{L^2} + A\cos\theta = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}\left(1 + \frac{A L^2}{\mu k}\cos\theta\right)$$

Define the **semi-latus rectum** $p \equiv \frac{L^2}{\mu k}$ and **orbital eccentricity** $\epsilon \equiv \frac{A L^2}{\mu k} \ge 0$:

$$\boxed{\frac{p}{r(\theta)} = 1 + \epsilon\cos\theta, \qquad p = \frac{L^2}{\mu k}}$$

This is the standard polar equation of a **conic section** with one focus at the origin!

#### Relating Eccentricity $\epsilon$ to Energy $E$ and Angular Momentum $L$

At periapsis ($\theta = 0$), radial velocity vanishes ($\dot{r} = 0$) and $r_{\min} = \frac{p}{1 + \epsilon} = \frac{L^2}{\mu k(1 + \epsilon)}$. Plugging $r_{\min}$ into $E = V_{\text{eff}}(r_{\min}) = -\frac{k}{r_{\min}} + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r_{\min}^2}$:

$$E = -\frac{\mu k^2}{L^2}(1 + \epsilon) + \frac{\mu k^2}{2 L^2}(1 + \epsilon)^2 = \frac{\mu k^2}{2 L^2}\left[(1 + 2\epsilon + \epsilon^2) - (2 + 2\epsilon)\right] = \frac{\mu k^2}{2 L^2}(\epsilon^2 - 1)$$

Solving directly for $\epsilon$:

$$\boxed{\epsilon = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2 E L^2}{\mu k^2}}}$$

#### Geometric & Energetic Identities for Bound Ellipses ($0 \le \epsilon < 1$, $E < 0$)

From $r_{\min} = \frac{p}{1 + \epsilon}$ and $r_{\max} = \frac{p}{1 - \epsilon}$:
- **Semi-major axis $a$:**
  $$2a = r_{\min} + r_{\max} = p\left(\frac{1}{1+\epsilon} + \frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\right) = \frac{2p}{1 - \epsilon^2} \implies a = \frac{p}{1 - \epsilon^2} = \frac{L^2/\mu k}{-2EL^2/\mu k^2} = \boxed{-\frac{k}{2E}}$$
  Remarkably, **orbital energy $E = -\frac{k}{2a}$ depends strictly on the semi-major axis $a$ and is completely independent of eccentricity $\epsilon$**!
- **Semi-minor axis $b$:**
  $$b = a\sqrt{1 - \epsilon^2} = \sqrt{a p} = \frac{L}{\sqrt{-2\mu E}}$$
- **Vis-Viva Velocity Equation:** Substituting $E = -\frac{k}{2a}$ into $E = \frac{1}{2}\mu v^2 - \frac{k}{r}$ yields the universal astrodynamics speed formula:
  $$\boxed{v^2 = \frac{k}{\mu}\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) = G(m_1 + m_2)\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)}$$
  *(Checks: Circle $r = a \implies v_c = \sqrt{GM/r}$; Parabola $a \to \infty \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/r} = \sqrt{2}\,v_c$.)*

### 2.3 Kepler's Three Laws & the Classical Virial Theorem

#### Kepler's Three Laws of Planetary Motion

1. **First Law (Law of Orbits):** Every planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun (more precisely, the system's Center of Mass) located at one focus—proven directly from $r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + \epsilon\cos\theta}$ for $E < 0$ ($0 \le \epsilon < 1$).
2. **Second Law (Law of Areas):** The radius vector joining the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time—proven from $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2\mu} = \text{const}$ (a direct consequence of angular momentum conservation).
3. **Third Law (Harmonic Law of Periods — Exact Two-Body Form):** Over one full orbital period $T$, the radius vector sweeps out the entire area of the ellipse $A = \pi a b$. Since $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2\mu}$ is constant:
   $$T = \frac{\text{Total Area}}{dA/dt} = \frac{2\mu \pi a b}{L}$$
   Substituting $b = a\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$ and $L = \sqrt{\mu k p} = \sqrt{\mu k a(1-\epsilon^2)}$ cancels the eccentricity factor $(1-\epsilon^2)$ identically:
   $$T = \frac{2\mu\pi a^2\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}}{\sqrt{\mu k a(1-\epsilon^2)}} \implies T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2\mu}{k}a^3$$
   For Newtonian gravitation, $k = G m_1 m_2$ and $\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$, so $\frac{\mu}{k} = \frac{1}{G(m_1 + m_2)}$:
   $$\boxed{T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1 + m_2)}a^3}$$
   In the Solar System ($m_{\text{planet}} \ll M_\odot$), $m_1 + M_\odot \approx M_\odot$, recovering Kepler's universal ratio $T^2/a^3 \approx \frac{4\pi^2}{GM_\odot}$. Retaining $(m_1 + m_2)$ is indispensable in astrophysics for determining total masses of visual binary star systems!

#### The Classical Virial Theorem (Clausius, 1870)

For a system of particles executing **bounded motion** (meaning coordinates $|\mathbf{r}_i|$ and momenta $|\mathbf{p}_i|$ remain finite for all time), define the scalar **virial function** $G_v(t) \equiv \sum_i \mathbf{p}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}_i$. Differentiate $G_v$ with respect to time:

$$\frac{dG_v}{dt} = \sum_i \dot{\mathbf{p}}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}_i + \sum_i \mathbf{p}_i\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i = \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}_i + 2T$$

Take the long-time average $\langle X \rangle \equiv \lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^\tau X(t)\,dt$ (or average over one exact period $T$ for periodic orbits):

$$\left\langle \frac{dG_v}{dt} \right\rangle = \lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{G_v(\tau) - G_v(0)}{\tau} = 0$$

because $G_v(t)$ stays strictly bounded while $\tau \to \infty$ (and for periodic motion $G_v(T) = G_v(0)$ identically). Therefore:

$$2\langle T \rangle = -\left\langle \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i\cdot\mathbf{r}_i \right\rangle = \left\langle \sum_i \mathbf{r}_i\cdot\nabla_i V \right\rangle$$

For a single-particle central power-law potential $V(r) = \alpha r^n$ (or any homogeneous function of degree $n$), **Euler's Homogeneous Function Theorem** gives $\mathbf{r}\cdot\nabla V = r\frac{dV}{dr} = n V(r)$, yielding the **Virial Theorem for Power-Law Potentials**:

$$\boxed{2\langle T \rangle = n\langle V \rangle}$$

**Two Canonical Physical Regimes:**
- **Inverse-Square Gravitation / Coulomb ($n = -1$):** $\langle T \rangle = -\frac{1}{2}\langle V \rangle$, so total energy $E = \langle T \rangle + \langle V \rangle = \frac{1}{2}\langle V \rangle = -\langle T \rangle$. *(Astrophysical paradox: as a star radiates energy away, $E$ becomes more negative, forcing $\langle T \rangle = -E$ to **increase**—self-gravitating stars possess **negative heat capacity** and heat up as they lose energy!)*
- **Isotropic Harmonic Oscillator ($n = +2$):** $\langle T \rangle = \langle V \rangle = \frac{1}{2}E$ (exact equipartition between average kinetic and potential energies).

### 2.4 Stability of Circular Orbits, Small Radial Oscillations & Bertrand's Theorem

Every attractive central force $f(r) < 0$ admits circular orbits of radius $r_0$ where radial acceleration vanishes ($\dot{r} = \ddot{r} = 0$):

$$\frac{dV_{\text{eff}}}{dr}\bigg|_{r_0} = -f(r_0) - \frac{L^2}{\mu r_0^3} = 0 \implies f(r_0) = -\frac{L^2}{\mu r_0^3}$$

Whether a tiny radial kick throws the particle into stable harmonic oscillations around $r_0$ or sends it spiraling catastrophically inward/outward depends on the curvature of $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ at $r_0$.

#### Exact Perturbative Stability Analysis

**(i) Linearized Radial Equation:** Perturb the circular orbit to $r(t) = r_0 + \xi(t)$ with $|\xi| \ll r_0$ at fixed angular momentum $L$. Expand $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ in a Taylor series around $r_0$:

$$V_{\text{eff}}(r_0 + \xi) = V_{\text{eff}}(r_0) + \underbrace{V'_{\text{eff}}(r_0)}_{0}\xi + \frac{1}{2}V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0)\xi^2 + O(\xi^3)$$

Substituting into $\mu\ddot{r} = -V'_{\text{eff}}(r)$ gives the linearized perturbation equation:

$$\mu\ddot{\xi} = -V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0)\,\xi \implies \ddot{\xi} + \omega_r^2\,\xi = 0, \qquad \omega_r^2 \equiv \frac{V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0)}{\mu}$$

Compute $V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0)$ explicitly and eliminate $\frac{L^2}{\mu r_0^3} = -f(r_0)$:

$$V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0) = -f'(r_0) + \frac{3L^2}{\mu r_0^4} = -f'(r_0) - \frac{3}{r_0}f(r_0)$$

**(ii) Universal Stability Criterion:** Bounded harmonic radial oscillations require $\omega_r^2 > 0$, i.e., $V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0) > 0$:

$$\boxed{f'(r_0) + \frac{3}{r_0}f(r_0) < 0 \iff \frac{d}{dr}\ln|f(r)|\bigg|_{r_0} > -\frac{3}{r_0}}$$

**(iii) Power-Law Classification ($f(r) = -c\,r^q$, with $c > 0$):**
Here $f'(r_0) = -c q r_0^{q-1} = \frac{q}{r_0}f(r_0)$, so:

$$V''_{\text{eff}}(r_0) = -\frac{q + 3}{r_0}f(r_0) = \frac{c(q+3)}{r_0^{1-q}} > 0 \iff \boxed{q > -3}$$

- **$q = -2$ (Inverse-Square Gravity):** $q + 3 = +1 > 0 \implies$ **Stable**.
- **$q = +1$ (Hooke's Law):** $q + 3 = +4 > 0 \implies$ **Stable**.
- **$q = -3$ (Inverse-Cube):** $V''_{\text{eff}} = 0 \implies$ **Neutrally unstable** (any kick sends the particle into a Cotes' spiral $r = ke^{\pm a\theta}$!).
- **$q < -3$ (e.g., Inverse-Fourth $q = -4$):** $V''_{\text{eff}} < 0 \implies$ **Strictly unstable** ($V_{\text{eff}}$ has a local *maximum*, not a minimum).

**(iv) Radial vs. Azimuthal Frequency Ratio & Bertrand's Theorem:**
The circular orbital frequency satisfies $\mu r_0 \omega_\theta^2 = -f(r_0) \implies \omega_\theta^2 = -\frac{f(r_0)}{\mu r_0}$. Taking the ratio with $\omega_r^2 = -\frac{q+3}{\mu r_0}f(r_0)$ gives:

$$\boxed{\frac{\omega_r}{\omega_\theta} = \sqrt{q + 3}}$$

The apsidal angle swept between consecutive radial minima is $\Delta\theta_{\text{aps}} = \frac{2\pi\omega_\theta}{\omega_r} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{q+3}}$. For nearly circular orbits to **close** without precessing, $\sqrt{q+3}$ must be rational. **Bertrand's Theorem** proves a vastly stronger result: among all central potentials, **only two force laws produce closed orbits for ALL bound initial conditions**—the inverse-square law ($q = -2$, $\omega_r/\omega_\theta = 1$: one perihelion per revolution) and the isotropic 3D harmonic oscillator ($q = +1$, $\omega_r/\omega_\theta = 2$: ellipse centered on the origin, two perihelia per revolution).

### 2.5 Geostationary Orbits & Hohmann Orbital Transfers

#### Geostationary Orbit (GEO)

A **geostationary satellite** remains permanently fixed over a single longitude on Earth's Equator. This enforces three simultaneous orbital constraints: (i) **circular** ($\epsilon = 0$, constant angular speed), (ii) **equatorial** (inclination $i = 0^\circ$, otherwise it traces a figure-eight analemma north–south), and (iii) **prograde** (west-to-east) with period equal to Earth's **sidereal rotation period** $T_{\text{sid}} = 86{,}164\text{ s}$ (not $86{,}400\text{ s}$ solar day!). By Kepler's Third Law ($m_{\text{sat}} \ll M_E$, $GM_E = 3.986\times 10^{14}\text{ m}^3\text{ s}^{-2}$):

$$\boxed{r_{\text{GEO}} = \left(\frac{GM_E T_{\text{sid}}^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} = 42{,}164\text{ km}, \qquad h_{\text{GEO}} = r_{\text{GEO}} - R_E = 35{,}786\text{ km}}$$

with constant orbital speed $v_{\text{GEO}} = \sqrt{GM_E / r_{\text{GEO}}} = 3.075\text{ km s}^{-1}$.

#### Hohmann Minimum-Energy Coplanar Transfer

To transfer a spacecraft economically from an inner circular orbit of radius $r_1$ to a coplanar outer circular orbit of radius $r_2 > r_1$, apply two instantaneous tangential velocity impulses ($\Delta v_1$ at perigee $r_1$, $\Delta v_2$ at apogee $r_2$) connected by a **semi-elliptical Hohmann transfer orbit** tangent to both circles.

- **Transfer Ellipse Geometry:** $r_{\min} = r_1$, $r_{\max} = r_2 \implies$
  $$a_t = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}, \qquad \epsilon_t = \frac{r_2 - r_1}{r_2 + r_1}$$
- **First Impulse (Perigee Injection at $r_1$):** Using the vis-viva equation $v_{t,p} = \sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{2}{r_1+r_2}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}\cdot\frac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}}$ and circular speed $v_{c1} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}}$:
  $$\boxed{\Delta v_1 = v_{t,p} - v_{c1} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1 + r_2}} - 1\right)}$$
- **Second Impulse (Apogee Circularization at $r_2$):** Similarly, $v_{t,a} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}\cdot\frac{2r_1}{r_1+r_2}}$ and $v_{c2} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}}$:
  $$\boxed{\Delta v_2 = v_{c2} - v_{t,a} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}}\left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{2r_1}{r_1 + r_2}}\right)}$$
- **Transfer Time (Half-Period of Transfer Ellipse):**
  $$\boxed{t_{\text{transfer}} = \frac{T_t}{2} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a_t^3}{GM}} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{(r_1 + r_2)^3}{8GM}}}$$

*(Sanity Check: As $r_2 \to r_1$, $\Delta v_1 \to 0$, $\Delta v_2 \to 0$, and $t_{\text{transfer}} \to \pi\sqrt{r_1^3/(GM)} = \frac{1}{2}T_1$. Note that for $r_2 / r_1 > 11.94$, a three-impulse bi-elliptic transfer actually uses slightly less total $\Delta v$ than Hohmann, at the cost of much longer flight time.)*

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Inverse Binet Spiral ($r = ke^{a\theta}$) & Comet Near-Half Orbit Time (18% Law)
> **Problem:**
> 1. A particle of mass $m$ moving in a central force field describes the logarithmic spiral $r = k e^{a\theta}$ ($k, a$ constants). Determine the central force law $f(r)$.
> 2. A body moving in an attractive inverse-square field traverses an elliptical orbit of eccentricity $\epsilon$ and period $T$. Prove that the time spent traversing the half of the orbit nearer to the focus is $t_{\text{near}} = T\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\right)$, and explain quantitatively why a long-period comet spends only $\approx 18\%$ of its period in the near half.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part 1 — Force Law for $r = ke^{a\theta}$:**
> Define $u(\theta) = \frac{1}{r} = \frac{1}{k}e^{-a\theta}$. Differentiate twice with respect to $\theta$:
> $$\frac{du}{d\theta} = -\frac{a}{k}e^{-a\theta} = -a u, \qquad \frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} = \frac{a^2}{k}e^{-a\theta} = a^2 u$$
> Substitute into Binet's differential orbit equation $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{m}{L^2 u^2}f(1/u)$:
> $$(a^2 + 1)u = -\frac{m}{L^2 u^2}f(r) \implies f(r) = -\frac{L^2(a^2 + 1)}{m}u^3$$
> Replacing $u = 1/r$:
> $$\boxed{f(r) = -\frac{L^2(a^2 + 1)}{m r^3}}$$
> *(An attractive inverse-cube force law—matching the exact marginal instability boundary $q = -3$ of Section 2.4!)*
>
> **Part 2 — Near-Half Elliptical Orbit Time:**
> Let the ellipse have semi-major axis $a$, semi-minor axis $b = a\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$, geometric center $O$, and focus $F$ at $x = +a\epsilon$ along the major axis. The minor axis ($x = 0$, endpoints $B_1(0, +b)$ and $B_2(0, -b)$) bisects the ellipse into the **near half** (containing perihelion $P(+a,0)$) and the **far half** (containing aphelion $A(-a,0)$).
>
> By Kepler's Second Law ($\frac{dA}{dt} = \text{const}$), the fraction of period $T$ spent moving from $B_2$ through $P$ to $B_1$ equals the ratio of the **area $A_{\text{near}}$ swept by the focus-to-planet radius vector** to the total ellipse area $A_{\text{tot}} = \pi a b$:
> - Area of the right half-ellipse $O B_1 P B_2 O$: $A_{\text{half}} = \frac{1}{2}\pi a b$.
> - Because focus $F$ lies inside the near half at distance $OF = a\epsilon$ from the minor axis, the swept region $F B_1 P B_2 F$ equals the half-ellipse **minus** triangle $B_1 F B_2$:
>   $$A_{\triangle B_1 F B_2} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{base } B_1 B_2)(\text{height } OF) = \frac{1}{2}(2b)(a\epsilon) = a b \epsilon$$
> - Therefore:
>   $$A_{\text{near}} = \frac{1}{2}\pi a b - a b \epsilon = a b\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \epsilon\right)$$
> - Applying $\frac{t_{\text{near}}}{T} = \frac{A_{\text{near}}}{A_{\text{tot}}}$:
>   $$\boxed{t_{\text{near}} = T\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{\epsilon}{\pi}\right)}$$
>
> **Why a Comet Spends Only $\sim 18\%$ Near the Sun:**
> Long-period comets travel on highly elongated ellipses with $\epsilon \to 1$ (e.g., Halley's Comet has $\epsilon \approx 0.967$). Taking the limit $\epsilon \to 1$:
> $$\frac{t_{\text{near}}}{T} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.5000 - 0.3183 = 0.1817 \approx \boxed{18.2\%}$$
> Physically, conservation of angular momentum ($v_\perp \propto 1/r$) accelerates the comet to enormous speeds near perihelion while slowing it to a crawl near aphelion, so it whips through the inner half of its orbit in less than a fifth of its period.

## 3. Elastic & Inelastic Collisions — Laboratory vs. Center-of-Mass Geometry

### 3.1 Frame Equivalence, Invariant Separation & CM Velocity Reduction

In particle accelerators and nuclear targets, experiments are measured in the **Laboratory (Lab) Frame**, where a projectile of mass $m_1$ and initial velocity $\mathbf{u}_1$ strikes a **stationary target** of mass $m_2$ ($\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{0}$). Yet the underlying collision algebra is dramatically simpler in the **Center-of-Mass (CM) Frame** (denoted by asterisks $*$), which translates at constant velocity $\mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$ relative to the Lab:

$$\mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_1 \mathbf{u}_1 + m_2 \mathbf{0}}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\mathbf{u}_1$$

**Invariance of Inter-Particle Separation (Lab vs. CM):** At any instant $t$, the CM coordinates of the two particles are $\mathbf{r}_1^* = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{R}(t)$ and $\mathbf{r}_2^* = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{R}(t)$. Subtracting cancels $\mathbf{R}(t)$ identically:

$$\mathbf{r}_1^* - \mathbf{r}_2^* = (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{R}) - (\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2 \implies \boxed{|\mathbf{r}_1^* - \mathbf{r}_2^*| = |\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|}$$

Thus both the instantaneous separation vector and relative velocity ($\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}^* = \mathbf{v}_1^* - \mathbf{v}_2^* = \mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2$) are strict Galilean invariants between Lab and CM frames.

#### Initial & Final CM Velocities in an Elastic Collision

Before collision, the velocities in the CM frame are strictly antiparallel with zero total momentum ($m_1\mathbf{u}_1^* + m_2\mathbf{u}_2^* = \mathbf{0}$):

$$\mathbf{u}_1^* = \mathbf{u}_1 - \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\mathbf{u}_1, \qquad \mathbf{u}_2^* = \mathbf{0} - \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} = -\frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\mathbf{u}_1 = -\mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$$

After an **elastic collision**, total CM momentum remains zero ($\mathbf{p}_1'^* = -\mathbf{p}_2'^*$, so $\mathbf{v}_1^*$ and $\mathbf{v}_2^*$ stay back-to-back) and total internal kinetic energy is conserved:

$$T_{\text{CM}, f} = \frac{p_1'^{*2}}{2m_1} + \frac{p_2'^{*2}}{2m_2} = \frac{p_1'^{*2}}{2\mu} = T_{\text{CM}, i} = \frac{p_1^{*2}}{2\mu} \implies p_1'^* = p_1^*$$

Consequently, **in an elastic collision, each particle's CM speed is completely unchanged**—the interaction merely rotates the back-to-back CM velocity line through the **CM scattering angle $\theta^*$**:

$$\boxed{v_1^* = u_1^* = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}u_1, \qquad v_2^* = u_2^* = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}u_1 = V_{\text{CM}}}$$

### 3.2 Exact Lab–CM Scattering Angle Transformations & Maximum Deflection Limit

Let the incident beam $\mathbf{u}_1$ define the $+x$-axis, and let the scattering plane be the $xy$-plane. After collision, projectile $m_1$ emerges in the CM frame at angle $+\theta^*$ above $+x$, while target $m_2$ recoils at angle $(\pi - \theta^*)$ below $+x$. Transforming back to the Lab frame via $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1^* + \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2^* + \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$ gives two closed kinematic triangles.

#### Projectile Lab Scattering Angle $\theta_1$

Resolving $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1^* + \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$ parallel ($x$) and perpendicular ($y$) to the incident beam:

$$v_1 \sin\theta_1 = v_1^* \sin\theta^*, \qquad v_1 \cos\theta_1 = v_1^* \cos\theta^* + V_{\text{CM}}$$

Dividing the two equations and introducing the **mass ratio** $\gamma \equiv \frac{V_{\text{CM}}}{v_1^*} = \frac{m_1}{m_2}$:

$$\boxed{\tan\theta_1 = \frac{\sin\theta^*}{\cos\theta^* + \gamma} = \frac{\sin\theta^*}{\cos\theta^* + \frac{m_1}{m_2}}}$$

#### Target Lab Recoil Angle $\phi_2$

Resolving $\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2^* + \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$ (with $\mathbf{v}_2^*$ directed at angle $\theta^*$ backward from $-x$, i.e., components $v_{2x}^* = -v_2^*\cos\theta^*$ and $v_{2y}^* = -v_2^*\sin\theta^*$):

$$v_2 \sin\phi_2 = v_2^* \sin\theta^*, \qquad v_2 \cos\phi_2 = V_{\text{CM}} - v_2^* \cos\theta^*$$

Because $v_2^* = V_{\text{CM}}$ identically for a stationary target, the target velocity triangle is **always isosceles** regardless of the mass ratio $m_1/m_2$! Canceling $V_{\text{CM}}$:

$$\tan\phi_2 = \frac{\sin\theta^*}{1 - \cos\theta^*} = \frac{2\sin(\theta^*/2)\cos(\theta^*/2)}{2\sin^2(\theta^*/2)} = \cot\!\left(\frac{\theta^*}{2}\right) = \tan\!\left(\frac{\pi - \theta^*}{2}\right)$$

$$\boxed{\phi_2 = \frac{\pi - \theta^*}{2} \qquad \left(0 \le \phi_2 \le \frac{\pi}{2}\right)}$$

*(Physical insight: A stationary target struck elastically can NEVER recoil into the backward hemisphere $\phi_2 > 90^\circ$!)*

#### Critical Mass-Ratio Regimes & Maximum Lab Deflection ($\gamma > 1$)

Visualize the vector addition $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} + \mathbf{v}_1^*$ geometrically: the tip of $\mathbf{v}_1$ traces a circle of radius $v_1^*$ centered at the tip of $\mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$. Three sharply distinct regimes emerge:

1. **Light Projectile on Heavy Target ($m_1 < m_2$, $\gamma < 1$):** Since $v_1^* > V_{\text{CM}}$, the origin lies **inside** the circle. As $\theta^*$ sweeps $[0, \pi]$, $\theta_1$ increases monotonically across the entire range **$[0, \pi]$** (full $180^\circ$ backscattering is possible—e.g., $\alpha$-particles bouncing off gold!).
2. **Equal Masses ($m_1 = m_2$, $\gamma = 1$):** The circle passes right through the origin ($V_{\text{CM}} = v_1^*$):
   $$\tan\theta_1 = \frac{\sin\theta^*}{1 + \cos\theta^*} = \tan\!\left(\frac{\theta^*}{2}\right) \implies \theta_1 = \frac{\theta^*}{2} \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$$
   Adding the recoil angle proves the classic **billiard-ball orthogonality theorem**: $\theta_1 + \phi_2 = \frac{\theta^*}{2} + \frac{\pi - \theta^*}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}$ (except for an exact head-on stop $\theta^* = \pi$).
3. **Heavy Projectile on Light Target ($m_1 > m_2$, $\gamma > 1$):** Since $V_{\text{CM}} > v_1^*$, the origin lies **outside** the circle! Therefore $\theta_1$ can never exceed the tangent angle $\theta_{1,\max}$ drawn from the origin to the circle. Maximizing $\tan\theta_1$ with respect to $\theta^*$:
   $$\frac{d}{d\theta^*}\left(\frac{\sin\theta^*}{\cos\theta^* + \gamma}\right) = \frac{\cos\theta^*(\cos\theta^* + \gamma) + \sin^2\theta^*}{(\cos\theta^* + \gamma)^2} = \frac{1 + \gamma\cos\theta^*}{(\cos\theta^* + \gamma)^2} = 0 \implies \cos\theta^*_{\max} = -\frac{1}{\gamma} = -\frac{m_2}{m_1}$$
   At this tangent point, $\mathbf{v}_1 \perp \mathbf{v}_1^*$, forming a right triangle with hypotenuse $V_{\text{CM}}$ and opposite leg $v_1^*$:
   $$\boxed{\sin\theta_{1,\max} = \frac{v_1^*}{V_{\text{CM}}} = \frac{m_2}{m_1} \qquad (m_1 > m_2)}$$
   **Double-Valuedness Warning:** Because any ray from the origin at angle $\theta_1 < \theta_{1,\max}$ pierces the velocity circle at **two points**, every allowed Lab deflection angle corresponds to **two distinct CM angles** ($\theta_a^*, \theta_b^*$) and two different final Lab energies of $m_1$!

### 3.3 Fractional Energy Loss, Neutron Moderation & Inelastic $Q$-Value Thresholds

#### Exact Elastic Energy Ratio & Nuclear Neutron Moderation

Square $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1^* + \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}$ directly using $v_1^* = \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}u_1$ and $V_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}u_1$:

$$v_1^2 = v_1^{*2} + V_{\text{CM}}^2 + 2 v_1^* V_{\text{CM}}\cos\theta^* = \frac{m_2^2 + m_1^2 + 2m_1 m_2\cos\theta^*}{(m_1 + m_2)^2}u_1^2$$

Dividing by $u_1^2$ gives the exact fraction of initial Lab kinetic energy $E_{1i} = \frac{1}{2}m_1 u_1^2$ retained by projectile $m_1$:

$$\boxed{\frac{E_{1f}}{E_{1i}} = \frac{m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2m_1 m_2\cos\theta^*}{(m_1 + m_2)^2} = 1 - \frac{2m_1 m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2}(1 - \cos\theta^*)}$$

Equivalently, the fractional kinetic energy **lost** by $m_1$ (transferred to target $m_2$) is:

$$\frac{\Delta E}{E_{1i}} = \frac{E_{1i} - E_{1f}}{E_{1i}} = \frac{2m_1 m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2}(1 - \cos\theta^*) = \frac{4m_1 m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\theta^*}{2}\right)$$

**Application to Reactor Neutron Moderation:** Let a fast fission neutron ($m_1 \approx 1\text{ u}$) collide elastically with a stationary moderator nucleus of mass number $A = m_2/m_1$:

$$\frac{E_f}{E_i} = \frac{1 + A^2 + 2A\cos\theta^*}{(1 + A)^2}, \qquad \left(\frac{\Delta E}{E_i}\right)_{\max} = \frac{4A}{(1 + A)^2} \quad (\text{head-on, } \theta^* = \pi)$$

- **Ordinary Hydrogen ($^1\text{H}$, $A = 1$):** Maximum fractional energy loss $= 100\%$ (a single head-on collision stops a fast neutron dead!).
- **Deuterium ($^2\text{H}$ in $\text{D}_2\text{O}$, $A = 2$):** Maximum fractional loss $= \frac{8}{9} = 88.9\%$.
- **Carbon ($^{12}\text{C}$ in graphite, $A = 12$):** Maximum fractional loss $= \frac{48}{169} = 28.4\%$.
- **Why $\text{D}_2\text{O}$ Beats $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in Natural-Uranium Reactors:** Although light water slows neutrons in fewer collisions, $^1\text{H}$ exhibits a $600\times$ larger thermal neutron-capture cross-section ($^1\text{H} + n \to {}^2\text{H} + \gamma$) than deuterium ($^2\text{H}$), poisoning the chain reaction unless uranium is enriched.

#### Inelastic Collisions — $Q$-Value, Energy Deficit & Threshold Energy

In an inelastic collision or nuclear reaction $m_1 + m_2 \to m_3 + m_4$, total kinetic energy is *not* conserved; define the **reaction $Q$-value** as the net change in kinetic energy:

$$Q \equiv T_f - T_i = T_{\text{CM}, f} - T_{\text{CM}, i}$$

where $Q < 0$ for **endothermic** (energy-absorbing / inelastic) reactions and $Q > 0$ for **exothermic** reactions. By König's Theorem, the Lab kinetic energy splits cleanly into an untouchable CM drift term (locked by linear momentum conservation!) and the available internal CM energy:

$$E_{1i} = \frac{1}{2}M V_{\text{CM}}^2 + T_{\text{CM}, i}, \qquad T_{\text{CM}, i} = \frac{1}{2}\mu u_1^2 = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}E_{1i}$$

1. **Maximum Fractional Energy Loss (Completely Inelastic Merger, $T_{\text{CM}, f} = 0$):**
   $$\frac{|\Delta T|_{\max}}{E_{1i}} = \frac{T_{\text{CM}, i}}{E_{1i}} = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$
2. **Endothermic Threshold Energy ($Q = -|Q| < 0$):** For the reaction to proceed at all, $T_{\text{CM}, f} = T_{\text{CM}, i} - |Q| \ge 0 \implies \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}E_{1i} \ge |Q|$:
   $$\boxed{E_{1,\text{th}} = |Q|\left(1 + \frac{m_1}{m_2}\right)}$$
   *(Sanity Check: As $m_2 \to \infty$, $E_{1,\text{th}} \to |Q|$; for $m_1 = m_2$, $E_{1,\text{th}} = 2|Q|$ because half the incoming energy is trapped in mandatory CM forward motion!)*
3. **Inelastic Angle Transformation ($m_1 + m_2 \to m_1 + m_2$ with excitation $|Q|$):** Since $T_{\text{CM}, f} = T_{\text{CM}, i} + Q$, the final CM speed shrinks to $v_1'^* = v_1^*\sqrt{1 + Q/T_{\text{CM}, i}}$, modifying the mass-ratio parameter in $\tan\theta_1 = \frac{\sin\theta^*}{\cos\theta^* + \gamma'}$ to:
   $$\gamma' = \frac{V_{\text{CM}}}{v_1'^*} = \frac{m_1}{m_2}\left(1 + \frac{Q}{T_{\text{CM}, i}}\right)^{-1/2}$$
   Notice that even when $m_1 < m_2$, a sufficiently endothermic reaction can push $\gamma' > 1$, imposing a strict maximum Lab scattering cone $\sin\theta_{1,\max} = 1/\gamma'$!

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Heavy-on-Light Deflection Ceiling & Neutron–Helium Elastic Scattering
> **Problem:**
> 1. An $\alpha$-particle ($m_1 = 4\text{ u}$) collides elastically with a stationary proton ($m_2 = 1\text{ u}$). Find the maximum possible Lab deflection angle $\theta_{1,\max}$ of the $\alpha$-particle and the exact fraction of kinetic energy it retains at that angle.
> 2. A neutron ($m_n = 1.675\times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$) of Lab kinetic energy $E_{1i} = 1.0\text{ MeV}$ ($1.602\times 10^{-13}\text{ J}$) collides elastically with a stationary $^4\text{He}$ nucleus ($A = 4$). Find: (a) the initial momenta of the neutron and $^4\text{He}$ nucleus in the CM frame, (b) the Lab scattering angle $\theta_1$, $^4\text{He}$ recoil angle $\phi_2$, and final Lab energy of the neutron if scattered at $\theta^* = 90^\circ$ in the CM frame, and (c) the maximum possible fractional energy loss of the neutron.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part 1 — $\alpha$-Particle on Stationary Proton ($m_1 = 4\text{ u} > m_2 = 1\text{ u}$):**
> $$\sin\theta_{1,\max} = \frac{m_2}{m_1} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25 \implies \boxed{\theta_{1,\max} = \arcsin(0.25) = 14.48^\circ}$$
> This tangent point occurs at $\cos\theta^* = -\frac{m_2}{m_1} = -\frac{1}{4}$. Substituting into the energy ratio:
> $$\frac{E_{1f}}{E_{1i}} = \frac{m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2m_1 m_2(-m_2/m_1)}{(m_1 + m_2)^2} = \frac{m_1^2 - m_2^2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{4 - 1}{4 + 1} = \boxed{0.60 \text{ (60\% retained, 40\% lost)}}$$
>
> **Part 2 — $1.0\text{ MeV}$ Neutron on Stationary $^4\text{He}$ ($A = 4$):**
> **(a) CM Momenta:** Initial Lab momentum of neutron:
> $$p_{1i} = \sqrt{2 m_n E_{1i}} = \sqrt{2(1.675\times 10^{-27})(1.602\times 10^{-13})} = \sqrt{5.367\times 10^{-40}} = 2.317\times 10^{-20}\text{ kg m s}^{-1}$$
> In the CM frame, $\mathbf{p}_1^* = -\mathbf{p}_2^* = \mu\mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\mathbf{p}_{1i} = \frac{4}{5}\mathbf{p}_{1i}$:
> $$\boxed{p_1^* = p_2^* = \frac{4}{5}(2.317\times 10^{-20}) = 1.853\times 10^{-20}\text{ kg m s}^{-1}\text{ (equal and opposite)}}$$
>
> **(b) Scattering at $\theta^* = 90^\circ$ ($\gamma = m_1/m_2 = 1/4$):**
> $$\tan\theta_1 = \frac{\sin 90^\circ}{\cos 90^\circ + 0.25} = 4.0 \implies \boxed{\theta_1 = 75.96^\circ}$$
> $$\phi_2 = \frac{180^\circ - 90^\circ}{2} = \boxed{45.0^\circ}$$
> $$E_{1f} = E_{1i}\frac{1 + A^2 + 2A\cos 90^\circ}{(1 + A)^2} = (1.0\text{ MeV})\frac{1 + 16}{25} = \boxed{0.68\text{ MeV}}$$
>
> **(c) Maximum Fractional Energy Loss ($\theta^* = 180^\circ$):**
> $$\left(\frac{\Delta E}{E_{1i}}\right)_{\max} = \frac{4A}{(1 + A)^2} = \frac{4(4)}{25} = \boxed{0.64 \text{ (64\%)}}$$

## 4. Rutherford Coulomb Scattering & Differential Cross-Section

### 4.1 Scattering Kinematics & Differential Cross-Section $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}$

When an experimentalist fires a collimated, monoenergetic beam of particles (flux $I_0$ = number of particles crossing unit area perpendicular to the beam per unit time) at a thin target foil, individual impact parameters $b$ are microscopically unobservable. What a detector measures is the rate $dN/dt$ of particles scattered into a solid-angle cone $d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta$ at deflection angle $\theta$.

Define the **differential scattering cross-section** $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}$ (dimensions of **area per steradian**, $\text{m}^2\text{ sr}^{-1}$, or $\text{barns sr}^{-1}$ where $1\text{ barn} = 10^{-28}\text{ m}^2$) as the effective target area per unit solid angle:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \equiv \sigma(\theta) = \frac{1}{I_0}\frac{dN/dt}{d\Omega}$$

Consider an azimuthally symmetric central force $f(r)$ where deflection angle $\theta(b)$ is a monotonic decreasing function of **impact parameter $b$** (the perpendicular distance between the initial velocity asymptote and the parallel line through the scattering center). Every incident particle passing through the infinitesimal annular ring $d\sigma = 2\pi b\,|db|$ between $b$ and $b + db$ scatters into the conical solid-angle shell $d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,|d\theta|$ between $\theta$ and $\theta + d\theta$. Equating incoming and outgoing particle fluxes:

$$I_0(2\pi b\,|db|) = I_0\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)(2\pi\sin\theta\,|d\theta|)$$

Solving directly gives the **universal classical cross-section formula**:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|}$$

*(Finite Target-Mass Conversion — CM to Lab Cross-Section: The formula above applies strictly in the CM frame with reduced mass $\mu$ and CM angle $\theta^*$. When converting to Lab observations, particle flux conservation requires $\sigma_{\text{lab}}(\theta_1)\,d\Omega_{\text{lab}} = \sigma_{\text{CM}}(\theta^*)\,d\Omega^*$. Differentiating $\cos\theta_1 = \frac{\cos\theta^* + \gamma}{\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2 + 2\gamma\cos\theta^*}}$ yields the exact Jacobian:)*

$$\sigma_{\text{lab}}(\theta_1) = \sigma_{\text{CM}}(\theta^*)\left|\frac{d(\cos\theta^*)}{d(\cos\theta_1)}\right| = \sigma_{\text{CM}}(\theta^*)\frac{\left(1 + \gamma^2 + 2\gamma\cos\theta^*\right)^{3/2}}{\left|1 + \gamma\cos\theta^*\right|}$$

*(Check: For a heavy gold nucleus, $\gamma = m_\alpha / M_{\text{Au}} \approx 4/197 \approx 0.02 \ll 1$, so $\sigma_{\text{lab}} \approx \sigma_{\text{CM}}$ to within $2\%$.)*

### 4.2 Exact Derivation of Hyperbolic Orbit & Impact Parameter $b(\theta)$

**(i) Physical Setup & SI Constants:** Consider a positively charged projectile (e.g., an $\alpha$-particle of charge $q_1 = +Z_1 e$, mass $m_1$) approaching a heavy stationary target nucleus (charge $q_2 = +Z_2 e$, mass $m_2 \gg m_1$, so $\mu \approx m_1$; for exact two-body treatment, simply replace $m_1 \to \mu$ and $E_{\text{lab}} \to E_{\text{CM}}$). The repulsive electrostatic Coulomb force in SI units is:

$$f(r) = +\frac{k}{r^2} = +k u^2, \qquad k \equiv \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} > 0$$

At $r \to \infty$, the particle approaches with speed $v_\infty$, kinetic energy $E = \frac{1}{2}\mu v_\infty^2 > 0$, and conserved angular momentum magnitude $L = \mu v_\infty b$, which directly links $L$, $E$, and $b$:

$$L^2 = \mu^2 v_\infty^2 b^2 = 2\mu E b^2$$

**(ii) Solving Binet's Equation for the Repulsive Hyperbola:** Substitute $f(1/u) = +k u^2$ into Binet's differential equation $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{\mu}{L^2 u^2}f(1/u)$:

$$\frac{d^2 u}{d\phi^2} + u = -\frac{\mu k}{L^2}$$

*(We use polar angle $\phi$ along the orbit so it never collides with the total scattering deflection angle $\theta$!)* Choosing the symmetry axis of the orbit (the line from the nucleus through the point of closest approach $r_{\min}$) as $\phi = 0$, the general solution is:

$$u(\phi) = \frac{1}{r(\phi)} = -\frac{\mu k}{L^2} + A\cos\phi = \frac{\mu k}{L^2}\left(\epsilon\cos\phi - 1\right)$$

where semi-latus rectum $p \equiv \frac{L^2}{\mu k}$ and eccentricity $\epsilon \equiv \frac{A L^2}{\mu k} > 0$:

$$\boxed{\frac{p}{r(\phi)} = \epsilon\cos\phi - 1}$$

Because $r > 0$ requires $\epsilon\cos\phi > 1$ at $\phi = 0$, we must have $\epsilon > 1$. Determining $\epsilon$ via energy conservation at closest approach $r_{\min} = \frac{p}{\epsilon - 1}$ (where $\dot{r} = 0$):

$$E = \frac{k}{r_{\min}} + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r_{\min}^2} = \frac{\mu k^2}{L^2}(\epsilon - 1) + \frac{\mu k^2}{2L^2}(\epsilon - 1)^2 = \frac{\mu k^2}{2L^2}(\epsilon^2 - 1)$$

Substituting $L^2 = 2\mu E b^2$ yields:

$$\epsilon = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2 E L^2}{\mu k^2}} = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{2 E b}{k}\right)^2} > 1$$

Because $\epsilon > 1$ for every $E > 0$, **the trajectory is unconditionally the repulsive branch of a hyperbola** with the nucleus situated at the exterior focus.

**(iii) Relating Impact Parameter $b$ to Scattering Angle $\theta$:**
The incoming and outgoing asymptotes correspond to $r \to \infty$, i.e., $u(\pm\phi_\infty) = 0$:

$$\epsilon\cos\phi_\infty - 1 = 0 \implies \cos\phi_\infty = \frac{1}{\epsilon}$$

Look at the geometry of the symmetric hyperbola: the angle between the two asymptotes enclosing the closest-approach axis is $2\phi_\infty$. Since the straight undeflected line spans $\pi$ radians, the total deflection angle $\theta$ satisfies:

$$2\phi_\infty + \theta = \pi \implies \phi_\infty = \frac{\pi - \theta}{2}$$

Taking the cosine of both sides:

$$\cos\phi_\infty = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\epsilon} \implies \epsilon = \csc\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

Equating the two expressions for $\epsilon^2 - 1$:

$$\left(\frac{2 E b}{k}\right)^2 = \epsilon^2 - 1 = \csc^2\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) - 1 = \cot^2\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

Taking the positive square root (since $b \ge 0$ and $\theta \in (0, \pi]$) yields the **Rutherford Impact Parameter Relation**:

$$\boxed{b(\theta) = \frac{k}{2E}\cot\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 E}\cot\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$$

*(Ultra-Fast Impulse Cross-Check: The net momentum change vector $\Delta\mathbf{p}$ points strictly along the symmetry bisector $\phi = 0$ with magnitude $|\Delta\mathbf{p}| = 2\mu v_\infty\sin(\theta/2)$. Equating this to the integrated Coulomb force along $\phi = 0$: $2\mu v_\infty\sin(\theta/2) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{k}{r^2}\cos\phi\,dt = \frac{k\mu}{L}\int_{-\phi_\infty}^{\phi_\infty}\cos\phi\,d\phi = \frac{2k\mu}{L}\sin\phi_\infty = \frac{2k\mu}{\mu v_\infty b}\cos(\theta/2) \implies b = \frac{k}{\mu v_\infty^2}\cot(\theta/2) = \frac{k}{2E}\cot(\theta/2)$! Identical in two lines!)*

### 4.3 Exact Derivation of the Rutherford Cross-Section & Distance of Closest Approach

#### Differential Cross-Section $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}$

Differentiate $b(\theta) = \frac{k}{2E}\cot(\theta/2)$ with respect to $\theta$:

$$\frac{db}{d\theta} = -\frac{k}{4E}\csc^2\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) \implies \left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right| = \frac{k}{4E\sin^2(\theta/2)}$$

Substitute $b$, $|db/d\theta|$, and the half-angle identity $\sin\theta = 2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)$ into $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{db}{d\theta}\right|$:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{\frac{k}{2E}\frac{\cos(\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}}{2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)}\cdot\frac{k}{4E\sin^2(\theta/2)} = \frac{k^2}{16 E^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}$$

Canceling $\cos(\theta/2)$ and restoring $k = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$ gives the celebrated **Rutherford Differential Scattering Cross-Section**:

$$\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left(\frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 E}\right)^2 \frac{1}{\sin^4(\theta/2)} = \frac{d_0^2}{16}\csc^4\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$$

where **$d_0 \equiv \frac{k}{E} = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 E}$** is the **head-on distance of closest approach** ($b = 0$, $\theta = \pi$, where all initial kinetic energy converts into Coulomb potential energy $E = k/d_0$).

#### Distance of Closest Approach $r_{\min}$ for Arbitrary Impact Parameter $b$

At periapsis ($\dot{r} = 0$), energy conservation reads $E = \frac{L^2}{2\mu r_{\min}^2} + \frac{k}{r_{\min}}$. Using $L^2/(2\mu) = E b^2$ and $k/E = d_0$:

$$1 = \frac{b^2}{r_{\min}^2} + \frac{d_0}{r_{\min}} \implies r_{\min}^2 - d_0 r_{\min} - b^2 = 0$$

Taking the positive physical root (and noting $b = \frac{d_0}{2}\cot(\theta/2) \implies \sqrt{(d_0/2)^2 + b^2} = \frac{d_0}{2}\csc(\theta/2)$):

$$\boxed{r_{\min} = \frac{d_0}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{d_0}{2}\right)^2 + b^2} = \frac{d_0}{2}\left[1 + \csc\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]}$$

*(Limiting Checks: Head-on $b = 0$ ($\theta = \pi$) $\implies r_{\min} = d_0$; Grazing $b \gg d_0$ ($\theta \to 0$) $\implies r_{\min} \to b$.)*

#### Integrated Cross-Section Beyond $\theta_0$ & Physical Significance

The total cross-section for scattering at **all angles $\theta \ge \theta_0$** corresponds directly to the disk of impact parameters $b \le b(\theta_0)$:

$$\sigma(\theta \ge \theta_0) = \int_{\theta_0}^\pi \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}(2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta) = \pi b(\theta_0)^2 = \frac{\pi d_0^2}{4}\cot^2\!\left(\frac{\theta_0}{2}\right)$$

For a thin target foil of thickness $t$ and atomic number density $n$ (atoms per $\text{m}^3$), the **fraction $f$ of incident particles scattered beyond $\theta_0$** (in the single-scattering thin-foil limit $n t \sigma \ll 1$) is $f = n t\,\sigma(\theta \ge \theta_0)$.

**Four Cornerstone Physical Insights of Rutherford's Formula:**
1. **Discovery of the Atomic Nucleus:** Geiger and Marsden (1909–1913) verified all four scalings—$\propto \csc^4(\theta/2)$, $\propto Z_2^2$, $\propto 1/E^2$, and $\propto t$—down to backscattering at $\theta = 150^\circ$, proving that an atom's positive charge sits inside a nucleus of radius $R_{\text{nuc}} < d_0 \sim 10^{-14}\text{ m}$.
2. **Divergent Total Cross-Section ($\theta_0 \to 0$):** As $\theta_0 \to 0$, $\sigma_{\text{tot}} \propto \cot^2(\theta_0/2) \to \infty$! Because the bare $1/r^2$ Coulomb force has **infinite range**, even particles passing kilometers away experience nonzero deflection. In real targets, atomic electron clouds **screen** the nuclear charge beyond $r \sim a_0$, cutting off the integral at $\theta_{\min} > 0$.
3. **High-Energy Breakdown & Nuclear Radius Measurement:** When beam energy $E$ rises until $d_0 \lesssim R_{\text{nuc}} + R_\alpha$, the $\alpha$-particle penetrates the short-range attractive **strong nuclear force**, causing sharp departures from $\csc^4(\theta/2)$ at large angles—yielding the first direct measurement of nuclear radii ($R \approx R_0 A^{1/3}$).
4. **Identical Attractive & Repulsive Cross-Sections + Exact Quantum Coincidence:** Because $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \propto k^2$, an attractive Coulomb potential ($-k/r^2$, e.g., $e^-$ on nucleus) produces the **exact same** Rutherford cross-section as a repulsive one! Even more astonishingly, the exact non-relativistic quantum-mechanical Coulomb cross-section is **identically equal** to Rutherford's classical formula—$\hbar$ cancels out completely!

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Alpha-Particle Impact Parameter, Closest Approach & Rutherford Cross-Section
> **Problem:** An $\alpha$-particle ($Z_1 = 2$) of kinetic energy $E = 6.0\times 10^{-13}\text{ J}$ ($3.745\text{ MeV}$) is scattered by a stationary gold nucleus ($Z_2 = 79$). Take $e = 1.602\times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$ and $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 8.988\times 10^9\text{ N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$. Calculate:
> 1. The head-on distance of closest approach $d_0$.
> 2. The impact parameter $b$ and distance of closest approach $r_{\min}$ for a scattering deflection of $\theta = 30^\circ$.
> 3. The Rutherford differential cross-section $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}$ at $\theta = 30^\circ$ (in $\text{m}^2\text{ sr}^{-1}$ and $\text{barns sr}^{-1}$).
> 4. The cross-section $\sigma(\theta \ge 90^\circ)$ for back-hemisphere scattering.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Step 1 — Coulomb Coupling Constant $k$ & Head-On Distance $d_0$:**
> $$k = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = (8.988\times 10^9)(2)(79)(1.602\times 10^{-19})^2 = (1420.1)(2.5664\times 10^{-38}) = 3.645\times 10^{-26}\text{ J m}$$
> $$d_0 = \frac{k}{E} = \frac{3.645\times 10^{-26}\text{ J m}}{6.0\times 10^{-13}\text{ J}} = 6.074\times 10^{-14}\text{ m} = \boxed{60.74\text{ fm}}$$
> *(Sanity Check: $R_{\text{Au}} \approx 7.0\text{ fm} \ll 60.74\text{ fm}$, confirming pure Coulomb scattering with zero nuclear contact.)*
>
> **Step 2 — Impact Parameter $b$ & Closest Approach $r_{\min}$ at $\theta = 30^\circ$:**
> Half-angle $\frac{\theta}{2} = 15^\circ$: $\cot(15^\circ) = 2 + \sqrt{3} = 3.7321$, $\csc(15^\circ) = 3.8637$.
> $$b = \frac{d_0}{2}\cot(15^\circ) = (3.037\times 10^{-14})(3.7321) = 1.133\times 10^{-13}\text{ m} = \boxed{113.3\text{ fm}}$$
> $$r_{\min} = \frac{d_0}{2}\big[1 + \csc(15^\circ)\big] = (3.037\times 10^{-14})(1 + 3.8637) = 1.477\times 10^{-13}\text{ m} = \boxed{147.7\text{ fm}}$$
>
> **Step 3 — Differential Cross-Section at $\theta = 30^\circ$:**
> $$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\bigg|_{30^\circ} = \frac{d_0^2}{16}\csc^4(15^\circ) = \frac{(6.074\times 10^{-14})^2}{16}(3.8637)^4 = (2.306\times 10^{-28})(222.85)$$
> $$\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\bigg|_{30^\circ} = 5.14\times 10^{-26}\text{ m}^2\text{ sr}^{-1} = 514\text{ barns sr}^{-1}}$$
>
> **Step 4 — Back-Hemisphere Scattering Cross-Section ($\theta \ge 90^\circ$):**
> Since $\cot(45^\circ) = 1$:
> $$\sigma(\theta \ge 90^\circ) = \pi b(90^\circ)^2 = \frac{\pi d_0^2}{4}\cot^2(45^\circ) = \frac{\pi(6.074\times 10^{-14})^2}{4} = \boxed{2.90\times 10^{-27}\text{ m}^2 = 29.0\text{ barns}}$$

## 6. Exam-Hall Execution — Traps & Hand-Drawing Diagram Specs

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> 1. **Attractive vs. Repulsive Sign in Binet's Equation:** For an attractive force ($f = -k/r^2$), Binet's RHS is $+\frac{\mu k}{L^2}$, yielding $\frac{p}{r} = 1 + \epsilon\cos\phi$; for repulsive Rutherford scattering ($f = +k/r^2$), the RHS is $-\frac{\mu k}{L^2}$, yielding $\frac{p}{r} = \epsilon\cos\phi - 1$ (with a **minus one**, never $+1$!). Flipping that sign puts the nucleus at the wrong focus and corrupts $\cos\phi_\infty = 1/\epsilon$.
> 2. **Confusing Polar Orbit Angle $\phi$ with Scattering Angle $\theta$:** In Rutherford derivations, students routinely write $\frac{p}{r} = \epsilon\cos\theta - 1$ and then plug the *same* symbol $\theta$ into $\cot(\theta/2)$! Always reserve $\phi$ for the trajectory polar angle and $\theta$ for the total deflection angle linked by $2\phi_\infty + \theta = \pi$.
> 3. **Forgetting Reduced Mass $\mu$ in Binary-Star / Finite-Target Formulas:** Writing $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3$ when two comparable masses orbit each other forfeits marks; always derive $T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1 + m_2)}a^3$ first and explicitly take $m_1 \ll m_2$ as a limit.
> 4. **Double-Valued Lab Angles When $m_1 > m_2$:** When calculating Lab differential cross-sections for $\gamma > 1$ at $\theta_1 < \theta_{1,\max}$, *two* CM angles contribute to the same detector angle $\theta_1$—the observed Lab cross-section is the **sum** of both branches!
> 5. **Mixing $b = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 E}\cot(\theta/2)$ Factors ($4\pi$ vs. $8\pi$ vs. $16\pi$):** Lock the exact hierarchy via $d_0 = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 E}$:
>    - Head-on closest approach: $d_0$ (denominator **$4\pi\epsilon_0$**)
>    - Impact parameter: $b = \frac{d_0}{2}\cot(\theta/2)$ (denominator **$8\pi\epsilon_0$**)
>    - Rutherford cross-section: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left(\frac{d_0}{4}\right)^2\csc^4(\theta/2)$ (denominator inside square: **$16\pi\epsilon_0$**)
> 6. **Unit Traps in Nuclear Scattering & Orbital Mechanics:** Convert MeV strictly to Joules ($1\text{ MeV} = 1.602\times 10^{-13}\text{ J}$), barns to $\text{m}^2$ ($1\text{ barn} = 10^{-28}\text{ m}^2$), femtometres to metres ($1\text{ fm} = 10^{-15}\text{ m}$), and geostationary altitude $h_{\text{GEO}} = r_{\text{GEO}} - R_E$ (don't report $r_{\text{GEO}} = 42{,}164\text{ km}$ when altitude $35{,}786\text{ km}$ is asked!).
> 7. **Stability Criterion Sign Error:** $V_{\text{eff}}''(r_0) > 0$ translates to $f'(r_0) + \frac{3}{r_0}f(r_0) < 0$ (strictly **less than zero** because $f(r) = -dV/dr$ carries a minus sign!). Verify quickly with $f = -k/r^2$: $f' + \frac{3}{r}f = +\frac{2k}{r^3} - \frac{3k}{r^3} = -\frac{k}{r^3} < 0\;\checkmark$.

### 2. Examiner Step-Marking & Numerical Trap Signature

- **Skipping the Polar Acceleration & Binet Chain-Rule Steps:** Jumping straight to $\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2} + u = -\frac{\mu}{L^2 u^2}f(1/u)$ without explicitly deriving $\dot{r} = -\frac{L}{\mu}\frac{du}{d\theta}$ and $\ddot{r} = -\frac{L^2}{\mu^2}u^2\frac{d^2 u}{d\theta^2}$ forfeits 4–6 step-marks on 15- and 20-mark orbit questions.
- **Missing Diagram & Sign Discipline in Rutherford Derivations:** Writing $\frac{p}{r} = 1 + \epsilon\cos\phi$ for repulsive Coulomb scattering or omitting the symmetry-axis diagram showing $2\phi_\infty + \theta = \pi$ makes the jump to $\epsilon = \csc(\theta/2)$ look memorized and triggers heavy penalties.
- **Unstated Reduction to 1D Effective Potential:** On questions like CSE 2026, merely quoting $U_{\text{eff}} = V(r) + \frac{J^2}{2mr^2}$ without writing out the two coupled polar equations ($m(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2) = -kr$ and $\frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot{\theta}) = 0$) and explicitly substituting $\dot{\theta} = J/(mr^2)$ leaves easy step-marks on the table.
- **Neglecting Physical Interpretation Sub-Questions:** Examiners frequently attach 3–5 marks to physical interpretation tags (e.g., *"why a comet spends only 18%"* in CSE 2016 or *"physical significance of the Rutherford formula"* in CSE 2005); failing to cite nuclear size bounds, $\theta \to 0$ screening divergence, and high-energy strong-force breakdown costs an entire grade band.
