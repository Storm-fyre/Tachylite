# Chapter 4: Mechanics of Continuous Media — Isotropic Elasticity & Viscous Fluid Dynamics

Real matter is neither a point particle nor an infinitely rigid crystal. Under applied loads, interatomic bonds stretch, twist, and bend reversibly in **elastic solids**, while molecules slide continuously past one another under the slightest tangential shear in **viscous fluids**. Bridging the microscopic atomic lattice to macroscopic engineering structures and pipelines requires the **continuum hypothesis**—smoothing discrete atoms into continuous stress, strain, density, and velocity fields governed by partial differential equations and boundary conditions.

This chapter develops both pillars of continuum mechanics in strict SI units with zero skipped algebra: generalized 3D Hooke's law, exact derivations of all inter-relations among $(Y, K, \eta, \sigma)$ and the thermodynamic bounds $-1 < \sigma < 0.5$, strain energy density, torsion of solid and hollow cylinders, bending of beams and cantilevers, inviscid Euler–Bernoulli fluid mechanics, Hagen–Poiseuille viscous laminar flow (with kinetic-energy and entrance corrections), Stokes' creeping-flow drag, Millikan's oil-drop mechanics, and the Reynolds turbulence transition.

## 1. Isotropic Elasticity, Generalized Hooke's Law & Inter-Relations Among Elastic Constants

### 1.1 Stress, Strain & Generalized 3D Hooke's Law

When external forces deform a solid body, internal restoring surface forces arise across every imaginary internal plane. **Stress** is the internal restoring force per unit cross-sectional area (SI units: $\text{N m}^{-2} = \text{Pa}$), decomposed into **normal stress** $T_x, T_y, T_z$ (tension $> 0$, compression $< 0$) acting perpendicular to a face and **shear (tangential) stress** $\tau_{ij}$ acting parallel to a face. Rotational equilibrium of an infinitesimal cube of edge $dx$ forces the Cauchy stress tensor to be strictly symmetric ($\tau_{xy} = \tau_{yx}$), leaving 6 independent stress components.

**Strain** measures dimensionless fractional geometric deformation via the local displacement vector field $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})$:
- **Longitudinal (normal) strains:** $\varepsilon_{xx} = \frac{\partial u_x}{\partial x}$, $\varepsilon_{yy} = \frac{\partial u_y}{\partial y}$, $\varepsilon_{zz} = \frac{\partial u_z}{\partial z}$.
- **Volumetric strain (dilatation):** For small deformations ($|\varepsilon_{ii}| \ll 1$), $(1 + \varepsilon_{xx})(1 + \varepsilon_{yy})(1 + \varepsilon_{zz}) - 1 \approx \varepsilon_{xx} + \varepsilon_{yy} + \varepsilon_{zz} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} \equiv \frac{\Delta V}{V}$.
- **Engineering shear strain (angle of shear in radians):** $\phi_{xy} = \frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_y}{\partial x} = 2\varepsilon_{xy}$.

For a **homogeneous, isotropic** solid (identical elastic response in all directions) within its proportional limit, **Hooke's Law** states that strain is strictly linear in stress, characterized by four canonical elastic moduli:

1. **Young's Modulus ($Y$, units $\text{Pa}$):** Under pure uniaxial stress $T_x$ ($T_y = T_z = 0$), $Y = \frac{T_x}{\varepsilon_{xx}} = \frac{F L}{A \Delta L}$.
2. **Poisson's Ratio ($\sigma$, dimensionless):** Under pure uniaxial tension along $x$, the rod contracts laterally along $y$ and $z$: $\sigma \equiv -\frac{\varepsilon_{yy}}{\varepsilon_{xx}} = -\frac{\varepsilon_{zz}}{\varepsilon_{xx}} = -\frac{\Delta d/d}{\Delta L/L}$.
3. **Bulk Modulus ($K$, units $\text{Pa}$):** Under uniform hydrostatic pressure increment $\Delta P$ ($T_x = T_y = T_z = -\Delta P$), $K \equiv -\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V / V}$ (with compressibility $\kappa = 1/K$).
4. **Shear / Rigidity Modulus ($\eta$, units $\text{Pa}$):** Under pure tangential shear stress $\tau$, $\eta \equiv \frac{\tau}{\phi}$.

By linear superposition, applying arbitrary orthogonal normal stresses $(T_x, T_y, T_z)$ simultaneously along the three coordinate axes yields **Generalized 3D Hooke's Law** for an isotropic solid:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_{xx} &= \frac{1}{Y}\Big[T_x - \sigma(T_y + T_z)\Big] \\
\varepsilon_{yy} &= \frac{1}{Y}\Big[T_y - \sigma(T_z + T_x)\Big] \\
\varepsilon_{zz} &= \frac{1}{Y}\Big[T_z - \sigma(T_x + T_y)\Big]
\end{aligned}}$$

Because rotational symmetry forces the 4th-rank elastic stiffness tensor of an isotropic solid to depend on **exactly 2 independent scalars** (Lamé constants $\lambda, \mu \equiv \eta$), the four engineering constants $(Y, K, \eta, \sigma)$ obey two exact algebraic constraints—fixing any two uniquely determines the remaining two.

### 1.2 Exact Derivations of Inter-Relations Among $(Y, K, \eta, \sigma)$ & Bounds on $\sigma$

#### Derivation 1: Relation Between $Y$, $K$, and $\sigma$

**(i) Setup:** Subject a unit isotropic cube to uniform outward hydrostatic tensile stress $T_x = T_y = T_z = +P$ (equivalently compressive pressure $-\Delta P$ with flipped signs).

**(ii) Algebra:** Substituting $T_x = T_y = T_z = P$ into Generalized Hooke's Law gives equal normal strains along all three axes:

$$\varepsilon_{xx} = \varepsilon_{yy} = \varepsilon_{zz} = \frac{P}{Y}(1 - 2\sigma)$$

The resulting fractional volume expansion (dilatation) is:

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \varepsilon_{xx} + \varepsilon_{yy} + \varepsilon_{zz} = \frac{3P(1 - 2\sigma)}{Y}$$

By definition of Bulk Modulus, $K = \frac{P}{\Delta V/V}$ (equivalently $-\frac{-\Delta P}{\Delta V/V}$ under compression):

$$\boxed{K = \frac{Y}{3(1 - 2\sigma)} \iff Y = 3K(1 - 2\sigma)}$$

*(Limiting Check: As $\sigma \to 0.5$, $\Delta V/V \to 0$ and $K \to \infty$—an incompressible solid like ideal rubber cannot change volume under hydrostatic compression.)*

#### Derivation 2: Relation Between $Y$, $\eta$, and $\sigma$

**(i) Pure Shear $\iff$ Orthogonal Tension + Compression Setup:** Consider a unit isotropic cube $ABCD$ (front face of side $L$) held under pure tangential shear stress $\tau$ directed along $+x$ on top face $CD$, $-x$ on bottom face $AB$, $+y$ on right face $BC$, and $-y$ on left face $AD$ (maintaining zero net torque).

**(ii) Step A — Stress Equivalence Along $45^\circ$ Diagonals:** Slice the cube along diagonal plane $AC$ (area $\sqrt{2}L^2$, normal pointing along diagonal $BD$ at $45^\circ$ to $x$). On the upper-left triangular prism $ADC$, the two shear forces $\tau L^2$ along $+x$ (top face) and $-\tau L^2$ along $-y$ (left face) combine into a resultant force directed along diagonal $BD$ of magnitude:

$$F_{\text{diag}} = \sqrt{(\tau L^2)^2 + (\tau L^2)^2} = \sqrt{2}\,\tau L^2$$

Dividing by the diagonal plane area $A_{\text{diag}} = \sqrt{2}L^2$ gives a pure **tensile normal stress $T_{BD} = +\tau$** perpendicular to $AC$ (and zero shear along $AC$!). Cutting orthogonally along $BD$ similarly produces an equal **compressive normal stress $T_{AC} = -\tau$** along $AC$, with $T_z = 0$. Thus **pure shear $\tau$ is mechanically identical to equal orthogonal tension $+\tau$ along $BD$ and compression $-\tau$ along $AC$**.

**(iii) Step B — Geometric Diagonal Strain:** Under small shear angle $\phi$ (top face $CD$ shifts right by $x = L\phi$ while bottom face $AB$ stays fixed), diagonal $BD$ (initial length $\sqrt{2}L$) elongates by projecting displacement $x$ onto the $45^\circ$ direction of $BD$:

$$\Delta(BD) = x\cos 45^\circ = \frac{L\phi}{\sqrt{2}} \implies \varepsilon_{BD} = \frac{\Delta(BD)}{BD} = \frac{L\phi / \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}L} = \frac{\phi}{2}$$

**(iv) Step C — Hooke's Law Along Diagonal $BD$:** Applying Generalized Hooke's Law along the orthogonal principal stress directions $(BD, AC, z)$ with $(T_{BD}, T_{AC}, T_z) = (+\tau, -\tau, 0)$:

$$\varepsilon_{BD} = \frac{1}{Y}\Big[T_{BD} - \sigma(T_{AC} + T_z)\Big] = \frac{1}{Y}\Big[\tau - \sigma(-\tau + 0)\Big] = \frac{\tau}{Y}(1 + \sigma)$$

Equating the geometric and elastic strains $\frac{\phi}{2} = \frac{\tau}{Y}(1 + \sigma)$ and substituting $\eta = \frac{\tau}{\phi}$ yields:

$$\boxed{Y = 2\eta(1 + \sigma) \iff \eta = \frac{Y}{2(1 + \sigma)}}$$

*(Dimensional & Limiting Check: Both sides carry units of $\text{Pa}$; for an incompressible material $\sigma = 0.5$, $Y = 3\eta$ exactly—a standard rubber benchmark!)*

#### Derivation 3 & 4: Eliminating $\sigma$ and Eliminating $Y$

- **Eliminate $\sigma$:** Write $1 - 2\sigma = \frac{Y}{3K}$ and $2 + 2\sigma = \frac{Y}{\eta}$. Adding both equations cancels $\sigma$ identically:
  $$3 = Y\left(\frac{1}{3K} + \frac{1}{\eta}\right) = Y\frac{\eta + 3K}{3K\eta} \implies \boxed{\frac{9}{Y} = \frac{3}{\eta} + \frac{1}{K} \iff Y = \frac{9K\eta}{3K + \eta}}$$
- **Eliminate $Y$:** Equate $3K(1 - 2\sigma) = 2\eta(1 + \sigma) \implies 3K - 2\eta = (6K + 2\eta)\sigma$:
  $$\boxed{\sigma = \frac{3K - 2\eta}{6K + 2\eta}}$$

#### Rigorous Proof of Theoretical Bounds on Poisson's Ratio ($-1 < \sigma < +0.5$)

Thermodynamic mechanical stability (positive-definite elastic strain energy against arbitrary deformations—otherwise a solid would spontaneously collapse or explode!) demands strictly positive moduli: $Y > 0$, $K > 0$, and $\eta > 0$:

1. From $Y = 3K(1 - 2\sigma)$: since $Y > 0$ and $K > 0$, we must have $1 - 2\sigma > 0 \implies \sigma < +\frac{1}{2} = +0.5$.
2. From $Y = 2\eta(1 + \sigma)$: since $Y > 0$ and $\eta > 0$, we must have $1 + \sigma > 0 \implies \sigma > -1$.

Combining both inequalities locks the universal theoretical range of Poisson's ratio for all isotropic elastic continua:

$$\boxed{-1 < \sigma < +0.5}$$

*(Physical Regime Note: Ordinary isotropic metals and rocks sit in $0.20 \le \sigma \le 0.35$; cork sits near $\sigma \approx 0$ [ideal for wine stoppers—no lateral expansion when pushed in!]; natural rubber approaches $\sigma \approx 0.49$; engineered **auxetic** re-entrant cellular foams achieve negative $-1 < \sigma < 0$ by expanding laterally when stretched!)*

### 1.3 Elastic Strain Energy Density ($u_{\text{el}}$)

Because Hookean restoring stresses grow linearly from $0$ to their final values during quasistatic reversible deformation, the work stored per unit volume (**elastic strain energy density**, units $\text{J m}^{-3} = \text{Pa}$) carries the universal factor of $\frac{1}{2}$:

- **Uniaxial Tension / Compression:** Stretching a bar ($L, A$) by $dl$ requires $dW = F\,dl = (A T)(L\,d\varepsilon) = V T\,d\varepsilon$:
  $$u_{\text{axial}} = \int_0^\varepsilon Y\varepsilon'\,d\varepsilon' = \frac{1}{2}Y\varepsilon^2 = \frac{1}{2}T\varepsilon = \frac{T^2}{2Y}$$
- **Pure Shear Deformation:** $u_{\text{shear}} = \frac{1}{2}\tau\phi = \frac{1}{2}\eta\phi^2 = \frac{\tau^2}{2\eta}$.
- **Hydrostatic Volume Compression:** $u_{\text{vol}} = \frac{1}{2}P\left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right) = \frac{1}{2}K\left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right)^2 = \frac{P^2}{2K}$.
- **General 3D Isotropic State:** Summing over all normal and shear modes:
  $$u_{\text{el}} = \frac{1}{2}\Big(T_x\varepsilon_{xx} + T_y\varepsilon_{yy} + T_z\varepsilon_{zz} + \tau_{xy}\phi_{xy} + \tau_{yz}\phi_{yz} + \tau_{zx}\phi_{zx}\Big)$$

## 2. Torsion of Cylinders & Bending of Beams / Cantilevers

### 2.1 Torsion of Solid & Hollow Circular Cylinders (Twisting Couple)

When a drive shaft or torsion fiber is twisted about its symmetry axis, every cross-sectional disk rotates rigidly by an angle proportional to its axial distance from the fixed end, subjecting the solid to **pure circumferential shear** without altering its length or volume.

#### Unabridged Derivation of Twisting Couple ($\Gamma$)

**(i) Geometry & Boundary Setup:** Consider a uniform solid circular cylinder of length $L$, outer radius $R$, and shear modulus $\eta$, rigidly clamped at $z = 0$ and twisted through total angle $\theta$ (radians) at the free end $z = L$.

**(ii) Elemental Coaxial Shell Integration:** Partition the cylinder into thin concentric hollow cylindrical shells of radius $r$ ($0 \le r \le R$) and radial thickness $dr$. On the shell of radius $r$, a longitudinal generator of length $L$ shifts circumferentially at $z = L$ by arc length $r\theta$, defining a uniform angle of shear $\phi(r)$:

$$L\phi(r) = r\theta \implies \phi(r) = \frac{r\theta}{L}$$

By Hooke's law for shear, the tangential restoring shear stress on shell $r$ is:

$$\tau(r) = \eta\phi(r) = \frac{\eta\theta}{L}r$$

Acting across the annular cross-sectional area $dA = 2\pi r\,dr$, this produces tangential force $dF = \tau(r)\,dA = \frac{2\pi\eta\theta}{L}r^2\,dr$ at lever arm $r$ from the axis, yielding elemental restoring torque:

$$d\Gamma = r\,dF = \frac{2\pi\eta\theta}{L}r^3\,dr$$

Integrating from $r = 0$ to $r = R$ gives the **Twisting Couple** $\Gamma$ and **Torsional Rigidity (Couple per unit radian twist)** $C \equiv \Gamma/\theta$:

$$\Gamma_{\text{solid}} = \frac{2\pi\eta\theta}{L}\int_0^R r^3\,dr = \frac{2\pi\eta\theta}{L}\left(\frac{R^4}{4}\right) \implies \boxed{\Gamma_{\text{solid}} = \frac{\pi\eta R^4}{2L}\theta = \frac{\eta J}{L}\theta, \qquad C_{\text{solid}} = \frac{\pi\eta R^4}{2L}}$$

where $J = \frac{\pi R^4}{2}$ is the **polar second moment of area** of the disk. The total stored **torsional strain energy** is $U_{\text{tor}} = \int_0^\theta C\theta'\,d\theta' = \frac{1}{2}C\theta^2 = \frac{\pi\eta R^4}{4L}\theta^2$.

**(iii) Hollow Circular Shaft ($R_1 \le r \le R_2$) & Strength-to-Weight Advantage:**
Integrating from inner radius $R_1$ to outer radius $R_2$ gives:

$$\boxed{\Gamma_{\text{hollow}} = \frac{\pi\eta\left(R_2^4 - R_1^4\right)}{2L}\theta}$$

Compare a hollow shaft ($R_1, R_2$) against a solid shaft ($R$) of **identical mass, length, and material**—requiring equal cross-sectional areas $\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2) = \pi R^2 \implies R^2 = R_2^2 - R_1^2$:

$$\frac{C_{\text{hollow}}}{C_{\text{solid}}} = \frac{R_2^4 - R_1^4}{R^4} = \frac{(R_2^2 - R_1^2)(R_2^2 + R_1^2)}{(R_2^2 - R_1^2)^2} = \frac{R_2^2 + R_1^2}{R_2^2 - R_1^2} > 1$$

Because shear stress $\tau(r) \propto r$ vanishes at the central axis, hollow shafts relocate lazy core metal to the high-stress outer rim—delivering dramatically higher torsional stiffness and power capacity per kilogram!

**(iv) Power-Transmitting Rotating Shaft Link:** A shaft rotating steadily at angular speed $\omega = \frac{2\pi N}{60}$ ($N$ in rpm) transmitting mechanical power $P = \Gamma\omega$ carries steady torque $\Gamma = P/\omega$, sustaining relative angular twist $\theta = \frac{2 P L}{\pi\eta R^4 \omega}$ and outer-rim circumferential shift $s = R\theta$.

### 2.2 Bending of Beams — Neutral Axis, Internal Bending Moment & Cantilever Depression

When a uniform horizontal bar bends downward under transverse loading, longitudinal layers above a central unstretched surface (**Neutral Surface**) undergo tension while layers below undergo compression. The line of intersection of the neutral surface with the vertical plane of bending is the **Neutral Axis**.

#### Derivation of Internal Bending Moment ($M_b = Y I_g / R_c$)

**(i) Assumptions (Euler–Bernoulli Beam Theory):** Beam length $L \gg$ cross-sectional depth $d$; cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the neutral axis during bending; small deflections ($|dy/dx| \ll 1$); shear deformation negligible compared to flexural bending.

**(ii) Filament Strain & Centroid Proof:** Consider an elementary bent segment of neutral-axis arc length $ds = R_c\,d\psi$ (radius of curvature $R_c$). A parallel longitudinal filament located at distance $z$ above the neutral axis stretches to arc length $ds' = (R_c + z)d\psi$, experiencing tensile strain $\varepsilon(z) = \frac{ds' - ds}{ds} = \frac{z}{R_c}$ and tensile stress $T(z) = Y\frac{z}{R_c}$.

For pure bending with zero net axial thrust, integrating normal forces across the cross-section gives:

$$F_{\text{axial}} = \iint_A T(z)\,dA = \frac{Y}{R_c}\iint_A z\,dA = 0 \implies \iint_A z\,dA = 0$$

proving rigorously that **the neutral axis always passes through the geometric centroid of the cross-section**.

**(iii) Restoring Bending Moment & Flexural Rigidity:** Integrating elemental torques $z\,T(z)\,dA$ about the neutral axis yields the internal restoring **bending moment** $M_b$:

$$\boxed{M_b = \iint_A z\,T(z)\,dA = \frac{Y}{R_c}\iint_A z^2\,dA = \frac{Y I_g}{R_c}}$$

where **$I_g \equiv \iint_A z^2\,dA$** (units: $\text{m}^4$) is the **geometrical moment of inertia** (second moment of area) and **$Y I_g$** is the **flexural rigidity**:
- **Rectangular section** (breadth $b$, vertical depth $d$): $I_g = \int_{-d/2}^{+d/2} z^2 (b\,dz) = \frac{b d^3}{12}$.
- **Circular section** (radius $r$): $I_g = \frac{\pi r^4}{4}$.

Using the exact curvature formula $\frac{1}{R_c} = \frac{y''}{[1 + (y')^2]^{3/2}} \approx \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}$ for small downward depression $y(x)$ gives the **Euler–Bernoulli Beam Differential Equation**:

$$\boxed{Y I_g \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} = M_{\text{ext}}(x)}$$

#### Depression of a Cantilever & Simply Supported Beam

1. **Light Cantilever (Length $L$) with End Load $W$ at Free End ($x = L$):**
   Clamped horizontally at $x = 0$: boundary conditions $y(0) = 0$, $y'(0) = 0$. At arbitrary cross-section $x$, the external clockwise bending moment exerted by load $W$ at $x = L$ is $M_{\text{ext}}(x) = W(L - x)$:
   $$Y I_g \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} = W(L - x)$$
   Integrating once with $y'(0) = 0$:
   $$Y I_g \frac{dy}{dx} = W\left(L x - \frac{x^2}{2}\right)$$
   Integrating a second time with $y(0) = 0$:
   $$y(x) = \frac{W}{Y I_g}\left(\frac{L x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{6}\right)$$
   Setting $x = L$ yields the **free-end cantilever depression**:
   $$\boxed{\delta_{\text{end}} = y(L) = \frac{W L^3}{3 Y I_g}}$$
   *(For a rectangular bar $I_g = bd^3/12$: $\delta = \frac{4 W L^3}{Y b d^3}$—doubling vertical depth $d$ slashes sagging by a factor of $8$, explaining why structural steel beams use tall vertical I-profiles!)*

2. **Uniformly Loaded Cantilever (Self-Weight $w = W_{\text{beam}}/L$ per unit length):**
   The overhang $[x, L]$ has weight $w(L - x)$ acting through its centroid at lever arm $\frac{L - x}{2}$ from $x$, producing $M_{\text{ext}}(x) = \frac{1}{2}w(L - x)^2$. Integrating twice with $y(0) = y'(0) = 0$:
   $$Y I_g \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{w}{2}\left(L^2 x - L x^2 + \frac{x^3}{3}\right) \implies Y I_g y(x) = \frac{w}{2}\left(\frac{L^2 x^2}{2} - \frac{L x^3}{3} + \frac{x^4}{12}\right)$$
   At the free end $x = L$ (with $W_{\text{beam}} = w L$):
   $$\boxed{\delta_{\text{self}} = \frac{w L^4}{8 Y I_g} = \frac{W_{\text{beam}} L^3}{8 Y I_g}}$$
   By linear superposition, a heavy cantilever carrying an end load $W$ sags by $\delta_{\text{tot}} = \frac{L^3}{3 Y I_g}\left(W + \frac{3}{8}W_{\text{beam}}\right)$.

3. **Simply Supported Beam (Span $L$, Central Load $W$):**
   Each support exerts upward reaction $W/2$. By mirror symmetry, the midpoint $x = L/2$ stays strictly horizontal ($y' = 0$), turning each half into an inverted cantilever of length $L/2$ lifted by $W/2$:
   $$\boxed{\delta_{\text{center}} = \frac{(W/2)(L/2)^3}{3 Y I_g} = \frac{W L^3}{48 Y I_g}}$$

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Torsional Twist & Rim Shift of a Power-Transmitting Drive Shaft
> **Problem:** A uniform solid circular steel shaft of diameter $8\text{ cm}$ and length $5\text{ m}$ transmits a steady power of $8\text{ kW}$ while rotating at $300\text{ revolutions per minute}$. Given the modulus of rigidity $\eta = 8\times 10^{11}\text{ dynes cm}^{-2}$, calculate the relative angular twist $\theta$ and the relative circumferential linear shift $s$ between the two ends of the shaft.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Step 1 — Strict CGS $\to$ SI Unit Conversions:**
> - Radius: $R = \frac{d}{2} = 4\text{ cm} = 0.04\text{ m} \implies R^4 = (0.04)^4 = 2.56\times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^4$.
> - Length: $L = 5\text{ m}$; Power: $P = 8\text{ kW} = 8000\text{ W}$.
> - Rigidity modulus: $1\text{ dyne cm}^{-2} = 0.1\text{ N m}^{-2} \implies \eta = 8\times 10^{11} \times 0.1 = 8.0\times 10^{10}\text{ N m}^{-2}$.
> - Angular velocity: $\omega = \frac{2\pi N}{60} = \frac{2\pi(300)}{60} = 10\pi = 31.416\text{ rad s}^{-1}$.
>
> **Step 2 — Steady Transmitted Torque $\Gamma$:**
> $$\Gamma = \frac{P}{\omega} = \frac{8000}{10\pi} = \frac{800}{\pi} = 254.65\text{ N m}$$
>
> **Step 3 — Relative Angular Twist $\theta$ & Rim Shift $s = R\theta$:**
> From $\Gamma = \frac{\pi\eta R^4}{2L}\theta$:
> $$\theta = \frac{2\Gamma L}{\pi\eta R^4} = \frac{2(254.65)(5)}{\pi(8.0\times 10^{10})(2.56\times 10^{-6})} = \frac{2546.5}{6.4340\times 10^5} = \boxed{3.96\times 10^{-3}\text{ rad} \text{ (}0.227^\circ\text{)}}$$
> Relative linear circumferential shift along the outer surface between the two ends:
> $$s = R\theta = (0.04\text{ m})(3.958\times 10^{-3}\text{ rad}) = 1.583\times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = \boxed{0.158\text{ mm}}$$

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Two-Wire Equal Stress vs. Equal Strain Suspension & Cantilever Depression
> **Problem:**
> 1. A light rigid rod of length $100\text{ cm}$ is suspended horizontally from a ceiling by two vertical wires of equal length tied to its ends—one of steel ($A_s = 0.05\text{ cm}^2$, $Y_s = 2.0\times 10^{11}\text{ N m}^{-2}$) and the other of brass ($A_b = 0.10\text{ cm}^2$, $Y_b = 1.0\times 10^{11}\text{ N m}^{-2}$). Find the position along the rod (measured from the steel wire) where a weight must be hung to produce: (a) equal stresses, and (b) equal strains in both wires.
> 2. A light steel rectangular cantilever ($L = 1.0\text{ m}$, breadth $b = 2.0\text{ cm}$, depth $d = 0.50\text{ cm}$, $Y = 2.0\times 10^{11}\text{ N m}^{-2}$) carries a $1.0\text{ kg}$ load at its free end ($g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$). Calculate its free-end depression.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part 1 — Two-Wire Suspension:**
> Let the weight $W$ hang at distance $x$ from the steel wire and $(100 - x)\text{ cm}$ from the brass wire. Rotational equilibrium about the suspension point gives $T_s x = T_b(100 - x) \implies \frac{T_s}{T_b} = \frac{100 - x}{x}$.
> - **(a) Equal Stresses ($\frac{T_s}{A_s} = \frac{T_b}{A_b}$):**
>   $$\frac{T_s}{T_b} = \frac{A_s}{A_b} = \frac{0.05}{0.10} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \frac{100 - x}{x} = \frac{1}{2} \implies 3x = 200 \implies \boxed{x = 66.67\text{ cm from steel wire}}$$
> - **(b) Equal Strains ($\frac{T_s}{A_s Y_s} = \frac{T_b}{A_b Y_b}$):**
>   $$\frac{T_s}{T_b} = \frac{A_s Y_s}{A_b Y_b} = \frac{(0.05)(2.0\times 10^{11})}{(0.10)(1.0\times 10^{11})} = 1 \implies \frac{100 - x}{x} = 1 \implies \boxed{x = 50.0\text{ cm (midpoint)}}$$
>
> **Part 2 — Cantilever Depression:**
> Geometrical moment of inertia: $I_g = \frac{b d^3}{12} = \frac{(0.020)(0.0050)^3}{12} = 2.0833\times 10^{-10}\text{ m}^4$.
> $$\delta = \frac{W L^3}{3 Y I_g} = \frac{(1.0)(9.8)(1.0)^3}{3(2.0\times 10^{11})(2.0833\times 10^{-10})} = \frac{9.8}{125.0} = 0.0784\text{ m} = \boxed{7.84\text{ cm}}$$

## 3. Ideal Inviscid Fluid Flow — Continuity, Euler's Equation & Bernoulli's Theorem

### 3.1 Eulerian vs. Lagrangian Viewpoints & the Material Derivative

Unlike a solid whose atoms stay anchored to equilibrium neighbors, fluid elements wander globally across space. Two complementary descriptions capture fluid kinematics:
- **Lagrangian description:** Follows the trajectory $\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{r}_0, t)$ of each labeled fluid parcel—identical to particle mechanics, but algebraically intractable when millions of parcels tangle.
- **Eulerian description:** Fixes spatial coordinates $\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)$ in the lab and specifies local velocity $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}, t)$, density $\rho(\mathbf{r}, t)$, and pressure $P(\mathbf{r}, t)$ fields.

To write Newton's Second Law in Eulerian coordinates, we must compute the acceleration $\mathbf{a} = \frac{D\mathbf{v}}{Dt}$ of a moving fluid parcel passing through $\mathbf{r}$ at time $t$ and reaching $\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{v}\,dt$ at $t + dt$. Applying the multivariable chain rule yields the **Material (Substantial) Derivative**:

$$\boxed{\frac{D\mathbf{v}}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + \left(v_x\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + v_y\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + v_z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\mathbf{v} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}}$$

The first term $\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}$ is **local acceleration** (zero in steady flow); the nonlinear second term $(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}$ is **convective acceleration** (nonzero even in strictly steady flow whenever fluid speeds up through a constriction or curves around a bend!).

---

### 3.2 Equation of Continuity (Conservation of Mass)

Consider a fixed arbitrary closed volume $V$ bounded by surface $S$ (outward area element $d\mathbf{S}$) with no mass sources or sinks inside. The rate of decrease of fluid mass inside $V$ must equal the net outward mass flux across $S$:

$$-\frac{d}{dt}\iiint_V \rho(\mathbf{r}, t)\,d^3r = \oiint_S \rho\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

Bringing $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}$ inside the fixed-volume integral and applying **Gauss's Divergence Theorem** to the RHS:

$$\iiint_V \left[\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v})\right] d^3r = 0$$

Because $V$ is completely arbitrary, the integrand vanishes pointwise, establishing the **Equation of Continuity**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}) = 0}$$

- **Incompressible Limit ($\rho = \text{const}$):** $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ (velocity field is strictly solenoidal).
- **Streamtube Integral Form:** Across two cross-sections $A_1, A_2$ of a steady incompressible streamtube, $Q = A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 = \text{const}$.

---

### 3.3 Euler's Equation & Unabridged Vector Derivation of Bernoulli's Theorem

**(i) Ideal Fluid Assumptions:** Zero shear viscosity ($\eta = 0$, so surface stress reduces purely to isotropic hydrostatic pressure $P$), subject to conservative gravitational body force $\mathbf{f} = -\nabla(gz)$ per unit mass.

**(ii) Euler's Equation of Motion:** The net pressure force on volume $V$ is $-\oiint_S P\,d\mathbf{S} = -\iiint_V \nabla P\,d^3r$, giving pressure force per unit volume $-\nabla P$. Equating mass $\times$ material acceleration to total force per unit volume yields **Euler's Equation**:

$$\boxed{\rho\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\right] = -\nabla P - \rho\nabla(gz)}$$

**(iii) Derivation of Bernoulli's Theorem:** Apply the exact vector identity $(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v} = \nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2\right) - \mathbf{v}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{v})$ to **steady** ($\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t} = \mathbf{0}$), **incompressible** ($\rho = \text{const}$) Euler flow and divide by $\rho$:

$$\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}v^2\right) - \mathbf{v}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{v}) = -\nabla\left(\frac{P}{\rho}\right) - \nabla(gz)$$

$$\implies \nabla\left(\frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{1}{2}v^2 + gz\right) = \mathbf{v}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{v})$$

Take the dot product with an infinitesimal displacement $d\mathbf{r}$ lying strictly along a **streamline** (so $d\mathbf{r} \parallel \mathbf{v}$). Since $\mathbf{v}\cdot[\mathbf{v}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{v})] \equiv 0$, the differential vanishes identically along every streamline, proving **Bernoulli's Theorem**:

$$\boxed{\frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{1}{2}v^2 + gz = \text{const} \iff P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho gz = \text{const} \qquad (\text{along any streamline})}$$

*(Irrotational Upgrade: If the flow is also **irrotational**, meaning vorticity $\boldsymbol{\zeta} \equiv \nabla\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\nabla\left(\frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{1}{2}v^2 + gz\right) = \mathbf{0}$ everywhere—making the Bernoulli constant **globally identical across all streamlines**!)*

- **Three Equivalent Physical Forms:**
  1. **Energy per unit volume** ($\text{J m}^{-3} = \text{Pa}$): $P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho gz = \text{const}$ (static pressure + dynamic pressure + hydrostatic head).
  2. **Energy per unit mass** ($\text{J kg}^{-1}$): $\frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{1}{2}v^2 + gz = \text{const}$.
  3. **Hydraulic head** ($\text{m}$): $\frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + z = \text{const}$ (pressure head + velocity head + elevation head).

### 3.4 Classical Engineering Applications of Bernoulli's Theorem

1. **Venturi Meter (Pipeline Discharge Measurement):** A horizontal pipe ($z_1 = z_2$) of area $A_1$ narrows smoothly to a throat of area $A_2 < A_1$. Continuity $Q = A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$ accelerates the fluid in the throat, dropping its static pressure by $\Delta P = P_1 - P_2$:
   $$\Delta P = \frac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}\rho Q^2\left(\frac{1}{A_2^2} - \frac{1}{A_1^2}\right) \implies \boxed{Q = A_1 A_2\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta P}{\rho\left(A_1^2 - A_2^2\right)}}}$$
2. **Pitot–Static Tube (Airspeed & Stream Speed Sensor):** A streamlined probe faces directly into a flow of static pressure $P$ and speed $v$. Fluid decelerates to zero at the nose **stagnation point** ($v_{\text{stag}} = 0$), raising stagnation pressure to $P_{\text{stag}} = P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2$:
   $$\boxed{v = \sqrt{\frac{2\left(P_{\text{stag}} - P\right)}{\rho}}}$$
3. **Torricelli's Efflux Speed (Draining Reservoir):** For a small orifice of area $A_2$ at depth $h$ below the free surface ($A_1 \gg A_2$) of an open tank ($P_1 = P_2 = P_{\text{atm}}$):
   $$\frac{1}{2}v_1^2 + gh = \frac{1}{2}v_2^2 \xrightarrow{v_1 = (A_2/A_1)v_2} \boxed{v_{\text{efflux}} = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 - (A_2/A_1)^2}} \xrightarrow{A_2 \ll A_1} \sqrt{2gh}}$$
   *(Identical to free-fall speed from height $h$; real sharp-edged orifices contract the outgoing jet to a **vena contracta** with discharge coefficient $C_d \approx 0.62$.)*

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Pipeline Pressure Drop & Venturi Meter Discharge with Mercury Manometer
> **Problem:**
> 1. In a horizontal pipeline of uniform cross-section, the pressure falls by $5\text{ N m}^{-2}$ between two points separated by $1\text{ km}$. Assuming ideal Bernoulli flow of oil ($\rho = 800\text{ kg m}^{-3}$), compute the change in kinetic energy per kilogram of oil between these two points (and briefly note the physical caveat for real incompressible pipe flow).
> 2. Water ($\rho_w = 1000\text{ kg m}^{-3}$) flows through a horizontal Venturi meter tapering from diameter $d_1 = 20\text{ cm}$ to throat diameter $d_2 = 10\text{ cm}$. A U-tube mercury manometer ($\rho_{\text{Hg}} = 13{,}600\text{ kg m}^{-3}$) connected across the inlet and throat shows a mercury level difference $h = 10\text{ cm}$. Calculate the volume flow rate $Q$ (in $\text{m}^3\text{ s}^{-1}$ and $\text{L s}^{-1}$, $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$).
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part 1 — Horizontal Oil Pipeline:**
> From Bernoulli's equation per unit mass along a horizontal streamline ($z_1 = z_2$):
> $$\frac{P_1}{\rho} + \frac{1}{2}v_1^2 = \frac{P_2}{\rho} + \frac{1}{2}v_2^2 \implies \Delta(\text{KE per kg}) = \frac{1}{2}(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \frac{P_1 - P_2}{\rho}$$
> $$\Delta(\text{KE per kg}) = \frac{5\text{ N m}^{-2}}{800\text{ kg m}^{-3}} = \boxed{6.25\times 10^{-3}\text{ J kg}^{-1}}$$
> *(Physical subtlety worth noting in Mains: Strictly speaking, for an incompressible fluid in a pipe of truly uniform cross-section, continuity $A v = \text{const}$ forbids speed changes, so in real pipelines a pressure fall over $1\text{ km}$ dissipates into viscous heat rather than kinetic energy; the calculation above follows the idealized inviscid energy conversion $\Delta P/\rho$.)*
>
> **Part 2 — Venturi Meter with Mercury Manometer:**
> - Areas: $A_1 = \frac{\pi d_1^2}{4} = \pi(0.10)^2 = 3.1416\times 10^{-2}\text{ m}^2$; $A_2 = \pi(0.05)^2 = 7.8540\times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$; ratio $\frac{A_2}{A_1} = \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$.
> - **Crucial Manometer Buoyancy Correction:** Water fills the manometer column above the lower mercury meniscus, so:
>   $$\Delta P = (\rho_{\text{Hg}} - \rho_w)g h = (13{,}600 - 1000)(9.8)(0.10) = 12{,}348\text{ Pa}$$
> - Throat velocity & discharge:
>   $$v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2\Delta P}{\rho_w\big[1 - (A_2/A_1)^2\big]}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(12{,}348)}{1000(1 - 1/16)}} = \sqrt{\frac{24{,}696}{937.5}} = 5.132\text{ m s}^{-1}$$
>   $$Q = A_2 v_2 = (7.8540\times 10^{-3})(5.132) = \boxed{0.0403\text{ m}^3\text{ s}^{-1} = 40.3\text{ L s}^{-1}}$$

## 4. Viscous Laminar Flow — Poiseuille's Equation, Stokes' Law & Reynolds Turbulence

### 4.1 Newton's Law of Viscosity, Kinematic Viscosity & Units

Real fluids resist internal shearing motion through **viscosity** (internal fluid friction). In steady **streamline (laminar) flow**, fluid moves in smooth parallel layers without transverse mixing. **Newton's Law of Viscosity** states that tangential shear stress $\tau$ between adjacent layers is proportional to the transverse velocity gradient $\frac{dv}{dy}$:

$$\boxed{\tau = \frac{F}{A} = \eta\frac{dv}{dy}}$$

- **Coefficient of Dynamic Viscosity ($\eta$):**
  - SI unit: $\text{Pa s} = \text{N s m}^{-2} = \text{kg m}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}$ (called **1 Poiseuille**, $\text{Pl}$).
  - CGS unit: **1 Poise ($\text{P}$)** $= 1\text{ dyne s cm}^{-2} = 1\text{ g cm}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}$.
  - Exact conversion: **$1\text{ Pa s} = 10\text{ Poise}$** ($1\text{ Poise} = 0.1\text{ Pa s}$).
- **Coefficient of Kinematic Viscosity ($\nu \equiv \eta/\rho$):** Measures momentum diffusivity!
  - SI unit: $\text{m}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$.
  - CGS unit: **1 Stokes ($\text{St}$)** $= 1\text{ cm}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$.
  - Exact conversion: **$1\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-1} = 10^4\text{ Stokes}$** ($1\text{ St} = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$).
- **Microscopic Origin & Temperature Dependence:**
  - In **liquids**, viscosity arises from short-range intermolecular cohesion resisting slip; heating weakens cohesion, so $\eta_{\text{liquid}}$ **decreases rapidly** with $T$.
  - In **gases**, viscosity arises from random thermal diffusion transporting fast/slow momentum transversely across adjacent layers ($\eta_{\text{gas}} = \frac{1}{3}\rho\bar{c}\lambda \propto \sqrt{T}$); heating speeds molecular diffusion, so $\eta_{\text{gas}}$ **increases with $\sqrt{T}$** and is remarkably independent of gas pressure (Maxwell's law)!

---

### 4.2 Hagen–Poiseuille's Equation with Kinetic-Energy & Entrance Corrections

#### Unabridged Derivation of Poiseuille's Formula

**(i) Explicit Physical Assumptions (5 Conditions):**
1. Flow is **steady** ($\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t} = \mathbf{0}$) and strictly **laminar** ($Re < 2000$), parallel to the tube axis.
2. Fluid is **incompressible** and **Newtonian** ($\eta = \text{const}$).
3. Rigid horizontal circular capillary of uniform radius $R$ and length $L \gg R$ (fully developed flow; zero gravity gradient along axis).
4. **No-slip boundary condition** at the solid wall: $v(R) = 0$.
5. Pressure is uniform over any cross-section ($\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} = 0$) and falls linearly along the tube: $-\frac{dP}{dz} = \frac{\Delta P}{L}$.

**(ii) Viscous Force Balance on a Coaxial Fluid Cylinder:** Isolate a coaxial fluid cylinder of radius $r$ ($0 < r < R$) and length $L$. Because the flow is steady and unaccelerated, the forward driving force from pressure drop $\Delta P = P_1 - P_2$ on end faces ($\pi r^2$) exactly balances the backward viscous shear drag on the curved cylindrical boundary (area $2\pi r L$):

$$\Delta P (\pi r^2) = -\eta(2\pi r L)\frac{dv}{dr}$$

*(Sign check: $v(r)$ decreases outward toward the wall, so $\frac{dv}{dr} < 0$, making the RHS positive!)* Solving for $\frac{dv}{dr}$:

$$\frac{dv}{dr} = -\frac{\Delta P}{2\eta L}r$$

Integrating from $r$ to $R$ using the no-slip boundary condition $v(R) = 0$:

$$\int_{v(r)}^0 dv' = -\frac{\Delta P}{2\eta L}\int_r^R r'\,dr' \implies \boxed{v(r) = \frac{\Delta P}{4\eta L}\left(R^2 - r^2\right)}$$

The velocity distribution forms an exact **paraboloid of revolution** (Poiseuille profile), peaking on the centerline at $v_{\max} = \frac{\Delta P R^2}{4\eta L}$ (with mean velocity $\bar{v} = \frac{Q}{\pi R^2} = \frac{1}{2}v_{\max}$).

**(iii) Total Volume Discharge Rate $Q$:** Integrate the volume passing per second through annular rings of area $2\pi r\,dr$:

$$Q = \int_0^R v(r)(2\pi r\,dr) = \frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\int_0^R \left(R^2 r - r^3\right)dr = \frac{\pi\Delta P}{2\eta L}\left[\frac{R^4}{2} - \frac{R^4}{4}\right]$$

$$\boxed{Q = \frac{\pi \Delta P R^4}{8\eta L}}$$

*(Dimensional Check: $[\Delta P R^4 / (\eta L)] = \frac{\text{Pa}\cdot\text{m}^4}{(\text{Pa s})\cdot\text{m}} = \text{m}^3\text{ s}^{-1}$. Note the dramatic **$R^4$ fourth-power sensitivity**: narrowing a pipe or blood artery by just $10\%$ shrinks flow by $34.4\%$ at fixed pressure, or forces a $+52.4\%$ jump in pressure to sustain normal flow!)*

#### Hydraulic Resistance & Capillary Networks (Series & Parallel)

Writing Poiseuille's law in Ohm's-law form $\Delta P = Q R_H$ defines **hydraulic resistance** $R_H = \frac{8\eta L}{\pi R^4}$:
- **Two Capillaries in Series ($L_1, R_1$ and $L_2, R_2$):** Continuity enforces equal discharge $Q_1 = Q_2 = Q$, while total pressure drop splits additively $\Delta P = \Delta P_1 + \Delta P_2 = Q(R_{H,1} + R_{H,2})$:
  $$\boxed{Q_{\text{series}} = \frac{\pi \Delta P}{8\eta}\left(\frac{L_1}{R_1^4} + \frac{L_2}{R_2^4}\right)^{-1}, \qquad \frac{\Delta P_1}{\Delta P_2} = \frac{L_1 / R_1^4}{L_2 / R_2^4}}$$
- **Two Capillaries in Parallel:** Equal pressure drop $\Delta P$, discharges add: $Q_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{\pi\Delta P}{8\eta}\left(\frac{R_1^4}{L_1} + \frac{R_2^4}{L_2}\right)$.

#### Kinetic-Energy (Hagenbach) & End (Couette) Corrections + Limitations

In precision capillary viscometry, two systematic deviations appear at finite flow rates:

1. **Kinetic Energy (Hagenbach) Correction:** Not all of the reservoir pressure head $\Delta P$ is dissipated against viscosity; part accelerates liquid from rest into the exiting parabolic jet! Computing the exact kinetic energy exiting per second:
   $$\dot{E}_k = \int_0^R \left(\frac{1}{2}\rho v(r)^2\right)v(r)(2\pi r\,dr) = \pi\rho v_{\max}^3\int_0^R \left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)^3 r\,dr = \frac{\pi\rho R^2}{8}v_{\max}^3$$
   Substituting $v_{\max} = \frac{2Q}{\pi R^2}$ gives $\dot{E}_k = \frac{\rho Q^3}{\pi^2 R^4}$. Dividing by $Q$ yields the effective pressure loss consumed by kinetic energy (notice it equals **$2 \times \frac{1}{2}\rho\bar{v}^2$**—twice the uniform-plug value!):
   $$\Delta P_{\text{eff}} = \Delta P - \frac{m\rho Q^2}{\pi^2 R^4}$$
   where theoretically $m = 1$ for an ideal parabolic jet and empirically $m \approx 1.12$ accounting for velocity-profile rearrangement at the inlet.
2. **End (Entrance-Length / Couette) Correction:** Over an entrance length $L_e \approx 0.06\,Re\cdot (2R)$, fluid entering with a flat plug profile experiences sharper wall gradients while developing into a full parabola, adding extra drag equivalent to increasing effective tube length to $L_{\text{eff}} = L + 1.64 R$:
   $$\boxed{\eta = \frac{\pi R^4}{8 Q(L + 1.64 R)}\left(\Delta P - \frac{m\rho Q^2}{\pi^2 R^4}\right)}$$
3. **Key Limitations of Poiseuille's Law:** Breaks down completely when (i) flow becomes turbulent ($Re > 2000$), (ii) fluid is compressible (gases require integrating $P\,dP \implies Q_{\text{out}} = \frac{\pi(P_1^2 - P_2^2)R^4}{16\eta L P_2}$!), (iii) tube is too short ($L \not\gg L_e$) or curved/flexible, or (iv) fluid is non-Newtonian (e.g., high-shear blood, polymer melts).

### 4.3 Stokes' Law, Terminal Velocity & Millikan Oil-Drop Mechanics

#### Dimensional Derivation & Physical Decomposition of Stokes' Law

Consider a smooth rigid sphere of radius $r$ moving slowly (**creeping flow**, $Re \ll 1$, where inertial acceleration $\rho(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}$ is negligible compared to viscous diffusion $\eta\nabla^2\mathbf{v}$) at steady velocity $v$ through an infinite, homogeneous viscous fluid of dynamic viscosity $\eta$.

Assume a power-law relation $F_v = k\,\eta^a r^b v^c$ (with dimensionless constant $k$). Equating SI dimensions on both sides:

$$[M L T^{-2}] = [M L^{-1} T^{-1}]^a [L]^b [L T^{-1}]^c = [M^a L^{-a + b + c} T^{-a - c}]$$

Matching exponents of $M$, $T$, and $L$:
- $M$: $a = 1$
- $T$: $-a - c = -2 \implies c = 1$
- $L$: $-a + b + c = 1 \implies -1 + b + 1 = 1 \implies b = 1$

Thus $F_v = k\,\eta r v$. Solving the exact linearized Stokes creeping-flow equations ($\nabla P = \eta\nabla^2\mathbf{v}$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$) with no-slip boundary conditions on the sphere surface yields **$k = 6\pi$**—splitting cleanly into **$4\pi\eta r v$ from tangential viscous skin friction** plus **$2\pi\eta r v$ from fore–aft pressure form drag**:

$$\boxed{F_v = 6\pi\eta r v}$$

#### Terminal Velocity ($v_t$) & Droplet Coalescence Scaling

A sphere of radius $r$ and density $\rho_s$ falling vertically through a fluid of density $\rho_f$ reaches **terminal velocity $v_t$** when net downward gravity balances upward Archimedes buoyancy plus Stokes drag:

$$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_s g = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_f g + 6\pi\eta r v_t \implies \boxed{v_t = \frac{2 r^2 (\rho_s - \rho_f) g}{9\eta}}$$

- **Coalescence Scaling Law:** If $N$ identical droplets of radius $r$ merge into one sphere of radius $R$, volume conservation gives $\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = N\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right) \implies R = N^{1/3}r$. Because $v_t \propto r^2$:
  $$\boxed{v_{t,\text{new}} = N^{2/3}\,v_t}$$

#### Application to Millikan's Oil-Drop Experiment & Cunningham Slip Correction

Robert Millikan (1909) measured the elementary electron charge $e$ by combining Stokes' law with vertical electrostatic balancing of microscopic oil droplets ($\rho_o$ in air $\rho_a$):

1. **Electric Field Off ($\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}$):** Droplet falls at constant terminal speed $v_1$, directly revealing its unseeably tiny radius $r$:
   $$\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3(\rho_o - \rho_a)g = 6\pi\eta r v_1 \implies r = \sqrt{\frac{9\eta v_1}{2(\rho_o - \rho_a)g}}$$
2. **Vertical Field $E$ Switched On:** Pulling a droplet of charge $q$ upward at steady terminal velocity $v_2$:
   $$q E - \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3(\rho_o - \rho_a)g = 6\pi\eta r v_2 \implies q E = 6\pi\eta r(v_1 + v_2)$$
   Eliminating $r$ yields the droplet's exact charge strictly in terms of measurable speeds:
   $$\boxed{q = 6\pi\eta\left[\frac{9\eta v_1}{2(\rho_o - \rho_a)g}\right]^{1/2}\frac{v_1 + v_2}{E}}$$
3. **Cunningham Slip Correction:** When droplet radius $r \sim 1\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ approaches the air mean free path $\lambda \approx 65\text{ nm}$, air ceases to behave as a smooth continuum and molecules slip past the drop, reducing drag to $F_v = \frac{6\pi\eta r v}{1 + A(\lambda/r)}$ ($A \approx 0.86$). Incorporating this correction resolved Millikan's early $e$-value precision to $0.1\%$!

### 4.4 Reynolds Number ($Re$) & the Laminar-to-Turbulent Transition

Why does slow honey stream smoothly out of a capillary while fast water erupts into chaotic swirling eddies? Osborne Reynolds (1883) showed that fluid stability hinges on a single dimensionless competition between **inertial convective forces** ($\sim \rho v^2 / D$, which amplify disturbances) and **viscous damping forces** ($\sim \eta v / D^2$, which smooth velocity gradients out):

$$\boxed{Re \equiv \frac{\text{Inertial Force}}{\text{Viscous Force}} = \frac{\rho v D}{\eta} = \frac{v D}{\nu}}$$

where $D$ is the characteristic flow length (**pipe diameter $D = 2R$** for pipe flow, or **sphere diameter $D = 2r$** for flow past a sphere).

1. **Transition Regimes in Circular Pipes:**
   - **$Re < 2000$:** Strictly **laminar (streamline)** flow; Poiseuille's law holds accurately, and pressure drop scales linearly with mean speed ($\Delta P \propto v$).
   - **$2000 < Re < 4000$:** **Transitional / intermittent** zone (sensitive to inlet roughness and vibrations).
   - **$Re > 4000$:** Fully **turbulent** flow; chaotic 3D vortices flatten the mean velocity profile from parabolic to blunt logarithmic, and frictional pressure drop escalates sharply ($\Delta P \propto v^2$).
   - **Critical Velocity:** $v_c = \frac{Re_c\,\eta}{\rho D}$ (lower for wider pipes and low-viscosity dense fluids).
2. **Validity Bound for Stokes' Law Past a Sphere:** Stokes' linear drag ($F_v = 6\pi\eta r v$) strictly requires creeping flow **$Re = \frac{2\rho_f r v}{\eta} \lesssim 0.5\text{–}1$**! At moderate $Re$ ($1 < Re < 10^3$), Oseen's first inertial correction multiplies Stokes drag by $\left(1 + \frac{3}{16}Re\right)$; at high $Re$ ($10^3 < Re < 2\times 10^5$), turbulent wake separation replaces linear Stokes drag with quadratic Newton drag $F_d = \frac{1}{2}C_d\rho_f(\pi r^2)v^2$ ($C_d \approx 0.47$).
3. **Principle of Dynamic Similarity:** Two geometrically similar flows (e.g., a $1:20$ scale aircraft wing in a pressurized wind tunnel vs. a full-scale airliner) obey identical scaled Navier–Stokes trajectories if and only if they share the exact same Reynolds number $Re$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Narrowed Coronary Artery (Poiseuille $R^4$) & Series Capillaries
> **Problem:**
> 1. A cardiologist reports that the radius of a patient's left anterior descending coronary artery has narrowed by $10\%$. Assuming blood viscosity $\eta$ and artery length $L$ remain unchanged and flow remains laminar, what percentage increase in blood pressure difference is required to maintain normal blood flow rate?
> 2. Two horizontal capillary tubes of lengths $2l$ and $l$ with internal radii $r$ and $2r$ respectively are connected in series with streamline water flow. If the pressure difference across the first capillary is $P$, determine the pressure difference across the second capillary.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Part 1 — Narrowed Coronary Artery:**
> Initial radius $R_1$, narrowed radius $R_2 = (1 - 0.10)R_1 = 0.90 R_1$. Maintaining constant volume flow rate $Q_1 = Q_2$ under Poiseuille's equation $Q = \frac{\pi\Delta P R^4}{8\eta L}$:
> $$\Delta P_1 R_1^4 = \Delta P_2 R_2^4 \implies \frac{\Delta P_2}{\Delta P_1} = \left(\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)^4 = \left(\frac{1}{0.90}\right)^4 = \frac{1}{0.6561} = 1.5242$$
> Percentage increase required:
> $$\frac{\Delta P_2 - \Delta P_1}{\Delta P_1}\times 100\% = (1.5242 - 1)\times 100\% = \boxed{52.4\%}$$
> *(Notice how the linear differential approximation $4 \times 10\% = 40\%$ severely underestimates the exact finite $R^{-4}$ jump of $52.4\%$!)*
>
> **Part 2 — Series Capillaries:**
> Since flow is in series, continuity demands equal volume flow rate $Q_1 = Q_2$:
> $$\frac{\pi P r^4}{8\eta(2l)} = \frac{\pi P_2 (2r)^4}{8\eta l} \implies \frac{P r^4}{2l} = \frac{16 P_2 r^4}{l} \implies \boxed{P_2 = \frac{P}{32}}$$

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Stokes Terminal Velocity, Droplet Coalescence & Reynolds Validity Audit
> **Problem:** A spherical water droplet ($\rho_w = 1000\text{ kg m}^{-3}$) of radius $r = 0.04\text{ mm}$ falls through still air ($\rho_a = 1.29\text{ kg m}^{-3}$, $\eta = 1.8\times 10^{-4}\text{ poise}$, $g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}$).
> 1. Calculate the terminal velocity $v_t$ of the single drop.
> 2. If $100$ such identical drops coalesce into a single spherical drop, find the new terminal velocity predicted by Stokes' law.
> 3. Compute the Reynolds numbers for both cases and comment physically on the validity of Stokes' law after coalescence.
>
> **Solution:**
> **Unit Conversions (CGS/mm $\to$ SI):**
> $r = 0.04\text{ mm} = 4.0\times 10^{-5}\text{ m}$; $\eta = 1.8\times 10^{-4}\text{ poise} = 1.8\times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa s}$.
>
> **Part 1 — Single Drop Terminal Velocity:**
> $$v_t = \frac{2 r^2(\rho_w - \rho_a)g}{9\eta} = \frac{2(4.0\times 10^{-5})^2(1000 - 1.29)(9.8)}{9(1.8\times 10^{-5})} = \frac{3.132\times 10^{-5}}{1.62\times 10^{-4}} = \boxed{0.193\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$
>
> **Part 2 — Coalescence of $N = 100$ Drops:**
> Volume conservation gives $R = N^{1/3}r = (100)^{1/3}(4.0\times 10^{-5}\text{ m}) = 1.857\times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$. Since $v_t \propto r^2$:
> $$v_{t,\text{new}} = N^{2/3}\,v_t = (100)^{2/3}(0.1933\text{ m s}^{-1}) = (21.544)(0.1933) = \boxed{4.17\text{ m s}^{-1}}$$
>
> **Part 3 — Reynolds Number Validity Audit ($Re = \frac{\rho_a (2r) v}{\eta}$):**
> - **Single drop:** $Re_1 = \frac{(1.29)(8.0\times 10^{-5})(0.1933)}{1.8\times 10^{-5}} = 1.11 \sim O(1)$ (sits cleanly at the upper validity edge of creeping Stokes flow).
> - **Coalesced drop:** $Re_2 = \frac{(1.29)(3.713\times 10^{-4})(4.165)}{1.8\times 10^{-5}} = 111 \gg 1$! Because $Re_2 \gg 1$, inertial wake drag becomes substantial, meaning the naive Stokes value $4.17\text{ m s}^{-1}$ significantly overestimates the real physical fall speed of the merged raindrop.

## 6. Exam-Hall Execution — Traps & Hand-Drawing Diagram Specs

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> 1. **CGS $\to$ SI Conversion Traps (Repeatedly Tested in CSE 2018 & 2020!):**
>    - Stress / Elastic Moduli: **$1\text{ dyne cm}^{-2} = 0.1\text{ N m}^{-2}$** (NOT $10^{-5}$!).
>    - Dynamic Viscosity: **$1\text{ Poise} = 0.1\text{ Pa s}$** ($1\text{ Pa s} = 10\text{ Poise}$).
>    - Kinematic Viscosity: **$1\text{ Stokes} = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$** ($1\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-1} = 10^4\text{ Stokes}$).
> 2. **Linear Approximation Trap on Poiseuille $R^4$ Changes (CSE 2026!):** Never use $\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -4\frac{\Delta R}{R}$ when radius narrows by $10\%$ or $20\%$! The exact ratio $(0.9)^{-4} = 1.5242$ gives a **$52.4\%$ increase**, whereas the linear approximation gives a wrong $40\%$.
> 3. **Diameter vs. Radius Factor of 16 in Torsion & Poiseuille:** Both $\Gamma \propto R^4$ and $Q \propto R^4$ scale with the **fourth power of radius**. Accidentally substituting diameter $d = 2R$ in place of $R$ introduces a catastrophic **factor of $16$ error**!
> 4. **Manometer Buoyancy Factor in Venturi Meters:** In a U-tube mercury manometer measuring water flow, $\Delta P = (\rho_{\text{Hg}} - \rho_w)gh$, **NOT** $\rho_{\text{Hg}}gh$! Forgetting $-\rho_w$ inflates $\Delta P$ by $7.9\%$.
> 5. **Confusing Geometrical Moment $I_g$ with Mass Moment of Inertia $I$:** In Euler–Bernoulli bending ($M_b = Y I_g / R_c$), $I_g = \iint z^2\,dA$ has units of **$\text{m}^4$** (no mass or density!), equaling $\frac{bd^3}{12}$ (rectangle) or $\frac{\pi r^4}{4}$ (circle)—never confuse it with $\frac{1}{12}Md^2$ or polar $J = \frac{\pi r^4}{2}$!
> 6. **Missing $-1 < \sigma$ Lower Bound:** Students commonly state $0 < \sigma < 0.5$ instead of the true thermodynamic stability bounds **$-1 < \sigma < 0.5$** proven from $Y = 2\eta(1+\sigma) > 0$ and $Y = 3K(1-2\sigma) > 0$. State the exact mathematical range $(-1, 0.5)$ first, then note that ordinary metals lie in $(0, 0.5)$.
> 7. **Radius vs. Diameter in Reynolds Number:** Always state explicitly whether you use $D = 2R$ (universal engineering convention: $Re_c \approx 2000$ for pipes, $Re \lesssim 1$ for Stokes spheres) vs. radius $R$ ($Re_{c,R} \approx 1000$). Using $D = 2R$ aligns with every modern textbook and UPSC grading key.

### 2. Examiner Step-Marking & Numerical Trap Signature

- **Skipping the Diagonal Stress Equivalence in $Y = 2\eta(1 + \sigma)$ (CSE 2016):** Jumping straight to $\varepsilon_{BD} = \frac{\tau}{Y}(1+\sigma)$ without first proving by diagonal-plane force balance that pure shear $\tau$ is equivalent to orthogonal tension $+\tau$ and compression $-\tau$ forfeits 3–4 step-marks out of 10.
- **Silent CGS-to-SI Conversion Errors (CSE 2018, 2020):** Examiners deliberately plant CGS units ($\text{dynes cm}^{-2}$, $\text{poise}$, $\text{cm}$, $\text{mm}$) inside SI stems. Failing to convert $8\times 10^{11}\text{ dynes cm}^{-2} \to 8\times 10^{10}\text{ Pa}$ or $1.8\times 10^{-4}\text{ poise} \to 1.8\times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa s}$ produces an exact $10\times$ numerical error.
- **Omitting Explicit Assumptions & Corrections in Poiseuille's Law (CSE 2013):** Merely writing $Q = \frac{\pi\Delta P R^4}{8\eta L}$ without listing laminar $Re < 2000$, no-slip $v(R) = 0$, horizontal axis, incompressible Newtonian fluid, and both kinetic-energy ($\Delta P_{\text{KE}}$) and entrance-length ($L + 1.64R$) corrections drops marks sharply on theory tags.
- **Ambiguity Between Twist Angle $\theta$ and Rim Shift $s = R\theta$ (CSE 2020):** When a question asks for *"relative shift between the ends of the shaft"*, boxing **both** the angular twist $\theta$ (in radians and degrees) and the circumferential linear arc shift $s = R\theta$ makes your script bulletproof against either marking-key interpretation.

### 3. Unseen Conceptual & Analytical Twists

1. **Compressible Isothermal Gas Flow Through a Capillary Tube (Modified Poiseuille Law):** An ideal gas of constant viscosity $\eta$ flows isothermally ($P/\rho = \text{const}$) in steady laminar motion through a horizontal capillary tube of length $L$ and radius $R$ from inlet pressure $P_1$ to outlet pressure $P_2$. Replacing constant volume discharge $Q$ with constant **mass discharge** $\dot{m} = \rho(z) Q(z)$ and integrating the differential Poiseuille law $Q(z) = -\frac{\pi R^4}{8\eta}\frac{dP}{dz}$ from $z = 0$ to $L$, prove that the volume flow rate measured at the outlet pressure $P_2$ is $Q_{\text{out}} = \frac{\pi R^4(P_1^2 - P_2^2)}{16\eta L P_2}$, and verify that it reduces to standard incompressible Poiseuille's formula in the limit $(P_1 - P_2) \ll P_2$.
2. **Maximum Safe Rotational Speed & Elastic Energy of a Twisted Hollow vs. Solid Shaft:** A solid cylindrical rod and a hollow cylindrical tube (inner radius $R_1$, outer radius $R_2$) of identical length $L$, total mass $M$, and shear modulus $\eta$ are twisted up to the same maximum allowable surface shear stress $\tau_{\max}$ before plastic yielding. Derive the exact ratios of (i) their maximum transmitted torques $\Gamma_{\max,\text{hollow}} / \Gamma_{\max,\text{solid}}$, and (ii) their maximum stored elastic torsional strain energies $U_{\max,\text{hollow}} / U_{\max,\text{solid}}$, showing why hollow shafts simultaneously store more energy and carry higher torque per kilogram.
