# Chapter 2: Power Supplies, Feedback Amplifiers, Oscillators & Operational Amplifiers

## 1. Rectifiers, Filters, and Voltage Regulators

### 1.1 Half-Wave, Center-Tapped Full-Wave, and Bridge Rectifiers

A regulated DC power supply converts alternating line voltage into ripple-free, load-invariant DC in four stages: **step-down transformer $\to$ diode rectifier $\to$ smoothing filter $\to$ voltage regulator**. Rectification exploits the unilateral conduction of $p\text{-}n$ junctions to flip or clip negative half-cycles of a sinusoidal secondary voltage $v_s(t) = V_m \sin\omega t$.

#### Analytical Derivation of Rectifier Figures of Merit

**Assumptions:** Piecewise-linear diode with constant forward resistance $r_f$, infinite reverse resistance, and negligible cut-in voltage ($V_\gamma \ll V_m$), feeding a resistive load $R_L$. Peak load current is $I_m = \frac{V_m}{r_f + R_L}$ for Half-Wave and Center-Tapped Full-Wave rectifiers, and $I_m = \frac{V_m}{2r_f + R_L}$ for the four-diode Bridge rectifier (two diodes conduct in series per half-cycle).

1. **Half-Wave Rectifier (HWR):** Current $i(\theta) = I_m \sin\theta$ for $0 \le \theta \le \pi$, and $0$ for $\pi < \theta \le 2\pi$.
   - *Average (DC) Current:*
     $$I_{dc} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi I_m \sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{I_m}{2\pi}\left[-\cos\theta\right]_0^\pi = \frac{I_m}{\pi}$$
   - *RMS Current:*
     $$I_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi I_m^2 \sin^2\theta\,d\theta} = I_m\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}} = \frac{I_m}{2}$$
   - *Ripple Factor ($\gamma \equiv I_{ac,rms}/I_{dc} = \sqrt{(I_{rms}/I_{dc})^2 - 1}$):*
     $$\gamma_{\text{HWR}} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{I_m/2}{I_m/\pi}\right)^2 - 1} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi^2}{4} - 1} = 1.211$$
   - *Rectification Efficiency ($\eta \equiv P_{dc}/P_{ac}$):*
     $$\eta_{\text{HWR}} = \frac{I_{dc}^2 R_L}{I_{rms}^2(r_f + R_L)} = \frac{(I_m/\pi)^2 R_L}{(I_m/2)^2(r_f + R_L)} = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{1}{1 + r_f/R_L}\right) \le 40.53\%$$
   - *Peak Inverse Voltage (PIV):* During the blocking half-cycle, the full secondary peak appears across the reverse-biased diode: $\text{PIV} = V_m$.

2. **Full-Wave Rectifiers — Center-Tapped (CT-FWR) vs. Bridge (BR):** Both halves of the input cycle drive unidirectional current $i(\theta) = I_m |\sin\theta|$ through $R_L$, doubling the ripple frequency to $2f$.
   - *DC and RMS Currents:*
     $$I_{dc} = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi I_m \sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2 I_m}{\pi}, \qquad I_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi I_m^2 \sin^2\theta\,d\theta} = \frac{I_m}{\sqrt{2}}$$
   - *Ripple Factor and Maximum Efficiency:*
     $$\boxed{\gamma_{\text{FWR}} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{I_m/\sqrt{2}}{2I_m/\pi}\right)^2 - 1} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi^2}{8} - 1} = 0.483, \qquad \eta_{\text{FWR}} = \frac{8}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{1}{1 + n r_f/R_L}\right) \le 81.06\%}$$
     where $n = 1$ for CT-FWR and $n = 2$ for Bridge.
   - *Crucial PIV Contrast:* In a **Center-Tapped FWR**, when diode $D_1$ conducts, the cathode of $D_2$ sits at $+V_m$ while its anode sits at $-V_m$ (bottom secondary terminal), subjecting the blocking diode to **$\text{PIV} = 2V_m$**. In a **Bridge Rectifier**, each blocking diode spans only one secondary winding, halving the voltage stress to **$\text{PIV} = V_m$** while eliminating the bulky center-tapped transformer.

| Parameter | Half-Wave (HWR) | Center-Tapped Full-Wave (CT-FWR) | Bridge Full-Wave (BR) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Number of Diodes** | 1 | 2 (requires center-tap transformer) | 4 (no center-tap required) |
| **DC Load Current ($I_{dc}$)** | $I_m / \pi$ | $2 I_m / \pi$ | $2 I_m / \pi$ |
| **RMS Load Current ($I_{rms}$)** | $I_m / 2$ | $I_m / \sqrt{2}$ | $I_m / \sqrt{2}$ |
| **Ripple Factor ($\gamma$)** | $1.211$ (AC > DC!) | $0.483$ | $0.483$ |
| **Max Ideal Efficiency ($\eta_{\max}$)** | $40.53\%$ | $81.06\%$ | $81.06\%$ |
| **Peak Inverse Voltage (PIV)** | $V_m$ | **$2 V_m$** | **$V_m$** |
| **Fundamental Ripple Frequency** | $f$ | $2f$ | $2f$ |
| **Transformer Utilization Factor** | $0.287$ | $0.693$ | **$0.812$** |

### 1.2 Passive Smoothing Filters - Shunt C, Choke-Input LC, and Pi Networks

Even a full-wave rectifier outputs $48.3\%$ AC ripple—unusable for analog or digital circuits. Passive reactive filters exploit the stark impedance contrast between DC ($\omega = 0$) and the ripple harmonics ($2\omega, 4\omega, \dots$): a **shunt capacitor** offers an open circuit to DC and a near-short to AC, while a **series inductor (choke)** offers zero reactance to DC and high opposition $2\omega L$ to AC ripple.

1. **Shunt Capacitor ($C$) Filter:** Placed directly across $R_L$, the capacitor charges rapidly to $V_m$ at each rectified peak and discharges slowly through $R_L$ during the interval between peaks ($T_r = 1/f_r$, where $f_r = f$ for HWR and $2f$ for FWR). For $R_L C \gg T_r$, the discharge approximates a linear sawtooth of peak-to-peak voltage drop $V_{r,pp} \approx \frac{I_{dc} T_r}{C} = \frac{V_{dc}}{f_r R_L C}$. Since a triangular wave has RMS value $V_{r,rms} = \frac{V_{r,pp}}{2\sqrt{3}}$:
   $$\boxed{\gamma_C = \frac{V_{r,rms}}{V_{dc}} \approx \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}\,f_r R_L C} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}\,f R_L C} & \text{(HWR)} \\[8pt] \dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{3}\,f R_L C} & \text{(FWR)} \end{cases}}$$
   *Limiting Checks:* Light loads ($R_L \to \infty$) drive $\gamma_C \to 0$ and $V_{dc} \to V_m$, whereas heavy loads ($R_L$ small) worsen ripple and cause poor voltage regulation plus high repetitive diode surge currents.

2. **Choke-Input ($LC$) Filter (FWR):** Expanding $v_{\text{FWR}}(t)$ as a Fourier series yields $v(t) = \frac{2V_m}{\pi}\left(1 - \frac{2}{3}\cos 2\omega t - \frac{2}{15}\cos 4\omega t - \cdots\right)$. The dominant second-harmonic ripple has RMS magnitude $V_{2,rms} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}V_{dc}$. Provided $X_L = 2\omega L \gg X_C = \frac{1}{2\omega C}$ and $R_L \gg X_C$, the $L\text{–}C$ divider attenuates this ripple by $\frac{X_C}{X_L} = \frac{1}{4\omega^2 L C}$:
   $$\boxed{\gamma_{LC} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\cdot\frac{X_C}{X_L} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{12\omega^2 LC} = \frac{1}{6\sqrt{2}\,\omega^2 LC}}$$
   *Physical Distinction:* Unlike the C-filter, **$\gamma_{LC}$ is independent of load resistance $R_L$** (as long as $L$ exceeds the critical inductance $L_c = \frac{R_L}{3\omega}$ to keep choke current continuous), yielding superior regulation under heavy loads.

3. **Capacitor-Input $\pi$-Filter ($C_1\text{–}L\text{–}C_2$):** Placing $C_1$ ahead of the $LC$ stage lifts $V_{dc}$ close to $V_m$ (like a C-filter) while the trailing $L\text{–}C_2$ section crushes ripple by an additional factor $\frac{X_{C2}}{X_L}$:
   $$\boxed{\gamma_{\pi} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\,X_{C1} X_{C2}}{R_L X_L} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{8\omega^3 C_1 C_2 L R_L}}$$
   This achieves ultra-low ripple at light-to-moderate currents, though regulation remains softer than a choke-input filter.

### 1.3 Zener Shunt and Series-Pass Voltage Regulators

Passive filtering suppresses AC ripple, yet $V_{dc}$ still drifts whenever AC mains voltage fluctuates (**line variation**) or load current changes (**load variation**). A **voltage regulator** clamps $V_o$ rock-steady against both disturbances:
$$\text{Line Regulation} = \frac{\Delta V_o}{\Delta V_{\text{in}}}, \qquad \%\text{Load Regulation} = \frac{V_{o,\text{NL}} - V_{o,\text{FL}}}{V_{o,\text{FL}}} \times 100\%$$

#### Shunt Zener Voltage Regulator

A reverse-biased Zener diode ($V_Z$, dynamic resistance $r_z$) sits in parallel with load $R_L$, fed from unregulated supply $V_{\text{in}}$ through series dropping resistor $R_s$. By KCL, total supply current splits as $I_s = \frac{V_{\text{in}} - V_Z}{R_s} = I_Z + I_L$. Whenever $V_{\text{in}}$ rises or $I_L$ drops, the Zener absorbs the excess current across its near-vertical breakdown curve, holding $V_o \approx V_Z$.

Reliable regulation over $V_{\text{in}} \in [V_{\text{in,min}}, V_{\text{in,max}}]$ and $I_L \in [I_{L,\min}, I_{L,\max}]$ bounds $R_s$ between two strict worst-case limits:
- **Preventing Zener Drop-Out** (at $V_{\text{in,min}}$ and $I_{L,\max}$, keep $I_Z \ge I_{Z,\min}$):
  $$R_s \le R_{s,\max} = \frac{V_{\text{in,min}} - V_Z}{I_{Z,\min} + I_{L,\max}}$$
- **Preventing Thermal Burnout** (at $V_{\text{in,max}}$ and $I_{L,\min}$, keep $I_Z \le I_{Z,\max} = P_{Z,\max}/V_Z$):
  $$R_s \ge R_{s,\min} = \frac{V_{\text{in,max}} - V_Z}{I_{Z,\max} + I_{L,\min}}$$

$$\boxed{\frac{V_{\text{in,max}} - V_Z}{I_{Z,\max} + I_{L,\min}} \le R_s \le \frac{V_{\text{in,min}} - V_Z}{I_{Z,\min} + I_{L,\max}}, \qquad \text{Line Reg.} = \frac{r_z \parallel R_L}{R_s + (r_z \parallel R_L)} \approx \frac{r_z}{R_s}}$$

#### Series-Pass Transistor Regulator

A bare shunt Zener wastes heavy standby power at no load and cannot drive high-current loads. Placing an $n\text{-}p\text{-}n$ **series-pass BJT** in emitter-follower configuration—Collector to $V_{\text{in}}$, Base clamped at $V_Z$, Emitter feeding $R_L$—fixes output voltage at $V_o = V_Z - V_{BE}$. The pass transistor multiplies the Zener's tiny base current by $(\beta + 1)$ to deliver large load current $I_L$, slashing output resistance to $R_o \approx \frac{r_z + r_\pi}{\beta + 1}$ and improving efficiency dramatically. Inserting an Op-Amp error amplifier between a resistive divider $(R_1, R_2)$ across $V_o$ and the reference $V_Z$ establishes closed-loop negative feedback with selectable $V_o = \left(1 + \frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)V_Z$ and milliohm output impedance.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2.1: Bridge Rectifier C-Filter & Zener Shunt Regulator Design
> **Problem:** (a) A $50\text{ Hz}$ full-wave bridge rectifier feeds a $500\text{ }\Omega$ load through a shunt capacitor filter. Find the minimum capacitance $C$ required to hold the ripple factor $\gamma \le 2\%$. (b) Design a Zener shunt regulator using a $V_Z = 10.0\text{ V}$, $P_{Z,\max} = 2.0\text{ W}$ ($I_{Z,\min} = 10\text{ mA}$) diode to maintain $10.0\text{ V}$ across a load drawing $I_L \in [20\text{ mA}, 80\text{ mA}]$ when the unregulated input varies between $15.0\text{ V}$ and $20.0\text{ V}$. Find the allowable range of $R_s$ and the minimum power rating of $R_s$ if $R_s = 50.0\text{ }\Omega$ is selected.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Part (a) — Minimum Filter Capacitance:** For a full-wave rectifier, $f_r = 2f = 100\text{ Hz}$:
>    $$\gamma_C = \frac{1}{4\sqrt{3}\,f R_L C} \le 0.02 \implies C \ge \frac{1}{4\sqrt{3}(50\text{ Hz})(500\text{ }\Omega)(0.02)} = \frac{1}{3464.1} = 288.7\text{ }\mu\text{F}$$
>    Select standard value $C = 330\text{ }\mu\text{F}$.
>
> 2. **Part (b) — Zener Series Resistor Bounds:** Maximum safe Zener current is $I_{Z,\max} = \frac{P_{Z,\max}}{V_Z} = \frac{2.0\text{ W}}{10.0\text{ V}} = 200\text{ mA}$.
>    - Upper bound (minimum input $15\text{ V}$, maximum load $80\text{ mA}$):
>      $$R_{s,\max} = \frac{15.0 - 10.0}{(10 + 80)\text{ mA}} = \frac{5.0\text{ V}}{0.090\text{ A}} = 55.6\text{ }\Omega$$
>    - Lower bound (maximum input $20\text{ V}$, minimum load $20\text{ mA}$):
>      $$R_{s,\min} = \frac{20.0 - 10.0}{(200 + 20)\text{ mA}} = \frac{10.0\text{ V}}{0.220\text{ A}} = 45.5\text{ }\Omega$$
>    - Selecting $R_s = 50.0\text{ }\Omega$ (squarely inside $[45.5, 55.6]\text{ }\Omega$): worst-case power dissipated in $R_s$ occurs at $V_{\text{in,max}} = 20.0\text{ V}$:
>      $$P_{Rs,\max} = \frac{(V_{\text{in,max}} - V_Z)^2}{R_s} = \frac{(20.0 - 10.0)^2}{50.0} = 2.00\text{ W}$$
>      *(Verify Zener bounds at $R_s = 50\text{ }\Omega$: $I_{Z,\min,\text{act}} = \frac{15-10}{50} - 80\text{ mA} = 20\text{ mA} > 10\text{ mA}$; $I_{Z,\max,\text{act}} = \frac{20-10}{50} - 20\text{ mA} = 180\text{ mA} < 200\text{ mA}$. Both safe!)*
>
> $$\boxed{\text{(a) } C_{\min} = 289\text{ }\mu\text{F}; \qquad \text{(b) } 45.5\text{ }\Omega \le R_s \le 55.6\text{ }\Omega,\; P_{Rs,\max} = 2.00\text{ W}}$$

## 2. Amplifier Frequency Response and Negative Feedback Theory

### 2.1 RC-Coupled CE Amplifier Frequency Response and Miller Effect

An RC-coupled common-emitter BJT amplifier uses external blocking/coupling capacitors ($C_{c1}, C_{c2}$) to isolate DC bias points between stages and an emitter bypass capacitor ($C_E$) to restore AC gain across $R_E$. Because every real capacitor has frequency-dependent reactance $X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$, the amplifier's voltage gain $|A_v(f)|$ is flat only over an intermediate passband and rolls off at both frequency extremes.

1. **Mid-Band Region ($f_1 \ll f \ll f_2$):** External coupling/bypass capacitors behave as AC short circuits while internal transistor junction capacitances ($C_\pi, C_\mu$) remain open circuits. Voltage gain attains its flat real maximum $A_{vm} = -g_m R_L'$ ($180^\circ$ phase inversion).

2. **Low-Frequency Roll-Off ($f \lesssim f_1$):** Reactance of $C_{c1}, C_{c2}$, and $C_E$ rises as $1/f$, dropping progressively more signal voltage across the series coupling caps and introducing unbypassed emitter degeneration. Modeled by a single dominant high-pass pole at **lower $-3\text{ dB}$ cut-off frequency $f_1 = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{\text{eq},L} C}$**:
   $$\boxed{A_v(jf) = \frac{A_{vm}}{1 - j(f_1 / f)}, \qquad |A_v(f)| = \frac{|A_{vm}|}{\sqrt{1 + (f_1 / f)^2}}, \qquad \phi_L(f) = 180^\circ + \arctan\!\left(\frac{f_1}{f}\right)}$$
   At $f = f_1$, $|A_v| = |A_{vm}|/\sqrt{2} = 0.707\,|A_{vm}|$ (half-power / $-3\text{ dB}$ point) and phase leads mid-band by $+45^\circ$.

3. **High-Frequency Roll-Off ($f \gtrsim f_2$) and the Miller Effect:** At high frequencies, internal base–emitter diffusion capacitance $C_\pi$ and collector–base depletion capacitance $C_\mu$ ($C_{bc}$) plus stray wiring capacitance shunt AC signal to ground. Crucially, because $C_\mu$ bridges an inverting amplifier of gain $A_{vm} = -|A_{vm}|$, a small base increment $v_b$ swings the collector by $-|A_{vm}| v_b$, driving current:
   $$i_\mu = j\omega C_\mu(v_b - v_c) = j\omega C_\mu(1 - A_{vm}) v_b = j\omega \underbrace{C_\mu(1 + |A_{vm}|)}_{C_{M,\text{in}}} v_b$$
   This **Miller Effect** magnifies tiny $C_\mu$ ($\sim 2\text{ pF}$) by $(1 + |A_{vm}|)$ into hundreds of picofarads at the input node ($C_{\text{in,tot}} = C_\pi + C_\mu(1 + |A_{vm}|)$), setting the dominant **upper $-3\text{ dB}$ cut-off frequency $f_2 = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{\text{sig}}' C_{\text{in,tot}}}$**:
   $$\boxed{A_v(jf) = \frac{A_{vm}}{1 + j(f / f_2)}, \qquad |A_v(f)| = \frac{|A_{vm}|}{\sqrt{1 + (f / f_2)^2}}, \qquad \phi_H(f) = 180^\circ - \arctan\!\left(\frac{f}{f_2}\right)}$$
   At $f = f_2$, $|A_v| = 0.707\,|A_{vm}|$ and phase lags mid-band by $-45^\circ$. The amplifier's **3 dB Bandwidth** is $\text{BW} = f_2 - f_1 \approx f_2$, and because $C_{M,\text{in}} \propto |A_{vm}|$, the **Gain–Bandwidth Product** ($\text{GBP} = |A_{vm}|\cdot f_2$) remains nearly constant—one cannot double mid-band gain without roughly halving upper cut-off frequency.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2.2: RC-Coupled Amplifier Cut-Off Frequency Numerical (UPSC CSE 2024 Archetype)
> **Problem:** An RC-coupled transistor amplifier has a mid-frequency gain $|A_{vm}| = 100$, lower cut-off frequency $f_1 = 20\text{ Hz}$, and upper cut-off frequency $f_2 = 50\text{ kHz}$. Find the two frequencies ($f_L$ and $f_H$) at which the voltage gain magnitude drops to $80$, and evaluate the phase shift relative to mid-band at each frequency.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Low-Frequency Side ($f_L$):** Using $|A_v(f_L)| = \frac{|A_{vm}|}{\sqrt{1 + (f_1 / f_L)^2}}$:
>    $$80 = \frac{100}{\sqrt{1 + (f_1 / f_L)^2}} \implies \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{f_1}{f_L}\right)^2} = \frac{100}{80} = 1.25$$
>    $$1 + \left(\frac{f_1}{f_L}\right)^2 = 1.5625 \implies \left(\frac{f_1}{f_L}\right)^2 = 0.5625 \implies \frac{f_1}{f_L} = 0.75$$
>    $$f_L = \frac{20\text{ Hz}}{0.75} = 26.67\text{ Hz}$$
>    Phase shift relative to mid-band ($180^\circ$): $\Delta\phi_L = +\arctan(0.75) = +36.87^\circ$ (total phase $216.9^\circ$).
>
> 2. **High-Frequency Side ($f_H$):** Using $|A_v(f_H)| = \frac{|A_{vm}|}{\sqrt{1 + (f_H / f_2)^2}}$:
>    $$\frac{f_H}{f_2} = 0.75 \implies f_H = 0.75 \times 50\text{ kHz} = 37.5\text{ kHz}$$
>    Phase shift relative to mid-band: $\Delta\phi_H = -\arctan(0.75) = -36.87^\circ$ (total phase $143.1^\circ$).
>    *(Sanity check: Because $|A_v| = 80 > 70.7$, both frequencies must lie strictly **inside** the $-3\text{ dB}$ passband $[20\text{ Hz}, 50\text{ kHz}]$—confirmed!)*
>
> $$\boxed{f_L = 26.67\text{ Hz}\;(\Delta\phi = +36.9^\circ), \qquad f_H = 37.5\text{ kHz}\;(\Delta\phi = -36.9^\circ)}$$

### 2.2 General Negative Feedback Theory and Gain-Bandwidth Invariance

Open-loop semiconductor amplifiers suffer from massive gain variation across temperature and transistor batches, narrow bandwidth, nonlinear harmonic distortion, and awkward port impedances. **Negative feedback** solves all four pathologies simultaneously by sampling a fixed fraction $\beta$ of the output signal $X_o$ and subtracting it from the input source $X_s$—trading excess raw open-loop gain $A$ for precision, linearity, and bandwidth.

#### Closed-Loop Transfer Function and Four Quantitative Proofs

**Assumptions:** Unilateral forward amplifier of gain $A$ ($X_o = A X_d$), unilateral passive feedback network of constant real factor $\beta$ ($X_f = \beta X_o$), and subtractive input mixer ($X_d = X_s - X_f$).

Substituting $X_d = X_s - \beta X_o$ into $X_o = A X_d$:
$$X_o = A(X_s - \beta X_o) \implies X_o(1 + A\beta) = A X_s$$

$$\boxed{A_f = \frac{X_o}{X_s} = \frac{A}{1 + A\beta}}$$

Here $T \equiv A\beta$ is the **loop gain** and $D \equiv 1 + A\beta$ is the **desensitivity factor** (return difference). Negative feedback corresponds to $|1 + A\beta| > 1$ (reducing gain); positive feedback corresponds to $|1 + A\beta| < 1$. In the strong-feedback limit $A\beta \gg 1$, $A_f \to \frac{1}{\beta}$, locking gain purely to precision passive resistors!

1. **Gain Stabilization (Desensitivity):** Differentiating $A_f = A(1 + A\beta)^{-1}$ with respect to $A$ (holding $\beta$ fixed):
   $$\frac{dA_f}{dA} = \frac{(1 + A\beta) - A\beta}{(1 + A\beta)^2} = \frac{1}{(1 + A\beta)^2}$$
   Dividing by $A_f / A = (1 + A\beta)^{-1}$ gives fractional sensitivity:
   $$\boxed{\frac{dA_f}{A_f} = \frac{1}{1 + A\beta}\left(\frac{dA}{A}\right)}$$
   Fractional gain drift drops by the exact factor $(1 + A\beta)$.

2. **Bandwidth Extension and Constant Gain–Bandwidth Product:** Insert single-pole high-frequency open-loop response $A(jf) = \frac{A_0}{1 + j(f / f_H)}$ into $A_f = \frac{A}{1 + A\beta}$:
   $$A_f(jf) = \frac{\frac{A_0}{1 + j(f/f_H)}}{1 + \frac{A_0\beta}{1 + j(f/f_H)}} = \frac{A_0}{(1 + A_0\beta) + j(f/f_H)} = \frac{\left(\frac{A_0}{1 + A_0\beta}\right)}{1 + j\left(\frac{f}{f_H(1 + A_0\beta)}\right)}$$
   Repeating identically with low-frequency pole $A(jf) = \frac{A_0}{1 - j(f_L/f)}$ yields:
   $$\boxed{f_{Hf} = f_H(1 + A_0\beta), \qquad f_{Lf} = \frac{f_L}{1 + A_0\beta}, \qquad A_{f0}\cdot f_{Hf} = \left(\frac{A_0}{1 + A_0\beta}\right)\cdot f_H(1 + A_0\beta) = A_0 f_H}$$
   Negative feedback extends $f_H$ upward and pushes $f_L$ downward by $(1 + A_0\beta)$, conserving the **Gain–Bandwidth Product** identically!

3. **Suppression of Nonlinear Distortion and Internal Noise:** Suppose the active output stage generates an internal distortion component $D_o$ (for the same output amplitude). With feedback active, $V_o = A(V_s - \beta V_o) + D_o \implies V_o(1 + A\beta) = A V_s + D_o$:
   $$\boxed{V_o = \frac{A}{1 + A\beta} V_s + \frac{D_o}{1 + A\beta} \implies D_f = \frac{D_o}{1 + A\beta}}$$
   *(Note: Noise injected directly at the **input** terminal $v_{n,\text{in}}$ passes through the exact same path as $V_s$, so negative feedback does **not** improve input Signal-to-Noise Ratio—it only suppresses distortion and noise generated *inside* subsequent amplifier stages!)*

### 2.3 Four Negative-Feedback Topologies and Impedance Sculpting

Negative feedback reshapes input impedance $Z_{\text{in}}$ and output impedance $Z_{\text{out}}$ deterministically according to **how the output is sampled** and **how feedback is mixed at the input**:
- **Input Mixing Rule:** **Series (voltage) mixing** opposes input current, **multiplying** $Z_{\text{in}}$ by $(1 + A\beta)$; **Shunt (current) mixing** diverts extra input current, **dividing** $Z_{\text{in}}$ by $(1 + A\beta)$.
- **Output Sampling Rule:** **Voltage (shunt) sampling** stiffens output voltage like an ideal voltage source, **dividing** $Z_{\text{out}}$ by $(1 + A\beta)$; **Current (series) sampling** stiffens output current like an ideal current source, **multiplying** $Z_{\text{out}}$ by $(1 + A\beta)$.

#### Quick Proof for Voltage-Series Topology (Non-Inverting Amplifier)

- *Input Impedance:* Source supplies $i_{\text{in}} = \frac{v_d}{Z_{\text{in}}}$ where $v_s = v_d + v_f = v_d + \beta A v_d = v_d(1 + A\beta)$. Thus $Z_{if} = \frac{v_s}{i_{\text{in}}} = \frac{v_d(1 + A\beta)}{v_d / Z_{\text{in}}} = Z_{\text{in}}(1 + A\beta)$.
- *Output Impedance:* Zero the input ($v_s = 0$, so $v_d = -v_f = -\beta v_t$) and apply test voltage $v_t$ at output: $i_t = \frac{v_t - A v_d}{Z_{\text{out}}} = \frac{v_t(1 + A\beta)}{Z_{\text{out}}} \implies Z_{of} = \frac{v_t}{i_t} = \frac{Z_{\text{out}}}{1 + A\beta}$.

Calculate: (a) feedback factor $\beta$ and exact closed-loop voltage gain $A_{vf}$, (b) closed-loop input and output impedances $Z_{if}, Z_{of}$, (c) closed-loop bandwidth $f_{Hf}$, and (d) percentage change in $A_{vf}$ if $A_{ol}$ degrades by $-20\%$.
>

>    $$\beta = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_f} = \frac{10\text{ k}\Omega}{10\text{ k}\Omega + 150\text{ k}\Omega} = \frac{1}{16} = 0.0625$$

>    $$A_{vf} = \frac{A_{ol}}{1 + A_{ol}\beta} = \frac{200{,}000}{12{,}501} = 15.999 \approx 16.00 \qquad \left(\text{ideal } 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1} = 16\right)$$
>
> 2. **Closed-Loop Input and Output Impedances (Voltage–Series):**
>    $$Z_{if} = Z_{\text{in}}(1 + A_{ol}\beta) = (2.0 \times 10^6\text{ }\Omega)(12{,}501) = 2.50 \times 10^{10}\text{ }\Omega = 25.0\text{ G}\Omega$$

>

>

>    $$\frac{\Delta A_{vf}}{A_{vf}} = \frac{1}{1 + A_{ol}\beta}\left(\frac{\Delta A_{ol}}{A_{ol}}\right) = \frac{-20\%}{12{,}501} = -0.0016\%$$

## 3. Positive Feedback and Sinusoidal Oscillators

### 3.1 Barkhausen Criterion and Self-Starting Mechanism

An **electronic oscillator** converts DC power into a continuous, periodic AC output waveform **with zero external AC input signal**. Under **positive (regenerative) feedback**, the feedback signal reinforces the input in phase, flipping the denominator sign of the closed-loop gain to $A_f = \frac{A}{1 - A\beta}$.

Setting the denominator to zero allows a finite sinusoidal output $v_o \ne 0$ even when $v_s = 0$, establishing the **Barkhausen Criterion** for sustained sinusoidal oscillation at angular frequency $\omega_0$:

$$\boxed{A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0) = 1 \iff \begin{cases} |A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0)| = 1 & \text{(Unity Loop Magnitude)} \\[4pt] \angle A(j\omega_0)\beta(j\omega_0) = 0^\circ \text{ or } 2n\pi \;(n \in \mathbb{Z}) & \text{(Zero Net Loop Phase)} \end{cases}}$$

**Self-Starting and Amplitude Stabilization:** At power-on, thermal noise supplies a tiny broad-spectrum seed across all frequencies. To ensure oscillations grow spontaneously out of noise at the unique resonant frequency $\omega_0$, circuits are intentionally designed with startup loop gain **slightly greater than unity ($|A\beta| \approx 1.05\text{–}1.2 > 1$)**, placing complex closed-loop poles slightly in the right half-plane. As amplitude grows exponentially, transistor nonlinearity or an automatic gain control (AGC) element gently lowers $|A|$ until $|A\beta| = 1$ locks in stably; if $|A\beta| < 1$, oscillations decay exponentially to zero; if $|A\beta| \gg 1$, hard rail clipping distorts the sine wave into a square wave.

### 3.2 RC Phase-Shift and Wien Bridge Oscillators

At audio and low radio frequencies ($1\text{ Hz–}1\text{ MHz}$), inductors become physically bulky, lossy, and expensive; **RC oscillators** replace coils with compact resistor–capacitor phase-shaping networks.

#### 1. RC Phase-Shift Oscillator (Complex-Impedance Derivation)

An inverting amplifier ($A = -|A|$) supplies $-180^\circ$ phase shift; a three-section cascaded identical high-pass $RC$ ladder supplies the remaining $-180^\circ$ ($\approx 60^\circ$ per stage, though not strictly equal due to inter-stage loading) at one unique frequency $f_0$. *(Two RC sections can never reach $180^\circ$ at any finite frequency, since each section shifts asymptotically $< 90^\circ$!)*

**Assumptions:** Ideal inverting amplifier of real gain $A = -|A|$ with $Z_{\text{out}} = 0$ driving three identical cascaded $C\text{–}R$ high-pass stages (series capacitor $X \equiv \frac{1}{j\omega C}$, shunt resistor $R$), with negligible loading on the final node $v_f = I_3 R$ (for a BJT or Op-Amp, the final $R$ equals $R_{\text{in}}$).

**Step-by-Step Complex Mesh Derivation:**

1. Write KVL for the three clockwise mesh currents $I_1, I_2, I_3$ driven by amplifier output $V_o$:
   - Mesh 1: $(R + X)I_1 - R I_2 = V_o$
   - Mesh 2: $-R I_1 + (2R + X)I_2 - R I_3 = 0$
   - Mesh 3: $-R I_2 + (2R + X)I_3 = 0$

2. Define the dimensionless complex impedance ratio $\alpha \equiv \frac{X}{R} = \frac{1}{j\omega RC} = -\frac{j}{\omega RC}$. Eliminate $I_2$ and $I_1$ strictly backward from Mesh 3:
   - From Mesh 3: $I_2 = (2 + \alpha) I_3$.
   - Into Mesh 2: $I_1 = (2 + \alpha) I_2 - I_3 = \left[(2 + \alpha)^2 - 1\right] I_3 = (3 + 4\alpha + \alpha^2) I_3$.
   - Into Mesh 1:
     $$\frac{V_o}{R} = (1 + \alpha) I_1 - I_2 = \left[(1 + \alpha)(3 + 4\alpha + \alpha^2) - (2 + \alpha)\right] I_3 = \left(1 + 6\alpha + 5\alpha^2 + \alpha^3\right) I_3$$

3. Since $V_f = I_3 R$, the feedback transfer function is $\beta(j\omega) = \frac{V_f}{V_o} = \frac{1}{1 + 6\alpha + 5\alpha^2 + \alpha^3}$. Substituting $\alpha = -\frac{j}{\omega RC}$, $\alpha^2 = -\frac{1}{\omega^2 R^2 C^2}$, and $\alpha^3 = +\frac{j}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3}$ into the full complex loop gain $A\beta(j\omega)$:
   $$A\beta(j\omega) = \frac{-|A|}{\left(1 - \dfrac{5}{\omega^2 R^2 C^2}\right) + j\left(\dfrac{1}{\omega^3 R^3 C^3} - \dfrac{6}{\omega RC}\right)}$$

4. **Separate Imaginary Part $= 0$** (for zero net phase shift):
   $$\frac{1}{\omega_0^3 R^3 C^3} - \frac{6}{\omega_0 RC} = 0 \implies \omega_0^2 R^2 C^2 = \frac{1}{6} \implies \omega_0 = \frac{1}{RC\sqrt{6}}$$
   $$\boxed{f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC\sqrt{6}}}$$

5. **Substitute $\omega_0^2 R^2 C^2 = \frac{1}{6}$ into Real Part $\ge 1$** (minimum sustained gain):
   $$A\beta(j\omega_0) = \frac{-|A|}{1 - 5(6)} = \frac{-|A|}{-29} = \frac{|A|}{29} \ge 1 \implies \boxed{|A| \ge 29}$$

*(BJT Loading Note: In a single-transistor CE RC phase-shift oscillator where collector resistor $R_C$ loads the first stage, defining $k = R_C / R$ shifts the exact solution to $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC\sqrt{6 + 4k}}$ and $h_{fe} \ge 23 + \frac{29}{k} + 4k$, minimized at $k = 2.69 \implies h_{fe,\min} = 44.5$.)*

#### 2. Wien Bridge Oscillator (Complex-Impedance Derivation)

A **Wien Bridge oscillator** replaces the three-stage ladder with a clean, two-component **lead–lag RC bridge** driving a **non-inverting** amplifier ($A = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}$, $0^\circ$ phase shift). Because only two matched components need ganged tuning, Wien bridges generate laboratory-standard, ultra-low-distortion audio sine waves across decades of frequency.

**Assumptions:** Ideal Op-Amp configured as a non-inverting amplifier ($A = 1 + R_f/R_1$); positive-feedback branch consists of series $Z_1 = R + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$ from $v_o$ to $v_+$ and parallel $Z_2 = R \parallel \frac{1}{j\omega C} = \frac{R}{1 + j\omega RC}$ from $v_+$ to ground.

**Step-by-Step Complex Derivation:**

1. Write the complex voltage-divider feedback fraction $\beta(j\omega) = \frac{v_+}{v_o} = \frac{Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2} = \frac{1}{1 + Z_1 / Z_2}$:
   $$\frac{Z_1}{Z_2} = \left(R + \frac{1}{j\omega C}\right)\left(\frac{1}{R} + j\omega C\right) = 1 + j\omega RC + \frac{1}{j\omega RC} + 1 = 2 + j\left(\omega RC - \frac{1}{\omega RC}\right)$$

2. Form the complete complex loop gain $A\beta(j\omega)$:
   $$A\beta(j\omega) = \frac{1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}}{3 + j\left(\omega RC - \dfrac{1}{\omega RC}\right)}$$

3. **Separate Imaginary Part $= 0$** (zero phase shift at resonance):
   $$\omega_0 RC - \frac{1}{\omega_0 RC} = 0 \implies \omega_0 RC = 1 \implies \boxed{f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}}$$

4. **Substitute $\omega_0 RC = 1$ into Real Part $\ge 1$** (minimum sustained gain):
   $$A\beta(j\omega_0) = \frac{1 + R_f/R_1}{3} \ge 1 \implies \boxed{A \ge 3 \iff \frac{R_f}{R_1} \ge 2}$$

**Bridge Balance & Amplitude Stabilization:** Viewing $(Z_1, Z_2)$ and $(R_f, R_1)$ as the four arms of a Wheatstone bridge explains the name: at $f_0$, $Z_1/Z_2 = 2$, so setting $R_f/R_1 = 2$ balances the bridge cleanly. Replacing $R_1$ with a positive-temperature-coefficient (PTC) tungsten filament lamp (or shunting $R_f$ with back-to-back clamping diodes) automatically reduces gain whenever amplitude grows, locking $A = 3$ with $<0.05\%$ total harmonic distortion.

### 3.3 Generalized LC Tank - Hartley, Colpitts, and Crystal Oscillators

Above $\sim 1\text{ MHz}$, RC stages fail because internal transistor capacitances overwhelm discrete networks. **LC tank oscillators** harness resonant energy sloshing between inductors and capacitors to oscillate cleanly from $100\text{ kHz}$ to $>500\text{ MHz}$.

#### Unified Complex-Impedance Derivation for All Three-Point LC Oscillators

Model the inverting active amplifier (CE BJT or CS FET) by its Norton small-signal equivalent—dependent current source $g_m v_i$ downward in parallel with effective output resistance $R_o$—driving a resonant **$\pi$-network of three pure lossless reactances**:
- $Z_1 = j X_1$ connected from **input (Base/Gate) to Ground**;
- $Z_2 = j X_2$ connected from **output (Collector/Drain) to Ground**;
- $Z_3 = j X_3$ connected between **output and input**.

**Assumptions:** High amplifier input impedance ($Z_{\text{in}} \gg |Z_1|$) so feedback current flows strictly through $Z_3$ and $Z_1$; lossless tank reactances $Z_k = j X_k$.

**Step-by-Step Complex Loop-Gain Derivation:**

1. Feedback voltage divider across $(Z_3, Z_1)$ yields $\beta = \frac{v_f}{v_o} = \frac{Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_3}$.
2. Total load impedance seen by the Norton source $-g_m v_i$ is $Z_L = R_o \parallel Z_2 \parallel (Z_1 + Z_3)$, so $\frac{1}{Z_L} = \frac{1}{R_o} + \frac{1}{Z_2} + \frac{1}{Z_1 + Z_3}$.
3. Open-loop voltage gain is $A = \frac{v_o}{v_i} = -g_m Z_L$. Multiplying $A$ by $\beta$:
   $$A\beta = \frac{-g_m Z_1}{(Z_1 + Z_3)\left(\frac{1}{R_o} + \frac{1}{Z_2} + \frac{1}{Z_1 + Z_3}\right)} = \frac{-g_m Z_1 Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2 + Z_3) + \frac{Z_2(Z_1 + Z_3)}{R_o}}$$
4. Substitute purely imaginary reactances $Z_k = j X_k$ (noting $Z_1 Z_2 = -X_1 X_2$ and $Z_2(Z_1 + Z_3) = -X_2(X_1 + X_3)$) to expose exact **Real and Imaginary** parts of the denominator:
   $$A\beta(j\omega) = \frac{+g_m X_1 X_2}{\left[-\dfrac{X_2(X_1 + X_3)}{R_o}\right] + j\left(X_1 + X_2 + X_3\right)}$$
5. **Set Imaginary Part $= 0$ (Resonance Condition):**
   $$\boxed{X_1 + X_2 + X_3 = 0 \implies X_1 + X_3 = -X_2}$$
6. **Substitute $X_1 + X_3 = -X_2$ into Real Part $\ge 1$ (Sustained Gain Condition):**
   $$A\beta(j\omega_0) = \frac{g_m X_1 X_2}{-\frac{X_2(-X_2)}{R_o}} = g_m R_o \frac{X_1}{X_2} \ge 1$$
   Since $g_m R_o > 0$, positive real loop gain forces $\frac{X_1}{X_2} > 0$: **$X_1$ and $X_2$ MUST be the same type of reactance (both inductors OR both capacitors), and $X_3 = -(X_1 + X_2)$ MUST be the opposite type!** And the exact minimum open-loop voltage gain is:
   $$\boxed{|A_v| = g_m R_o \ge \frac{X_2}{X_1} = \frac{X_{\text{out}}}{X_{\text{in}}}}$$

#### Specializing Directly to Hartley and Colpitts Oscillators

1. **Hartley Oscillator (Tapped Inductor Tank):** Set $X_1 = +\omega L_1$ (input-to-ground), $X_2 = +\omega L_2$ (output-to-ground), and $X_3 = -\frac{1}{\omega C}$ (output-to-input). Including mutual inductance $M$ between the two coils ($L_{\text{eq}} = L_1 + L_2 + 2M$):
   - From $X_1 + X_2 + X_3 = 0$: $\omega_0(L_1 + L_2 + 2M) = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C}$, yielding:
     $$\boxed{f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(L_1 + L_2 + 2M)C}}, \qquad |A_v|_{\min} = \frac{L_2 + M}{L_1 + M} = \frac{L_{\text{out}}}{L_{\text{in}}}}$$
   - *(Note: If $L_1$ is labeled on the collector/output side and $L_2$ on the base/input side, the gain ratio naturally writes as $L_1 / L_2$; always state explicitly which coil faces the output!)*

2. **Colpitts Oscillator (Split-Capacitor Tank):** Set $X_1 = -\frac{1}{\omega C_1}$ (input-to-ground), $X_2 = -\frac{1}{\omega C_2}$ (output-to-ground), and $X_3 = +\omega L$ (output-to-input):
   - From $X_1 + X_2 + X_3 = 0$: $\omega_0 L = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C_1} + \frac{1}{\omega_0 C_2} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C_{\text{eq}}}$ where $C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}$:
     $$\boxed{f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L C_{\text{eq}}}}, \qquad C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}, \qquad |A_v|_{\min} = \frac{X_2}{X_1} = \frac{C_1}{C_2} = \frac{C_{\text{in}}}{C_{\text{out}}}}$$
   - *Advantage over Hartley:* Eliminates tapped-coil mutual inductance parasitics, yields cleaner high-frequency stability because transistor junction capacitances absorb harmlessly in parallel with $C_1$ and $C_2$, and operates smoothly up to UHF.

#### Piezoelectric Crystal Oscillator

In LC oscillators, thermal drift of $L$ and $C$ degrades frequency stability to $\sim 10^{-3}\text{–}10^{-4}$. A **quartz ($\text{SiO}_2$) crystal** exploits the **piezoelectric effect** (mechanical stress induces electric polarization and vice versa) to act as an electromechanical resonator with an extraordinary quality factor **$Q = \frac{\omega_s L_s}{R_s} \sim 10^4\text{–}10^6$**.

Its electrical equivalent circuit consists of a series motional branch ($L_s \sim \text{H}$, $C_s \sim 0.01\text{ pF}$, tiny $R_s$) shunted by the parallel holder capacitance $C_p$ ($\sim 5\text{ pF} \gg C_s$). Neglecting $R_s$, its net reactance exhibits two razor-close resonant frequencies:
$$X(\omega) = -\frac{1}{\omega C_p}\left(\frac{\omega^2 - \omega_s^2}{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}\right), \qquad \boxed{f_s = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_s C_s}}, \qquad f_p = f_s\sqrt{1 + \frac{C_s}{C_p}}}$$
- Below $f_s$ and above $f_p$, the crystal is capacitive ($X < 0$).
- Inside the ultra-narrow window **$f_s < f < f_p$** (typically $< 0.1\%$ wide!), $X(\omega) > 0$ rises almost vertically—acting as an **ultra-high-$Q$ inductor**! Replacing $L$ in a Colpitts circuit with a quartz crystal (a **Pierce Crystal Oscillator**) pins oscillation frequency squarely inside $[f_s, f_p]$ with stability $\Delta f / f \sim 10^{-6}\text{–}10^{-8}$ (essential for microprocessor clocks and RF transceivers).

## 4. Operational Amplifiers - Architecture, Linear Circuits, and Nonlinear Topologies

### 4.1 Differential Stage, CMRR, Slew Rate, and Virtual Short

An **Operational Amplifier (Op-Amp)** is a high-gain, direct-coupled differential voltage amplifier built internally from four cascaded monolithic blocks: (1) an emitter-coupled **differential input stage** (high $R_{\text{in}}$, huge CMRR); (2) a high-gain intermediate CE voltage stage (with internal Miller compensation capacitor $C_c \sim 30\text{ pF}$); (3) a DC level shifter; and (4) a low-$R_{\text{out}}$ class-AB push-pull output stage.

#### Differential Amplifier & Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

Any pair of input signals $(v_1, v_2)$ decomposes cleanly into a desired **differential-mode signal** $v_d = v_1 - v_2$ and an unwanted **common-mode interference signal** (e.g., $50\text{ Hz}$ hum picked up equally on both wires) $v_{cm} = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2}$. A real differential stage produces:
$$v_o = A_d v_d + A_{cm} v_{cm} = A_d v_d\left(1 + \frac{1}{\text{CMRR}}\cdot\frac{v_{cm}}{v_d}\right)$$
$$\boxed{\text{CMRR} \equiv \left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|, \qquad \text{CMRR}_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}\left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|}$$
In a matched emitter-coupled BJT pair sharing tail resistor $R_{EE}$, differential signals create an AC virtual ground at the common emitter (yielding $A_d \approx \frac{g_m R_C}{2}$ single-ended), whereas common-mode signals suffer massive negative emitter degeneration ($A_{cm} \approx -\frac{R_C}{r_e + 2R_{EE}}$), yielding $\text{CMRR} \approx g_m R_{EE}$. Replacing $R_{EE}$ with an active BJT current mirror drives $R_{EE} \to \text{M}\Omega$ and boosts CMRR above $90\text{–}120\text{ dB}$.

#### Ideal vs. Practical Op-Amp Parameters and Slew Rate

| Parameter | Ideal Op-Amp | Practical ($\mu\text{A}741$) | Physical Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Open-Loop Voltage Gain ($A_{ol}$)** | $\infty$ | $2 \times 10^5$ ($106\text{ dB}$) | Forces $v_+ - v_- = v_o / A_{ol} \to 0$ under feedback |
| **Input Resistance ($R_{\text{in}}$)** | $\infty$ | $2\text{ M}\Omega$ (BJT) / $10^{12}\text{ }\Omega$ (FET) | Zero input terminal currents ($i_+ = i_- = 0$) |
| **Output Resistance ($R_{\text{out}}$)** | $0$ | $75\text{ }\Omega$ | Drives loads without voltage sag |
| **Bandwidth / Unity-Gain $f_T$** | $\infty$ | $f_T \approx 1\text{ MHz}$ | Single-pole roll-off ($f_{H,ol} \approx 5\text{ Hz}$) |
| **CMRR** | $\infty$ | $90\text{ dB}$ ($3.16 \times 10^4$) | Suppresses common-mode hum |
| **Input Offset Voltage ($V_{io}$)** | $0$ | $1\text{–}5\text{ mV}$ | Differential DC input needed to force $v_o = 0$ |
| **Slew Rate ($\text{SR}$)** | $\infty$ | $0.5\text{ V/}\mu\text{s}$ | Max rate of output swing $\left|\frac{dv_o}{dt}\right|_{\max} = \frac{I_{\text{tail}}}{C_c}$ |

**Slew Rate & Full-Power Bandwidth Derivation:** Finite charging current $I_{\text{tail}}$ into the internal compensation capacitor $C_c$ caps the output slope at $\text{SR} = \left|\frac{dv_o}{dt}\right|_{\max}$. For a sinusoidal output $v_o(t) = V_p \sin(2\pi f t)$, peak slope is $2\pi f V_p$. Distortion-free swing requires $2\pi f V_p \le \text{SR}$:
$$\boxed{f_{\max} = \frac{\text{SR}}{2\pi V_p}}$$
Exceeding $f_{\max}$ distorts a sine wave into a smaller-amplitude **triangular wave** of slope $\pm\text{SR}$.

#### The Two Golden Rules of Linear Negative-Feedback Op-Amp Analysis

Whenever an ideal Op-Amp operates in its **unsaturated linear region ($-V_{\text{sat}} < v_o < +V_{\text{sat}}$) with negative feedback** (path from output back to the inverting `−` input):
1. **Zero Input Current:** $i_+ = i_- = 0$ (since $R_{\text{in}} \to \infty$).
2. **Virtual Short:** $v_- = v_+$ (since $v_+ - v_- = v_o / A_{ol} \to 0$). When $v_+$ is grounded, the inverting node sits at $v_- = 0$ **without being physically shorted to ground**—called a **Virtual Ground**. *(Warning: Never apply Virtual Short to open-loop comparators or positive-feedback Schmitt triggers!)*

### 4.2 Core Linear Op-Amp Circuits - Inverting, Non-Inverting, Adder, and Subtractor

#### 1. Inverting Amplifier

Input $v_{\text{in}}$ feeds inverting terminal (`−`) through $R_1$; feedback resistor $R_f$ bridges $v_o$ to `−`; non-inverting terminal (`+`) is grounded ($v_+ = 0$).
- By Virtual Ground, $v_- = v_+ = 0$, and $i_- = 0$.
- KCL at node `−`: $\frac{v_{\text{in}} - 0}{R_1} + \frac{v_o - 0}{R_f} = 0$:
  $$\boxed{A_{vf} = \frac{v_o}{v_{\text{in}}} = -\frac{R_f}{R_1}, \qquad R_{\text{in}} = R_1}$$
- *Finite-$A_{ol}$ Sanity Check:* Retaining $v_- = -v_o / A_{ol}$ in KCL yields $A_{vf} = \frac{-R_f / R_1}{1 + \frac{1 + R_f/R_1}{A_{ol}}}$, which cleanly approaches $-R_f/R_1$ as $A_{ol} \to \infty$.

#### 2. Non-Inverting Amplifier and Voltage Follower

Input $v_{\text{in}}$ drives `+` directly; resistive divider ($R_f$ from $v_o$ to `−`, $R_1$ from `−` to ground) sets negative feedback.
- By Virtual Short, $v_- = v_+ = v_{\text{in}}$, and $i_- = 0$.
- KCL at node `−`: $\frac{v_{\text{in}} - 0}{R_1} = \frac{v_o - v_{\text{in}}}{R_f} \implies v_o = v_{\text{in}}\left(1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}\right)$:
  $$\boxed{A_{vf} = \frac{v_o}{v_{\text{in}}} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}, \qquad R_{\text{in}} \to \infty}$$
- **Voltage Follower (Unity-Gain Buffer):** Setting $R_f = 0$ (direct short from $v_o$ to `−`) and $R_1 \to \infty$ (open circuit) yields $v_o = v_{\text{in}}$ ($A_{vf} = 1$), with gigohm input resistance and milliohm output resistance—perfect for isolating fragile sensors from heavy loads.

#### 3. Inverting Summing Amplifier (Weighted Adder)

Inputs $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ connect through resistors $R_1, R_2, \dots, R_n$ to the virtual-ground node `−` ($v_- = v_+ = 0$), with feedback $R_f$ to $v_o$. Because $v_- = 0$, each input branch injects current $i_k = v_k / R_k$ completely **independent** of all other inputs (zero cross-loading!).
- KCL at node `−`: $\frac{v_1}{R_1} + \frac{v_2}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{v_n}{R_n} + \frac{v_o}{R_f} = 0$:
  $$\boxed{v_o = -R_f\left(\frac{v_1}{R_1} + \frac{v_2}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{v_n}{R_n}\right)}$$
- *Special Cases:* Equal resistors $R_1 = \dots = R_n = R_f \implies v_o = -(v_1 + \dots + v_n)$ (pure adder); $R_k = n R_f \implies v_o = -\frac{1}{n}\sum v_k$ (averager); binary-weighted $R, 2R, 4R, \dots \implies$ Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC).

#### 4. Differential Amplifier (Subtractor)

Input $v_1$ feeds `−` through $R_1$ (with feedback $R_f$ from $v_o$ to `−`); input $v_2$ feeds `+` through voltage divider $R_2$ (series) and $R_3$ (to ground).
- Since $i_+ = 0$, $v_+ = v_2\left(\frac{R_3}{R_2 + R_3}\right)$, and by Virtual Short $v_- = v_+$.
- KCL at node `−`: $\frac{v_1 - v_-}{R_1} + \frac{v_o - v_-}{R_f} = 0 \implies v_o = -\frac{R_f}{R_1} v_1 + \left(1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}\right)v_-$:
  $$v_o = -\frac{R_f}{R_1} v_1 + \left(1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}\right)\left(\frac{R_3}{R_2 + R_3}\right) v_2$$
- Imposing the balanced bridge ratio **$\frac{R_f}{R_1} = \frac{R_3}{R_2}$** collapses $\left(1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}\right)\frac{R_3}{R_2 + R_3} = \frac{R_f}{R_1}$:
  $$\boxed{v_o = \frac{R_f}{R_1}(v_2 - v_1) \qquad \left(\text{for } \frac{R_f}{R_1} = \frac{R_3}{R_2}\right)}$$
  *(Notice that setting $v_1 = v_2 = v_{cm}$ gives $v_o = 0$, proving theoretically infinite CMRR when resistors are perfectly matched!)*

### 4.3 Analog Computation - Integrator, Differentiator, and Active Filters

Replacing either resistor in the inverting amplifier with a capacitor $C$ ($i_C = C\frac{dv_C}{dt}$) turns the Op-Amp into a real-time calculus engine.

#### 1. Op-Amp Integrator (Ideal vs. Practical Lossy)

Input resistor $R$ feeds virtual ground `−`; capacitor $C$ provides feedback from $v_o$ to `−` ($v_- = v_+ = 0$).
- KCL at node `−`: $\frac{v_{\text{in}}(t)}{R} + C\frac{dv_o}{dt} = 0 \implies \frac{dv_o}{dt} = -\frac{1}{RC} v_{\text{in}}(t)$.
- Integrating from $0$ to $t$:
  $$\boxed{v_o(t) = -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t v_{\text{in}}(\tau)\,d\tau + v_o(0), \qquad H(j\omega) = -\frac{1}{j\omega RC}}$$
- **Limiting-Case Diagnosis & Practical Fix:**
  - *As $\omega \to \infty$:* $|H(j\omega)| \to 0$ (naturally rejects high-frequency noise!).
  - *As $\omega \to 0$ (DC):* The capacitor behaves as an open circuit, driving $|H| \to \infty$. Even a $1\text{ mV}$ input offset voltage $V_{io}$ integrates relentlessly until $v_o$ slams permanently into $\pm V_{\text{sat}}$!
  - **Practical Integrator:** Shunting $C$ with a large resistor $R_f \gg R$ (typically $R_f \ge 10 R$) caps DC gain at $-R_f / R$ and yields $H(j\omega) = \frac{-R_f / R}{1 + j(\omega / \omega_a)}$ where $f_a = \frac{1}{2\pi R_f C}$; accurate integration proceeds cleanly for all signal frequencies **$f \gg f_a$** (e.g., $f \ge 10 f_a$). A square wave integrates into a triangular wave.

#### 2. Op-Amp Differentiator (Ideal vs. Practical Stabilized)

Swap $R$ and $C$: input capacitor $C$ feeds `−`, feedback resistor $R$ bridges $v_o$ to `−`.
- KCL at virtual ground `−`: $C\frac{dv_{\text{in}}}{dt} + \frac{v_o(t)}{R} = 0$:
  $$\boxed{v_o(t) = -RC\frac{dv_{\text{in}}(t)}{dt}, \qquad H(j\omega) = -j\omega RC}$$
- **Limiting-Case Diagnosis & Practical Fix:**
  - *As $\omega \to 0$:* $|H| \to 0$ (cleanly blocks DC offsets).
  - *As $\omega \to \infty$:* $|H(j\omega)| = \omega RC \to \infty$, amplifying high-frequency noise massively and triggering closed-loop phase instability!
  - **Practical Differentiator:** Inserting a small series input resistor $R_s \ll R$ before $C$ (plus optional tiny $C_f$ across $R$) flattens high-frequency gain at $-R / R_s$ above break frequency $f_b = \frac{1}{2\pi R_s C}$, preserving clean differentiation over **$f \ll f_b$**. A triangular wave differentiates into a square wave.

#### 3. First-Order Active Low-Pass and High-Pass Filters

Unlike passive $RC$ filters—which suffer passband attenuation and shift their cut-off frequency whenever load $R_L$ changes—**active Op-Amp filters** isolate the reactive shaping network with infinite input impedance, deliver passband voltage gain $A_0 = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}$, and eliminate heavy inductors.

- **First-Order Active Low-Pass Filter (LPF):** Place a passive $R\text{–}C$ lag network ($R$ series, $C$ to ground) on the `+` terminal of a non-inverting Op-Amp ($A_0 = 1 + R_f/R_1$):
  $$v_+ = \frac{1/(j\omega C)}{R + 1/(j\omega C)} v_{\text{in}} = \frac{v_{\text{in}}}{1 + j\omega RC} \implies \boxed{H_{\text{LPF}}(j\omega) = \frac{v_o}{v_{\text{in}}} = \frac{A_0}{1 + j(f / f_c)}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}}$$
  Flat gain $A_0$ for $f \ll f_c$; $-3\text{ dB}$ at $f_c$; $-20\text{ dB/decade}$ roll-off for $f \gg f_c$.

- **First-Order Active High-Pass Filter (HPF):** Swap $R$ and $C$ on the `+` terminal ($C$ series, $R$ to ground):
  $$\boxed{H_{\text{HPF}}(j\omega) = \frac{v_o}{v_{\text{in}}} = \frac{A_0\,j(f / f_c)}{1 + j(f / f_c)}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}}$$
  Stops DC ($H(0) = 0$); rises at $+20\text{ dB/decade}$ below $f_c$; flat passband $A_0$ for $f \gg f_c$ (bounded above by Op-Amp closed-loop bandwidth).

### 4.4 Nonlinear Op-Amp Circuits - Comparator and Schmitt Trigger

Removing negative feedback unleashes the Op-Amp's colossal open-loop gain ($A_{ol} \sim 10^5$), converting it into a razor-sharp binary threshold detector whose output latches exclusively at its saturation rails $\pm V_{\text{sat}}$ ($\approx \pm (V_{CC} - 1.5\text{ V})$).

#### 1. Open-Loop Voltage Comparator

An analog signal $v_{\text{in}}$ drives one input while a fixed reference $V_{\text{ref}}$ drives the other.
- **Non-Inverting Comparator** ($v_{\text{in}}$ at `+`, $V_{\text{ref}}$ at `−`):
  $$v_o = \begin{cases} +V_{\text{sat}}, & v_{\text{in}} > V_{\text{ref}} \\ -V_{\text{sat}}, & v_{\text{in}} < V_{\text{ref}} \end{cases}$$
- **Inverting Comparator** ($v_{\text{in}}$ at `−`, $V_{\text{ref}}$ at `+`): Output flips polarity ($v_o = -V_{\text{sat}}$ when $v_{\text{in}} > V_{\text{ref}}$, $+V_{\text{sat}}$ when $v_{\text{in}} < V_{\text{ref}}$).
- Setting $V_{\text{ref}} = 0$ creates a **Zero-Crossing Detector** converting any sine or triangular wave directly into a synchronous square wave. *Fatal Vulnerability:* If tiny noise rides on a slowly varying $v_{\text{in}}$ near $V_{\text{ref}}$, the output chatters erratically back and forth between $\pm V_{\text{sat}}$.

#### 2. Regenerative Schmitt Trigger (Comparator with Hysteresis)

Adding **positive feedback** from $v_o$ back to the `+` terminal via resistive divider ($R_2$ from $v_o$ to `+`, $R_1$ from `+` to ground; feedback fraction $\beta = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}$) while applying $v_{\text{in}}$ to the `−` terminal splits the single trigger threshold into **two distinct switching thresholds**:

- When $v_o = +V_{\text{sat}}$, node `+` sits at the **Upper Threshold Point ($V_{\text{UTP}}$)**:
  $$V_{\text{UTP}} = +\beta V_{\text{sat}} = +\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{\text{sat}}$$
  Output stays locked at $+V_{\text{sat}}$ until rising $v_{\text{in}}$ crosses *above* $V_{\text{UTP}}$, triggering instantaneous regenerative snap to $-V_{\text{sat}}$.
- Once $v_o = -V_{\text{sat}}$, node `+` immediately drops to the **Lower Threshold Point ($V_{\text{LTP}}$)**:
  $$V_{\text{LTP}} = -\beta V_{\text{sat}} = -\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{\text{sat}}$$
  Output stays locked at $-V_{\text{sat}}$ until falling $v_{\text{in}}$ crosses *below* $V_{\text{LTP}}$, snapping back to $+V_{\text{sat}}$.

$$\boxed{V_{\text{UTP}} = +\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{\text{sat}}, \qquad V_{\text{LTP}} = -\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{\text{sat}}, \qquad V_H = V_{\text{UTP}} - V_{\text{LTP}} = \frac{2 R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{\text{sat}}}$$

This dead-band **hysteresis width $V_H$** makes the Schmitt trigger completely immune to noise smaller than $V_H$, yielding clean noise-free rectangular pulses and serving as the core of Op-Amp square-wave relaxation oscillators.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2.5: Op-Amp Slew Rate, CMRR Error & Schmitt Trigger Design
> **Problem:** (a) An Op-Amp with slew rate $\text{SR} = 0.5\text{ V/}\mu\text{s}$ outputs a distortion-free sine wave. Calculate the maximum permissible frequency $f_{\max}$ for output peak amplitudes $V_p = 0.10\text{ V}$ and $V_p = 10.0\text{ V}$. (b) A differential amplifier has $A_d = 100$ and $\text{CMRR} = 80\text{ dB}$. If $v_1 = 1.005\text{ V}$ and $v_2 = 0.995\text{ V}$, compute the desired differential output and the percentage common-mode error. (c) Design an inverting Schmitt trigger with $\pm V_{\text{sat}} = \pm 12.0\text{ V}$ to obtain a hysteresis width $V_H = 4.0\text{ V}$ ($V_{\text{UTP}} = +2.0\text{ V}, V_{\text{LTP}} = -2.0\text{ V}$).
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Part (a) — Full-Power Slew-Rate Bandwidth:** Using $f_{\max} = \frac{\text{SR}}{2\pi V_p}$ with $\text{SR} = 0.5 \times 10^6\text{ V/s}$:
>    - At $V_p = 0.10\text{ V}$: $f_{\max} = \frac{0.5 \times 10^6}{2\pi(0.10)} = 795.8\text{ kHz}$.
>    - At $V_p = 10.0\text{ V}$: $f_{\max} = \frac{0.5 \times 10^6}{2\pi(10.0)} = 7.96\text{ kHz}$ *(slew rate severely bottlenecks large-swing signals well below the small-signal GBP!)*
>
> 2. **Part (b) — CMRR Error:** $\text{CMRR} = 10^{80/20} = 10^4 \implies A_{cm} = \frac{A_d}{\text{CMRR}} = \frac{100}{10^4} = 0.010$.
>    - $v_d = v_1 - v_2 = 0.010\text{ V}$; $v_{cm} = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2} = 1.000\text{ V}$.
>    - Desired differential output: $v_{o,d} = A_d v_d = (100)(0.010) = 1.000\text{ V}$.
>    - Common-mode error voltage: $v_{o,cm} = A_{cm} v_{cm} = (0.010)(1.000) = 0.010\text{ V}$, i.e., **$1.0\%$ error**.
>
> 3. **Part (c) — Schmitt Trigger Design:** From $V_{\text{UTP}} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} V_{\text{sat}}$:
>    $$\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{2.0\text{ V}}{12.0\text{ V}} = \frac{1}{6} \implies R_1 + R_2 = 6 R_1 \implies R_2 = 5 R_1$$
>    Choose $R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$ and $R_2 = 50\text{ k}\Omega$ (standard $51\text{ k}\Omega$).
>
> $$\boxed{\text{(a) } 796\text{ kHz},\; 7.96\text{ kHz}; \quad \text{(b) } 1.00\text{ V}\; (1.0\%\text{ error}); \quad \text{(c) } R_2 = 5 R_1\; (10\text{ k}\Omega, 50\text{ k}\Omega)}$$

## 5. High-Yield Traps and Exam-Hall Diagram Blueprints

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> 1. **PIV Trap — Center-Tapped vs. Bridge Rectifier:** Center-tapped FWR subjects the blocking diode to **$\text{PIV} = 2V_m$** (not $V_m$), whereas Bridge FWR has $\text{PIV} = V_m$ (with $2r_f$ forward drop). Always specify whether secondary RMS voltage is *per half-winding* or *end-to-end* before computing $V_m = \sqrt{2}\,V_{rms}$.
> 2. **Inverting Phase Shift in Oscillators:** In RC Phase-Shift, Hartley, and Colpitts oscillators, the CE/inverting amplifier contributes **$-180^\circ$**, so the feedback network must supply **$-180^\circ$** (negative real $\beta$)—never $+1$! Conversely, a Wien Bridge uses a **non-inverting** amplifier ($0^\circ$), so $\beta$ must be real and positive ($+1/3$).
> 3. **RC Phase-Shift Gain vs. Wien Bridge Gain:** RC Phase-Shift requires $|A| \ge 29$ and $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC\sqrt{6}}$ (never forget the $\sqrt{6}$!); Wien Bridge requires $A \ge 3$ (so **$R_f / R_1 \ge 2$**, NOT $3$!) and $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$.
> 4. **Hartley vs. Colpitts Gain Ratio Inversion:** Always check impedance ratio $X_{\text{out}}/X_{\text{in}}$: for Hartley ($X \propto L$), $|A_v|_{\min} = L_{\text{out}}/L_{\text{in}}$; for Colpitts ($X \propto 1/C$), capacitances **invert**, giving $|A_v|_{\min} = C_{\text{in}}/C_{\text{out}}$!
> 5. **Virtual Short Violation on Comparators & Schmitt Triggers:** Never write $v_- = v_+$ when feedback is absent (open-loop comparator) or connected to the **non-inverting `+` pin** (positive feedback Schmitt trigger)! The Op-Amp is slammed into $\pm V_{\text{sat}}$.
> 6. **Inverting Op-Amp Sign & Bandwidth Gain Factor:** Inverting gain is **$-R_f / R_1$** (never drop the minus sign!), yet its closed-loop bandwidth uses the **noise gain $(1 + R_f/R_1)$**: $f_{Hf} = \frac{\text{GBP}}{1 + R_f/R_1}$, **not** $\frac{\text{GBP}}{R_f/R_1}$!
> 7. **dB Power vs. Voltage Conversion:** $\text{CMRR}_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}(A_d/A_{cm})$ (voltage ratio uses **$20\log_{10}$**, so $80\text{ dB} = 10^4$, NOT $10^8$!). Similarly, half-power cut-off is $0.707\,|A_{vm}|$ ($-3\text{ dB}$).
> 8. **Slew Rate Unit Conversion:** $\text{SR} = 0.5\text{ V/}\mu\text{s} = 0.5 \times 10^6\text{ V/s}$ (forgetting the $10^6$ factor yields $f_{\max}$ off by six orders of magnitude).

> 4. **Sinusoidal Oscillators (Wien Bridge, RC Phase-Shift, Hartley, Colpitts):**
>    - *Wien Bridge:* Op-Amp with series $R\text{–}C$ from $v_o$ to `+`, parallel $R\text{–}C$ from `+` to ground, $R_f$ from $v_o$ to `−`, $R_1$ from `−` to ground; optionally redraw as a diamond Wheatstone bridge to show balance.
>    - *RC Phase-Shift:* Inverting Op-Amp (or CE BJT) feeding three cascaded high-pass $C\text{–}R$ sections looping back to input, labeling mesh currents $I_1, I_2, I_3$.
>    - *Hartley & Colpitts:* Draw the unified $\pi$-tank ($Z_1$ at base-ground, $Z_2$ at collector-ground, $Z_3$ collector-base) alongside the AC-coupled BJT with RF choke (RFC) blocking AC from $V_{CC}$.

### Examiner Step-Marking and Numerical Trap Signature

- **Barkhausen Rigor Carries Half the Marks:** Examiners penalize scripts that jump straight to $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$ or $\frac{1}{2\pi RC\sqrt{6}}$ without writing the full complex loop gain $A\beta(j\omega)$, explicitly separating **$\text{Im} = 0$** (frequency) and **$\text{Re} \ge 1$** (minimum gain threshold), and stating the self-starting condition ($|A\beta| > 1$ initially).
- **Non-Ideal Op-Amp Datasheets Are Trending (2017, 2023, 2026):** Whenever UPSC supplies $A_{ol}$, $Z_{\text{in}}$, $Z_{\text{out}}$, or $\text{GBP}$, candidates who lazily substitute the ideal $A_{vf} = 1 + R_f/R_1$ without computing $\beta$, $(1 + A_{ol}\beta)$, $Z_{if} = Z_{\text{in}}(1 + A_{ol}\beta)$, and $Z_{of} = Z_{\text{out}}/(1 + A_{ol}\beta)$ forfeit almost the entire problem.
- **Comparator Waveform Alignment:** On sketch-based comparator questions (like 2026), step-marks require vertical dotted alignment lines from every threshold crossing $v_{\text{in}}(t) = V_{\text{ref}}$ directly down to the vertical switching edges of $v_o(t)$, explicitly verifying whether $v_{\text{in}}$ feeds `+` (non-inverting) or `−` (inverting) before drawing $+12\text{ V}$ vs. $-12\text{ V}$ segments.
- **Closed-Loop Bandwidth Noise-Gain Trap:** For an inverting Op-Amp ($R_1 = 10\text{ k}\Omega, R_2 = 150\text{ k}\Omega$), feedback factor is still $\beta = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{1}{16}$ (same as non-inverting!), so closed-loop bandwidth is $\frac{\text{GBP}}{1 + R_2/R_1} = \frac{10^6}{16} = 62.5\text{ kHz}$—candidates dividing by $|A_v| = 15$ lose accuracy marks.
