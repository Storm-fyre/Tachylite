# Chapter 1: Semiconductor Physics, p-n Junctions, Thermistors, Solar Cells & Transistors (BJT / JFET / MOSFET)

## 1. Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

### 1.1 Band Structure, Effective Mass, and Effective Density of States

In a crystalline semiconductor such as silicon ($E_g = 1.12\text{ eV}$ at $300\text{ K}$) or germanium ($E_g = 0.67\text{ eV}$), overlap of atomic orbitals splits discrete energy levels into a nearly filled **valence band** (top edge $E_v$) separated by a forbidden band gap $E_g = E_c - E_v$ from a nearly empty **conduction band** (bottom edge $E_c$). At $T = 0\text{ K}$, every covalent bond is intact and the crystal behaves as an insulator.

At finite temperature $T > 0\text{ K}$, thermal lattice vibrations break covalent bonds, promoting electrons to the conduction band and leaving behind empty states called **holes** in the valence band. Within the periodic crystal potential, both carriers move as quasi-free wave packets whose inertia is encoded by band curvature through their **effective masses**:
$$m^* = \hbar^2 \left(\frac{d^2 E}{dk^2}\right)^{-1}$$
Near $E_c$, the band curves upward ($m_e^* > 0$); near $E_v$, the band curves downward, so an absent electron behaves as a positively charged quasiparticle ($+e$) with positive effective mass $m_h^* > 0$.

#### Derivation of Equilibrium Carrier Concentrations ($n, p$) and Effective DOS ($N_c, N_v$)

**Assumptions:**
1. Three-dimensional isotropic, parabolic band edges: $E - E_c = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m_e^*}$ for conduction electrons, and $E_v - E = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m_h^*}$ for valence holes.
2. **Non-degenerate semiconductor** (Maxwell–Boltzmann limit): the Fermi level $E_F$ lies at least $3 k_B T$ inside the band gap, so $E_c - E_F \gg k_B T$ and $E_F - E_v \gg k_B T$.
3. Thermal equilibrium at uniform temperature $T$, governed by the Fermi–Dirac occupation probability $f(E) = \left[1 + \exp\left(\frac{E - E_F}{k_B T}\right)\right]^{-1}$.

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**

1. Counting spin-degenerate ($g_s = 2$) standing-wave states in $k$-space per unit crystal volume yields the conduction-band density of states:
   $$g_c(E)\,dE = 2 \cdot \frac{4\pi k^2\,dk}{(2\pi)^3} = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} (E - E_c)^{1/2}\,dE \qquad (E \ge E_c)$$

2. In the non-degenerate limit $E - E_F \ge E_c - E_F \gg k_B T$, the $+1$ in the Fermi–Dirac denominator drops out:
   $$f(E) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{E - E_F}{k_B T}\right)$$

3. Integrating $n = \int_{E_c}^{\infty} g_c(E) f(E)\,dE$ (extending the upper limit to $\infty$ because the exponential tail kills contributions far above $E_c$):
   $$n = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \exp\left(-\frac{E_c - E_F}{k_B T}\right) \int_{E_c}^{\infty} (E - E_c)^{1/2} \exp\left(-\frac{E - E_c}{k_B T}\right) dE$$

4. Substitute the dimensionless variable $u = \frac{E - E_c}{k_B T}$, so $E - E_c = k_B T u$ and $dE = k_B T\,du$:
   $$n = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^* k_B T}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \exp\left(-\frac{E_c - E_F}{k_B T}\right) \int_{0}^{\infty} u^{1/2} e^{-u}\,du$$

5. Evaluate the standard Gamma integral $\int_{0}^{\infty} u^{1/2} e^{-u}\,du = \Gamma\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}$, and substitute $\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}$:
   $$\frac{1}{2\pi^2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \cdot \left(\frac{2m_e^* k_B T \cdot 4\pi^2}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} = \frac{1}{4\pi^{3/2}} \cdot 8\pi^{3/2} \left(\frac{2\pi m_e^* k_B T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} = 2\left(\frac{2\pi m_e^* k_B T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}$$

6. Repeating identically for valence-band holes using the empty-state probability $1 - f(E) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{E_F - E}{k_B T}\right)$ for $E \le E_v$:

$$\boxed{n = N_c \exp\left(-\frac{E_c - E_F}{k_B T}\right), \qquad p = N_v \exp\left(-\frac{E_F - E_v}{k_B T}\right)}$$

$$\boxed{N_c = 2\left(\frac{2\pi m_e^* k_B T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}, \qquad N_v = 2\left(\frac{2\pi m_h^* k_B T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}}$$

Physically, $N_c$ and $N_v$ represent the **effective densities of states** collapsed onto the band edges $E_c$ and $E_v$ respectively.

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
- **Dimensions:** Inside the parentheses, $\left[\frac{m k_B T}{h^2}\right] = \frac{\text{kg}\cdot(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2})}{(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1})^2} = \text{m}^{-2}$; raising to the $3/2$ power gives $\text{m}^{-3}$, matching volume number density. Notice $N_c = 2/\lambda_{\text{th}}^3$, where $\lambda_{\text{th}} = h/\sqrt{2\pi m_e^* k_B T}$ is the electron thermal de Broglie wavelength.
- **Limits:** As $T \to 0\text{ K}$, $N_c \to 0$ and the Boltzmann factor vanishes exponentially, so $n, p \to 0$. If doping pushes $E_F$ within $\lesssim 3 k_B T$ of $E_c$ or inside the band (degenerate regime), the Boltzmann approximation fails and full Fermi–Dirac integrals $F_{1/2}(\eta)$ are required.

### 1.2 Law of Mass Action and Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

Multiplying the equilibrium expressions for $n$ and $p$ reveals a striking cancellation: the Fermi level $E_F$, which encodes all doping details, drops out completely.

$$np = N_c N_v \exp\left(-\frac{E_c - E_F + E_F - E_v}{k_B T}\right) = N_c N_v \exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{k_B T}\right)$$

In an **intrinsic (pure)** semiconductor, every thermally excited conduction electron leaves one valence hole, so $n = p = n_i$. Substituting $n = p = n_i$ yields both the **Law of Mass Action** and the **intrinsic carrier concentration**:

$$\boxed{np = n_i^2, \qquad n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2k_B T}\right) = 2\left(\frac{2\pi k_B T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} (m_e^* m_h^*)^{3/4} \exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2k_B T}\right)}$$

The Law of Mass Action holds for **both intrinsic and non-degenerate extrinsic** semiconductors in thermal equilibrium: boosting majority electrons by adding donors simultaneously increases the electron–hole recombination rate, suppressing minority holes by the exact factor needed to hold $np = n_i^2$ constant at fixed $T$.

#### Position of the Intrinsic Fermi Level ($E_{Fi}$)

Setting $n = p$ in an intrinsic crystal:
$$N_c \exp\left(-\frac{E_c - E_{Fi}}{k_B T}\right) = N_v \exp\left(-\frac{E_{Fi} - E_v}{k_B T}\right)$$
Taking natural logarithms and isolating $2E_{Fi}$:
$$\ln\left(\frac{N_c}{N_v}\right) = \frac{E_c + E_v - 2E_{Fi}}{k_B T} \implies E_{Fi} = \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} - \frac{k_B T}{2}\ln\left(\frac{N_c}{N_v}\right)$$
Substituting $\frac{N_c}{N_v} = \left(\frac{m_e^*}{m_h^*}\right)^{3/2}$:

$$\boxed{E_{Fi} = \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} + \frac{3}{4}k_B T \ln\left(\frac{m_h^*}{m_e^*}\right)}$$

**Limiting Checks:** At $T = 0\text{ K}$ or whenever $m_h^* = m_e^*$, $E_{Fi}$ lies **exactly at midgap** $(E_c + E_v)/2$. Because $m_h^* \ne m_e^*$ in real crystals, $E_{Fi}$ tilts gently with $T$ toward the band with the *smaller* effective mass (fewer states) to equalize $n$ and $p$; at $300\text{ K}$, this shift is only a few $\text{meV}$ compared to $E_g \sim 1\text{ eV}$.

### 1.3 Extrinsic Semiconductors and Fermi-Level Evolution

Controlled substitution of trace impurities (**doping**, typically $10^{20}\text{–}10^{25}\text{ m}^{-3}$ in a lattice of $\sim 5 \times 10^{28}\text{ atoms/m}^3$) tailors electrical conductivity across ten orders of magnitude.

- **$n$-Type Semiconductor:** Group V **donor** atoms (P, As, Sb) replace tetravalent Si/Ge. Four valence electrons complete covalent bonds; the fifth electron orbits the $+e$ donor ion core in a medium of dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r$ with effective mass $m_e^*$. By the scaled hydrogenic Bohr model, its binding energy is tiny:
  $$E_c - E_d \approx \frac{m_e^*}{m_0 \varepsilon_r^2}(13.6\text{ eV}) \approx 0.045\text{ eV} \quad (\text{P in Si})$$
  Because $E_c - E_d \sim 1.7 k_B T$ at $300\text{ K}$, virtually all donors ionize into immobile $+e$ ions ($N_d^+ \approx N_d$) and free conduction electrons.
- **$p$-Type Semiconductor:** Group III **acceptor** atoms (B, Al, Ga, In) leave one unfilled covalent bond at a shallow energy level $E_a$ ($\approx 0.045\text{ eV}$ above $E_v$ for B in Si). Thermal electrons from the valence band easily fill these acceptor states, leaving immobile $-e$ ions ($N_a^- \approx N_a$) and mobile valence holes.

#### Charge Neutrality and Exact Extrinsic Carrier Densities

Every unit volume of a uniformly doped crystal must remain electrically neutral:
$$n_0 + N_a^- = p_0 + N_d^+$$
In the **complete-ionization regime** ($N_d^+ \approx N_d$, $N_a^- \approx N_a$), substituting $p_0 = n_i^2 / n_0$ produces a quadratic equation for $n_0$:
$$n_0^2 - (N_d - N_a)n_0 - n_i^2 = 0$$
Selecting the physical positive root for an $n$-type (or net-donor compensated, $N_d > N_a$) semiconductor:

$$\boxed{n_0 = \frac{(N_d - N_a) + \sqrt{(N_d - N_a)^2 + 4n_i^2}}{2}, \qquad p_0 = \frac{n_i^2}{n_0}}$$

In the strongly extrinsic regime ($N_d - N_a \gg 2n_i$, with $N_a = 0$):
$$n_0 \approx N_d \quad (\text{majority}), \qquad p_0 \approx \frac{n_i^2}{N_d} \quad (\text{minority})$$
Symmetrically, for strongly $p$-type material ($N_a \gg n_i$, $N_d = 0$): $p_0 \approx N_a$ and $n_0 \approx n_i^2 / N_a$.

#### Fermi-Level Shift with Doping and Temperature

Inverting $n_0 = N_c e^{-(E_c - E_F)/(k_B T)} = n_i e^{(E_F - E_{Fi})/(k_B T)}$ and $p_0 = N_v e^{-(E_F - E_v)/(k_B T)} = n_i e^{(E_{Fi} - E_F)/(k_B T)}$ immediately proves the direction of Fermi-level shift:

- **$n$-Type ($n_0 \approx N_d > n_i$):**
  $$\boxed{E_F = E_c - k_B T \ln\left(\frac{N_c}{N_d}\right) = E_{Fi} + k_B T \ln\left(\frac{N_d}{n_i}\right)}$$
  Since $N_d > n_i$, $\ln(N_d/n_i) > 0$, so $E_F$ **shifts upward toward $E_c$**; heavier donor doping pulls $E_F$ progressively closer to $E_c$.

- **$p$-Type ($p_0 \approx N_a > n_i$):**
  $$\boxed{E_F = E_v + k_B T \ln\left(\frac{N_v}{N_a}\right) = E_{Fi} - k_B T \ln\left(\frac{N_a}{n_i}\right)}$$
  Since $N_a > n_i$, $\ln(N_a/n_i) > 0$, so $E_F$ **shifts downward toward $E_v$**; heavier acceptor doping pulls $E_F$ progressively closer to $E_v$.

As temperature increases from $0\text{ K}$ upward, an extrinsic $n$-type semiconductor passes through **three distinct physical regimes**:

| Temperature Regime | Dominant Mechanism | Electron Density $n(T)$ | Fermi-Level Position $E_F(T)$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Freeze-out** ($T \lesssim 100\text{ K}$ in Si) | Partial thermal ionization of donors | $n \approx \sqrt{\frac{N_c N_d}{2}}\,e^{-\frac{E_c - E_d}{2k_B T}}$ (rises exponentially) | Starts at $\frac{E_c + E_d}{2}$ at $T \to 0\text{ K}$; stays between $E_d$ and $E_c$ |
| **2. Extrinsic / Saturation** ($\sim 150\text{–}450\text{ K}$) | Full donor ionization; $N_d \gg n_i(T)$ | $n \approx N_d = \text{constant}$ (flat plateau) | Drops steadily below $E_d$ toward midgap as $N_c(T) \propto T^{3/2}$ rises |
| **3. Intrinsic** ($T \gtrsim 500\text{ K}$) | Band-to-band thermal excitation dominates ($n_i \gg N_d$) | $n \approx n_i(T) \propto T^{3/2} e^{-E_g / (2k_B T)}$ (steep rise) | Converges asymptotically onto $E_{Fi}$ near midgap |

### 1.4 Drift, Diffusion, and the Einstein Relation

Charge transport in semiconductor devices rests on two distinct physical mechanisms: **drift** driven by an electric field $\mathcal{E}$, and **diffusion** driven by spatial concentration gradients.

#### Drift Transport and Mobility

Between random scattering collisions (with lattice phonons and ionized impurities) separated by mean free time $\tau$, an electric field $\mathcal{E}$ accelerates carriers to a steady average drift velocity $\mathbf{v}_{dn} = -\mu_n \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}$ for electrons and $\mathbf{v}_{dp} = +\mu_p \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}$ for holes, where **mobility** is:
$$\mu_n = \frac{e\tau_n}{m_e^*}, \qquad \mu_p = \frac{e\tau_p}{m_h^*}$$
Because electrons drift opposite to $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}$ while carrying charge $-e$, both electron and hole drift currents flow **parallel** to $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}$:
$$J_{\text{drift}} = (-e) n (-\mu_n \mathcal{E}) + (+e) p (+\mu_p \mathcal{E}) = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)\mathcal{E} = \sigma \mathcal{E}$$
$$\boxed{\sigma = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p), \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma}}$$
Note the dual temperature dependence of mobility: at low $T$, **ionized impurity scattering** dominates ($\mu_{\text{imp}} \propto T^{+3/2}$); at room and high $T$, **acoustic phonon (lattice) scattering** dominates ($\mu_{\text{lat}} \propto T^{-3/2}$). Consequently, in the flat extrinsic plateau ($n \approx N_d$), resistivity $\rho = (e N_d \mu_n)^{-1}$ actually **rises** with temperature—exactly like a metal—before plunging exponentially once intrinsic excitation takes over!

#### Diffusion Transport

Thermal agitation drives particles down their concentration gradient according to **Fick's First Law**: particle flux $\Phi_n = -D_n \frac{dn}{dx}$ and $\Phi_p = -D_p \frac{dp}{dx}$, where $D_n, D_p$ are diffusion coefficients ($\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}$). Multiplying particle flux by carrier charge yields the diffusion current densities:
$$J_{n,\text{diff}} = (-e)\left(-D_n \frac{dn}{dx}\right) = +e D_n \frac{dn}{dx}, \qquad J_{p,\text{diff}} = (+e)\left(-D_p \frac{dp}{dx}\right) = -e D_p \frac{dp}{dx}$$
Combining drift and diffusion gives the fundamental **1D Drift–Diffusion Transport Equations**:
$$\boxed{J_n = e\mu_n n \mathcal{E} + e D_n \frac{dn}{dx}, \qquad J_p = e\mu_p p \mathcal{E} - e D_p \frac{dp}{dx}}$$

#### Derivation of the Einstein Relation

Because both mobility ($\mu$) and diffusivity ($D$) are governed by the exact same thermal-scattering mean free path, they cannot be independent.

**Assumptions:**
1. A non-uniformly doped, non-degenerate semiconductor in **thermal equilibrium** at temperature $T$ (no external bias, zero net current: $J_n = 0$ and $J_p = 0$ everywhere).
2. In thermal equilibrium, the Fermi level $E_F$ is **spatially constant** ($\frac{dE_F}{dx} = 0$), while the spatially varying doping induces a built-in electrostatic potential $\phi(x)$ and field $\mathcal{E}(x) = -\frac{d\phi}{dx}$.

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**

1. Because an electron carries charge $-e$, its electrostatic potential energy shifts every band edge (including $E_{Fi}$) by $-e\phi(x)$:
   $$E_{Fi}(x) = \text{const} - e\phi(x) \implies \frac{dE_{Fi}}{dx} = -e\frac{d\phi}{dx} = +e\mathcal{E}(x)$$

2. Express equilibrium electron concentration relative to the spatially varying intrinsic level:
   $$n(x) = n_i \exp\left(\frac{E_F - E_{Fi}(x)}{k_B T}\right)$$

3. Differentiate $n(x)$ with respect to $x$ (holding $E_F$ constant):
   $$\frac{dn}{dx} = n(x) \left(-\frac{1}{k_B T}\frac{dE_{Fi}}{dx}\right) = -\frac{e\mathcal{E}(x)}{k_B T} n(x)$$

4. Impose the detailed-balance equilibrium condition $J_n = 0$:
   $$J_n = e\mu_n n(x) \mathcal{E}(x) + e D_n \left(-\frac{e\mathcal{E}(x)}{k_B T} n(x)\right) = 0$$

5. Cancel the nonzero common factor $e n(x) \mathcal{E}(x)$:
   $$\mu_n - \frac{e D_n}{k_B T} = 0 \implies \frac{D_n}{\mu_n} = \frac{k_B T}{e}$$

6. Repeating identically for holes using $p(x) = n_i \exp\left(\frac{E_{Fi}(x) - E_F}{k_B T}\right) \implies \frac{dp}{dx} = +\frac{e\mathcal{E}}{k_B T} p$ in $J_p = e\mu_p p\mathcal{E} - e D_p \frac{dp}{dx} = 0$ yields $\frac{D_p}{\mu_p} = \frac{k_B T}{e}$:

$$\boxed{\frac{D_n}{\mu_n} = \frac{D_p}{\mu_p} = \frac{k_B T}{e} \equiv V_T}$$

**Dimensional and Numerical Sanity Checks:**
- **Dimensions:** $[D/\mu] = \frac{\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}} = \text{V}$, and $[k_B T / e] = \frac{\text{J}}{\text{C}} = \text{V}$.
- **Thermal Voltage at $T = 300\text{ K}$:**
  $$V_T = \frac{k_B T}{e} = \frac{(1.38065 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/K})(300\text{ K})}{1.60218 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}} = 0.02585\text{ V} = 25.85\text{ mV}$$
- **Limits:** As $T \to 0\text{ K}$, thermal velocity vanishes, halting diffusion ($D \to 0$) even if mobility remains finite.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 1.1: Carrier Densities, Fermi-Level Shift, Conductivity, and Diffusivity in Doped Silicon
> **Problem:** Silicon at $T = 300\text{ K}$ is uniformly doped with phosphorus at $N_d = 1.0 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3}$. Given $n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\text{ m}^{-3}$, $N_c = 2.8 \times 10^{25}\text{ m}^{-3}$, $\mu_n = 0.135\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, $\mu_p = 0.048\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, and $V_T = k_B T / e = 0.02585\text{ V}$, compute: (a) equilibrium electron and hole concentrations, (b) position of $E_F$ relative to $E_c$ and $E_{Fi}$, (c) electrical conductivity and resistivity, and (d) electron diffusion coefficient $D_n$.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Carrier Concentrations:** Since $N_d = 1.0 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3} \gg n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\text{ m}^{-3}$ and room temperature ensures complete donor ionization:
>    $$n_0 \approx N_d = 1.0 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3}$$
>    By the Law of Mass Action ($n_0 p_0 = n_i^2$):
>    $$p_0 = \frac{n_i^2}{n_0} = \frac{(1.5 \times 10^{16})^2}{1.0 \times 10^{22}} = \frac{2.25 \times 10^{32}}{1.0 \times 10^{22}} = 2.25 \times 10^{10}\text{ m}^{-3}$$
>
> 2. **Fermi-Level Position:**
>    $$E_c - E_F = k_B T \ln\left(\frac{N_c}{N_d}\right) = (0.02585\text{ eV})\ln\left(\frac{2.8 \times 10^{25}}{1.0 \times 10^{22}}\right) = 0.02585 \times \ln(2800) = 0.02585 \times 7.9374 = 0.205\text{ eV}$$
>    Relative to the intrinsic Fermi level $E_{Fi}$:
>    $$E_F - E_{Fi} = k_B T \ln\left(\frac{N_d}{n_i}\right) = (0.02585\text{ eV})\ln\left(\frac{1.0 \times 10^{22}}{1.5 \times 10^{16}}\right) = 0.02585 \times 13.410 = 0.347\text{ eV}$$
>    *(Check: $E_c - E_F = 0.205\text{ eV} \approx 7.9\,k_B T > 3\,k_B T$, validating the non-degenerate Boltzmann approximation; also $0.205 + 0.347 = 0.552\text{ eV} \approx E_g/2$.)*
>
> 3. **Conductivity and Resistivity:** Because $n_0 \mu_n \gg p_0 \mu_p$:
>    $$\sigma \approx e n_0 \mu_n = (1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C})(1.0 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3})(0.135\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}) = 216.3\text{ S}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}$$
>    $$\rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} = 4.62 \times 10^{-3}\text{ }\Omega\cdot\text{m}$$
>
> 4. **Electron Diffusion Coefficient (Einstein Relation):**
>    $$D_n = \mu_n V_T = (0.135\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1})(0.02585\text{ V}) = 3.49 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2\text{s}^{-1}$$
>
> $$\boxed{n_0 = 1.0\times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3},\; p_0 = 2.25\times 10^{10}\text{ m}^{-3},\; E_c - E_F = 0.205\text{ eV},\; \sigma = 216.3\text{ S/m},\; D_n = 3.49\times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}}$$

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 1.2: Hole Mobility from Effective Mass & Intrinsic Fermi-Level Displacement
> **Problem:** In a III–V compound semiconductor at $T = 300\text{ K}$, electron and hole effective masses are $m_e^* = 0.07\,m_0$ and $m_h^* = 0.40\,m_0$. If the hole mean scattering time is half the electron mean scattering time ($\tau_h = 0.5\,\tau_e$) and electron mobility is $\mu_n = 0.80\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, determine: (a) the hole mobility $\mu_p$, and (b) the displacement of the intrinsic Fermi level $E_{Fi}$ from midgap.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Hole Mobility:** From $\mu = e\tau / m^*$, take the ratio:
>    $$\frac{\mu_p}{\mu_n} = \left(\frac{\tau_h}{\tau_e}\right)\left(\frac{m_e^*}{m_h^*}\right) = (0.50)\left(\frac{0.07\,m_0}{0.40\,m_0}\right) = 0.0875$$
>    $$\mu_p = 0.0875 \times 0.80\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1} = 0.070\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$$
>
> 2. **Intrinsic Fermi-Level Shift:**
>    $$E_{Fi} - \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} = \frac{3}{4}k_B T \ln\left(\frac{m_h^*}{m_e^*}\right) = \frac{3}{4}(0.02585\text{ eV})\ln\left(\frac{0.40}{0.07}\right)$$
>    $$= (0.01939\text{ eV})\ln(5.7143) = 0.01939 \times 1.7430 = +0.0338\text{ eV} = +33.8\text{ meV}$$
>    Because $m_h^* > m_e^*$, $E_{Fi}$ sits $33.8\text{ meV}$ **above** midgap (closer to $E_c$).
>
> $$\boxed{\mu_p = 0.070\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}, \qquad E_{Fi} - E_{\text{mid}} = +33.8\text{ meV}}$$

## 2. p-n Junctions, Special Diodes, Thermistors, and Solar Cells

### 2.1 Equilibrium p-n Junction, Built-In Potential, and Depletion Width

When a $p$-type region (acceptor density $N_a$) meets an $n$-type region (donor density $N_d$) inside a single crystal, steep concentration gradients immediately launch holes from $p \to n$ and electrons from $n \to p$ by diffusion. As mobile carriers vacate the metallurgical boundary, they uncover fixed, negatively charged acceptor ions ($-e N_a$) on the $p$-side and fixed, positively charged donor ions ($+e N_d$) on the $n$-side.

This bare dipole sheet—called the **depletion (space-charge) region**—builds an internal electric field $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}$ pointing from the $n$-side toward the $p$-side. That field drives a counter-flowing drift current until drift cancels diffusion exactly for both electrons and holes separately, flattening the Fermi level $E_F$ continuously across the junction and bending the band edges upward on the $p$-side by an electrostatic barrier $e V_{\text{bi}}$.

#### Derivation of Built-In (Contact) Potential ($V_{\text{bi}}$)

In thermal equilibrium ($J_p = 0$), substitute the Einstein relation $D_p = \mu_p \frac{k_B T}{e}$ and $\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\phi}{dx}$ into $J_p = e\mu_p p\mathcal{E} - e D_p \frac{dp}{dx} = 0$:
$$-\mu_p p \frac{d\phi}{dx} = \mu_p \frac{k_B T}{e}\frac{dp}{dx} \implies -\frac{e}{k_B T}\,d\phi = \frac{dp}{p}$$
Integrating from the neutral $p$-region ($\phi = \phi_p$, $p = p_{p0} \approx N_a$) to the neutral $n$-region ($\phi = \phi_n$, $p = p_{n0} \approx n_i^2 / N_d$):
$$-\frac{e}{k_B T}(\phi_n - \phi_p) = \ln\left(\frac{p_{n0}}{p_{p0}}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{n_i^2}{N_a N_d}\right)$$
Defining the built-in potential $V_{\text{bi}} \equiv \phi_n - \phi_p > 0$:

$$\boxed{V_{\text{bi}} = \frac{k_B T}{e}\ln\left(\frac{N_a N_d}{n_i^2}\right)}$$

Notice that $e V_{\text{bi}}$ equals the difference between the bulk Fermi positions before contact: $(E_F - E_{Fi})_n + (E_{Fi} - E_F)_p$. Because $V_{\text{bi}}$ is cancelled by equal and opposite metal–semiconductor contact potentials around any closed external wire, it **cannot** drive current in an external circuit in thermal equilibrium.

#### Derivation of Depletion Width ($W$), Peak Field ($\mathcal{E}_{\max}$), and Junction Capacitance ($C_j$)

**Assumptions (Depletion / Abrupt Step-Junction Approximation):**
1. Metallurgical junction at $x = 0$: constant doping $N_a$ for $x < 0$ and $N_d$ for $x > 0$.
2. Space-charge region occupies $-x_p \le x \le x_n$ with zero mobile carriers inside and complete charge neutrality outside:
   $$\rho(x) = \begin{cases} -e N_a, & -x_p \le x < 0 \\ +e N_d, & 0 < x \le x_n \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
3. Relative permittivity of the semiconductor is $\varepsilon_s = \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0$.

**Step-by-Step Integration of 1D Poisson's Equation:**

1. Write Poisson's equation $\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dx} = -\frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2} = \frac{\rho(x)}{\varepsilon_s}$ on each side of $x = 0$:
   - On the $p$-side ($-x_p \le x < 0$): $\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dx} = -\frac{e N_a}{\varepsilon_s}$. Integrating with boundary condition $\mathcal{E}(-x_p) = 0$:
     $$\mathcal{E}(x) = -\frac{e N_a}{\varepsilon_s}(x + x_p)$$
   - On the $n$-side ($0 < x \le x_n$): $\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dx} = +\frac{e N_d}{\varepsilon_s}$. Integrating with boundary condition $\mathcal{E}(x_n) = 0$:
     $$\mathcal{E}(x) = -\frac{e N_d}{\varepsilon_s}(x_n - x)$$

2. Continuity of $\mathcal{E}(x)$ across $x = 0$ (no surface charge sheet) demands global **space-charge neutrality** and gives the peak field magnitude $|\mathcal{E}_{\max}|$ at $x = 0$:
   $$N_a x_p = N_d x_n, \qquad \mathcal{E}_{\max} = \mathcal{E}(0) = -\frac{e N_a x_p}{\varepsilon_s} = -\frac{e N_d x_n}{\varepsilon_s}$$
   *(The depletion region penetrates inversely with doping: $x_p / x_n = N_d / N_a$—much deeper into the lightly doped side!)*

3. Integrate $\mathcal{E}(x) = -\frac{d\phi}{dx}$ across $[-x_p, x_n]$ to obtain the total potential barrier $V_{\text{bi}} = \phi(x_n) - \phi(-x_p) = -\int_{-x_p}^{x_n} \mathcal{E}(x)\,dx$. Geometrically, $-\mathcal{E}(x)$ is a triangle of base $W = x_p + x_n$ and height $|\mathcal{E}_{\max}|$:
   $$V_{\text{bi}} = \frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{E}_{\max}|(x_p + x_n) = \frac{e}{2\varepsilon_s}\left(N_a x_p^2 + N_d x_n^2\right)$$

4. Express $x_p$ and $x_n$ in terms of total width $W = x_p + x_n$ using $N_a x_p = N_d x_n$:
   $$x_p = W\frac{N_d}{N_a + N_d}, \qquad x_n = W\frac{N_a}{N_a + N_d}$$

5. Substitute into the potential drop equation:
   $$V_{\text{bi}} = \frac{e}{2\varepsilon_s} W^2 \left[\frac{N_a N_d^2 + N_d N_a^2}{(N_a + N_d)^2}\right] = \frac{e}{2\varepsilon_s}\left(\frac{N_a N_d}{N_a + N_d}\right) W^2$$

6. Solve for $W$; under an applied external bias $V$ ($V > 0$ forward bias lowers the barrier; $V < 0$ reverse bias raises the barrier), replace $V_{\text{bi}}$ by $(V_{\text{bi}} - V)$:

$$\boxed{W(V) = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{e}\left(\frac{1}{N_a} + \frac{1}{N_d}\right)(V_{\text{bi}} - V)}, \qquad |\mathcal{E}_{\max}(V)| = \frac{2(V_{\text{bi}} - V)}{W(V)}}$$

7. **Junction (Depletion / Transition) Capacitance ($C_j$):** Varying reverse bias $V_R = -V$ uncovers or covers charge $Q = e A N_d x_n = A\sqrt{2 e \varepsilon_s \frac{N_a N_d}{N_a + N_d}(V_{\text{bi}} + V_R)}$ at the depletion edges, behaving exactly like a parallel-plate capacitor of plate spacing $W(V)$:
   $$\boxed{C_j(V) = \left|\frac{dQ}{dV}\right| = \frac{\varepsilon_s A}{W(V)} = A\sqrt{\frac{e\varepsilon_s}{2(V_{\text{bi}} - V)}\left(\frac{N_a N_d}{N_a + N_d}\right)} \propto (V_{\text{bi}} - V)^{-1/2}}$$
   *(Under forward bias, stored minority-carrier charge injected into the quasi-neutral regions dominates instead, producing a much larger **diffusion capacitance** $C_d = \frac{\tau I}{\eta V_T}$.)*

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
- **Dimensions:** $\left[\frac{\varepsilon_s V}{e N}\right] = \frac{(\text{C}\cdot\text{V}^{-1}\text{m}^{-1})(\text{V})}{(\text{C})(\text{m}^{-3})} = \text{m}^2 \implies [W] = \text{m}$.
- **One-Sided Abrupt Junction ($p^+n$, where $N_a \gg N_d$):** $\frac{1}{N_a} + \frac{1}{N_d} \approx \frac{1}{N_d}$, so $W \approx x_n \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s (V_{\text{bi}} - V)}{e N_d}}$; virtually all depletion occurs on the lightly doped $n$-side.

### 2.2 Shockley Diode Equation and Non-Ideal Behavior

Applying a **forward bias** ($V > 0$, positive terminal on $p$-side) opposes the built-in field, shrinks the depletion barrier to $V_{\text{bi}} - V$, and unleashes an exponential torrent of majority carriers across the junction as injected **minority carriers** (holes into $n$-side, electrons into $p$-side), where they diffuse away and recombine. Applying a **reverse bias** ($V < 0$) widens the barrier to $V_{\text{bi}} + |V|$, halting majority diffusion and leaving only a tiny, thermally generated minority-carrier extraction current $I_0$.

#### Derivation of the Shockley Ideal Diode Equation

**Assumptions:**
1. **Low-level injection:** Injected minority density remains much smaller than majority doping density, keeping quasi-neutral regions field-free ($\mathcal{E} \approx 0$ outside $[-x_p, x_n]$).
2. **Negligible generation–recombination inside the narrow depletion layer** (so electron and hole currents are continuous across $[-x_p, x_n]$).
3. **Long-base diode:** Neutral $n$- and $p$-region lengths are much larger than minority diffusion lengths ($L_p = \sqrt{D_p \tau_p}$, $L_n = \sqrt{D_n \tau_n}$).
4. **Boltzmann quasi-equilibrium across the depletion region** ("Law of the Junction").

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**

1. Across the lowered barrier $(V_{\text{bi}} - V)$, the Boltzmann ratio fixes minority-carrier concentrations at the depletion edges $x = x_n$ and $x = -x_p$:
   $$p_n(x_n) = p_{p0}\exp\left[-\frac{e(V_{\text{bi}} - V)}{k_B T}\right] = \underbrace{p_{p0}\exp\left(-\frac{eV_{\text{bi}}}{k_B T}\right)}_{p_{n0}} \exp\left(\frac{eV}{k_B T}\right) = p_{n0}\exp\left(\frac{eV}{k_B T}\right)$$
   Symmetrically, $n_p(-x_p) = n_{p0}\exp\left(\frac{eV}{k_B T}\right)$. Thus the excess minority concentrations at the edges are:
   $$\Delta p_n(x_n) = p_{n0}\left(e^{\frac{eV}{k_B T}} - 1\right), \qquad \Delta n_p(-x_p) = n_{p0}\left(e^{\frac{eV}{k_B T}} - 1\right)$$

2. In the field-free neutral $n$-region ($x \ge x_n$), steady-state continuity balances hole diffusion against recombination ($\tau_p$):
   $$D_p \frac{d^2 (\Delta p_n)}{dx^2} - \frac{\Delta p_n}{\tau_p} = 0 \implies \frac{d^2 (\Delta p_n)}{dx^2} = \frac{\Delta p_n}{L_p^2} \qquad \left(L_p \equiv \sqrt{D_p \tau_p}\right)$$

3. Applying boundary conditions $\Delta p_n(x_n) = p_{n0}(e^{eV/(k_B T)} - 1)$ and $\Delta p_n(\infty) = 0$ yields exponential spatial decay:
   $$\Delta p_n(x) = p_{n0}\left(e^{\frac{eV}{k_B T}} - 1\right) \exp\left(-\frac{x - x_n}{L_p}\right)$$

4. Evaluate the hole diffusion current density entering the $n$-region at $x = x_n$:
   $$J_p(x_n) = -e D_p \left.\frac{d(\Delta p_n)}{dx}\right|_{x = x_n} = \frac{e D_p p_{n0}}{L_p}\left(e^{\frac{eV}{k_B T}} - 1\right)$$

5. Symmetrically, solving $D_n \frac{d^2(\Delta n_p)}{dx^2} = \frac{\Delta n_p}{\tau_n}$ for $x \le -x_p$ with $\Delta n_p(-\infty) = 0$ gives $\Delta n_p(x) = \Delta n_p(-x_p) e^{(x + x_p)/L_n}$, yielding electron diffusion current at $x = -x_p$:
   $$J_n(-x_p) = +e D_n \left.\frac{d(\Delta n_p)}{dx}\right|_{x = -x_p} = \frac{e D_n n_{p0}}{L_n}\left(e^{\frac{eV}{k_B T}} - 1\right)$$

6. With negligible depletion-layer recombination, $J_n$ and $J_p$ are constant across the depletion layer, so total current $I = A\left[J_p(x_n) + J_n(-x_p)\right]$. Using $p_{n0} = n_i^2 / N_d$ and $n_{p0} = n_i^2 / N_a$ and including the empirical **ideality factor $\eta$**:

$$\boxed{I = I_0 \left[\exp\left(\frac{eV}{\eta k_B T}\right) - 1\right], \qquad I_0 = e A n_i^2 \left(\frac{D_p}{L_p N_d} + \frac{D_n}{L_n N_a}\right)}$$

#### Key Practical Parameters, Limiting Cases, and Temperature Dependence

- **Ideality Factor ($\eta$):** $\eta = 1$ for pure diffusion (Ge diodes and Si at moderate forward bias); $\eta \approx 2$ when Sah–Noyce–Shockley recombination inside the space-charge region dominates (Si diodes at very low forward bias).
- **Limiting Cases:**
  - *Thermal equilibrium ($V = 0$):* $I = 0$.
  - *Forward bias ($V \gg \eta V_T$):* $I \approx I_0 e^{V / (\eta V_T)}$ (exponential turn-on around knee voltage $\approx 0.7\text{ V}$ for Si, $\approx 0.3\text{ V}$ for Ge).
  - *Reverse bias ($V \ll -\eta V_T$):* $e^{V/(\eta V_T)} \to 0$, so $I \approx -I_0$ (tiny reverse saturation current: $\text{nA–pA}$ in Si, $\mu\text{A}$ in Ge because $n_i^2 \propto e^{-E_g / (k_B T)}$ is $\sim 10^6\times$ larger in Ge).
- **Dynamic (AC) Resistance ($r_d$):** Differentiating $I(V)$ under forward bias ($I \gg I_0$):
  $$\frac{1}{r_d} = \frac{dI}{dV} = \frac{e I_0}{\eta k_B T} e^{\frac{eV}{\eta k_B T}} \approx \frac{I}{\eta V_T} \implies \boxed{r_d = \frac{\eta k_B T}{e I} = \frac{\eta V_T}{I}}$$
  At $300\text{ K}$ with $\eta = 1$, $r_d \approx \frac{25.85\text{ mV}}{I(\text{mA})}$.
- **Temperature Sensitivity:** Since $I_0 \propto n_i^2 \propto T^3 e^{-E_g / (k_B T)}$, $I_0$ roughly **doubles for every $10^\circ\text{C}$ rise** in temperature, while the forward voltage $V$ at fixed current drops by $\frac{dV}{dT} \approx -2.0\text{ mV/K}$ for Si.

### 2.3 Breakdown Mechanisms, Tunnel Diode, and Light-Emitting Diode

#### Zener vs. Avalanche Reverse Breakdown

When reverse bias across a $p\text{-}n$ junction exceeds a critical breakdown voltage $V_{BR}$, reverse current skyrockets steeply while terminal voltage stays clamped nearly constant. Two distinct microscopic mechanisms produce this non-destructive breakdown (provided external series resistance limits Joule heating below $P_{\max}$):

1. **Zener Breakdown (Quantum Tunneling):** In **heavily doped** junctions ($N_a, N_d \gtrsim 10^{24}\text{ m}^{-3}$), the depletion width is ultrathin ($W \lesssim 10\text{ nm}$), creating colossal electric fields ($\mathcal{E} \gtrsim 10^8\text{ V/m}$) at modest reverse voltages ($V_Z \lesssim 5\text{ V}$). Steep band bending pulls the $n$-side conduction band below the $p$-side valence band across an atomic-scale barrier, allowing valence electrons on the $p$-side to **quantum-mechanically tunnel** directly into empty conduction states on the $n$-side without thermal excitation. Because rising temperature narrows the band gap $E_g(T)$, tunneling becomes easier at higher $T$, giving Zener breakdown a **negative temperature coefficient** ($\frac{dV_Z}{dT} < 0$).

2. **Avalanche Breakdown (Impact Ionization):** In **lightly or moderately doped** junctions, the depletion region is wide and breakdown occurs at higher voltages ($V_{BR} \gtrsim 6\text{ V}$). Minority carriers sweeping across the depletion layer accelerate to kinetic energies exceeding $E_g$ before colliding with lattice atoms, knocking covalent electrons free (**impact ionization**). These secondary carriers accelerate and ionize further atoms in a geometric cascade described by the empirical multiplication factor $M = \left[1 - (V_R / V_{BR})^n\right]^{-1}$. Raising temperature intensifies lattice phonon vibrations, shortening the carrier mean free path; a larger electric field is therefore needed to reach ionization energy between collisions, giving avalanche breakdown a **positive temperature coefficient** ($\frac{dV_{BR}}{dT} > 0$).

| Feature | Zener Breakdown | Avalanche Breakdown |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Doping Level** | Heavy doping on both sides ($\sim 10^{24}\text{–}10^{25}\text{ m}^{-3}$) | Light to moderate doping ($\lesssim 10^{23}\text{ m}^{-3}$) |
| **Depletion Layer Width ($W$)** | Ultrathin ($\lesssim 10\text{ nm}$) | Wide ($\sim 0.1\text{–}10\text{ }\mu\text{m}$) |
| **Microscopic Mechanism** | Direct band-to-band **quantum tunneling** across forbidden gap | **Impact ionization** & carrier multiplication cascade ($M \to \infty$) |
| **Typical Breakdown Voltage** | $V_Z \lesssim 5\text{ V}$ (Si) | $V_{BR} \gtrsim 6\text{ V}$ (Si) *(both mechanisms coexist near $5\text{–}6\text{ V}$)* |
| **Temperature Coefficient ($dV_{BR}/dT$)** | **Negative** (band gap $E_g$ shrinks with rising $T$) | **Positive** (phonon scattering shortens mean free path with rising $T$) |
| **Reverse $I\text{–}V$ Knee** | Slightly rounded knee | Very sharp, abrupt breakdown knee |
| **Precision Engineering Note** | Zero-TC voltage references are fabricated at $V_Z \approx 5.1\text{–}5.6\text{ V}$ where negative Zener TC and positive Avalanche TC cancel exactly | Used in high-voltage transient suppressors, IMPATT diodes, and Avalanche Photodiodes (APDs) |

#### Tunnel (Esaki) Diode

A **tunnel diode** pushes doping to extreme **degeneracy** ($N_a, N_d \sim 10^{25}\text{–}10^{26}\text{ m}^{-3}$), driving $E_F$ deep *inside* the valence band on the $p$-side and *inside* the conduction band on the $n$-side, with a razor-thin depletion layer ($\sim 10\text{ nm}$). Because quantum tunneling occurs at the Fermi velocity with picosecond transit times, the diode exhibits an iconic **N-shaped $I\text{–}V$ characteristic** with four bias regimes:

1. **Reverse Bias ($V < 0$):** Filled $p$-side valence states sit opposite empty $n$-side conduction states; reverse tunneling current rises steeply and monotonically (essentially zero reverse breakdown voltage).
2. **Small Forward Bias ($0 < V < V_P$):** Filled $n$-side conduction states align directly across the thin barrier with empty $p$-side valence states above $E_{Fp}$; forward tunneling current surges rapidly to a **peak current $I_P$ at peak voltage $V_P$** ($\sim 50\text{–}100\text{ mV}$).
3. **Negative Differential Resistance (NDR) Region ($V_P < V < V_V$):** Increasing forward bias shifts the $n$-side conduction band upward past the empty $p$-side valence states, *reducing* band overlap. Tunneling current drops steeply to a **valley current $I_V$ at valley voltage $V_V$** ($\sim 300\text{–}500\text{ mV}$), producing $\frac{dI}{dV} < 0$.
4. **Normal Injection Region ($V > V_V$):** Band-to-band tunneling ceases completely; ordinary forward thermionic diffusion current takes over exponentially.

Because the NDR region supplies AC power to cancel tank losses, tunnel diodes function as **ultra-high-frequency microwave oscillators ($>10\text{ GHz}$)** and picosecond digital switches.

#### Light-Emitting Diode (LED)

A forward-biased $p\text{-}n$ junction emits spontaneous optical photons when injected electrons recombine radiatively across the band gap with holes (**electroluminescence**). The emitted photon energy matches $E_g$, fixing the peak emission wavelength:
$$\boxed{\lambda \approx \frac{hc}{E_g} = \frac{1240\text{ eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{E_g(\text{eV})}}$$
**Why Silicon and Germanium Cannot Serve as Efficient LEDs:** Both Si and Ge are **indirect-bandgap** semiconductors—the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum sit at *different* crystal momentum vectors ($\Delta \mathbf{k} \ne 0$). Because an optical photon carries negligible momentum ($k_{\text{ph}} = 2\pi/\lambda \ll \pi/a$), radiative recombination in Si/Ge requires simultaneous emission or absorption of a lattice phonon to conserve crystal momentum—a slow, low-probability second-order process overwhelmed by non-radiative heat dissipation.

Efficient LEDs therefore use **direct-bandgap** III–V alloys where $\Delta \mathbf{k} = 0$: **GaAs** ($E_g = 1.42\text{ eV}$, infrared $870\text{ nm}$), **$\text{GaAs}_{1-x}\text{P}_x$** (red–yellow $650\text{–}590\text{ nm}$), and **GaN / InGaN** ($E_g \approx 2.7\text{–}3.4\text{ eV}$, blue–UV, coated with yellow YAG:Ce phosphor for white illumination). External quantum efficiency $\eta_{\text{ext}} = \eta_{\text{int}}\cdot\eta_{\text{opt}}$ is severely constrained by **total internal reflection** at the high-index semiconductor–air interface ($\theta_c = \arcsin(1/n_s) \approx 16^\circ$ for GaAs with $n_s \approx 3.6$), which is mitigated using epoxy dome lenses and textured surfaces.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2.2: Zener Diode Thermal Burnout Limit & Direct-Bandgap LED Emission
> **Problem:** (a) A $1.0\text{ W}$ Zener diode has a breakdown voltage $V_Z = 3.0\text{ V}$ and dynamic resistance $r_z = 10\text{ m}\Omega$. If the voltage across the diode fluctuates by $\Delta V = 100\text{ mV}$, calculate the resulting current surge $\Delta I$ and determine what happens to the diode. (b) Calculate the peak emission wavelength of a direct-bandgap $\text{GaAs}_{0.6}\text{P}_{0.4}$ LED having $E_g = 1.90\text{ eV}$.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Zener Power Limit and Surge Current:**
>    - Maximum safe steady-state current before thermal damage:
>      $$I_{Z,\max} = \frac{P_{\max}}{V_Z} = \frac{1.0\text{ W}}{3.0\text{ V}} = 0.333\text{ A} = 333.3\text{ mA}$$
>    - Current fluctuation caused by $\Delta V = 100\text{ mV} = 0.10\text{ V}$ across $r_z = 10\text{ m}\Omega = 0.010\text{ }\Omega$:
>      $$\Delta I = \frac{\Delta V}{r_z} = \frac{0.10\text{ V}}{0.010\text{ }\Omega} = 10.0\text{ A}$$
>    - Instantaneous power dissipation during the surge: $P \approx (3.1\text{ V})(10.0\text{ A}) \approx 31\text{ W} \gg 1.0\text{ W}$ (and $\Delta I = 10\text{ A} \approx 30\,I_{Z,\max}$). Therefore, **the diode immediately suffers thermal burnout** unless protected by an external current-limiting series resistor.
>

>    $$\lambda = \frac{hc}{E_g} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s})(2.998 \times 10^{8}\text{ m/s})}{(1.90)(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J})} = \frac{1.9865 \times 10^{-25}}{3.0438 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.53 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} = 653\text{ nm}$$
>    This falls cleanly in the visible **red** optical band.
>

### 2.4 Thermistors - Carrier Physics, Characteristics, and Applications

A **thermistor** (*THERMal resISTOR*) is a bulk, thermally sensitive semiconductor resistor whose electrical resistance changes dramatically—by roughly $3\text{–}6\%\text{ K}^{-1}$, an order of magnitude higher than metallic RTDs like platinum ($+0.385\%\text{ K}^{-1}$)—with small temperature variations. Thermistors fall into two physical families: **NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient)** devices, whose resistance drops exponentially as temperature rises, and **PTC (Positive Temperature Coefficient)** switching ceramics, whose resistance rockets upward abruptly above a Curie phase-transition temperature.

#### Why Pure Silicon and Germanium Are NOT Normally Used as Thermistors

Although intrinsic Si and Ge exhibit negative temperature coefficients of resistance, practical thermistors are **rarely** fabricated from elemental Si or Ge for four concrete physical and engineering reasons:
1. **Non-Monotonic $R(T)$ in Extrinsic Regime:** Even parts-per-billion trace impurities create an extrinsic saturation plateau around room temperature where carrier concentration stays flat ($n \approx N_d$) while phonon scattering degrades mobility ($\mu \propto T^{-3/2}$), causing resistance to *increase* with $T$ before dropping in the intrinsic regime—ruining monotonic sensing.
2. **Poor Reproducibility and Interchangeability:** Resistivity in Si/Ge depends hyper-sensitively on trace doping fluctuations, making mass manufacture of identical calibrated sensors prohibitively expensive.
3. **Narrow Operating Temperature Range:** Ge turns intrinsic below $100^\circ\text{C}$, while both suffer surface oxidation and contact instability at elevated temperatures.
4. **Low Resistivity / High Thermal Sensitivity Trade-Off:** Controlled high base resistances ($1\text{ k}\Omega\text{–}1\text{ M}\Omega$) with stable thermal activation energies are far easier to engineer in ceramic oxides.

Instead, **NTC thermistors** are sintered polycrystalline mixtures of **transition-metal oxides**—primarily spinel oxides of **manganese ($\text{Mn}_3\text{O}_4$), nickel ($\text{NiO}$), cobalt ($\text{Co}_2\text{O}_3$), iron ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$), copper ($\text{CuO}$), and uranium**—pressed into beads, discs, or rods and fired at $>1000^\circ\text{C}$. **PTC thermistors** are fabricated from donor-doped ferroelectric ceramics such as **barium titanate ($\text{BaTiO}_3$)** doped with rare earths (La, Y, Sb).

#### Derivation of the NTC Thermistor Resistance Law and Temperature Coefficient ($\alpha_T$)

In transition-metal oxide semiconductors, electrical conduction proceeds via thermally activated small-polaron hopping (or carrier excitation across an activation energy $E_a$). The carrier density (and hopping mobility) scales with temperature as $e^{-E_a / (k_B T)}$, yielding resistivity $\rho(T) = \rho_\infty \exp\left(\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right)$. Defining the **material characteristic temperature** $\beta \equiv \frac{E_a}{k_B}$ (typically $\beta \approx 2000\text{–}5000\text{ K}$):
$$R(T) = R_\infty \exp\left(\frac{\beta}{T}\right)$$
Evaluating at a reference temperature $T_0$ (commonly $298.15\text{ K} = 25^\circ\text{C}$) where $R(T_0) = R_0 = R_\infty e^{\beta / T_0}$ and taking the ratio $R(T) / R_0$ eliminates $R_\infty$:

$$\boxed{R(T) = R_0 \exp\left[\beta\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_0}\right)\right]}$$

Differentiating $\ln R(T) = \ln R_0 + \beta\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_0}\right)$ with respect to $T$ yields the **temperature coefficient of resistance ($\alpha_T$)**:

$$\boxed{\alpha_T \equiv \frac{1}{R}\frac{dR}{dT} = -\frac{\beta}{T^2}}$$

**Dimensional and Limiting Checks:** $[\beta] = \text{K}$, so $[\alpha_T] = \text{K}/\text{K}^2 = \text{K}^{-1}$. Note that $\alpha_T$ is **not constant**—its magnitude decreases as $1/T^2$ at higher temperatures. For precision wide-range thermometry, the three-parameter **Steinhart–Hart equation** refines the $\beta$-model to $\pm 0.01\text{ K}$ accuracy:
$$\frac{1}{T} = A + B\ln R + C(\ln R)^3$$

#### PTC Switching Mechanism (Heywang Grain-Boundary Model)

In donor-doped polycrystalline $\text{BaTiO}_3$, trapped acceptor states at grain boundaries form local Schottky potential barriers of height $\phi_B \propto \frac{1}{\varepsilon_r(T)}$. Below the ferroelectric **Curie temperature $T_C$** ($\sim 120^\circ\text{C}$, tunable via Sr or Pb substitution), spontaneous polarization neutralizes the boundary charges and $\varepsilon_r$ is enormous, keeping $R(T)$ low ($10\text{–}100\text{ }\Omega$). Immediately above $T_C$, the crystal transforms to a paraelectric phase whose dielectric constant plunges via the Curie–Weiss law $\varepsilon_r = C/(T - T_C)$; the grain-boundary barrier $\phi_B$ shoots up, causing $R(T)$ to jump by **$10^3\text{–}10^6\times$** over a $20\text{–}40\text{ K}$ window.

#### Self-Heating Static $V\text{–}I$ Characteristic of an NTC Thermistor

When current $I$ passes through a thermistor of ambient temperature $T_{\text{amb}}$, steady-state thermal equilibrium equates electrical Joule heating to heat dissipated to the surroundings:
$$P = VI = I^2 R(T) = \delta (T - T_{\text{amb}})$$
where $\delta$ is the **dissipation constant** ($\text{W/K}$, defined as the power required to raise the thermistor body by $1\text{ K}$ above ambient—strongly dependent on whether the device sits in still air, flowing gas, or liquid).

This thermal feedback divides the static $V\text{–}I$ curve into two distinct regions:
1. **Low-Current Ohmic Region ($I \ll I_{\text{peak}}$):** $I^2 R \ll \delta$, so self-heating is negligible ($T \approx T_{\text{amb}}$), $R \approx R_0 = \text{constant}$, and $V = I R_0$ rises linearly (used for pure temperature measurement).
2. **Self-Heating / Negative Differential Resistance (NDR) Region ($I > I_{\text{peak}}$):** Joule heating elevates $T$ substantially, plunging $R(T)$ faster than $I$ grows. Terminal voltage $V = I R(T)$ reaches a maximum $V_{\max}$ at $\frac{dV}{dI} = 0$ and then **falls with increasing current** ($\frac{dV}{dI} < 0$). Differentiating $V = I R(T)$ and $I^2 R(T) = \delta(T - T_{\text{amb}})$ yields the exact turnover condition:
   $$\frac{dV}{dI} = R + I\frac{dR}{dT}\frac{dT}{dI} = 0 \implies \frac{\beta(T_{\text{peak}} - T_{\text{amb}})}{T_{\text{peak}}^2} = 1$$
   Operating in this NDR regime requires a series ballast resistor to prevent runaway current surges.

#### Principal Engineering Applications of Thermistors

1. **High-Resolution Thermometry & Bridge Sensing:** Placed in one arm of a Wheatstone bridge (with tiny excitation current to avoid self-heating) for medical thermometers, HVAC, battery packs, and spacecraft thermal loops.
2. **Thermal-Runaway Compensation in BJT Amplifiers:** Shunted across the base–emitter biasing divider (or in series with $R_1$); as ambient temperature rises, the NTC thermistor's falling resistance pulls down base bias voltage $V_B$, counteracting the thermally driven surge in collector current $I_C$.
3. **Inrush Current Limiting (Soft-Start Protection):** Wired in series with SMPS filter capacitors or tungsten filaments; cold high resistance chokes the initial turn-on surge, after which load current self-heats the thermistor into a low-resistance steady state.
4. **Time-Delay Relays:** Exploits the finite thermal time constant $\tau_{\text{th}} = C_{\text{th}}/\delta$ required for self-heating to drop resistance and allow relay coil energization.
5. **Fluid Flow, Liquid-Level, and Vacuum (Thermal Conductivity) Sensing:** Operated in the self-heating region; moving fluid or liquid immersion abruptly increases $\delta$, cooling the thermistor and shifting its terminal voltage sharply.
6. **Self-Resetting Overcurrent Protection & Self-Regulating Heaters (PTC):** Excess fault current heats a PTC ceramic above $T_C$, snapping it to megaohm resistance until power is removed.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2.3: NTC Thermistor Characterization and Self-Heating Error Analysis
> **Problem:** An NTC oxide thermistor measures $R_0 = 10.0\text{ k}\Omega$ at $T_0 = 25^\circ\text{C}$ ($298.15\text{ K}$) and $R_1 = 2.50\text{ k}\Omega$ at $T_1 = 60^\circ\text{C}$ ($333.15\text{ K}$). Its dissipation constant in still air is $\delta = 5.0\text{ mW/K}$. Calculate: (a) its characteristic temperature constant $\beta$, (b) its temperature coefficient $\alpha_T$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$, and (c) the self-heating temperature error $\Delta T$ if a constant measurement current of $1.0\text{ mA}$ is driven through it at $25^\circ\text{C}$ ambient.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Material Constant ($\beta$):** Taking natural logs of $R_1 / R_0 = \exp\left[\beta\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_0}\right)\right]$:
>    $$\ln\left(\frac{2.50}{10.0}\right) = \beta\left(\frac{1}{333.15} - \frac{1}{298.15}\right)$$
>    $$-1.3863 = \beta\left(\frac{298.15 - 333.15}{333.15 \times 298.15}\right) = \beta(-3.5237 \times 10^{-4}\text{ K}^{-1})$$
>    $$\beta = \frac{1.3863}{3.5237 \times 10^{-4}} = 3934\text{ K}$$
>
> 2. **Temperature Coefficient of Resistance ($\alpha_T$) at $25^\circ\text{C}$:**
>    $$\alpha_T = -\frac{\beta}{T_0^2} = -\frac{3934}{(298.15)^2} = -0.0443\text{ K}^{-1} = -4.43\%\text{ K}^{-1}$$
>
> 3. **Self-Heating Error at $I = 1.0\text{ mA}$:**
>    - First-order estimate using cold resistance $R_0$:
>      $$P \approx I^2 R_0 = (1.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ A})^2 (10.0 \times 10^{3}\text{ }\Omega) = 10.0\text{ mW} \implies \Delta T_1 = \frac{P}{\delta} = \frac{10.0\text{ mW}}{5.0\text{ mW/K}} = 2.00\text{ K}$$
>    - Self-consistent refinement (accounting for resistance drop due to heating): At $T = 300.0\text{ K}$, $R \approx 10.0\text{ k}\Omega \times e^{3934(1/300.0 - 1/298.15)} = 9.21\text{ k}\Omega \implies P = 9.21\text{ mW} \implies \Delta T = \frac{9.21}{5.0} = 1.84\text{ K}$.
>    *(This $1.84\text{ K}$ measurement artifact explains why precision sensing limits bias current to $\le 100\text{ }\mu\text{A}$, keeping $\Delta T < 0.02\text{ K}$.)*
>
> $$\boxed{\beta = 3934\text{ K}, \qquad \alpha_T(25^\circ\text{C}) = -4.43\%\text{ K}^{-1}, \qquad \Delta T_{\text{self-heat}} \approx 1.84\text{ K}}$$

### 2.5 Solar Cells - Photovoltaic Mechanism, Characteristics, and Efficiency

A **solar cell** is a large-area, zero-external-bias $p\text{-}n$ junction diode engineered to convert optical energy directly into DC electrical power via the **photovoltaic effect**.

#### Device Structure and Four Mandatory Energy-Conversion Processes

A standard crystalline-silicon solar cell consists of: (i) a very thin, heavily doped **$n^+$ top emitter** ($\sim 0.2\text{–}0.5\text{ }\mu\text{m}$) facing sunlight so short-wavelength photons penetrate to the depletion region before recombining; (ii) a quarter-wave **anti-reflection coating (ARC)** (e.g., $\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$ or $\text{TiO}_2$) over micro-textured silicon pyramids to suppress surface reflection from $\sim 35\%$ down to $<3\%$; (iii) a fine front metallic **finger–busbar grid** balancing low series resistance against minimal optical shadowing; (iv) a thicker, lightly doped **$p$-type absorber base** ($\sim 200\text{–}300\text{ }\mu\text{m}$) with long minority-carrier diffusion length $L_n$; and (v) a full back ohmic contact (often with a $p^+$ **Back Surface Field** to reflect minority electrons away from rear recombination).

Converting incident photons into useful external electrical work requires **four sequential physical processes**:
1. **Optical Absorption & Carrier Generation:** Photons with energy $h\nu \ge E_g$ are absorbed via Beer–Lambert attenuation $\Phi(x) = \Phi_0 e^{-\alpha(\lambda) x}$, exciting electron–hole pairs across the band gap.
2. **Carrier Transport & Charge Separation:** Photogenerated minority carriers created inside the depletion layer—or within one diffusion length ($L_n, L_p$) of its edges—diffuse to the space-charge zone and are violently swept apart by the built-in electric field $\mathcal{E}_{\text{bi}}$ (electrons driven to the $n$-side, holes to the $p$-side), generating a reverse-directed **photocurrent $I_L$**.
3. **Photovoltage Development:** Under open-circuit conditions, separated electrons accumulate on the $n$-side and holes on the $p$-side, lowering the junction barrier and creating a forward-biasing **photovoltage $V$** across the terminals until the internal forward diode current $I_D(V)$ cancels $I_L$ exactly.
4. **External Power Delivery:** Connecting a load resistance $R_L$ across the terminals diverts part of $I_L$ through $R_L$, delivering useful power $P = V I_{\text{out}}$ in the **fourth quadrant** of the standard diode $I\text{–}V$ plane ($V > 0$, diode terminal current $I < 0$).

#### Derivation of Illuminated $I\text{–}V$ Equation, $V_{oc}$, $I_{sc}$, Fill Factor, and Efficiency

**Assumptions:** Superposition holds (photogenerated current $I_L$ adds linearly opposite to the dark Shockley diode current), negligible parasitic resistances initially ($R_s = 0$, $R_{sh} \to \infty$).

1. **Illuminated Diode Equation:** In standard diode sign convention (positive current entering the $p$-terminal), the photocurrent flows from $n \to p$ (negative direction), shifting the dark $I\text{–}V$ curve downward by $I_L$:
   $$\boxed{I = I_0\left[\exp\left(\frac{eV}{\eta k_B T}\right) - 1\right] - I_L \qquad (\text{4th-quadrant diode convention})}$$
   Equivalently, defining load output current $I_{\text{out}} = -I > 0$ (plotting power delivery in the first quadrant):
   $$I_{\text{out}} = I_L - I_0\left[\exp\left(\frac{eV}{\eta k_B T}\right) - 1\right]$$

2. **Short-Circuit Current ($I_{sc}$):** Setting terminal voltage $V = 0$ (ideal short circuit, $R_L = 0$):
   $$\boxed{I_{sc} = I_{\text{out}}(V = 0) = I_L \propto G}$$
   Thus $I_{sc}$ scales **linearly** with incident optical irradiance $G$ ($\text{W/m}^2$).

3. **Open-Circuit Voltage ($V_{oc}$):** Setting load current $I_{\text{out}} = 0$ ($R_L \to \infty$):
   $$0 = I_L - I_0\left[\exp\left(\frac{e V_{oc}}{\eta k_B T}\right) - 1\right] \implies \exp\left(\frac{e V_{oc}}{\eta k_B T}\right) = \frac{I_L}{I_0} + 1$$
   $$\boxed{V_{oc} = \frac{\eta k_B T}{e}\ln\left(\frac{I_L}{I_0} + 1\right) \approx \frac{\eta k_B T}{e}\ln\left(\frac{I_L}{I_0}\right)}$$
   Unlike $I_{sc}$, $V_{oc}$ increases only **logarithmically** with irradiance and **drops markedly with rising temperature** ($\approx -2.2\text{ mV/K}$ for Si) because $I_0 \propto e^{-E_g / (k_B T)}$ explodes exponentially with $T$.

4. **Maximum Power Point ($V_m, I_m$):** Delivered electrical power is $P(V) = V I_{\text{out}} = V\left[I_L - I_0(e^{eV/(\eta k_B T)} - 1)\right]$. Setting $\frac{dP}{dV} = 0$:
   $$I_L + I_0 - I_0 e^{\frac{eV_m}{\eta k_B T}} - \frac{e V_m}{\eta k_B T} I_0 e^{\frac{eV_m}{\eta k_B T}} = 0 \implies \boxed{\exp\left(\frac{eV_m}{\eta k_B T}\right)\left(1 + \frac{eV_m}{\eta k_B T}\right) = 1 + \frac{I_L}{I_0} = \exp\left(\frac{eV_{oc}}{\eta k_B T}\right)}$$
   Substituting $I_0 e^{eV_m/(\eta k_B T)} = \frac{I_L + I_0}{1 + eV_m/(\eta k_B T)}$ back into $I_{\text{out}}$ gives the exact current at maximum power:
   $$I_m \approx I_L \left(\frac{eV_m / (\eta k_B T)}{1 + eV_m / (\eta k_B T)}\right)$$

5. **Fill Factor ($\text{FF}$) and Conversion Efficiency ($\eta_{\text{cell}}$):** The **Fill Factor** measures the "squareness" of the $I\text{–}V$ knee—geometrically, the area ratio of the largest inscribed power rectangle ($V_m I_m$) to the bounding rectangle ($V_{oc} I_{sc}$):
   $$\boxed{\text{FF} = \frac{P_{\max}}{V_{oc} I_{sc}} = \frac{V_m I_m}{V_{oc} I_{sc}}, \qquad \eta_{\text{cell}} = \frac{P_{\max}}{P_{\text{in}}} = \frac{\text{FF} \cdot V_{oc} I_{sc}}{G \cdot A}}$$
   High-grade silicon cells achieve $\text{FF} \approx 0.78\text{–}0.85$ and $\eta_{\text{cell}} \approx 20\text{–}26\%$.

#### Practical Equivalent Circuit and Factors Limiting Solar Cell Efficiency

Real cells contain a **series resistance $R_s$** (emitter sheet resistance, contact fingers, bulk wafer) and a parallel **shunt resistance $R_{sh}$** (manufacturing defects/micro-cracks leaking current across the junction), modifying the characteristic to:
$$I_{\text{out}} = I_L - I_0\left[\exp\left(\frac{e(V + I_{\text{out}} R_s)}{\eta k_B T}\right) - 1\right] - \frac{V + I_{\text{out}} R_s}{R_{sh}}$$
Large $R_s$ slopes the vertical leg near $V_{oc}$ inward; small $R_{sh}$ slopes the horizontal leg near $I_{sc}$ downward—both severely degrade $\text{FF}$ and $P_{\max}$.

**Complete Taxonomy of Factors Affecting Solar Cell Efficiency:**
- **Fundamental Bandgap Trade-Off (Shockley–Queisser Limit):** Photons with $h\nu < E_g$ transmit unabsorbed (**transmission loss**); photons with $h\nu > E_g$ rapidly shed their excess energy $(h\nu - E_g)$ as lattice heat (**thermalization loss**). Small $E_g$ boosts $I_{sc}$ but slashes $V_{oc}$; large $E_g$ boosts $V_{oc}$ but starves $I_{sc}$. Under the standard AM1.5G solar spectrum ($1000\text{ W/m}^2$), this trade-off peaks at $E_g \approx 1.34\text{ eV}$ ($\eta_{\text{SQ}} \approx 33.7\%$ for a single junction; GaAs sits near optimal at $1.42\text{ eV}$, Si at $1.12\text{ eV}$ caps theoretically at $\sim 29\%$).
- **Recombination Losses:** Bulk Shockley–Read–Hall (trap), radiative, Auger, and front/rear surface recombination reduce minority lifetime $\tau$ and diffusion length $L$, lowering collection probability and raising $I_0$ (which depresses $V_{oc}$).
- **Optical Losses:** Fresnel surface reflection (curbed by ARC & texturing) and top metal grid shading.
- **Parasitic Electrical Losses:** High $R_s$ ($I^2 R_s$ Joule heat) and low $R_{sh}$ (leakage pathways) shrink $\text{FF}$.
- **Operating Temperature & Spectrum:** Rising cell temperature degrades $V_{oc}$ far faster than the tiny band-gap-narrowing rise in $I_{sc}$, reducing silicon efficiency by roughly $-0.4\%\text{ to }-0.5\%$ (relative) per kelvin.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 2.4: Silicon Solar Cell Maximum Power Point, Fill Factor, and Conversion Efficiency
> **Problem:** A monocrystalline silicon solar cell of active area $A = 100\text{ cm}^2 = 0.010\text{ m}^2$ operates at $T = 300\text{ K}$ ($\eta = 1$, $V_T = 0.02585\text{ V}$) under AM1.5G sunlight of irradiance $G = 1000\text{ W/m}^2$. Given dark saturation current $I_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\text{ A}$ and photocurrent $I_L = 3.50\text{ A}$, compute: (a) $I_{sc}$ and $V_{oc}$, (b) maximum-power voltage $V_m$, current $I_m$, and $P_{\max}$, (c) Fill Factor $\text{FF}$ and conversion efficiency $\eta_{\text{cell}}$, and (d) the new $I_{sc}$ and $V_{oc}$ if sunlight intensity doubles at constant temperature.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Short-Circuit Current & Open-Circuit Voltage:**
>    $$I_{sc} = I_L = 3.50\text{ A}$$
>    $$V_{oc} = V_T \ln\left(\frac{I_L}{I_0} + 1\right) \approx (0.02585\text{ V})\ln\left(\frac{3.50}{1.0 \times 10^{-10}}\right) = 0.02585 \times 24.279 = 0.628\text{ V}$$
>
> 2. **Maximum Power Point ($V_m, I_m, P_{\max}$):** Let $u \equiv V_m / V_T$. Taking logs of $e^u (1 + u) = I_L / I_0 = 3.50 \times 10^{10}$:
>    $$u + \ln(1 + u) = \ln(3.50 \times 10^{10}) = 24.279$$
>    Iterating from $u \approx 24.28 - \ln(25) = 21.06 \implies u + \ln(1 + u) = 21.06 + 3.094 = 24.15 \implies$ refine to $u = 21.18$ ($21.18 + \ln 22.18 = 21.18 + 3.099 = 24.279$, exact!):
>    $$V_m = u V_T = 21.18 \times 0.02585\text{ V} = 0.548\text{ V}$$
>    $$I_m = I_L \left(\frac{u}{1 + u}\right) = (3.50\text{ A})\left(\frac{21.18}{22.18}\right) = 3.342\text{ A}$$
>    $$P_{\max} = V_m I_m = (0.5475\text{ V})(3.342\text{ A}) = 1.830\text{ W}$$
>

>    $$\text{FF} = \frac{P_{\max}}{V_{oc} I_{sc}} = \frac{1.830}{(0.6276)(3.50)} = 0.833 \quad (83.3\%)$$

>
> 4. **Effect of Doubling Irradiance ($I_L' = 2 I_L = 7.00\text{ A}$ at $300\text{ K}$):**
>    $$I_{sc}' = 7.00\text{ A}, \qquad V_{oc}' = V_{oc} + V_T \ln 2 = 0.6276 + (0.02585)(0.6931) = 0.646\text{ V}$$

## 3. Bipolar Junction Transistors - Structure, Characteristics, Biasing, and h-Parameters

### 3.1 Physical Structure, Operating Modes, and Current Transport

A **Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)** is a three-terminal, two-junction semiconductor sandwich ($n\text{-}p\text{-}n$ or $p\text{-}n\text{-}p$) in which **both** electrons and holes participate in current transport. Two separate diodes wired back-to-back cannot act as a transistor; true transistor action demands strict physical asymmetry across its three regions:
- **Emitter ($E$):** **Heavily doped** ($n^{++}$ or $p^{++}$) to inject a massive majority current into the base with near-unity injection efficiency ($\gamma \approx 0.995$).
- **Base ($B$):** **Lightly doped** and **extremely narrow** ($W_B \ll L_B$, typically $0.1\text{–}1\text{ }\mu\text{m}$), ensuring that $>99\%$ of emitter-injected carriers diffuse straight across to the collector edge without recombining (base transport factor $\alpha_T \approx 0.995$).
- **Collector ($C$):** **Moderately doped** and **physically largest in area/volume** to sustain high reverse breakdown voltage across the collector–base junction and safely dissipate Joule power $P_D \approx V_{CE} I_C$.

#### Four Operating Modes of a BJT

| Operating Mode | Emitter–Base Junction (EBJ) | Collector–Base Junction (CBJ) | Physical State & Primary Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Forward-Active** | Forward-biased | Reverse-biased | Linear current-controlled current source ($I_C = \beta I_B$); **analog amplification** |
| **Saturation** | Forward-biased | Forward-biased | Both junctions inject; $V_{CE,\text{sat}} \approx 0.2\text{ V}$ (Si); **closed digital switch** |
| **Cutoff** | Reverse-biased | Reverse-biased | Only tiny leakage currents flow ($I_C \approx 0$); **open digital switch** |
| **Reverse-Active** | Reverse-biased | Forward-biased | Collector acts as poor emitter; very low gain $\beta_R \sim 1\text{–}5$; rarely used |

#### Current Gain Relations ($\alpha, \beta$) and Leakage Currents ($I_{CBO}, I_{CEO}$)

By Kirchhoff's Current Law at the transistor node (magnitude convention: in $n\text{-}p\text{-}n$, conventional $I_B$ and $I_C$ enter the transistor while $I_E$ leaves; in $p\text{-}n\text{-}p$, $I_E$ enters while $I_B$ and $I_C$ leave):
$$I_E = I_C + I_B$$
In the forward-active mode, the fraction $\alpha = \gamma \alpha_T$ ($\approx 0.95\text{–}0.998$) of emitter current reaches the collector, adding to the thermally generated reverse leakage current $I_{CBO}$ (collector-to-base leakage with emitter open):
$$I_C = \alpha I_E + I_{CBO}$$
Substitute $I_E = I_C + I_B$ to express $I_C$ in terms of the base control current $I_B$:
$$I_C = \alpha(I_C + I_B) + I_{CBO} \implies (1 - \alpha) I_C = \alpha I_B + I_{CBO}$$
Dividing through by $(1 - \alpha)$ and defining the **common-emitter current gain** $\beta \equiv \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha}$:

$$\boxed{I_C = \beta I_B + (1 + \beta) I_{CBO} = \beta I_B + I_{CEO}}$$

$$\boxed{\beta = \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\beta}{1 + \beta}, \qquad I_{CEO} = (1 + \beta) I_{CBO}}$$

**Physical Insight:** Because $I_{CEO} = (1 + \beta) I_{CBO}$, leaving the base open ($I_B = 0$) amplifies the tiny CBJ leakage current by $(1 + \beta) \sim 100\times$! That multiplier is the root cause of thermal runaway in poorly biased CE circuits.

### 3.2 CB, CE, and CC Characteristics and the Early Effect

#### Static Input and Output Characteristics

1. **Common-Base (CB) Configuration** (input: $I_E$ vs. $V_{EB}$; output: $I_C$ vs. $V_{CB}$ for constant $I_E$):
   - *Input curve:* Resembles a forward-biased $p\text{-}n$ diode ($V_{BE} \approx 0.7\text{ V}$ for Si), shifting slightly leftward as $|V_{CB}|$ increases due to base-width narrowing.
   - *Output curve:* Nearly horizontal flat lines at $I_C \approx \alpha I_E \approx I_E$ across the active region ($V_{CB} > 0$ for $n\text{-}p\text{-}n$), dropping abruptly to zero when $V_{CB}$ goes slightly forward-biased (saturation). Very low input resistance ($r_e \sim 25\text{ }\Omega$), very high output resistance ($\sim 1\text{ M}\Omega$), no phase inversion ($0^\circ$), ideal for high-frequency/wideband stages.

2. **Common-Emitter (CE) Configuration** (input: $I_B$ vs. $V_{BE}$; output: $I_C$ vs. $V_{CE}$ for constant $I_B$):
   - *Input curve:* Forward diode curve ($I_B$ in $\mu\text{A}$ vs. $V_{BE}$), shifting slightly *rightward* at larger $V_{CE}$ because a thinner base lowers recombination, reducing $I_B$ for a fixed $V_{BE}$.
   - *Output curve:* Exhibits three clear regions: (i) **Saturation** ($V_{CE} \lesssim 0.2\text{ V}$), where $I_C$ rises sharply regardless of $I_B$; (ii) **Active** ($0.2\text{ V} < V_{CE} < BV_{CEO}$), where $I_C \approx \beta I_B$ slopes gently upward with $V_{CE}$; and (iii) **Cutoff** ($I_B = 0$), where only $I_{CEO}$ flows. Provides simultaneous high current gain, high voltage gain, highest power gain, and **$180^\circ$ voltage phase inversion**.

3. **Common-Collector (CC / Emitter Follower)** (input at Base, output taken at Emitter, Collector tied to AC ground):
   - Voltage gain is slightly below unity ($A_v \approx \frac{(1+\beta)R_E}{h_{ie} + (1+\beta)R_E} \approx 0.98\text{–}0.99$, $0^\circ$ phase shift), current gain is maximum ($A_i \approx 1 + \beta$), input resistance is very high ($R_{\text{in}} \approx (1+\beta)R_E$), and output resistance is very low ($R_{\text{out}} \approx \frac{R_s + h_{ie}}{1+\beta}$). Serves universally as an **impedance-matching buffer stage** between a high-impedance source and a low-impedance load.

| Parameter | Common-Base (CB) | Common-Emitter (CE) | Common-Collector (CC) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Current Gain ($A_i$)** | Low ($\alpha < 1$) | High ($\beta \sim 50\text{–}300$) | Highest ($1 + \beta$) |
| **Voltage Gain ($A_v$)** | High ($\sim 50\text{–}300$) | High ($\sim 50\text{–}300$) | Unity ($\lesssim 1$) |
| **Power Gain ($A_p$)** | Moderate | **Highest** | Low–Moderate |
| **Input Resistance ($R_{\text{in}}$)** | Very Low ($\sim 20\text{–}50\text{ }\Omega$) | Moderate ($\sim 1\text{–}2\text{ k}\Omega$) | **Very High** ($\sim 50\text{–}500\text{ k}\Omega$) |
| **Output Resistance ($R_{\text{out}}$)** | **Very High** ($\sim 0.5\text{–}2\text{ M}\Omega$) | High ($\sim 20\text{–}100\text{ k}\Omega$) | **Very Low** ($\sim 20\text{–}100\text{ }\Omega$) |
| **Voltage Phase Shift** | $0^\circ$ | **$180^\circ$ (Inverting)** | $0^\circ$ |
| **Primary Application** | RF / high-frequency / current buffer | General-purpose audio & voltage amplification | Impedance-matching buffer / output driver |

#### The Early Effect (Base-Width Modulation)

Increasing reverse voltage $V_{CB}$ (hence $V_{CE}$) widens the collector–base depletion layer predominantly into the lightly doped base, shrinking the effective neutral base thickness $W_B$. This **Early effect** causes four measurable consequences:
1. **Sloped Active Output Curves & Finite $r_o$:** Because collector diffusion current varies inversely with base width ($I_C \propto \frac{e D_n n_p(0)}{W_B}$), shrinking $W_B$ causes $I_C$ to rise linearly with $V_{CE}$. Extrapolating all active-region curves backward intersects the negative $V_{CE}$ axis at a single point $-V_A$, the **Early Voltage** ($\sim 50\text{–}150\text{ V}$):
   $$I_C = I_{C0}\left(1 + \frac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\right) \implies \boxed{r_o = \left(\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{CE}}\right)^{-1} = \frac{V_A + V_{CEQ}}{I_{CQ}} \approx \frac{V_A}{I_{CQ}}}$$
2. **Slight Rise in $\alpha$ and $\beta$:** Narrower $W_B$ reduces base transit time and recombination, lowering $I_B$ and boosting $\beta$.
3. **Nonzero Reverse Voltage Feedback ($h_{re} \ne 0$):** Changing $V_{CE}$ subtly perturbs $V_{BE}$ at constant $I_B$.
4. **Punch-Through Breakdown:** At extreme reverse $V_{CB}$, the CB depletion edge touches the EB depletion edge ($W_B \to 0$), shorting collector directly to emitter with destructive current surge.

### 3.3 DC Load Line, Q-Point Biasing, and Thermal Stability Factors

To amplify an AC signal cleanly without clipping positive or negative half-cycles, DC biasing must anchor the transistor's **quiescent operating point (Q-point)** $(V_{CEQ}, I_{CQ})$ squarely near the center of the active region.

#### DC Load Line Analysis

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law around the collector–emitter output loop of a CE stage with collector resistor $R_C$ and emitter resistor $R_E$:
$$V_{CC} = I_C R_C + V_{CE} + I_E R_E \approx I_C(R_C + R_E) + V_{CE}$$
Solving for $I_C$ gives the linear **DC Load Line** overlaid on the output characteristics:
$$\boxed{I_C = -\frac{1}{R_C + R_E} V_{CE} + \frac{V_{CC}}{R_C + R_E}}$$
- **Cutoff Intercept ($I_C = 0$):** $V_{CE} = V_{CC}$.
- **Saturation Intercept ($V_{CE} \approx 0$):** $I_{C,\text{sat}} = \frac{V_{CC}}{R_C + R_E}$.
- **Optimal Symmetrical-Swing Q-Point:** $V_{CEQ} \approx \frac{V_{CC}}{2}$, $I_{CQ} \approx \frac{I_{C,\text{sat}}}{2}$.

#### Thermal Runaway and General Stability Factor ($S$)

Because $I_{CBO}$ doubles every $10^\circ\text{C}$, a rise in junction temperature boosts $I_C = \beta I_B + (1+\beta)I_{CBO}$, raising power dissipation $P_D = V_{CE} I_C$, which heats the junction further. Without stabilizing negative DC feedback, this **thermal runaway** loop drives the Q-point straight into saturation or melts the crystal.

To quantify bias stability against leakage drift, define the **Stability Factor $S$** (holding $\beta$ and $V_{BE}$ constant; ideal minimum is $S = 1$, smaller is better):
$$S \equiv \left.\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial I_{CBO}}\right|_{\beta, V_{BE}}$$
Differentiating $I_C = \beta I_B + (1 + \beta) I_{CBO}$ with respect to $I_C$:
$$1 = \beta \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C} + (1 + \beta)\frac{\partial I_{CBO}}{\partial I_C} = \beta \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C} + \frac{1 + \beta}{S}$$
Solving algebraically for $S$:

$$\boxed{S = \frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta\left(\frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C}\right)}}$$

*(Analogous stability factors $S' = \frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{BE}}$ and $S'' = \frac{\partial I_C}{\partial \beta}$ quantify drift from $V_{BE}(T)$ at $-2\text{ mV/K}$ and unit-to-unit $\beta$ spread; circuits that minimize $S$ simultaneously suppress $S'$ and $S''$.)*

#### Exact Q-Point and Stability Factor Across Three Canonical Biasing Circuits

1. **Fixed-Base Bias** ($R_B$ from $V_{CC}$ to Base, Emitter grounded):
   - $I_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE}}{R_B}$ has zero dependence on $I_C$, so $\frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C} = 0$:
     $$S_{\text{fixed}} = 1 + \beta$$
   - For $\beta = 100$, $S = 101$—unacceptable thermal instability.

2. **Collector-to-Base Feedback Bias** ($R_B$ connected from Collector to Base, Emitter grounded):
   - KVL along $V_{CC} \to R_C \to R_B \to V_{BE}$:
     $$V_{CC} = (I_C + I_B)R_C + I_B R_B + V_{BE} \implies I_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE} - I_C R_C}{R_B + R_C}$$
   - Differentiating yields negative feedback $\frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C} = -\frac{R_C}{R_B + R_C}$, giving:
     $$\boxed{I_B = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{BE}}{R_B + (1 + \beta)R_C}, \qquad S_{\text{CFB}} = \frac{1 + \beta}{1 + \beta\frac{R_C}{R_B + R_C}}}$$
   - *Physical Action:* If $I_C$ rises, $V_C = V_{CC} - (I_C + I_B)R_C$ drops, starving $I_B$ and pulling $I_C$ back down.

3. **Voltage-Divider (Self) Bias** ($R_1$ from $V_{CC}$ to Base, $R_2$ from Base to Ground, Emitter resistor $R_E$):
   - Replace the base divider by its exact **Thevenin equivalent**:
     $$V_{Th} = V_{CC}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}, \qquad R_{Th} = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$
   - Apply KVL around the base–emitter loop ($V_{Th} - I_B R_{Th} - V_{BE} - (I_C + I_B)R_E = 0$):
     $$I_B = \frac{V_{Th} - V_{BE} - I_C R_E}{R_{Th} + R_E} \implies \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C} = -\frac{R_E}{R_{Th} + R_E}$$
   - Substituting $I_C = \beta I_B$ into KVL gives exact quiescent currents, and substituting $\frac{\partial I_B}{\partial I_C}$ into $S$ gives the stability factor:
     $$\boxed{I_B = \frac{V_{Th} - V_{BE}}{R_{Th} + (1 + \beta)R_E}, \qquad I_{CQ} = \beta I_B, \qquad S_{\text{VD}} = \frac{1 + \beta}{1 + \beta\frac{R_E}{R_{Th} + R_E}} = \frac{(1 + \beta)\left(1 + \frac{R_{Th}}{R_E}\right)}{1 + \beta + \frac{R_{Th}}{R_E}}}$$
   - **Limiting Checks:**
     - *Stiff divider ($R_{Th} \ll \beta R_E$ and $R_{Th}/R_E \to 0$):* $I_{CQ} \approx \frac{V_{Th} - V_{BE}}{R_E}$ becomes **completely independent of $\beta$**, and $S \to 1 + \frac{R_{Th}}{R_E} \to 1$ (ideal stability!).
     - *Zero emitter resistance ($R_E \to 0$):* $S \to 1 + \beta$, recovering unstable fixed bias.
     - *Practical rule of thumb:* Choose $R_{Th} \le 0.1\,\beta R_E$ (balancing stability against input loading by $R_1 \parallel R_2$). A bypass capacitor $C_E$ across $R_E$ restores full AC voltage gain while preserving full DC thermal stability.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 3.1: Voltage-Divider Biased CE Amplifier Q-Point & Stability Factor
> **Problem:** A silicon $n\text{-}p\text{-}n$ BJT ($\beta = 100$, $V_{BE} = 0.7\text{ V}$) is biased in a voltage-divider CE circuit with $V_{CC} = 15\text{ V}$, $R_1 = 40\text{ k}\Omega$, $R_2 = 10\text{ k}\Omega$, $R_C = 2.2\text{ k}\Omega$, and $R_E = 1.0\text{ k}\Omega$. Find the exact Q-point $(I_{CQ}, V_{CEQ})$ and stability factor $S$.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Thevenin Equivalent at the Base:**
>    $$V_{Th} = 15\text{ V} \times \frac{10\text{ k}\Omega}{40\text{ k}\Omega + 10\text{ k}\Omega} = 3.00\text{ V}, \qquad R_{Th} = \frac{40 \times 10}{40 + 10} = 8.00\text{ k}\Omega$$
>
> 2. **Exact Quiescent Currents:**
>    $$I_B = \frac{V_{Th} - V_{BE}}{R_{Th} + (1 + \beta)R_E} = \frac{3.00 - 0.70}{8.00\text{ k}\Omega + (101)(1.00\text{ k}\Omega)} = \frac{2.30\text{ V}}{109.0\text{ k}\Omega} = 21.10\text{ }\mu\text{A}$$
>    $$I_{CQ} = \beta I_B = 100 \times 21.10\text{ }\mu\text{A} = 2.11\text{ mA}, \qquad I_{EQ} = 101 I_B = 2.13\text{ mA}$$
>
> 3. **Quiescent Collector–Emitter Voltage ($V_{CEQ}$):**
>    $$V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - I_{CQ} R_C - I_{EQ} R_E = 15.0 - (2.110)(2.20) - (2.131)(1.00) = 15.0 - 4.642 - 2.131 = 8.23\text{ V}$$
>    *(Well centered near $V_{CC}/2 = 7.5\text{ V}$, firmly in the linear active region since $V_{CEQ} = 8.23\text{ V} \gg V_{CE,\text{sat}} \approx 0.2\text{ V}$.)*
>
> 4. **Stability Factor ($S$):**
>    $$S = \frac{(1 + \beta)(1 + R_{Th}/R_E)}{1 + \beta + R_{Th}/R_E} = \frac{(101)(1 + 8.0)}{101 + 8.0} = \frac{909}{109} = 8.34$$
>    *(Compare against fixed bias $S = 101$: a $12\times$ reduction in thermal drift!)*
>
> $$\boxed{I_{CQ} = 2.11\text{ mA}, \qquad V_{CEQ} = 8.23\text{ V}, \qquad S = 8.34}$$

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 3.2: Collector-to-Base Feedback Bias (Exact UPSC CSE 2021 Numerical Archetype)
> **Problem:** An $n\text{-}p\text{-}n$ transistor with $\beta = 49$ is used in common-emitter mode with $V_{CC} = 10\text{ V}$ and $R_C = 2.0\text{ k}\Omega$. A $100\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor ($R_B$) is connected between collector and base (emitter grounded). Assuming $V_{BE} = 0\text{ V}$, calculate: (a) quiescent collector current $I_{CQ}$ and $V_{CEQ}$, (b) stability factor $S$, and (c) how $I_{CQ}$ changes if practical Si $V_{BE} = 0.7\text{ V}$ is retained.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **KVL Along Collector–Base Feedback Loop:** Note that the current through $R_C$ is $I_C + I_B = (1 + \beta)I_B$, **not** just $I_C$:
>    $$V_{CC} = (I_C + I_B)R_C + I_B R_B + V_{BE} = \left[(1 + \beta)R_C + R_B\right]I_B + V_{BE}$$
>
> 2. **Case $V_{BE} = 0\text{ V}$:**
>    $$I_B = \frac{10\text{ V}}{(50)(2.0\text{ k}\Omega) + 100\text{ k}\Omega} = \frac{10\text{ V}}{200\text{ k}\Omega} = 50.0\text{ }\mu\text{A}$$
>    $$I_{CQ} = \beta I_B = 49 \times 50.0\text{ }\mu\text{A} = 2.45\text{ mA}$$
>    $$V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} - (I_{CQ} + I_B)R_C = 10.0 - (2.50\text{ mA})(2.0\text{ k}\Omega) = 5.00\text{ V}$$
>
> 3. **Stability Factor ($S$):**
>    $$S = \frac{1 + \beta}{1 + \beta\frac{R_C}{R_B + R_C}} = \frac{50}{1 + 49\left(\frac{2}{102}\right)} = \frac{50}{1 + 0.9608} = 25.5$$
>
> 4. **Comparison with $V_{BE} = 0.7\text{ V}$:**
>    $$I_B = \frac{10.0 - 0.7}{200\text{ k}\Omega} = 46.5\text{ }\mu\text{A} \implies I_{CQ} = 49 \times 46.5\text{ }\mu\text{A} = 2.28\text{ mA}$$
>
> $$\boxed{I_{CQ}(V_{BE}=0) = 2.45\text{ mA},\; V_{CEQ} = 5.00\text{ V},\; S = 25.5; \quad I_{CQ}(V_{BE}=0.7\text{ V}) = 2.28\text{ mA}}$$

### 3.4 Small-Signal Two-Port h-Parameter Model

For small midband AC excitations around a stable Q-point (where DC supplies act as AC ground and large coupling/bypass capacitors act as AC short circuits), the nonlinear BJT behaves as a linear two-port network. Choosing input current $i_b$ and output voltage $v_{ce}$ as independent variables yields the **hybrid ($h$)-parameter representation**—so named because its four coefficients carry mixed dimensions ($\Omega$, dimensionless, dimensionless, $\text{S}$) and are directly measurable using AC short-circuit output ($v_{ce} = 0$) and open-circuit input ($i_b = 0$) tests.

#### Two-Port Equations and Physical Meaning (CE Configuration)

$$\boxed{v_{be} = h_{ie} i_b + h_{re} v_{ce}}$$
$$\boxed{i_c = h_{fe} i_b + h_{oe} v_{ce}}$$

| $h$-Parameter | Formal Definition | Physical Meaning & Hybrid-$\pi$ Link | Typical Value (at $I_{CQ} \approx 1\text{–}2\text{ mA}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$h_{ie}$** ($\Omega$) | $\left.\frac{v_{be}}{i_b}\right\|_{v_{ce}=0}$ | Input impedance with output AC-shorted ($= r_\pi = \frac{\beta V_T}{I_{CQ}}$) | $1.0\text{–}2.5\text{ k}\Omega$ |
| **$h_{re}$** (unitless) | $\left.\frac{v_{be}}{v_{ce}}\right\|_{i_b=0}$ | Reverse voltage feedback ratio (Early base-width modulation) | $10^{-4}\text{–}5 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| **$h_{fe}$** (unitless) | $\left.\frac{i_c}{i_b}\right\|_{v_{ce}=0}$ | Small-signal forward current gain ($= \beta_{\text{ac}} = g_m r_\pi$) | $50\text{–}300$ |
| **$h_{oe}$** ($\text{S}$) | $\left.\frac{i_c}{v_{ce}}\right\|_{i_b=0}$ | Output admittance with input AC-open ($= 1/r_o \approx I_{CQ}/V_A$) | $10\text{–}50\text{ }\mu\text{S}$ |

#### Exact Derivation of CE Amplifier Performance ($A_i, R_i, A_v, R_o$)

**Assumptions:** The CE two-port is driven by an AC voltage source $v_s$ with series resistance $R_s$ and terminated by an effective AC collector load $R_L$ (where $R_L = R_C \parallel R_{L,\text{ext}}$). Adopt the standard load-current direction $i_L = -i_c$ (flowing upward into the collector terminal from ground through $R_L$), so $v_{ce} = i_L R_L = -i_c R_L$. Define the determinant $\Delta_h \equiv h_{ie} h_{oe} - h_{re} h_{fe}$.

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**

1. **Current Gain ($A_i = i_L / i_b = -i_c / i_b$):** Substitute $v_{ce} = -i_c R_L$ into the output equation $i_c = h_{fe} i_b + h_{oe} v_{ce}$:
   $$i_c = h_{fe} i_b - h_{oe} R_L i_c \implies i_c(1 + h_{oe} R_L) = h_{fe} i_b$$
   $$\boxed{A_i = \frac{i_L}{i_b} = -\frac{i_c}{i_b} = \frac{-h_{fe}}{1 + h_{oe} R_L}}$$

2. **Input Resistance ($R_i = v_{be} / i_b$):** Substitute $v_{ce} = i_L R_L = A_i R_L i_b$ into the input equation $v_{be} = h_{ie} i_b + h_{re} v_{ce}$:
   $$v_{be} = h_{ie} i_b + h_{re} A_i R_L i_b \implies \boxed{R_i = \frac{v_{be}}{i_b} = h_{ie} + h_{re} A_i R_L = h_{ie} - \frac{h_{re} h_{fe} R_L}{1 + h_{oe} R_L}}$$

3. **Voltage Gain ($A_v = v_{ce} / v_{be}$):** Using $v_{ce} = A_i R_L i_b$ and $v_{be} = R_i i_b$:
   $$A_v = \frac{A_i R_L}{R_i} = \frac{\left(\frac{-h_{fe} R_L}{1 + h_{oe} R_L}\right)}{h_{ie} - \frac{h_{re} h_{fe} R_L}{1 + h_{oe} R_L}} = \frac{-h_{fe} R_L}{h_{ie}(1 + h_{oe} R_L) - h_{re} h_{fe} R_L}$$
   $$\boxed{A_v = \frac{-h_{fe} R_L}{h_{ie} + \Delta_h R_L}}$$
   *(The explicit minus sign captures the **$180^\circ$ phase inversion** of every CE amplifier! Overall source-to-load gain is $A_{vs} = A_v \frac{R_i'}{R_i' + R_s}$, where $R_i' = R_1 \parallel R_2 \parallel R_i$.)*

4. **Output Admittance ($Y_o = 1/R_o$):** Short the independent input source ($v_s = 0$), disconnect $R_L$, apply a test voltage $v_t$ at the output port, and find $Y_o = i_t / v_t$.
   - Input loop KVL with $v_s = 0$: $0 = (R_s + h_{ie})i_b + h_{re} v_t \implies i_b = -\frac{h_{re}}{R_s + h_{ie}} v_t$.
   - Substitute $i_b$ into the output equation $i_t = h_{fe} i_b + h_{oe} v_t$:
     $$i_t = \left(h_{oe} - \frac{h_{fe} h_{re}}{h_{ie} + R_s}\right) v_t \implies \boxed{Y_o = \frac{1}{R_o} = h_{oe} - \frac{h_{fe} h_{re}}{h_{ie} + R_s}}$$

#### Simplified Midband Model & Unbypassed Emitter Resistor ($R_E$)

Whenever $h_{oe} R_L \le 0.1$ and $h_{re} \approx 0$ (valid for almost all practical loads $R_L \lesssim 5\text{ k}\Omega$), the exact formulas collapse cleanly to within $\sim 5\text{–}10\%$:
$$A_i \approx -h_{fe}, \qquad R_i \approx h_{ie}, \qquad A_v \approx -\frac{h_{fe} R_L}{h_{ie}} = -g_m R_L, \qquad R_o \approx \frac{1}{h_{oe}}$$
If an emitter resistor $R_E$ is **left unbypassed** for AC signals (local series–series negative feedback), the emitter voltage $v_e = (1 + h_{fe}) i_b R_E$ adds to $h_{ie} i_b$, yielding:
$$R_i \approx h_{ie} + (1 + h_{fe}) R_E, \qquad A_v \approx \frac{-h_{fe} R_L}{h_{ie} + (1 + h_{fe}) R_E} \approx -\frac{R_L}{R_E}$$
Notice how leaving $R_E$ unbypassed trades excess gain for vastly higher input impedance and $\beta$-independent gain stability.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 3.3: Exact vs. Approximate h-Parameter CE Amplifier Evaluation
> **Problem:** A CE BJT amplifier has $h_{ie} = 1.1\text{ k}\Omega$, $h_{re} = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}$, $h_{fe} = 50$, and $h_{oe} = 25\text{ }\mu\text{S}$. It drives an effective collector load $R_L = 4.0\text{ k}\Omega$ and is fed from a source of resistance $R_s = 1.0\text{ k}\Omega$. Compute the exact values of $A_i$, $R_i$, $A_v$, and $R_o$, and compare with the simplified ($h_{re} = 0, h_{oe} = 0$) model.
>
> **Solution:**
> 1. **Determinant $\Delta_h$:**
>    $$\Delta_h = h_{ie} h_{oe} - h_{re} h_{fe} = (1100)(25 \times 10^{-6}) - (2.5 \times 10^{-4})(50) = 0.0275 - 0.0125 = 0.0150$$
>
> 2. **Exact Parameters:**
>    - **Current Gain:** $A_i = \frac{-h_{fe}}{1 + h_{oe} R_L} = \frac{-50}{1 + (25 \times 10^{-6})(4000)} = \frac{-50}{1.10} = -45.45$.
>    - **Input Resistance:** $R_i = h_{ie} + h_{re} A_i R_L = 1100 + (2.5 \times 10^{-4})(-45.45)(4000) = 1100 - 45.45 = 1054.5\text{ }\Omega = 1.055\text{ k}\Omega$.
>    - **Voltage Gain:** $A_v = \frac{-h_{fe} R_L}{h_{ie} + \Delta_h R_L} = \frac{-(50)(4000)}{1100 + (0.0150)(4000)} = \frac{-200{,}000}{1160} = -172.4$.
>      *(Cross-check: $A_v = \frac{A_i R_L}{R_i} = \frac{(-45.45)(4000)}{1054.55} = -172.4$, exact agreement!)*
>    - **Output Resistance:**
>      $$Y_o = h_{oe} - \frac{h_{fe} h_{re}}{h_{ie} + R_s} = 25 \times 10^{-6} - \frac{(50)(2.5 \times 10^{-4})}{1100 + 1000} = (25.00 - 5.95)\text{ }\mu\text{S} = 19.05\text{ }\mu\text{S}$$
>      $$R_o = \frac{1}{Y_o} = 52.5\text{ k}\Omega$$
>
> 3. **Comparison with Simplified Model ($h_{re} = 0, h_{oe} = 0$):**
>    $$A_{i,\text{approx}} = -50, \qquad R_{i,\text{approx}} = 1.10\text{ k}\Omega, \qquad A_{v,\text{approx}} = -\frac{50 \times 4000}{1100} = -181.8 \quad (\text{error } 5.5\%)$$
>
> $$\boxed{A_i = -45.5,\; R_i = 1.055\text{ k}\Omega,\; A_v = -172.4,\; R_o = 52.5\text{ k}\Omega}$$

## 4. Field-Effect Transistors - JFET, MOSFET, and CMOS Switching

### 4.1 Junction Field-Effect Transistor - Pinch-Off and Shockley Law

Unlike a BJT—which injects minority carriers across a forward-biased junction and draws continuous base current—a **Junction Field-Effect Transistor (JFET)** is a **unipolar, voltage-controlled** device. Majority carriers alone (electrons in an $n$-channel JFET; holes in a $p$-channel JFET) drift through a semiconductor bar from **Source ($S$)** to **Drain ($D$)**, while a transverse electric field from a **reverse-biased $p\text{-}n$ Gate ($G$)** squeezes the conductive cross-section like a foot pressing on a garden hose.

Because the gate–channel junction stays strictly reverse-biased during normal operation, DC gate current is minuscule ($\sim \text{pA–nA}$), granting the JFET enormous input impedance ($10^8\text{–}10^{10}\text{ }\Omega$) and low flicker/shot noise.

#### Structure and Derivation of Pinch-Off Voltage ($V_P$)

Consider a symmetric $n$-channel JFET of donor doping $N_d$, channel length $L$, and **total metallurgical thickness $2a$** (so the **half-channel width is $a$**), sandwiched between two heavily doped $p^+$ gate regions tied together electrically.

1. At any position $x$ along the channel where channel potential relative to source is $V(x)$ (with $V(0) = 0$ at source and $V(L) = V_{DS}$ at drain), the local reverse bias across the one-sided abrupt $p^+n$ gate junction is $V_R(x) = V_{\text{bi}} - V_{GS} + V(x)$ (remember $V_{GS} \le 0$ for an $n$-channel JFET, so $-V_{GS} = |V_{GS}| \ge 0$).
2. By the one-sided abrupt junction depletion formula (Section 2.1), each symmetric depletion layer penetrates a distance $W(x)$ into the $n$-channel:
   $$W(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s \left[V_{\text{bi}} - V_{GS} + V(x)\right]}{e N_d}}$$
   Because $V(x)$ rises monotonically from $0$ to $V_{DS}$, the depletion layers are **wedge-shaped**—thickest at the drain end ($x = L$).
3. **Pinch-Off Condition:** The two opposing depletion wedges meet at the channel centerline when $W(x) = a$. Setting $W = a$ defines the **internal pinch-off potential** $V_{P0}$:
   $$\boxed{V_{P0} = \frac{e N_d a^2}{2\varepsilon_s}, \qquad |V_P| = V_{P0} - V_{\text{bi}}}$$
   *(In routine exam numericals where $V_{\text{bi}}$ is unstated, $|V_P| \approx \frac{e N_d a^2}{2\varepsilon_s}$; always verify whether the given channel thickness is the full width $2a$ or the half-width $a$!)*

#### Drain Output Characteristics and Physical Meaning of Pinch-Off

1. **Ohmic (Triode / Linear) Region ($0 < V_{DS} < V_{DS,\text{sat}}$):** For small $V_{DS}$, the channel behaves as a voltage-controlled resistor whose conductance decreases as $|V_{GS}|$ increases.
2. **Pinch-Off / Saturation Region ($V_{DS} \ge V_{DS,\text{sat}} = V_{GS} - V_P = |V_P| - |V_{GS}|$):** As $V_{DS}$ reaches $V_{DS,\text{sat}}$, the drain end pinches down to a thin high-field neck. **Drain current does NOT drop to zero at pinch-off**—if $I_D$ vanished, the ohmic voltage drop along the channel would vanish, immediately reopening the channel! Instead, carriers arriving at the pinch-off point are swept across the depleted drain gap by the intense electric field; any additional $V_{DS}$ above $V_{DS,\text{sat}}$ drops across this drain space-charge region, leaving the potential across the unpinched channel locked at $V_{DS,\text{sat}}$ and clamping $I_D$ nearly flat.
   - At $V_{GS} = 0$, the saturated drain current reaches its maximum value, denoted **$I_{DSS}$** (*Drain-to-Source Saturation current with Gate Shorted to Source*).
   - When $V_{GS} = V_P$ (also written $V_{GS(\text{off})}$), the entire channel pinches shut even at $V_{DS} = 0$, cutting $I_D$ completely to zero (**Cutoff**).
3. **Breakdown Region:** Excessive $V_{DS}$ triggers avalanche breakdown of the reverse-biased gate–drain junction at $BV_{DGO}$.

#### Shockley Transfer Equation and Small-Signal Parameters ($g_m, r_d, \mu$)

Across the entire saturation region ($V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_P$), the nonlinear square-law transfer relation between $I_D$ and $V_{GS}$ is accurately captured by **Shockley's Equation**:

$$\boxed{I_D = I_{DSS}\left(1 - \frac{V_{GS}}{V_P}\right)^2}$$

*(Sign check: For $n$-channel JFET, $V_P < 0$ and $V_P \le V_{GS} \le 0$; for $p$-channel JFET, $V_P > 0$ and $0 \le V_{GS} \le V_P$. In both cases, the ratio $V_{GS}/V_P$ is positive and lies in $[0, 1]$!)*

Differentiating Shockley's equation with respect to $V_{GS}$ yields the **small-signal transconductance ($g_m$)** (magnitude always positive; note sign of $V_P$):

$$\boxed{g_m = \left|\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}}\right|_{V_{DS}} = \frac{2 I_{DSS}}{|V_P|}\left(1 - \frac{V_{GS}}{V_P}\right) = g_{m0}\left(1 - \frac{V_{GS}}{V_P}\right) = g_{m0}\sqrt{\frac{I_D}{I_{DSS}}}, \qquad g_{m0} \equiv \frac{2 I_{DSS}}{|V_P|}}$$

Together with the **AC drain resistance** $r_d = \left.\frac{\partial V_{DS}}{\partial I_D}\right|_{V_{GS}}$ (finite due to slight channel-length shortening beyond pinch-off) and the **amplification factor** $\mu = -\left.\frac{\partial V_{DS}}{\partial V_{GS}}\right|_{I_D}$, the three JFET small-signal parameters satisfy the exact cyclic identity:
$$\boxed{\mu = g_m r_d}$$

#### Self-Biasing of a JFET

Because an $n$-channel JFET requires negative $V_{GS}$ while operating from a single positive supply $+V_{DD}$, a resistor $R_S$ is placed between Source and Ground while the Gate is tied to Ground through a large resistor $R_G$ ($\sim 1\text{ M}\Omega$). Since $I_G \approx 0$, $V_G = 0$ and $V_S = +I_D R_S$, automatically generating stabilizing negative feedback:
$$V_{GS} = V_G - V_S = -I_D R_S \quad (n\text{-channel}), \qquad V_{GS} = +I_D R_S \quad (p\text{-channel with } V_{DD} < 0)$$

> [!example]- Worked Archetype 4.1: JFET Pinch-Off Voltage & Self-Biased p-Channel JFET (UPSC CSE 2017 Archetype)
> **Problem:**
> (a) An $n$-channel silicon JFET ($\varepsilon_r = 12$) has donor doping $N_d = 2.0 \times 10^{21}\text{ m}^{-3}$ and total channel thickness $2a = 4.0\text{ }\mu\text{m}$. Neglecting $V_{\text{bi}}$, calculate its pinch-off voltage $V_P$ and saturation voltage $V_{DS,\text{sat}}$ at $V_{GS} = -2.0\text{ V}$.
> (b) A self-biased **$p$-channel JFET** has drain supply $V_{DD} = -12\text{ V}$ connected through $R_D = 2.0\text{ k}\Omega$ to the drain, source connected through $R_S = 1.0\text{ k}\Omega$ to ground, and gate grounded via $R_G = 5.0\text{ k}\Omega$. Given pinch-off voltage $|V_P| = 5.0\text{ V}$ ($V_P = +5.0\text{ V}$) and measured $|V_{DS}| = 6.0\text{ V}$, calculate $I_{DSS}$ and the Q-point transconductance $g_m$.

>    $$|V_P| = \frac{e N_d a^2}{2\varepsilon_s} = \frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})(2.0 \times 10^{21})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})^2}{2(1.0625 \times 10^{-10})} = \frac{1.282 \times 10^{-9}}{2.125 \times 10^{-10}} = 6.03\text{ V}$$

>    - KVL along the output loop: $0 - I_D R_S - |V_{DS}| - I_D R_D = -12\text{ V}$:

### 4.2 Enhancement and Depletion MOSFETs and CMOS Low-Power Switching

A **Metal–Oxide–Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (MOSFET)** replaces the JFET's reverse-biased gate junction with a nanometric insulating layer of **silicon dioxide ($\text{SiO}_2$)** of thickness $t_{\text{ox}}$ (capacitance per unit area $C_{\text{ox}} = \varepsilon_{\text{ox}} / t_{\text{ox}}$) beneath a metal or heavily doped polysilicon gate. Because the gate is electrically isolated by glass, DC gate current is essentially zero and input resistance soars to **$10^{12}\text{–}10^{15}\text{ }\Omega$**—making MOSFETs susceptible only to **Electrostatic Discharge (ESD)** dielectric breakdown if left unprotected.

#### Enhancement-Mode $n$-MOSFET (E-MOSFET) - Structure and Surface Inversion

An $n$-channel E-MOSFET is built on a lightly doped **$p$-type silicon substrate (body $B$)** containing two heavily doped **$n^+$ wells** for Source ($S$) and Drain ($D$), separated by channel length $L$ and covered by a thin gate oxide and gate electrode ($G$). Crucially, **no physical $n$-channel exists at $V_{GS} = 0$** (the device is **normally-OFF**, with two back-to-back $n^+p$ junctions blocking current).

Sweeping positive gate voltage $V_{GS}$ (body tied to source) drives the $p$-substrate surface beneath the oxide through three MOS-capacitor regimes:
1. **Accumulation ($V_{GS} < 0$):** Negative gate attracts majority holes to the $\text{Si–SiO}_2$ interface; channel stays OFF.
2. **Depletion ($0 < V_{GS} < V_T$):** Positive gate repels holes away from the surface, uncovering a depletion region of negative acceptor ions; still no free electrons to conduct.
3. **Strong Inversion ($V_{GS} \ge V_T$):** Once $V_{GS}$ reaches the **threshold voltage $V_T$** (typically $+0.5\text{ to }+2\text{ V}$ for $n$-MOS), downward surface band bending reaches $\phi_s = 2\phi_F$, attracting minority electrons to the interface until surface electron density equals bulk hole density. This induced **$n$-type inversion layer** bridges Source to Drain, turning the MOSFET **ON**!

#### Gradual-Channel Derivation of Enhancement MOSFET $I_D\text{–}V_{DS}$ Equations

**Assumptions:** Long-channel gradual-channel approximation (transverse gate field $\gg$ longitudinal drain field), constant surface electron mobility $\mu_n$, channel width $W$ and length $L$, source grounded ($V(0) = 0$) and drain at $V(L) = V_{DS} > 0$.

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**

1. At distance $x$ from the source, the local gate-to-channel overdrive potential is $[V_{GS} - V(x) - V_T]$. By parallel-plate electrostatics across the gate oxide ($C_{\text{ox}} = \varepsilon_{\text{ox}} / t_{\text{ox}}$), the mobile inversion electron charge per unit area is:
   $$Q_n(x) = -C_{\text{ox}}\left[V_{GS} - V_T - V(x)\right] \qquad \left(V(x) \le V_{GS} - V_T\right)$$

2. Under longitudinal field $\mathcal{E}_x = -\frac{dV}{dx}$, electrons drift with velocity $v_d = -\mu_n \mathcal{E}_x = +\mu_n \frac{dV}{dx}$ toward the drain. Multiplying mobile charge density $|Q_n(x)|$, channel width $W$, and drift velocity yields drain current $I_D$ (constant at every $x$ by current continuity):
   $$I_D = W \mu_n |Q_n(x)| \frac{dV}{dx} = W \mu_n C_{\text{ox}} \left[V_{GS} - V_T - V(x)\right] \frac{dV}{dx}$$

3. Separate variables and integrate from source ($x = 0, V = 0$) to drain ($x = L, V = V_{DS}$):
   $$\int_0^L I_D\,dx = W \mu_n C_{\text{ox}} \int_0^{V_{DS}} \left(V_{GS} - V_T - V\right) dV$$
   $$I_D L = W \mu_n C_{\text{ox}} \left[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \frac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2\right]$$
   Dividing by $L$ gives the **Linear (Triode / Ohmic) Region** law ($0 \le V_{DS} \le V_{GS} - V_T$):
   $$\boxed{I_D = \mu_n C_{\text{ox}} \frac{W}{L} \left[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \frac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2\right] \qquad (\text{Triode: } V_{DS} \le V_{GS} - V_T)}$$

4. **Pinch-Off & Saturation Region ($V_{DS} \ge V_{DS,\text{sat}} = V_{GS} - V_T$):** At $V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_T$, inversion charge $Q_n(L)$ vanishes at the drain edge (channel pinch-off). Substituting $V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_T$ locks the saturated drain current to the quadratic transfer law:
   $$\boxed{I_{D,\text{sat}} = \frac{1}{2}\mu_n C_{\text{ox}} \frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_T)^2 = k(V_{GS} - V_T)^2 \qquad (\text{Saturation: } V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T)}$$
   where $k_n' \equiv \mu_n C_{\text{ox}}$ is the process transconductance parameter and $k \equiv \frac{1}{2}k_n' (W/L)$.

5. **Channel-Length Modulation & Transconductance:** For $V_{DS} > V_{GS} - V_T$, the pinch-off point retreats slightly toward the source by $\Delta L$, shrinking effective length to $L - \Delta L$ and multiplying $I_{D,\text{sat}}$ by $(1 + \lambda V_{DS})$—the MOSFET counterpart of the BJT Early effect ($\lambda = 1/V_A$):
   $$I_D = \frac{1}{2}\mu_n C_{\text{ox}}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_T)^2(1 + \lambda V_{DS}), \qquad r_o = \frac{1}{\lambda I_D}$$
   $$\boxed{g_m = \left.\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}}\right|_{V_{DS}} = \mu_n C_{\text{ox}}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_T) = \sqrt{2 \mu_n C_{\text{ox}} \frac{W}{L} I_D}}$$

**Dimensional and Limiting-Case Sanity Checks:**
- **Dimensions:** $[\mu_n C_{\text{ox}} (W/L) V^2] = (\text{m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1})(\text{F}\cdot\text{m}^{-2})(1)(\text{V}^2) = \frac{\text{C}}{\text{V}}\cdot\frac{\text{V}}{\text{s}} = \text{C/s} = \text{A}$.
- **Deep Triode Limit ($V_{DS} \ll V_{GS} - V_T$):** Dropping the $\frac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2$ term gives $I_D \approx \mu_n C_{\text{ox}}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS}$, proving the MOSFET operates as a linear voltage-controlled switch of on-resistance $R_{\text{on}} = \left[\mu_n C_{\text{ox}}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_T)\right]^{-1}$.
- **Cutoff Limit ($V_{GS} \le V_T$):** $I_D = 0$.

#### Depletion-Mode MOSFET (D-MOSFET)

Structurally identical to an E-MOSFET except that a narrow **$n$-type channel is physically diffused/implanted** between the $n^+$ Source and Drain during fabrication. Consequently, a D-MOSFET is **normally-ON** at $V_{GS} = 0$ (conducting $I_D = I_{DSS}$). Because its gate is insulated by $\text{SiO}_2$, it operates seamlessly in **two polarities**:
- **Depletion Mode ($V_{GS} < 0$):** Negative gate repels channel electrons, reducing $I_D$ down to zero at $V_{GS} = V_P < 0$, obeying the same Shockley law $I_D = I_{DSS}(1 - V_{GS}/V_P)^2$ as a JFET.
- **Enhancement Mode ($V_{GS} > 0$):** Positive gate draws *extra* electrons into the channel without drawing any gate current (impossible in a JFET!), pushing $I_D > I_{DSS}$.

#### Complementary MOS (CMOS) Low-Power Digital Switching

**CMOS** pairs one **$p$-channel E-MOSFET (PMOS)** and one **$n$-channel E-MOSFET (NMOS)** in series between $+V_{DD}$ and Ground. In the canonical **CMOS Inverter**, the PMOS source ties to $V_{DD}$, the NMOS source ties to Ground, both gates tie together as $V_{\text{in}}$, and both drains tie together as $V_{\text{out}}$:
- **Input LOW ($V_{\text{in}} = 0\text{ V}$):** NMOS has $V_{GS,n} = 0 < V_{Tn}$ (**OFF**); PMOS has $V_{GS,p} = -V_{DD} < V_{Tp}$ (**ON**), pulling $V_{\text{out}} = V_{DD}$ (Logic `1`).
- **Input HIGH ($V_{\text{in}} = V_{DD}$):** NMOS has $V_{GS,n} = V_{DD} > V_{Tn}$ (**ON**); PMOS has $V_{GS,p} = 0 > V_{Tp}$ (**OFF**), pulling $V_{\text{out}} = 0\text{ V}$ (Logic `0`).

**Why CMOS Dominates Modern VLSI (Power Derivation):**
1. **Near-Zero Static Power:** In *either* stable logic state (`0` or `1`), one transistor is completely OFF, blocking any direct DC conducting path between $V_{DD}$ and Ground; static dissipation reduces strictly to subthreshold/junction leakage ($P_{\text{stat}} = I_{\text{leak}} V_{DD} \approx 0$ in ideal static equilibrium).
2. **Dynamic Capacitive Switching Power:** Power is consumed predominantly during logic transitions as load capacitance $C_L$ (next-stage gates + interconnect) charges and discharges at switching frequency $f$:
   - On a `0 → 1` output transition, $V_{DD}$ transfers charge $Q = C_L V_{DD}$ through the ON PMOS, supplying total energy $E_{\text{supp}} = Q V_{DD} = C_L V_{DD}^2$. Exactly half ($\frac{1}{2}C_L V_{DD}^2$) is stored electrostatically on $C_L$ and half is dissipated as heat in the PMOS channel resistance.
   - On the subsequent `1 → 0` transition, the stored $\frac{1}{2}C_L V_{DD}^2$ discharges to ground as heat through the ON NMOS.
   - Multiplying total energy dissipated per full cycle ($C_L V_{DD}^2$) by the switching rate $\alpha f$ (activity factor $\alpha \in [0, 1]$) yields:
   $$\boxed{P_{\text{dyn}} = \alpha C_L V_{DD}^2 f}$$
   Because $P_{\text{dyn}}$ scales quadratically with $V_{DD}$, lowering supply voltages ($5\text{ V} \to 1.0\text{ V}$) slashes chip heat by $25\times$, enabling billion-transistor microprocessors with full rail-to-rail noise margins.

| Parameter | BJT | JFET | MOSFET (E / D) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

For $V_{GS} = 3.0\text{ V}$, determine the operating region and drain current $I_D$ when: (i) $V_{DS} = 4.0\text{ V}$ (also find $g_m$), and (ii) $V_{DS} = 0.50\text{ V}$. (b) A CMOS logic gate driving total load capacitance $C_L = 50\text{ fF}$ switches at $f = 2.0\text{ GHz}$ ($\alpha = 1$) from a $V_{DD} = 1.2\text{ V}$ rail. Calculate its dynamic power dissipation $P_{\text{dyn}}$.
>

> 1. **Part (a) — MOSFET Pinch-Off Boundary & Currents:**
>    - Overdrive voltage: $V_{DS,\text{sat}} = V_{GS} - V_T = 3.0 - 1.0 = 2.0\text{ V}$.
>    - Combined gain factor: $k_n'(W/L) = (200 \times 10^{-6}\text{ A/V}^2)(20) = 4.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ A/V}^2 = 4.00\text{ mA/V}^2$.
>    - **Case (i) $V_{DS} = 4.0\text{ V}$:** Since $V_{DS} = 4.0\text{ V} \ge V_{DS,\text{sat}} = 2.0\text{ V}$, the MOSFET operates in **Saturation**:
>      $$I_D = \frac{1}{2} k_n'\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_T)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(4.00\text{ mA/V}^2)(2.0\text{ V})^2 = 8.00\text{ mA}$$

>    - **Case (ii) $V_{DS} = 0.50\text{ V}$:** Since $V_{DS} = 0.50\text{ V} < V_{DS,\text{sat}} = 2.0\text{ V}$, the MOSFET operates in the **Triode (Linear) Region**:
>      $$I_D = k_n'\frac{W}{L}\left[(V_{GS} - V_T)V_{DS} - \frac{1}{2}V_{DS}^2\right] = (4.00\text{ mA/V}^2)\left[(2.0)(0.50) - \frac{1}{2}(0.50)^2\right] = 4.00(0.875) = 3.50\text{ mA}$$
>

>    $$P_{\text{dyn}} = \alpha C_L V_{DD}^2 f = (1)(50 \times 10^{-15}\text{ F})(1.2\text{ V})^2(2.0 \times 10^{9}\text{ Hz}) = 1.44 \times 10^{-4}\text{ W} = 144\text{ }\mu\text{W}$$
>
> $$\boxed{\text{(a)(i) Sat.: } I_D = 8.00\text{ mA},\; g_m = 8.00\text{ mS}; \quad \text{(a)(ii) Triode: } I_D = 3.50\text{ mA}; \quad \text{(b) } P_{\text{dyn}} = 144\text{ }\mu\text{W}}$$

## 5. High-Yield Traps and Exam-Hall Diagram Blueprints

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> 1. **Thermal Voltage & $\text{eV}$ vs. $\text{J}$ Consistency:** At $300\text{ K}$, $V_T = k_B T / e = 25.85\text{ mV}$ ($k_B T = 0.02585\text{ eV}$). In $e V / (k_B T)$, if $V$ is in volts, divide by $V_T = 0.02585\text{ V}$; in $E_g / (2 k_B T)$, if $E_g$ is in $\text{eV}$, divide by $0.02585\text{ eV}$—never mix $\text{J}$ and $\text{eV}$!
> 2. **Depletion Penetration Asymmetry:** By $N_a x_p = N_d x_n$, the depletion layer extends **much deeper into the LIGHTLY doped side** ($x_p / x_n = N_d / N_a$)—never invert the ratio!
> 3. **Zener vs. Avalanche Temperature Coefficient Signs:** Zener tunneling ($V_Z \lesssim 5\text{ V}$) has a **negative TC** ($dV_Z/dT < 0$ because $E_g$ shrinks with $T$); Avalanche impact ionization ($V_{BR} \gtrsim 6\text{ V}$) has a **positive TC** ($dV_{BR}/dT > 0$ because phonon scattering shortens mean free path).
> 4. **Collector-to-Base Feedback KVL Trap (UPSC 2021):** The current through collector resistor $R_C$ is **$I_C + I_B = (1 + \beta)I_B$**, NOT merely $I_C$! Writing $\beta R_C$ instead of $(1 + \beta)R_C$ introduces a subtle error when $\beta = 49$ (where $1 + \beta = 50$ divides cleanly!).
> 5. **$h$-Parameter Sign Conventions:** In CE configuration, load current $i_L = -i_c$, giving **negative** $A_i = \frac{-h_{fe}}{1 + h_{oe} R_L}$ and **negative** $A_v = \frac{-h_{fe} R_L}{h_{ie} + \Delta_h R_L}$ ($180^\circ$ phase inversion); forgetting the minus sign in $R_i = h_{ie} + h_{re} A_i R_L$ reverses the sign of the feedback correction!
> 6. **JFET Full Width ($2a$) vs. Half-Width ($a$) Trap:** Pinch-off potential is $V_{P0} = \frac{e N_d a^2}{2\varepsilon_s}$ using the **half-channel thickness $a$**! Plugging full thickness $2a$ inflates $V_P$ by a catastrophic factor of $4\times$.
> 7. **JFET & MOSFET Saturation Boundary Sign Trap:** Always verify saturation before applying quadratic laws! For an $n$-JFET ($V_P < 0$), $V_{DS,\text{sat}} = V_{GS} - V_P = |V_P| - |V_{GS}|$; for a $p$-JFET ($V_P > 0$, $V_{GS} > 0$), $|V_{DS}| \ge V_P - V_{GS}$; for an $n$-channel E-MOSFET ($V_T > 0$), $V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_T$ (if smaller, you MUST use the Triode formula!).
> 8. **Solar Cell Quadrant & Irradiance Scaling:** Illuminated diode current shifts *downward* into the **4th quadrant** ($I = I_D - I_L$). Doubling sunlight irradiance **doubles $I_{sc}$** linearly, while $V_{oc}$ rises only logarithmically by $+V_T \ln 2 \approx +18\text{ mV}$.

### Examiner Step-Marking and Numerical Trap Signature

- **Intrinsic Fermi Level Proofs Require Full $N_c, N_v$ Integral Steps:** On questions asking to show $E_{Fi} = (E_c + E_v)/2$ (like 2016), jumping directly to $f(E_c) = 1 - f(E_v)$ earns only partial credit; full marks require deriving $n = N_c e^{-(E_c - E_F)/(k_B T)}$ and $p = N_v e^{-(E_F - E_v)/(k_B T)}$, setting $n = p$ to get $E_{Fi} = \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} + \frac{3}{4}k_B T \ln(m_h^*/m_e^*)$, and explicitly stating the assumption $m_e^* = m_h^*$ (or $T = 0\text{ K}$) that places it at exact midgap.
- **Solar Cell Questions Reward Quantitative Equations + Taxonomy (2026):** Beyond naming the four physical steps (absorption, charge separation, photovoltage, external load), top scripts write the exact illuminated Shockley equation, $V_{oc}$, Fill Factor $\text{FF} = V_m I_m / (V_{oc} I_{sc})$, and organize efficiency limits systematically into Bandgap (Shockley–Queisser thermalization + transmission), Recombination, Optical (reflection + shading), Parasitic ($R_s, R_{sh}$), and Thermal losses.
- **D-MOSFET Dual-Mode Curves Are Frequently Tested (2010, 2014):** Examiners specifically check that the $I_D\text{–}V_{DS}$ family includes **both** $V_{GS} < 0$ (depletion mode, below $I_{DSS}$) and $V_{GS} > 0$ (enhancement mode, above $I_{DSS}$), paired with the transfer curve crossing the $I_D$ axis at $(0, I_{DSS})$ and extending into the first quadrant.
- **BJT Biasing & Switching Answers Need Explicit Junction Polarities:** Always state clearly: Forward-Active = EBJ forward + CBJ reverse; Cutoff (OFF switch) = both junctions reverse; Saturation (ON switch) = both junctions forward ($V_{CE,\text{sat}} \approx 0.2\text{ V}$).
