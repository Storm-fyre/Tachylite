# Chapter 1: Quantum Foundations, Wave-Particle Duality, Wave Packets, Uncertainty & Dirac Formalism

## 1. Wave-Particle Duality - Breakdown of Classical Physics and Matter Waves

At the close of the nineteenth century, classical mechanics and Maxwellian electrodynamics appeared to divide physical reality cleanly into two disjoint categories: localized **particles** governed by Newton's laws and extended **waves** governed by linear superposition and diffraction. That partition collapsed when radiation interacted with matter at atomic energies, revealing that electromagnetic fields exchange energy and momentum in discrete corpuscular packets while material particles diffract like coherent waves.

Quantum mechanics does not replace particles with classical waves or vice versa. Rather, every microscopic entity propagates according to a complex probability amplitude governed by wave superposition and registers in a detector as a discrete, localized quantum event.

### 1.1 Photoelectric Effect and Einstein's Light Quantum

When ultraviolet light strikes a clean metallic surface in vacuum, electrons are ejected instantaneously if the radiation frequency $\nu$ exceeds a metal-specific threshold $\nu_0$. Classical electromagnetic theory treats light as a continuous wave whose energy is distributed uniformly across the wavefront, leading to three catastrophic experimental failures:

1. **Frequency threshold:** Classical theory predicts that sufficiently intense light of *any* frequency should eventually transfer enough energy to liberate an electron. Experimentally, no electrons emerge for $\nu < \nu_0$, regardless of intensity.
2. **Kinetic energy independence from intensity:** Classical theory predicts that stronger electric field amplitude $E_0$ increases the maximum kinetic energy $K_{\max}$ of photoelectrons. Experimentally, $K_{\max}$ depends strictly linearly on $\nu$ and is completely independent of intensity, which controls only the photocurrent (number of emitted electrons per second).
3. **Zero time lag:** Classical energy absorption across an atomic cross-section ($\sim 10^{-20}\text{ m}^2$) at feeble intensities predicts ejection delays of minutes to hours. Experimentally, emission occurs within $< 10^{-9}\text{ s}$.

Extending Planck's 1900 quantization of cavity oscillators to the free electromagnetic field itself, Einstein (1905) postulated that monochromatic radiation of frequency $\nu$ consists of localized energy quanta (**photons**), each carrying energy $E$ and linear momentum $p$:

$$E = h\nu = \hbar\omega, \qquad p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \hbar k$$

where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$, $\hbar = h/(2\pi) = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$, $\omega = 2\pi\nu$, and $k = 2\pi/\lambda$.

In a single-photon absorption event inside a metal of work function $\phi_0$ (the minimum binding energy required to remove an electron from the Fermi level to vacuum), energy conservation yields **Einstein's photoelectric equation**. Electrons emerging directly from the surface without inelastic collisions retain the maximum kinetic energy $K_{\max}$, which is measured by the reverse **stopping potential** $V_s$ that just halts the photocurrent:

$$\boxed{K_{\max} = eV_s = h\nu - \phi_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0)}$$

where the threshold frequency and cutoff wavelength are $\nu_0 = \phi_0/h$ and $\lambda_0 = hc/\phi_0$.

- **Dimensional & Limiting-Case Checks:** Both $h\nu$ and $eV_s$ carry dimensions of energy ($\text{J}$). Plotting $V_s$ against $\nu$ yields a straight line of universal slope $dV_s/d\nu = h/e$ independent of the target metal, while the vertical intercept $-\phi_0/e$ identifies the metal. As $\nu \to \nu_0^+$, $K_{\max} \to 0$; for $\nu < \nu_0$, single-photon ejection is energetically forbidden.

### 1.2 Relativistic Compton Scattering

While the photoelectric effect established that light carries quantized *energy*, decisive proof that a photon also carries quantized *linear momentum* $p = h/\lambda$ came from Arthur Compton's 1923 X-ray scattering experiments. Classical Thomson scattering predicts that an oscillating electromagnetic wave forces a free electron to oscillate at the exact incident frequency $\nu$, re-radiating secondary X-rays of strictly unchanged wavelength $\lambda' = \lambda$ at every scattering angle.

Compton observed instead that X-rays scattered from a graphite target split into two peaks: an unshifted line at $\lambda$ and a longer-wavelength shifted line at $\lambda' > \lambda$, with the shift $\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda$ depending purely on the scattering angle $\theta$ and remaining entirely independent of both the incident wavelength and the target material.

#### Physical Setup & Conservation Equations

Consider an incident X-ray photon of frequency $\nu$ (energy $E = h\nu$, momentum $p = h\nu/c$) moving along the $+x$-axis and colliding elastically with a free, stationary electron of rest mass $m_e$ (initial energy $m_e c^2$, initial momentum $0$). After the collision:
- The scattered photon emerges at angle $\theta$ to the $+x$-axis with frequency $\nu'$, energy $E' = h\nu'$, and momentum $p' = h\nu'/c$.
- The recoil electron moves at angle $\phi$ below the $+x$-axis with relativistic momentum $p_e$ and relativistic total energy $E_e = \sqrt{p_e^2 c^2 + m_e^2 c^4}$.

Enforcing conservation of linear momentum along $x$ and $y$, and conservation of relativistic total energy:

1. **$x$-momentum conservation:**
   $$\frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac{h\nu'}{c}\cos\theta + p_e\cos\phi \implies p_e c\cos\phi = h\nu - h\nu'\cos\theta \tag{1}$$

2. **$y$-momentum conservation:**
   $$0 = \frac{h\nu'}{c}\sin\theta - p_e\sin\phi \implies p_e c\sin\phi = h\nu'\sin\theta \tag{2}$$

3. **Relativistic energy conservation:**
   $$h\nu + m_e c^2 = h\nu' + E_e \implies E_e = h(\nu - \nu') + m_e c^2 \tag{3}$$

#### Unbroken Step-by-Step Algebra

Square and add Equations $(1)$ and $(2)$ to eliminate the recoil electron angle $\phi$:

$$p_e^2 c^2(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi) = (h\nu - h\nu'\cos\theta)^2 + (h\nu'\sin\theta)^2$$

$$p_e^2 c^2 = h^2\nu^2 - 2h^2\nu\nu'\cos\theta + h^2\nu'^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)$$

$$p_e^2 c^2 = h^2\nu^2 + h^2\nu'^2 - 2h^2\nu\nu'\cos\theta \tag{4}$$

Now square Equation $(3)$:

$$E_e^2 = \left[h(\nu - \nu') + m_e c^2\right]^2 = h^2(\nu^2 + \nu'^2 - 2\nu\nu') + 2m_e c^2 h(\nu - \nu') + m_e^2 c^4 \tag{5}$$

Invoke the relativistic energy–momentum invariant $E_e^2 - p_e^2 c^2 = m_e^2 c^4$ by subtracting Equation $(4)$ from Equation $(5)$:

$$m_e^2 c^4 = \left[h^2\nu^2 + h^2\nu'^2 - 2h^2\nu\nu' + 2m_e c^2 h(\nu - \nu') + m_e^2 c^4\right] - \left[h^2\nu^2 + h^2\nu'^2 - 2h^2\nu\nu'\cos\theta\right]$$

Cancelling $m_e^2 c^4$, $h^2\nu^2$, and $h^2\nu'^2$ cleanly leaves:

$$0 = -2h^2\nu\nu'(1 - \cos\theta) + 2m_e c^2 h(\nu - \nu')$$

Rearrange and divide both sides by $2m_e c h \nu \nu'$:

$$m_e c^2 (\nu - \nu') = h\nu\nu'(1 - \cos\theta) \implies \frac{c}{\nu'} - \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos\theta)$$

Substituting $\lambda = c/\nu$ and $\lambda' = c/\nu'$ yields the **Compton shift formula**:

$$\boxed{\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}(1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda_C(1 - \cos\theta)}$$

where the **Compton wavelength of the electron** is:

$$\boxed{\lambda_C \equiv \frac{h}{m_e c} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{(9.109 \times 10^{-31})(2.998 \times 10^8)} = 2.426 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m} = 0.02426\text{ \AA}}$$

#### Recoil Electron Kinematics and the Unshifted Line

Dividing Equation $(1)$ by Equation $(2)$ gives the exact electron recoil angle $\phi$:

$$\cot\phi = \frac{\nu - \nu'\cos\theta}{\nu'\sin\theta} = \frac{\frac{\nu}{\nu'} - \cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$$

Using $\frac{\nu}{\nu'} = \frac{\lambda'}{\lambda} = 1 + \frac{h\nu}{m_e c^2}(1 - \cos\theta) = 1 + \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$ with dimensionless photon energy $\alpha \equiv \frac{h\nu}{m_e c^2}$:

$$\cot\phi = \frac{(1 - \cos\theta) + \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)}{\sin\theta} = (1 + \alpha)\frac{2\sin^2(\theta/2)}{2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)}$$

$$\boxed{\cot\phi = \left(1 + \frac{h\nu}{m_e c^2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$$

Since $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ implies $\tan(\theta/2) \ge 0$, we always have $\cot\phi \ge 0$, so the recoil electron is strictly confined to the forward hemisphere ($0 \le \phi \le \pi/2$). The recoil electron kinetic energy $K_e = E - E' = h\nu\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{\lambda'}\right)$ becomes:

$$\boxed{K_e = h\nu\,\frac{\alpha(1 - \cos\theta)}{1 + \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)}}$$

Its maximum value occurs at head-on backscattering ($\theta = \pi$, $\phi = 0$), defining the **Compton edge**: $K_{e,\max} = h\nu \frac{2\alpha}{1 + 2\alpha}$.

#### Dimensional & Limiting-Case Sanity Checks

- **Dimensions:** $[\lambda_C] = \frac{\text{J}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} = \frac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} = \text{m}$.
- **Angular limits:** At $\theta = 0$ (grazing forward scattering), $\Delta\lambda = 0$. At $\theta = \pi/2$, $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_C$. At $\theta = \pi$ (backscattering), $\Delta\lambda_{\max} = 2\lambda_C = 0.04852\text{ \AA}$.
- **Origin of the unshifted peak:** Inner-shell electrons tightly bound to a carbon atom cannot recoil independently; the entire atom of mass $M_{\text{atom}} \approx 12 \times 1836\,m_e \approx 2.2 \times 10^4 m_e$ recoils as a unit. Replacing $m_e$ by $M_{\text{atom}}$ shrinks the shift to $\Delta\lambda \sim 10^{-4}\lambda_C \approx 10^{-6}\text{ \AA}$, indistinguishable from $\lambda$.
- **Classical optical limit ($\lambda \gg \lambda_C$):** For visible light ($\lambda \approx 5000\text{ \AA}$), the fractional shift is $\Delta\lambda/\lambda \lesssim 10^{-5}$, recovering classical Thomson scattering; this explains why Compton scattering requires X-rays or $\gamma$-rays.

### 1.3 de Broglie Matter Waves and Relativistic Kinematics

Guided by the symmetry between radiation and matter, Louis de Broglie (1924) proposed that the Einstein–Planck relations $E = h\nu$ and $p = h/\lambda$ are universal laws of nature applying equally to massive particles. A particle of relativistic momentum $p = \gamma m_0 v$ (with Lorentz factor $\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$) possesses an associated **matter-wave wavelength**:

$$\boxed{\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\gamma m_0 v}}$$

This hypothesis immediately explained Bohr's previously ad hoc angular momentum quantization postulate: a stationary circular orbit of radius $r$ persists without destructive self-interference only if its circumference accommodates an integral number of de Broglie wavelengths:

$$2\pi r = n\lambda = \frac{nh}{p} \implies L = rp = n\frac{h}{2\pi} = n\hbar \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$

#### Exact Relativistic de Broglie Wavelength in Terms of Kinetic Energy $K$

For a particle of rest mass $m_0$ and kinetic energy $K$, the relativistic total energy is $E = K + m_0 c^2$. Substituting into the relativistic dispersion relation $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4$:

$$(K + m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4$$

$$K^2 + 2K m_0 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4 \implies pc = \sqrt{K(K + 2m_0 c^2)}$$

Substituting $p = \frac{1}{c}\sqrt{K(K + 2m_0 c^2)}$ into $\lambda = h/p$ gives the **exact relativistic de Broglie formula**:

$$\boxed{\lambda = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{K(K + 2m_0 c^2)}}}$$

#### Limiting Cases and Laboratory Working Forms

1. **Non-relativistic limit ($K \ll m_0 c^2$):** Neglecting $K$ relative to $2m_0 c^2$ inside the radical gives:
   $$\lambda_{\text{NR}} = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2 m_0 c^2 K}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_0 K}}$$
   For a particle of charge $q$ accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$ ($K = qV \ll m_0 c^2$):
   $$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_0 q V}}$$
   For non-relativistic electrons ($q = e$, $m_0 = m_e$), evaluating the numerical prefactor yields the standard laboratory shortcut $\lambda_e \approx \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V\text{ (volts)}}}\text{ \AA}$.

2. **Ultra-relativistic limit ($K \gg m_0 c^2$):** Neglecting $2m_0 c^2$ relative to $K$ recovers the massless photon relation $\lambda \approx hc/K$.

3. **Condition for $\lambda_{\text{de Broglie}} = \lambda_C$:** Setting $\frac{h}{\gamma m_0 v} = \frac{h}{m_0 c}$ gives $\gamma v = c$:
   $$\frac{v}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = c \implies v^2 = c^2 - v^2 \implies v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\,c, \qquad K = (\sqrt{2} - 1)m_0 c^2 \approx 0.414\,m_0 c^2$$

4. **Thermal neutrons at temperature $T$:** Equating average kinetic energy $K = \frac{3}{2}k_B T$ gives $\lambda_{\text{th}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{3 m_n k_B T}}$. At $T = 300\text{ K}$, $\lambda_{\text{th}} \approx 1.45\text{ \AA}$, comparable to crystal lattice spacings—making thermal neutron diffraction an indispensable probe of crystal and magnetic structures.

### 1.4 Davisson–Germer Electron Diffraction Experiment

Direct experimental verification of de Broglie's hypothesis arrived in 1927 when C. J. Davisson and L. H. Germer directed a collimated low-energy electron beam at normal incidence onto a nickel single crystal and measured the scattered intensity as a function of accelerating voltage $V$ and colatitude scattering angle $\phi$ (measured from the incident beam direction).

Classical particle scattering off a metallic surface predicts a smooth, monotonic intensity falloff with angle. Instead, at $V = 54\text{ V}$, Davisson and Germer recorded a razor-sharp diffraction peak at $\phi = 50^\circ$, behaving identically to X-ray Bragg reflection from crystallographic planes.

- **From diffraction geometry:** For nickel, the surface atomic spacing along the scattering plane is $D = 2.15\text{ \AA} = 0.215\text{ nm}$. Treating the surface atoms as a reflection grating gives the first-order ($n = 1$) constructive interference condition $D\sin\phi = n\lambda$. Equivalently, the corresponding internal Bragg planes have interplanar spacing $d = D\sin(\phi/2) = 0.91\text{ \AA}$ and glancing angle $\theta = \frac{180^\circ - \phi}{2} = 65^\circ$, so $2d\sin\theta = 2D\sin(\phi/2)\cos(\phi/2) = D\sin\phi$:
  $$\lambda_{\text{exp}} = D\sin 50^\circ = (2.15\text{ \AA})(0.7660) = 1.65\text{ \AA}$$
- **From de Broglie's formula:** For $V = 54\text{ V}$ electrons ($K = 54\text{ eV} \ll m_e c^2$):
  $$\lambda_{\text{th}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV}} = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{54}}\text{ \AA} = 1.67\text{ \AA}$$

The agreement to within $\sim 1\%$ provided quantitative confirmation of matter waves; the tiny residual discrepancy arises because electrons entering the crystal experience an attractive average inner potential ($\sim 15\text{ V}$), acting as an electron-optical refractive index $n > 1$ that slightly shortens the wavelength inside the nickel lattice. Simultaneously, G. P. Thomson independently observed concentric Debye–Scherrer diffraction rings by transmitting high-energy ($10\text{–}60\text{ keV}$) electrons through thin polycrystalline gold foils.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Relativistic vs. Non-Relativistic de Broglie Wavelength of a $2.0\text{ MeV}$ Electron
> **Problem:** (a) Calculate the exact relativistic de Broglie wavelength of an electron having kinetic energy $K = 2.00\text{ MeV}$. (b) What percentage error would result if the non-relativistic expression were mistakenly used?
>
> **Solution:**
> **Constants:** $m_0 c^2 = 0.511\text{ MeV}$, $hc = 1239.84\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm} = 1.23984 \times 10^{-12}\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{m}$.
>
> **(a) Exact relativistic calculation:**
> Since $K = 2.00\text{ MeV}$ is nearly four times the electron rest energy ($0.511\text{ MeV}$), relativistic kinematics is mandatory:
> $$pc = \sqrt{K(K + 2m_0 c^2)} = \sqrt{2.00\left(2.00 + 2 \times 0.511\right)} = \sqrt{2.00 \times 3.022} = \sqrt{6.044} = 2.4585\text{ MeV}$$
> $$\lambda_{\text{rel}} = \frac{hc}{pc} = \frac{1239.84\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm}}{2.4585\text{ MeV}} = 504.3\text{ fm} = \boxed{5.043 \times 10^{-13}\text{ m}} \quad (= 5.043 \times 10^{-3}\text{ \AA})$$
>
> **(b) Non-relativistic approximation and percentage error:**
> $$\lambda_{\text{NR}} = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2 m_0 c^2 K}} = \frac{1239.84}{\sqrt{1.022 \times 2.00}} = \frac{1239.84}{1.4297} = 867.2\text{ fm} = 8.672 \times 10^{-13}\text{ m}$$
> $$\text{Percentage Error} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{NR}} - \lambda_{\text{rel}}}{\lambda_{\text{rel}}} \times 100\% = \frac{867.2 - 504.3}{504.3} \times 100\% = \boxed{+72.0\%}$$
> Neglecting special relativity severely underestimates the momentum $p$ and hence grossly overestimates the de Broglie wavelength.

## 2. Free Particle and 1D Gaussian Wave Packet - Superposition, Velocities and Dispersive Spreading

A monochromatic de Broglie plane wave $\psi_k(x,t) = A e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ represents a particle with perfectly definite momentum $p = \hbar k$, yet its probability density $|\psi_k(x,t)|^2 = |A|^2$ is uniform from $x = -\infty$ to $x = +\infty$. Such a wave cannot be normalized ($\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi_k|^2\,dx = \infty$) and describes a particle with completely indeterminate position—violating our observation of localized trajectories in cloud chambers and particle detectors.

To represent a localized free particle, we exploit the linearity of quantum wave propagation and construct a **wave packet**: a continuous Fourier superposition of plane waves spanning a narrow band $\Delta k$ around a central wave number $k_0$. Destructive interference suppresses the wave outside a finite spatial envelope $\Delta x$, while constructive interference builds a localized pulse moving with the particle.

### 2.1 Fourier Wave-Packet Representation

A general one-dimensional free-particle wave packet evolving under the dispersion relation $\omega = \omega(k)$ is written as the symmetric Fourier integral:

$$\Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{i[kx - \omega(k)t]}\,dk$$

Evaluating at $t = 0$ and inverting the Fourier transform determines the momentum-space spectral amplitude $\phi(k)$ uniquely from the initial spatial profile $\Psi(x,0)$:

$$\phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi(x,0)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx$$

By Parseval's (Plancherel's) theorem, normalization in position space guarantees identical normalization in wave-number space:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\phi(k)|^2\,dk = 1$$

---

### 2.2 Phase Velocity vs. Group Velocity (Non-Relativistic and Relativistic Proofs)

Suppose $\phi(k)$ is sharply peaked within a narrow interval $\Delta k$ around $k_0$. Expanding the angular frequency $\omega(k)$ in a Taylor series about $k_0$:

$$\omega(k) = \omega_0 + \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}(k - k_0) + \frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{d^2\omega}{dk^2}\right|_{k_0}(k - k_0)^2 + \cdots$$

where $\omega_0 \equiv \omega(k_0)$. Keeping the linear term first (which governs rigid envelope translation without distortion) and defining $v_g \equiv \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}$:

$$\Psi(x,t) \approx e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)} \left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{i(k - k_0)(x - v_g t)}\,dk\right] = e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)}\,F(x - v_g t)$$

This factorization separates two distinct velocities:

- **Phase velocity ($v_p$):** Speed of constant-phase crests of the internal carrier wave $e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)}$:
  $$v_p = \frac{\omega_0}{k_0} = \frac{E}{p}$$
- **Group velocity ($v_g$):** Speed of the localized probability envelope $|F(x - v_g t)|^2$ (stationary-phase point $\frac{d}{dk}[kx - \omega(k)t]_{k_0} = 0$):
  $$v_g = \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0} = \frac{dE}{dp}$$

#### Case 1: Non-Relativistic Free Particle

With $E = \frac{p^2}{2m}$, the de Broglie dispersion relation is $\omega(k) = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}$:

$$v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{\hbar k}{2m} = \frac{p}{2m} = \frac{v}{2}, \qquad \boxed{v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{p}{m} = v}$$

The individual phase ripples move at *half* the classical velocity, continuously forming at the trailing edge of the packet and vanishing at the leading edge, while the probability envelope travels at the exact particle velocity $v$.

#### Case 2: Relativistic Free Particle

Using the relativistic dispersion invariant $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4$, substitute $E = \hbar\omega$ and $p = \hbar k$:

$$\hbar^2 \omega^2 = \hbar^2 c^2 k^2 + m_0^2 c^4$$

Differentiating implicitly with respect to $k$:

$$2\hbar^2 \omega \frac{d\omega}{dk} = 2\hbar^2 c^2 k \implies v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c^2 k}{\omega} = \frac{p c^2}{E}$$

Inserting relativistic momentum $p = \gamma m_0 v$ and total energy $E = \gamma m_0 c^2$:

$$\boxed{v_g = \frac{(\gamma m_0 v) c^2}{\gamma m_0 c^2} = v}, \qquad \boxed{v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{E}{p} = \frac{\gamma m_0 c^2}{\gamma m_0 v} = \frac{c^2}{v} > c}$$

Multiplying the two results yields the celebrated relativistic identity:

$$\boxed{v_p v_g = c^2}$$

Furthermore, writing $E = \sqrt{p^2 c^2 + m_0^2 c^4}$ in $v_p = E/p$ with $p = h/\lambda$ expresses the superluminal phase velocity directly in terms of the de Broglie wavelength:

$$v_p = c\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{m_0 c}{p}\right)^2} = c\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{m_0 c \lambda}{h}\right)^2} = c\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_C}\right)^2}$$

Because $v_p > c$ describes only the phase speed of an infinite monochromatic component carrying zero information or energy, special relativity is never violated; energy, momentum, and probability all propagate at $v_g = v < c$.

### 2.3 Exact Time-Evolution and Dispersive Spreading of a 1D Gaussian Wave Packet

Retaining the quadratic dispersion term $\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2\omega}{dk^2}(k - k_0)^2 = \frac{\hbar}{2m}(k - k_0)^2$ reveals a striking quantum phenomenon: unlike an electromagnetic pulse in vacuum (where $\omega = ck$ gives $d^2\omega/dk^2 = 0$), a massive free-particle wave packet **spreads inevitably in time** even in empty space because its higher-$k$ momentum components outrun its lower-$k$ components. The 1D Gaussian wave packet admits a completely exact closed-form solution.

#### Physical Setup and Initial State ($t = 0$)

Prepare a free particle of mass $m$ ($V(x) = 0$) at $t = 0$ in a normalized Gaussian wave packet centered at $x = 0$ with mean wave number $k_0 = p_0/\hbar$ and initial position standard deviation $\Delta x_0$:

$$\Psi(x,0) = A \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\Delta x_0^2} + i k_0 x\right)$$

#### Step 1: Normalization and Initial Position Variance

Evaluating the normalization integral using the standard Gaussian identity $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$ (for $\text{Re}(\alpha) > 0$):

$$1 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\Psi(x,0)|^2\,dx = |A|^2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\Delta x_0^2}\right)dx = |A|^2 \sqrt{2\pi\Delta x_0^2}$$

Taking $A$ real and positive gives:

$$A = \frac{1}{(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}}$$

By evenness of $|\Psi(x,0)|^2$, $\langle x\rangle_0 = 0$. Using $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi/\alpha^3}$ with $\alpha = \frac{1}{2\Delta x_0^2}$:

$$\langle x^2\rangle_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta x_0^2}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}(2\Delta x_0^2)^{3/2} = \Delta x_0^2 \implies \Delta x(0) = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle_0 - \langle x\rangle_0^2} = \Delta x_0$$

#### Step 2: Momentum-Space Distribution $\phi(k)$ and Minimum Uncertainty at $t = 0$

Compute the Fourier transform of $\Psi(x,0)$:

$$\phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \frac{1}{(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{4\Delta x_0^2} - i(k - k_0)x\right]dx$$

Complete the square in the exponent using $\alpha x^2 - \beta x = \alpha\left(x - \frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\right)^2 - \frac{\beta^2}{4\alpha}$, which establishes the master complex Gaussian integral:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha x^2 + \beta x}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta^2}{4\alpha}\right) \qquad (\text{Re}(\alpha) > 0)$$

Here $\alpha = \frac{1}{4\Delta x_0^2}$ and $\beta = -i(k - k_0)$, so $\sqrt{\pi/\alpha} = 2\sqrt{\pi}\Delta x_0$ and $\frac{\beta^2}{4\alpha} = -\Delta x_0^2(k - k_0)^2$:

$$\phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \frac{1}{(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}} \left(2\sqrt{\pi}\Delta x_0\right)\exp\left[-\Delta x_0^2(k - k_0)^2\right]$$

Simplifying the prefactor: $\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}\Delta x_0}{\sqrt{2\pi}(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\Delta x_0}{(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}} = \left(\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}$. Thus:

$$\phi(k) = \left(\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} \exp\left[-\Delta x_0^2(k - k_0)^2\right]$$

Its squared modulus $|\phi(k)|^2 = \sqrt{\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac{(k - k_0)^2}{2(1/2\Delta x_0)^2}\right]$ is a normalized Gaussian centered at $k_0$ with standard deviation $\Delta k_0 = \frac{1}{2\Delta x_0}$, hence initial momentum uncertainty:

$$\Delta p_0 = \hbar\Delta k_0 = \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta x_0} \implies \Delta x_0 \Delta p_0 = \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

At $t = 0$, the Gaussian wave packet saturates the Heisenberg bound—it is a **minimum-uncertainty wave packet**.

#### Step 3: Exact Time Evolution $\Psi(x,t)$

Inserting $\phi(k)$ and the free-particle dispersion $\omega(k) = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}$ into the Fourier synthesis integral:

$$\Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left[-\Delta x_0^2(k - k_0)^2 + ikx - i\frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}t\right]dk$$

Change integration variable to $q = k - k_0$ ($k = k_0 + q$, $k^2 = k_0^2 + 2k_0 q + q^2$, $dk = dq$):

$$-\Delta x_0^2 q^2 + i(k_0 + q)x - i\frac{\hbar t}{2m}(k_0^2 + 2k_0 q + q^2) = i\left(k_0 x - \frac{\hbar k_0^2}{2m}t\right) - \left(\Delta x_0^2 + \frac{i\hbar t}{2m}\right)q^2 + i\left(x - \frac{\hbar k_0}{m}t\right)q$$

Identify the group velocity $v_g = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m}$, carrier frequency $\omega_0 = \frac{\hbar k_0^2}{2m}$, and complex spreading parameter $\gamma(t) \equiv \Delta x_0^2 + \frac{i\hbar t}{2m}$ (note $\text{Re}(\gamma) = \Delta x_0^2 > 0$, ensuring absolute convergence):

$$\Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left[-\gamma(t)q^2 + i(x - v_g t)q\right]dq$$

Applying the master Gaussian integral with $\alpha = \gamma(t)$ and $\beta = i(x - v_g t)$:

$$\Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\gamma(t)}}\,e^{i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t)} \exp\left[-\frac{(x - v_g t)^2}{4\gamma(t)}\right]$$

Simplify the prefactor by factoring $\Delta x_0^2$ out of $\gamma(t) = \Delta x_0^2\left(1 + \frac{i\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}\right)$:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{2\Delta x_0^2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + \frac{i\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}}} = \frac{1}{(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}\sqrt{1 + \frac{i\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}}}$$

Rationalize the exponent $\frac{1}{\gamma(t)}$:

$$\frac{1}{\gamma(t)} = \frac{\Delta x_0^2 - \frac{i\hbar t}{2m}}{\Delta x_0^4 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m}\right)^2} = \frac{1 - \frac{i\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}}{\Delta x_0^2\left[1 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}\right)^2\right]}$$

Define the **time-dependent spatial width** $\Delta x(t)$ and characteristic **spreading time** $\tau$:

$$\boxed{\Delta x(t) = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}\right)^2} = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^2}}, \qquad \boxed{\tau \equiv \frac{2m\Delta x_0^2}{\hbar}}$$

Then the complete time-evolving wave function is:

$$\Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\Delta x_0^2)^{1/4}\sqrt{1 + i(t/\tau)}} \exp\left[i(k_0 x - \omega_0 t) - \frac{(x - v_g t)^2}{4[\Delta x(t)]^2}\left(1 - i\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\right]$$

#### Step 4: Probability Density $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$ and Uncertainty Growth

Taking the squared modulus kills all pure phase factors ($|e^{i\theta}| = 1$), while $\left|\sqrt{1 + i(t/\tau)}\right|^2 = \sqrt{1 + (t/\tau)^2} = \frac{\Delta x(t)}{\Delta x_0}$:

$$\boxed{|\Psi(x,t)|^2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Delta x(t)} \exp\left[-\frac{(x - v_g t)^2}{2[\Delta x(t)]^2}\right]}$$

#### Physical Insights, Dimensional and Limiting-Case Sanity Checks

1. **Dimensional check:** $\left[\frac{\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}\right] = \frac{(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}^{-1})(\text{s})}{(\text{kg})(\text{m}^2)} = 1$ (dimensionless).
2. **Probability conservation & trajectory:** For all $t$, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = 1$, and the packet centroid tracks the exact classical uniform-motion trajectory $\langle x(t)\rangle = v_g t = \frac{p_0}{m}t$.
3. **Why $\Delta x(t)\Delta p(t)$ increases:** The time-dependent momentum amplitude is $\phi(k,t) = \phi(k)e^{-i\omega(k)t}$, so $|\phi(k,t)|^2 = |\phi(k)|^2$ is strictly time-independent (momentum is conserved for a free particle). Hence $\Delta p(t) = \Delta p_0 = \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta x_0}$ remains constant, while:
   $$\Delta x(t)\Delta p(t) = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\Delta x_0^2}\right)^2} \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$$
4. **Classical correspondence & asymptotic spreading ($t \gg \tau$):** For $t \gg \tau$, $\Delta x(t) \approx \frac{\hbar}{2m\Delta x_0}t = \frac{\Delta p_0}{m}t = (\Delta v_0)\,t$. The packet expands linearly at the exact initial velocity spread dictated by Heisenberg's uncertainty principle! In the classical limit $\hbar \to 0$ (or macroscopic $m \to \infty$), $\tau \to \infty$ and $\Delta x(t) \to \Delta x_0$—no observable spreading occurs.
5. **Time-reversal symmetry:** Because $\Delta x(t)$ depends on $t^2$, a packet prepared at $t = -t_1 < 0$ with an inward phase chirp contracts down to its minimum waist $\Delta x_0$ at $t = 0$ before expanding for $t > 0$.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Spreading Time of an Atomic-Scale Electron vs. a Macroscopic Marble
> **Problem:** Compare the characteristic spreading time $\tau$ and the width-doubling time $t_2$ for: (a) a free electron initially localized to atomic dimensions $\Delta x_0 = 1.00\text{ \AA} = 1.00 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$, and find its width after $t = 1.00\text{ s}$; (b) a $1.00\text{ g}$ marble initially localized to $\Delta x_0 = 1.00\ \mu\text{m} = 1.00 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$.
>
> **Solution:**
> From $\Delta x(t) = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + (t/\tau)^2}$ with $\tau = \frac{2m\Delta x_0^2}{\hbar}$:
> - At $t = \tau$, the width increases by $\sqrt{2}$.
> - Width doubles ($\Delta x(t_2) = 2\Delta x_0$) when $\sqrt{1 + (t_2/\tau)^2} = 2 \implies t_2 = \sqrt{3}\,\tau \approx 1.732\,\tau$.
>
> **(a) Free electron ($m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}$, $\Delta x_0 = 1.00 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$):**
> $$\tau_e = \frac{2(9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg})(1.00 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m})^2}{1.0546 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}} = \frac{1.8218 \times 10^{-50}}{1.0546 \times 10^{-34}} = \boxed{1.73 \times 10^{-16}\text{ s}}$$
> $$t_{2,e} = \sqrt{3}\,\tau_e = 1.732 \times 1.727 \times 10^{-16}\text{ s} = \boxed{2.99 \times 10^{-16}\text{ s}}$$
> After $t = 1.00\text{ s} \gg \tau_e$:
> $$\Delta x(1.00\text{ s}) \approx \Delta x_0 \frac{t}{\tau_e} = \frac{\hbar t}{2m_e \Delta x_0} = \frac{(1.0546 \times 10^{-34})(1.00)}{2(9.109 \times 10^{-31})(1.00 \times 10^{-10})} = \boxed{5.79 \times 10^{5}\text{ m} = 579\text{ km}}$$
>
> **(b) $1.00\text{ g}$ marble ($m = 1.00 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}$, $\Delta x_0 = 1.00 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$):**
> $$\tau_{\text{marble}} = \frac{2(1.00 \times 10^{-3})(1.00 \times 10^{-6})^2}{1.0546 \times 10^{-34}} = \boxed{1.90 \times 10^{19}\text{ s}}$$
> $$t_{2,\text{marble}} = \sqrt{3}\,\tau_{\text{marble}} = 3.29 \times 10^{19}\text{ s} = \frac{3.29 \times 10^{19}}{3.156 \times 10^7\text{ s/yr}} \approx \boxed{1.04 \times 10^{12}\text{ years}}$$
> Even over $75\times$ the age of the universe ($1.38 \times 10^{10}\text{ yr}$), a macroscopic marble does not noticeably spread, whereas a localized free electron explodes to hundreds of kilometers in a single second.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Relativistic Group and Phase Velocities of a $500\text{ MeV}$ Proton
> **Problem:** A proton (rest energy $m_p c^2 = 938.3\text{ MeV}$) is accelerated to kinetic energy $K = 500\text{ MeV}$. Compute its de Broglie group velocity $v_g$, its phase velocity $v_p$, and verify $v_p v_g = c^2$.
>
> **Solution:**
> Total relativistic energy:
> $$E = K + m_p c^2 = 500 + 938.3 = 1438.3\text{ MeV}$$
> Lorentz factor:
> $$\gamma = \frac{E}{m_p c^2} = \frac{1438.3}{938.3} = 1.5329$$
> Speed ratio $\beta = v/c$:
> $$\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{(1.5329)^2}} = \sqrt{1 - 0.4256} = \sqrt{0.5744} = 0.7579$$
> **Group velocity:**
> $$v_g = v = \beta c = 0.7579\,c = \boxed{2.27 \times 10^{8}\text{ m/s}}$$
> **Phase velocity:**
> $$v_p = \frac{c^2}{v} = \frac{c}{\beta} = \frac{c}{0.7579} = 1.319\,c = \boxed{3.96 \times 10^{8}\text{ m/s}}$$
> **Product check:** $v_p v_g = (1.3194\,c)(0.7579\,c) = 1.000\,c^2$.

## 3. Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle - Foundations, Thought Experiments and Energy Estimates

Fourier analysis already forced an inverse trade-off $\Delta x_0\,\Delta k_0 \ge 1/2$ for any wave packet: squeezing a pulse into a shorter interval $\Delta x$ requires superposing a wider bandwidth $\Delta k$ of plane waves. Multiplying by $\hbar$ via de Broglie's law $p = \hbar k$ transforms a mathematical property of waves into the deepest structural principle of quantum physics—Werner Heisenberg's 1927 Uncertainty Principle.

### 3.1 The Fundamental Uncertainty Relations and Intrinsic vs. Instrumental Error

For any canonically conjugate pair of physical observables, quantum mechanics sets an absolute lower bound on the product of their statistical root-mean-square spreads (standard deviations $\Delta A = \sqrt{\langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2}$):

1. **Position–Momentum Uncertainty (for each Cartesian axis independently):**
   $$\boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}, \qquad \Delta y\,\Delta p_y \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}, \qquad \Delta z\,\Delta p_z \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}}$$
   Orthogonal components (such as $x$ and $p_y$) commute ($[\hat{x}, \hat{p}_y] = 0$) and impose *no* joint uncertainty limit on each other.

2. **Energy–Time Uncertainty:**
   $$\boxed{\Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}}$$
   Unlike position $\hat{x}$, time $t$ in non-relativistic quantum mechanics is a classical parameter, not a Hermitian operator. Consequently, $\Delta t$ is *not* the standard deviation of a clock measurement; rather, in the rigorous **Mandelstam–Tamm formulation**, $\Delta t \equiv \frac{\Delta Q}{|d\langle\hat{Q}\rangle/dt|}$ is the characteristic timescale over which the expectation value of any observable $\hat{Q}$ changes appreciably (by one standard deviation). Equivalently, for an unstable excited atomic or nuclear state of mean lifetime $\tau$, its energy is intrinsically broadened by a **natural linewidth** $\Delta E \ge \frac{\hbar}{2\tau}$ (so a state with an exact energy $\Delta E = 0$ must have an infinite lifetime $\tau = \infty$, i.e., be a true stationary state).

3. **Angular Momentum–Azimuthal Angle Uncertainty:**
   $$\Delta L_z\,\Delta\phi \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \quad (\text{valid for small angular spread } \Delta\phi \ll 2\pi)$$
   Because $\phi$ is periodic modulo $2\pi$, $\Delta\phi$ is bounded above by $\sim \pi$; for an exact eigenstate of $\hat{L}_z$ ($\Delta L_z = 0$), the azimuthal probability density is completely uniform across $[0, 2\pi)$. Rigorously, angular momentum uncertainty is expressed via the commutator $[\hat{L}_x, \hat{L}_y] = i\hbar\hat{L}_z$ as $\Delta L_x\,\Delta L_y \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}|\langle\hat{L}_z\rangle|$.

#### Intrinsic Quantum Uncertainty vs. Instrumental Inaccuracy

A frequent misconception treats $\Delta x$ as clumsy experimental error caused by finite ruler ticks or imperfect detectors. The distinction is fundamental:

- **Instrumental inaccuracy (error)** is a technical limitation of the measuring device (thermal noise, optical aberration, finite pixel size). It can, in principle, be reduced arbitrarily close to zero by engineering better instruments while assuming the particle still possesses a crisp pre-existing coordinate $x_0$ and momentum $p_0$.
- **Quantum uncertainty ($\Delta x, \Delta p_x$)** is an **intrinsic, irreducible property of the quantum state $|\psi\rangle$ itself**. Prepare $10^9$ identical particles in the exact same state $|\psi\rangle$ and use ideal, zero-error detectors: measuring $x$ on half the ensemble yields an inescapable statistical distribution of width $\Delta x$, and measuring $p_x$ on the other half yields a distribution of width $\Delta p_x$ strictly obeying $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \ge \hbar/2$. A quantum particle simply does not possess simultaneous sharp values of $x$ and $p_x$ prior to measurement.

### 3.2 Heisenberg's Thought Experiments

Before Hilbert-space proofs existed, Heisenberg illustrated why no conceivable measurement apparatus can beat $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \sim h$, because wave diffraction and particle recoil trade off against each other in every physical probe.

#### 1. The $\gamma$-Ray Microscope

Suppose we attempt to measure the $x$-coordinate of a free electron using an ideal optical microscope whose objective lens subtends a half-aperture angle $\alpha$ at the electron, illuminated by photons of wavelength $\lambda$.

- **Position uncertainty (wave diffraction limit):** By Rayleigh's optical resolution criterion, two points separated by less than the Abbe resolving limit cannot be distinguished by the objective lens:
  $$\Delta x \approx \frac{\lambda}{2\sin\alpha}$$
  To make $\Delta x$ arbitrarily tiny, one must use ultra-short wavelength radiation ($\gamma$-rays, $\lambda \to 0$) and a wide aperture ($\alpha \to \pi/2$).
- **Momentum uncertainty (Compton recoil):** For the electron to be seen, at least one photon must scatter off it and enter the objective cone between angles $-\alpha$ and $+\alpha$ relative to the microscope axis ($y$-axis). Because we cannot know *which* path inside the cone the photon took before focusing on the eyepiece, the $x$-component of the scattered photon's momentum is uncertain across the interval $\left[-\frac{h}{\lambda}\sin\alpha,\; +\frac{h}{\lambda}\sin\alpha\right]$:
  $$\Delta p_x \approx \frac{2h}{\lambda}\sin\alpha \quad \left(\text{or half-range } \frac{h}{\lambda}\sin\alpha\right)$$
  By momentum conservation, this exact uncertainty is transferred as an unknown Compton kick to the electron.
- **Uncertainty product:** Multiplying the two expressions cancels both $\lambda$ and $\sin\alpha$ identically:
  $$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \approx \left(\frac{\lambda}{2\sin\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{2h}{\lambda}\sin\alpha\right) = h \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$$
  Shrinking $\lambda$ sharpens $\Delta x$ at the exact cost of inflating the Compton recoil $\Delta p_x$; shrinking $\alpha$ reduces $\Delta p_x$ at the exact cost of blurring the diffraction spot $\Delta x$.

#### 2. Single-Slit Electron Diffraction

Consider a collimated monoenergetic beam of electrons traveling along the $+y$-axis with definite momentum $p_y = p$ and initial transverse momentum $p_x = 0$, incident normally on an opaque screen containing a narrow horizontal slit of width $a$ along the $x$-axis.

- **Position localization at the slit:** The moment an electron passes through the slit, its $x$-coordinate is localized within the aperture width:
  $$\Delta x \approx a$$
- **Transverse momentum spread via matter-wave diffraction:** Because electrons carry de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$, the slit produces a single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction pattern on a distant detector screen. The vast majority of electrons fall inside the central diffraction maximum bounded by the first minima at angle $\pm\theta$, where:
  $$a\sin\theta = \lambda = \frac{h}{p} \implies \sin\theta = \frac{h}{ap}$$
  An electron deflected by angle $\theta$ acquires a transverse $x$-momentum $p_x = p\sin\theta$. Since the impact point anywhere inside the central fringe is unpredictable, the transverse momentum uncertainty is:
  $$\Delta p_x \approx p\sin\theta = p\left(\frac{h}{ap}\right) = \frac{h}{a}$$
- **Uncertainty product:**
  $$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \approx a \cdot \frac{h}{a} = h \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$$
  Narrowing the slit to localize $x$ inevitably flares out the diffraction cone, enlarging $\Delta p_x$.

### 3.3 Order-of-Magnitude Ground-State Energy and Confinement Estimates

The Uncertainty Principle explains a profound mystery of classical physics: why matter is stable and does not collapse. Confining a quantum particle into a region of shrinking size $R$ forces its momentum spread—and hence its kinetic energy $T \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{2mR^2}$—to diverge quadratically as $1/R^2$, resisting attractive potentials and establishing a non-zero ground-state energy (**zero-point energy**).

#### Application 1: Ground-State Energy and Bohr Radius of the Hydrogen Atom

Consider an electron of mass $m_e$ bound to a stationary proton by the attractive SI Coulomb potential $V(r) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$. If the electron is localized within a spherical cloud of characteristic radius $r$, its position uncertainty is $\Delta r \sim r$. Using the standard three-dimensional radial uncertainty scale $p \sim \Delta p \sim \frac{\hbar}{r}$ (noting that in 3D, $(\Delta\mathbf{r})^2 = 3(\Delta x)^2$ and $(\Delta\mathbf{p})^2 = 3(\Delta p_x)^2$, giving $\Delta r\,\Delta p \ge \frac{3}{2}\hbar$; using $pr \sim \hbar$ matches the exact radial momentum scale), the total energy as a function of cloud radius $r$ is:

$$E(r) = \frac{p^2}{2m_e} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e r^2} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$

To find the equilibrium ground-state size, minimize $E(r)$ with respect to $r$:

$$\frac{dE}{dr} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e r^3} + \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} = 0 \implies \frac{\hbar^2}{m_e r} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$$

Solving for $r = r_{\min}$ recovers the exact **Bohr radius** $a_0$:

$$\boxed{r_{\min} = \frac{4\pi\epsilon_0 \hbar^2}{m_e e^2} \equiv a_0 = 0.529 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.529\text{ \AA}}$$

Verify it is a strict minimum via the second derivative:

$$\left.\frac{d^2 E}{dr^2}\right|_{a_0} = \frac{3\hbar^2}{m_e a_0^4} - \frac{2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0^3} = \frac{3\hbar^2}{m_e a_0^4} - \frac{2\hbar^2}{m_e a_0^4} = \frac{\hbar^2}{m_e a_0^4} > 0$$

Substituting $r = a_0$ back into $E(r)$ yields the exact Hydrogen ground-state energy:

$$E_{\min} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e a_0^2} - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0}\right) - \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 a_0} = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 a_0}$$

$$\boxed{E_{\min} = -\frac{m_e e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2\hbar^2} = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\text{ J} = -13.6\text{ eV}}$$

*(Limiting check: As $r \to 0$, the repulsive quantum confinement kinetic energy $+\hbar^2/(2m_e r^2)$ overwhelms the $-1/r$ Coulomb pull, preventing atomic collapse; as $\hbar \to 0$, $a_0 \to 0$ and $E_{\min} \to -\infty$, reproducing the classical Rutherford instability.)*

#### Application 2: Zero-Point Energy of the 1D Linear Harmonic Oscillator

For a particle of mass $m$ in a harmonic potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2$, symmetry of the ground state guarantees $\langle x\rangle = 0$ and $\langle p\rangle = 0$. Therefore, $\langle x^2\rangle = (\Delta x)^2$ and $\langle p^2\rangle = (\Delta p)^2$ hold *identically* without approximation. The exact energy expectation value is:

$$\langle H\rangle = \frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\langle x^2\rangle = \frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2$$

Applying the exact Heisenberg inequality $\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta x}$:

$$\langle H\rangle \ge \frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2$$

Minimizing the right-hand side with respect to $\Delta x$:

$$\frac{d}{d(\Delta x)}\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2\right] = -\frac{\hbar^2}{4m(\Delta x)^3} + m\omega^2\Delta x = 0$$

$$(\Delta x)^4 = \frac{\hbar^2}{4m^2\omega^2} \implies (\Delta x)_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}$$

*(Instant AM–GM verification: For any $a, b > 0$, $a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab}$, so $\langle H\rangle \ge 2\sqrt{\frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2} = 2\sqrt{\frac{\hbar^2\omega^2}{16}} = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$!)*

Substituting $(\Delta x)_{\min}$ yields the exact ground-state zero-point energy:

$$\boxed{E_0 = \frac{1}{4}\hbar\omega + \frac{1}{4}\hbar\omega = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega}$$

Notice that this bound is **exact**—not merely an order-of-magnitude estimate—because the harmonic oscillator ground-state wave function is a pure Gaussian that saturates $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \hbar/2$, and it automatically satisfies the quantum virial theorem $\langle T\rangle = \langle V\rangle = \frac{1}{4}\hbar\omega$.

#### Application 3: Non-Existence of Electrons Inside the Atomic Nucleus

Before Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932, physicists wondered whether $\beta$-decay electrons already resided inside the nucleus alongside protons. The Uncertainty Principle demolishes this hypothesis quantitatively.

Consider a typical medium-sized nucleus of radius $R \approx 5.0\text{ fm} = 5.0 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m}$. If an electron were confined inside the nucleus, its position uncertainty could not exceed $\Delta x \le R = 5.0\text{ fm}$. Applying $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$, the minimum momentum of the confined electron is at least:

$$p_{\min} \sim \Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2R} \implies p_{\min}c = \frac{\hbar c}{2R} = \frac{197.3\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm}}{2 \times 5.0\text{ fm}} = 19.73\text{ MeV}$$

*(Using $\Delta p \sim \hbar/R$ doubles this to $p_{\min}c \approx 39.5\text{ MeV}$; either convention leads to the identical physical conclusion.)*

Because $p_{\min}c = 19.73\text{ MeV} \gg m_e c^2 = 0.511\text{ MeV}$, the confined electron would be **ultra-relativistic**—using non-relativistic $K = p^2/2m_e$ is strictly forbidden! Computing the exact relativistic total energy and kinetic energy:

$$E = \sqrt{(p_{\min}c)^2 + (m_e c^2)^2} = \sqrt{(19.73)^2 + (0.511)^2} = 19.74\text{ MeV}$$

$$\boxed{K_e = E - m_e c^2 \approx 19.2\text{ MeV}}$$

Compare this with experiment:
1. Typical nuclear $\beta$-decay electrons emerge with maximum kinetic energies of only $1\text{–}4\text{ MeV}$, far below the $\gtrsim 20\text{ MeV}$ required for confinement (and for a small nucleus $R \sim 2\text{ fm}$, $K_e \gtrsim 50\text{ MeV}$). Moreover, the maximum electrostatic Coulomb binding energy of an electron at the nuclear surface is only $\sim \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R} \sim 5\text{–}10\text{ MeV}$, utterly insufficient to hold the electron bound.
2. **Contrast with a nucleon (proton/neutron, $m_N c^2 = 939\text{ MeV}$):** Confined to the same $R = 5.0\text{ fm}$, the same momentum $p_{\min}c = 19.73\text{ MeV}$ satisfies $p_{\min}c \ll m_N c^2$, so non-relativistic kinematics applies:
   $$K_N = \frac{(p_{\min}c)^2}{2 m_N c^2} = \frac{(19.73\text{ MeV})^2}{2(939\text{ MeV})} \approx 0.21\text{ MeV}$$
   Even for tighter confinement ($\Delta p \sim \hbar/R$ or $R \sim 1.5\text{ fm}$), $K_N \sim 1\text{–}20\text{ MeV}$, comfortably smaller than the attractive strong nuclear potential well depth ($\sim 40\text{–}50\text{ MeV}$).

Therefore, protons and neutrons can stably inhabit the nucleus, while free electrons cannot; $\beta$-decay electrons are created at the instant of weak decay ($n \to p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$).

> Narrowing the initial aperture radius $r_0 = \Delta y_0$ shrinks the initial beam width but increases the transverse momentum spread $\Delta p_y \ge \frac{\hbar}{2r_0}$, causing transverse velocity spread $\Delta v_y = \frac{\Delta p_y}{m_e} = \frac{\hbar}{2m_e r_0}$ and transverse drift $\Delta y_{\text{drift}} = (\Delta v_y)t = \frac{\hbar t}{2m_e r_0}$.

## 4. Schrödinger Equation, Probability Current and Dirac Formalism

De Broglie waves and Heisenberg uncertainty constrain *kinematics*, but physics demands a *dynamical equation of motion* predicting how a quantum state evolves inside an arbitrary external potential $V(\mathbf{r},t)$—the quantum counterpart of Newton's second law. Erwin Schrödinger supplied that wave equation in 1926, Max Born anchored it to measurable probabilities, and Paul Dirac unified wave mechanics and matrix mechanics into an abstract Hilbert-space algebra of states and operators.

### 4.1 Born Statistical Interpretation, Admissibility Conditions and Normalization

The complex wave function $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is not itself directly observable; it is a **probability amplitude**. By **Max Born's statistical postulate** (1926), the quantity:

$$\rho(\mathbf{r},t) \equiv |\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^2 = \Psi^*(\mathbf{r},t)\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$$

is the **probability density**, such that $\rho(\mathbf{r},t)\,d^3r$ gives the probability of finding the particle within the infinitesimal volume element $d^3r = dx\,dy\,dz$ centered at $\mathbf{r}$ at time $t$.

Since the particle must be found *somewhere* in all space with certainty (probability $1$), every physically realizable bound-state wave function must be **normalizable** (belonging to the square-integrable Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$):

$$\boxed{\int_{\text{all space}} |\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^2\,d^3r = 1}$$

Because the Schrödinger equation is linear and homogeneous, if $\Psi$ is a solution then $N\Psi$ is also a solution for any constant $N \in \mathbb{C}$. Choosing $|N| = \left(\int |\Psi|^2\,d^3r\right)^{-1/2}$ normalizes the wave function; the overall global phase $e^{i\alpha}$ of $N$ cancels in $|\Psi|^2$ and all expectation values and is physically unobservable (though *relative* phases between superposed states govern interference!).

#### Standard Physical Admissibility Conditions on $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$

For $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ to represent a physical quantum state, it must be:
1. **Single-valued** at every spatial point $\mathbf{r}$ (otherwise probability density $|\Psi|^2$ would be ambiguous).
2. **Finite everywhere** and **square-integrable** ($\Psi \to 0$ faster than $1/r^{3/2}$ as $r \to \infty$ for 3D bound states; bounded for 1D/3D scattering states).
3. **Continuous everywhere** (a jump discontinuity in $\Psi$ would make $\nabla\Psi$ a Dirac delta and kinetic energy $\int |\nabla\Psi|^2 d^3r$ infinite).
4. **Smooth ($\nabla\Psi$ continuous)** everywhere the potential $V(\mathbf{r})$ is finite (including finite step discontinuities!). The first derivative $\nabla\Psi$ suffers a finite cusp discontinuity *only* at idealized singular potentials (infinite rigid walls or Dirac delta potentials $V(x) = \alpha\delta(x)$).

---

### 4.2 Construction of the Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation (TDSE)

The Schrödinger equation is a fundamental postulate verified by experiment, not a theorem derived from classical physics; nevertheless, its exact form is forced by demanding compatibility with de Broglie–Einstein waves and non-relativistic energy conservation.

Consider a free particle moving with momentum $\mathbf{p}$ and energy $E$, represented by the complex plane wave:

$$\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = A\exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r} - Et)\right]$$

Differentiating with respect to $t$ and $\mathbf{r}$ extracts the dynamical variables $E$ and $\mathbf{p}$ linearly:

$$\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}E\Psi \implies \boxed{\hat{E} = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}$$

$$\nabla\Psi = \frac{i}{\hbar}\mathbf{p}\Psi \implies \boxed{\hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar\nabla}$$

Consequently, the kinetic energy operator is $\hat{T} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2$. Promoting the classical non-relativistic Hamiltonian relation $E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(\mathbf{r},t)$ to an operator identity acting on $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ produces the **Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation (TDSE)**:

$$\boxed{i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r},t)\right]\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \hat{H}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)}$$

#### Why Must the TDSE Be Linear, First-Order in Time, and Explicitly Complex?

- **Linearity:** Required to preserve the superposition principle observed in double-slit interference.
- **First-order in time:** Guarantees that specifying the state $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t_0)$ alone (without $\partial\Psi/\partial t$) uniquely determines all future evolution and conserves total probability $\int |\Psi|^2\,d^3r = 1$ at all times.
- **Explicit imaginary factor $i$:** A real diffusion equation ($\partial_t \Psi = D\nabla^2\Psi$) damps oscillations exponentially instead of supporting traveling waves. The explicit $i$ makes $\hat{H}$ generate *unitary* oscillatory phase evolution $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ with conserved norm and non-vanishing probability flux.

When the potential $V(\mathbf{r})$ is **time-independent**, separation of variables $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \psi(\mathbf{r})T(t)$ divides the TDSE by $\psi(\mathbf{r})T(t)$ into purely time-dependent and purely space-dependent halves equal to a real separation constant $E$:

$$\frac{i\hbar}{T(t)}\frac{dT(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{\psi(\mathbf{r})}\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r})\right]\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E$$

Integrating the time equation gives $T(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, leaving the **Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation (TISE)**—the eigenvalue equation for stationary energy eigenstates:

$$\boxed{\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r})\right]\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\,\psi(\mathbf{r})}$$

---

### 4.3 Probability Current Density $\mathbf{J}$ and Local Conservation (Continuity Equation)

Normalization $\int |\Psi|^2\,d^3r = 1$ at $t = 0$ would be useless if time evolution under the TDSE allowed total probability to drift away from unity. Even stronger than global conservation, quantum mechanics enforces **local conservation of probability**: probability cannot vanish in one region and teleport instantaneously to another; any change inside a closed volume must flow continuously across its boundary surface via a probability flux $\mathbf{J}$.

#### Unabridged Derivation of $\mathbf{J}$ and the Continuity Equation

**Setup:** Assume the potential $V(\mathbf{r},t)$ is strictly **real** ($V^* = V$). Write the TDSE for $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ and its complex conjugate for $\Psi^*(\mathbf{r},t)$:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi + V\Psi \implies \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi - \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi \tag{6}$$

$$-i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi^* + V\Psi^* \implies \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi^* + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^* \tag{7}$$

Differentiate the probability density $\rho(\mathbf{r},t) = \Psi^*\Psi$ with respect to time using the product rule:

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi$$

Substitute Equations $(6)$ and $(7)$:

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \Psi^*\left(\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi - \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi\right) + \left(-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi^* + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*\right)\Psi$$

Because $V$ is real, the potential terms $-\frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*\Psi$ and $+\frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*\Psi$ cancel identically:

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*\right)$$

Apply the vector calculus identity $\nabla\cdot\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*\right) = (\nabla\Psi^*\cdot\nabla\Psi + \Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi) - (\nabla\Psi\cdot\nabla\Psi^* + \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*) = \Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*$:

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla\cdot\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*\right) = -\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*\right)\right]$$

*(Using $i = -1/i$ to pull out the minus sign cleanly.)* Defining the **Probability Current Density** $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r},t)$:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r},t) \equiv \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*\right) = \frac{1}{m}\operatorname{Re}\left(\Psi^*\hat{\mathbf{p}}\Psi\right)}$$

yields the exact **Quantum Continuity Equation**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r},t) = 0}$$

#### Global Conservation of Normalization & Complex-Potential Remark

Integrating the continuity equation over an arbitrary volume $\Omega$ bounded by closed surface $\partial\Omega$ and applying Gauss's Divergence Theorem:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega} \rho(\mathbf{r},t)\,d^3r = -\int_{\Omega} (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J})\,d^3r = -\oint_{\partial\Omega} \mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

Extending $\Omega \to \mathbb{R}^3$ for any square-integrable wave function ($\Psi \to 0$ and $\nabla\Psi \to 0$ rapidly as $r \to \infty$), the surface integral at infinity vanishes identically:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^3} |\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^2\,d^3r = 0$$

Thus a wave function normalized at $t = 0$ stays automatically normalized for all future time. *(Note: If the potential is given a negative imaginary part $V = V_R - iW$ with $W > 0$, the cancellation fails and $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\frac{2W}{\hbar}\rho < 0$, acting as a probability sink—used in the nuclear optical model to describe particle absorption by a target nucleus.)*

#### Dimensional & Limiting-Case Sanity Checks for $\mathbf{J}$

- **Dimensions (3D):** $[\Psi] = \text{m}^{-3/2}$, $[\nabla] = \text{m}^{-1}$, so $[\mathbf{J}] = \frac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{kg}} \cdot \text{m}^{-3} \cdot \text{m}^{-1} = \text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$ (probability crossing unit area per unit time; in 1D, $[J] = \text{s}^{-1}$).
- **Plane wave $\Psi = A e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}$:** $\nabla\Psi = i\mathbf{k}\Psi$ and $\nabla\Psi^* = -i\mathbf{k}\Psi^*$, yielding $\mathbf{J} = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}(2i\mathbf{k}|A|^2) = |A|^2 \frac{\hbar\mathbf{k}}{m} = \rho\mathbf{v}$ (exact fluid flux = density $\times$ velocity!).
- **Purely real spatial wavefunction $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = R(\mathbf{r})e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ (e.g., 1D bound states):** $\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^* = R\nabla R - R\nabla R = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{0}$ (standing wave with zero net probability transport!).
- **Polar Madelung form $\Psi = \sqrt{\rho}\,e^{iS/\hbar}$:** Direct substitution yields $\mathbf{J} = \rho\frac{\nabla S}{m}$, identifying $\mathbf{v} = \nabla S/m$ as the local quantum phase-gradient flow velocity.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Probability Current Density of Superposed Counter-Propagating Plane Waves
> **Problem:** Compute the probability current density $J(x,t)$ for a 1D scattering wave function composed of an incident wave traveling right and a reflected wave traveling left:
> $$\Psi(x,t) = \left(A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}\right)e^{-i\omega t} \qquad (A, B \in \mathbb{C},\; k \in \mathbb{R})$$
> Interpret the result physically and check the standing-wave limit $|A| = |B|$.
>
> **Solution:**
> Write out $\Psi$, $\Psi^*$, and $\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}$:
> $$\Psi(x,t) = \left(A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}\right)e^{-i\omega t}, \qquad \Psi^*(x,t) = \left(A^* e^{-ikx} + B^* e^{ikx}\right)e^{i\omega t}$$
> $$\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} = ik\left(A e^{ikx} - B e^{-ikx}\right)e^{-i\omega t}$$
> Multiply $\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}$ (notice $e^{i\omega t}e^{-i\omega t} = 1$):
> $$\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} = ik\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2 - A^* B e^{-2ikx} + B^* A e^{2ikx}\right)$$
> Take the complex conjugate to obtain $\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x} = \left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)^*$:
> $$\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x} = -ik\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2 - A B^* e^{2ikx} + B A^* e^{-2ikx}\right) = ik\left(-|A|^2 + |B|^2 + B^* A e^{2ikx} - A^* B e^{-2ikx}\right)$$
> Subtract the two expressions—observe that **both oscillating interference cross-terms cancel identically**:
> $$\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x} = 2ik\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2\right)$$
> Insert into the 1D probability current formula $J = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\right)$:
> $$\boxed{J = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2\right) = J_{\text{inc}} - J_{\text{ref}}}$$
> **Physical Interpretation:** Although the probability density $|\Psi|^2 = |A|^2 + |B|^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}(A^* B e^{-2ikx})$ displays spatial interference fringes, the net probability flux $J$ is strictly uniform in space and equals the incident rightward flux minus the reflected leftward flux—justifying the exact definition of reflection coefficient $R = |J_{\text{ref}}|/|J_{\text{inc}}| = |B|^2/|A|^2$. When $|A| = |B|$ (total reflection, standing wave), $J = 0$ identically.

### 4.4 Dirac Formalism - Hilbert Space, Bra-Ket Architecture and Hermitian Operators

Just as vectors in 3D Euclidean space exist independently of any particular Cartesian or spherical coordinate axes, a quantum state exists as an abstract vector $|\psi\rangle$ (**ket**) inside a complex, complete inner-product vector space called a **Hilbert space** $\mathcal{H}$. Wave functions $\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \langle\mathbf{r}|\psi\rangle$ and momentum amplitudes $\phi(\mathbf{p}) = \langle\mathbf{p}|\psi\rangle$ are merely coordinate representations of the same underlying ket $|\psi\rangle$.

#### Axioms of Dirac Bra-Ket Notation

1. **Kets and Dual Bras:** To every ket $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}$ corresponds a unique dual linear functional $\langle\psi| \in \mathcal{H}^*$ (**bra**). Linear combinations obey antilinearity under bra-ket conjugation:
   $$\alpha|\psi\rangle + \beta|\phi\rangle \;\longleftrightarrow\; \alpha^*\langle\psi| + \beta^*\langle\phi|$$
2. **Inner Product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \in \mathbb{C}$:**
   - Conjugate symmetry: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^*$.
   - Linearity in the ket: $\langle\chi|\left(\alpha|\psi\rangle + \beta|\phi\rangle\right) = \alpha\langle\chi|\psi\rangle + \beta\langle\chi|\phi\rangle$.
   - Positive-definiteness: $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle \ge 0$, with $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 0 \iff |\psi\rangle = 0$ (the null ket).
   - Position-space realization: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \int_{\text{all space}} \phi^*(\mathbf{r})\psi(\mathbf{r})\,d^3r$.
3. **Orthonormality and Completeness (Resolution of the Identity):** An orthonormal basis $\{|n\rangle\}$ satisfies $\langle m|n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$ and spans $\mathcal{H}$:
   $$\boxed{\sum_{n} |n\rangle\langle n| = \hat{I}} \quad (\text{discrete}), \qquad \boxed{\int |\mathbf{r}\rangle\langle\mathbf{r}|\,d^3r = \hat{I}} \quad (\text{continuous, } \langle\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{r}'\rangle = \delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'))$$
   Expanding any ket $|\psi\rangle = \hat{I}|\psi\rangle = \sum_n |n\rangle\langle n|\psi\rangle = \sum_n c_n |n\rangle$ immediately identifies the expansion coefficient $c_n = \langle n|\psi\rangle$ and Parseval's normalization condition $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1$.

#### Classical vs. Quantum Observables Matrix

| Structural Feature | Classical Mechanics (Hamiltonian) | Quantum Mechanics (Dirac–Hilbert) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **State of the System** | Point $(q_i, p_i)$ in $2N$-dimensional phase space | Normalized ray $|\psi(t)\rangle$ in complex Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ |
| **Physical Observable** | Real-valued smooth function $A(q, p)$ on phase space | Linear **Hermitian operator** $\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$ acting on $\mathcal{H}$ |
| **Allowed Measurement Values** | Continuous continuum of function values $A(q,p)$ | Real **eigenvalues** $a_n$ of $\hat{A}|n\rangle = a_n|n\rangle$ (discrete or continuous) |
| **Outcome Prediction** | Deterministic exact value $A(q(t), p(t))$ | Probabilistic: Born probability $P(a_n) = |\langle n|\psi\rangle|^2$, mean $\langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle$ |
| **State After Measurement** | Unperturbed (ideal measurement is non-invasive) | Collapses onto eigenstate $|n\rangle$ corresponding to measured eigenvalue $a_n$ |
| **Algebraic Bracket** | Poisson bracket $\{A, B\}_{\text{PB}}$ | Commutator $\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]$ (Dirac quantization rule) |
| **Joint Measurability** | All observables simultaneously sharp | Simultaneous sharp values exist iff $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$ |
| **Dynamical Time Evolution** | $\frac{dA}{dt} = \{A, H\}_{\text{PB}} + \frac{\partial A}{\partial t}$ | TDSE $i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle = \hat{H}|\psi\rangle$; $\frac{d\langle\hat{A}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle[\hat{A},\hat{H}]\rangle + \langle\frac{\partial\hat{A}}{\partial t}\rangle$ |

#### Hermitian (Self-Adjoint) Operators and Their Three Core Theorems

The **Hermitian adjoint** (conjugate) $\hat{A}^\dagger$ of any linear operator $\hat{A}$ is defined uniquely by requiring, for all $|\phi\rangle, |\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}$:

$$\langle\phi|\hat{A}^\dagger|\psi\rangle \equiv \langle\psi|\hat{A}|\phi\rangle^* \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left(\hat{A}|\phi\rangle\right)^\dagger = \langle\phi|\hat{A}^\dagger$$

From this definition follow the fundamental adjoint rules: $(\hat{A}^\dagger)^\dagger = \hat{A}$, $(c\hat{A})^\dagger = c^*\hat{A}^\dagger$, $(\hat{A} + \hat{B})^\dagger = \hat{A}^\dagger + \hat{B}^\dagger$, and $(\hat{A}\hat{B})^\dagger = \hat{B}^\dagger\hat{A}^\dagger$ (order reversal).

An operator $\hat{A}$ is **Hermitian** if $\hat{A}^\dagger = \hat{A}$, meaning:

$$\boxed{\langle\phi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{A}|\phi\rangle^* \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \int \phi^*(\mathbf{r})\,\hat{A}\psi(\mathbf{r})\,d^3r = \int (\hat{A}\phi(\mathbf{r}))^*\psi(\mathbf{r})\,d^3r}$$

*Direct verification that $\hat{p}_x = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ is Hermitian on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$:* Integrating by parts and using $\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\big|_{-\infty}^{+\infty} = 0$ for square-integrable states:

$$\langle\phi|\hat{p}_x|\psi\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi^*\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)dx = \underbrace{\left[-i\hbar\phi^*\psi\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}}_{=\,0} + i\hbar\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\partial\phi^*}{\partial x}\psi\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\right)^*\psi\,dx = \langle\hat{p}_x\phi|\psi\rangle$$

Hence $\hat{p}_x^\dagger = \hat{p}_x$. Similarly, since $x$ is real, $\hat{x}^\dagger = \hat{x}$, and for real $V(x)$ the Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + V(\hat{x})$ is strictly Hermitian.

##### Theorem 1: Eigenvalues of a Hermitian Operator Are Strictly Real

*Proof:* Let $\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$ and let $|\psi_n\rangle \neq 0$ be an eigenket with eigenvalue $a_n$: $\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n|\psi_n\rangle$. Taking the inner product from the left with $\langle\psi_n|$:

$$\langle\psi_n|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle \tag{8}$$

Now take the complex conjugate of Equation $(8)$ and apply Hermiticity $\langle\psi_n|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle^* = \langle\psi_n|\hat{A}^\dagger|\psi_n\rangle = \langle\psi_n|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle$:

$$\langle\psi_n|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n^*\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle \tag{9}$$

Subtracting Equation $(9)$ from Equation $(8)$:

$$(a_n - a_n^*)\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle = 0$$

Since $|\psi_n\rangle$ is a non-null state, $\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle > 0$, forcing $a_n = a_n^* \in \mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$

*Physical significance:* Every laboratory instrument reads a real number, which is why every physical observable must be represented by a Hermitian operator.

##### Theorem 2: Eigenstates Belonging to Distinct Eigenvalues Are Mutually Orthogonal

*Proof:* Let $\hat{A}|\psi_m\rangle = a_m|\psi_m\rangle$ and $\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n|\psi_n\rangle$ with $a_m \neq a_n$. Take the inner product of the second equation with $\langle\psi_m|$:

$$\langle\psi_m|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_n\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle \tag{10}$$

Now apply Hermiticity on the left ($\langle\psi_m|\hat{A} = (\hat{A}|\psi_m\rangle)^\dagger = (a_m|\psi_m\rangle)^\dagger = a_m^*\langle\psi_m| = a_m\langle\psi_m|$, using Theorem 1 that $a_m$ is real):

$$\langle\psi_m|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = a_m\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle \tag{11}$$

Subtracting Equation $(11)$ from Equation $(10)$:

$$(a_n - a_m)\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = 0$$

Because $a_m \neq a_n$, it follows strictly that $\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = 0$. $\blacksquare$

*(Degeneracy note: If two or more linearly independent eigenstates share the same degenerate eigenvalue $a_m = a_n$, they are not automatically orthogonal, but any linear combination of them remains an eigenstate with eigenvalue $a_n$—allowing the **Gram–Schmidt procedure** to construct an orthonormal basis spanning the degenerate subspace.)*

##### Theorem 3: Hermiticity of Operator Products and Commutators

Let $\hat{A} = \hat{A}^\dagger$ and $\hat{B} = \hat{B}^\dagger$. Then:
1. $(\hat{A}\hat{B})^\dagger = \hat{B}^\dagger\hat{A}^\dagger = \hat{B}\hat{A}$. Thus **the product $\hat{A}\hat{B}$ is Hermitian if and only if $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$**!
2. The **symmetrized anticommutator** $\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{A}, \hat{B}\} = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{A}\hat{B} + \hat{B}\hat{A})$ is **always Hermitian**: $\frac{1}{2}(\hat{A}\hat{B} + \hat{B}\hat{A})^\dagger = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{B}\hat{A} + \hat{A}\hat{B})$.
3. The **commutator** $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = \hat{A}\hat{B} - \hat{B}\hat{A}$ is **anti-Hermitian** ($[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]^\dagger = -[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]$), whereas **$i[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]$ is always Hermitian**: $(i[\hat{A}, \hat{B}])^\dagger = (-i)(-[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]) = +i[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]$.

### 4.5 Expectation Values, Commutator Algebra and Simultaneous Eigenstates

#### Expectation Values and Variance

The **expectation value** of an observable $\hat{A}$ in a normalized state $|\psi\rangle$ is the statistical average of infinitely many measurements performed on an identically prepared ensemble:

$$\boxed{\langle\hat{A}\rangle \equiv \langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle = \int_{\text{all space}} \psi^*(\mathbf{r})\,\hat{A}\psi(\mathbf{r})\,d^3r}$$

*(If $|\psi\rangle$ is not yet normalized, always divide by the norm: $\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \frac{\langle\psi|\hat{A}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}$.)*

Inserting the spectral completeness resolution $\sum_n |n\rangle\langle n| = \hat{I}$ of $\hat{A}$'s orthonormal eigenstates gives:

$$\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \sum_{n} \langle\psi|\hat{A}|n\rangle\langle n|\psi\rangle = \sum_{n} a_n |\langle n|\psi\rangle|^2 = \sum_{n} a_n P(a_n)$$

confirming agreement with standard probability theory. The **variance** (squared uncertainty) of $\hat{A}$ is:

$$(\Delta A)^2 \equiv \langle(\hat{A} - \langle\hat{A}\rangle\hat{I})^2\rangle = \langle\hat{A}^2 - 2\langle\hat{A}\rangle\hat{A} + \langle\hat{A}\rangle^2\hat{I}\rangle = \langle\hat{A}^2\rangle - \langle\hat{A}\rangle^2$$

Notice that $\Delta A = 0 \iff (\hat{A} - \langle\hat{A}\rangle\hat{I})|\psi\rangle = 0 \iff |\psi\rangle$ is an exact eigenstate of $\hat{A}$!

#### Canonical Commutator $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}_x] = i\hbar$ and Essential Identities

The **commutator** of two operators is $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] \equiv \hat{A}\hat{B} - \hat{B}\hat{A}$. To evaluate any differential-operator commutator without dropping derivatives, **always act on an arbitrary smooth test function $f(x)$**:

$$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}_x]f(x) = x\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) - \left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\left(x f(x)\right) = -i\hbar x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + i\hbar\left(f(x) + x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = i\hbar\,f(x)$$

Stripping the arbitrary test function $f(x)$ establishes the **fundamental canonical commutation relations** (in 3D, for $j, k \in \{x, y, z\}$):

$$\boxed{[\hat{r}_j, \hat{p}_k] = i\hbar\delta_{jk}, \qquad [\hat{r}_j, \hat{r}_k] = 0, \qquad [\hat{p}_j, \hat{p}_k] = 0}$$

Key algebraic commutator identities used throughout quantum mechanics:
1. **Antisymmetry:** $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = -[\hat{B}, \hat{A}]$, and $[\hat{A}, \hat{A}] = [\hat{A}, f(\hat{A})] = 0$.
2. **Leibniz product rules:**
   $$[\hat{A}, \hat{B}\hat{C}] = [\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\hat{C} + \hat{B}[\hat{A}, \hat{C}], \qquad [\hat{A}\hat{B}, \hat{C}] = \hat{A}[\hat{B}, \hat{C}] + [\hat{A}, \hat{C}]\hat{B}$$
3. **Jacobi identity:** $[\hat{A}, [\hat{B}, \hat{C}]] + [\hat{B}, [\hat{C}, \hat{A}]] + [\hat{C}, [\hat{A}, \hat{B}]] = 0$.
4. **Function-differentiation rules (valid for any differentiable function):**
   $$[\hat{p}_x, g(\hat{x})] = -i\hbar\frac{dg(\hat{x})}{dx}, \qquad [\hat{x}, f(\hat{p}_x)] = i\hbar\frac{df(\hat{p}_x)}{dp_x}$$
   *(Proof of first via test function $\psi$: $[\hat{p}_x, g]\psi = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(g\psi) + i\hbar g\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = -i\hbar\frac{dg}{dx}\psi$.)*

#### Compatible Observables Theorem (Simultaneous Eigenstates)

**Theorem:** Two Hermitian operators $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ possess a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates if and only if they commute ($[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$).

- *Necessity ($\Rightarrow$):* Suppose $\{|n\rangle\}$ is a complete basis of simultaneous eigenstates satisfying $\hat{A}|n\rangle = a_n|n\rangle$ and $\hat{B}|n\rangle = b_n|n\rangle$. Then for every basis ket $|n\rangle$:
  $$[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]|n\rangle = \hat{A}\hat{B}|n\rangle - \hat{B}\hat{A}|n\rangle = \hat{A}(b_n|n\rangle) - \hat{B}(a_n|n\rangle) = (a_n b_n - b_n a_n)|n\rangle = 0$$
  Since any state $|\psi\rangle = \sum_n c_n |n\rangle$ is a linear combination of $|n\rangle$, $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]|\psi\rangle = 0$ identically, so $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$.
- *Sufficiency ($\Leftarrow$, non-degenerate case):* Suppose $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$ and $\hat{A}|n\rangle = a_n|n\rangle$ with non-degenerate eigenvalues $a_n$. Applying $\hat{A}$ to the ket $(\hat{B}|n\rangle)$:
  $$\hat{A}(\hat{B}|n\rangle) = \hat{B}\hat{A}|n\rangle = \hat{B}(a_n|n\rangle) = a_n(\hat{B}|n\rangle)$$
  Thus $\hat{B}|n\rangle$ is also an eigenket of $\hat{A}$ with the exact same eigenvalue $a_n$. Because $a_n$ is non-degenerate, $\hat{B}|n\rangle$ must be a scalar multiple of $|n\rangle$: $\hat{B}|n\rangle = b_n|n\rangle$. *(In a $g$-fold degenerate subspace of $\hat{A}$, $\hat{B}$ leaves the subspace invariant, and diagonalizing the $g \times g$ Hermitian restriction matrix of $\hat{B}$ inside that subspace produces a simultaneous eigenbasis of both $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$.)* $\blacksquare$

### 4.6 Generalized (Robertson–Schrödinger) Uncertainty Relation

Armed with Dirac algebra, we can now prove Heisenberg's uncertainty principle rigorously for *any* two Hermitian observables $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ in *any* normalized state $|\psi\rangle$—with zero appeal to thought experiments or Gaussian shapes.

#### Step 1: Proof of the Hilbert-Space Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality

For any two kets $|f\rangle, |g\rangle \in \mathcal{H}$ and any complex scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, positive-definiteness of the inner product requires:

$$\langle f + \lambda g | f + \lambda g \rangle = \langle f|f\rangle + \lambda\langle f|g\rangle + \lambda^*\langle g|f\rangle + |\lambda|^2\langle g|g\rangle \ge 0$$

Assuming $|g\rangle \neq 0$ (if $|g\rangle = 0$, the inequality holds trivially as $0 \ge 0$), choose $\lambda = -\frac{\langle g|f\rangle}{\langle g|g\rangle}$:

$$\langle f|f\rangle - \frac{|\langle f|g\rangle|^2}{\langle g|g\rangle} - \frac{|\langle f|g\rangle|^2}{\langle g|g\rangle} + \frac{|\langle f|g\rangle|^2}{\langle g|g\rangle} \ge 0 \implies \boxed{\langle f|f\rangle\langle g|g\rangle \ge |\langle f|g\rangle|^2}$$

Equality holds if and only if $|f + \lambda g\rangle = 0$, i.e., $|f\rangle$ and $|g\rangle$ are linearly dependent ($|g\rangle = c|f\rangle$).

#### Step 2: Operator Construction and Real/Imaginary Decomposition

Define the shifted zero-mean Hermitian operators $\Delta\hat{A} \equiv \hat{A} - \langle\hat{A}\rangle\hat{I}$ and $\Delta\hat{B} \equiv \hat{B} - \langle\hat{B}\rangle\hat{I}$. Notice that $(\Delta\hat{A})^\dagger = \Delta\hat{A}$, $(\Delta\hat{B})^\dagger = \Delta\hat{B}$, and because scalars commute with all operators, $[\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}] = [\hat{A}, \hat{B}]$.

Now define the two kets:

$$|f\rangle \equiv \Delta\hat{A}|\psi\rangle, \qquad |g\rangle \equiv \Delta\hat{B}|\psi\rangle$$

Using Hermiticity of $\Delta\hat{A}$ and $\Delta\hat{B}$, their squared norms are the exact variances:

$$\langle f|f\rangle = \langle\psi|(\Delta\hat{A})^2|\psi\rangle = (\Delta A)^2, \qquad \langle g|g\rangle = \langle\psi|(\Delta\hat{B})^2|\psi\rangle = (\Delta B)^2$$

Inserting $|f\rangle$ and $|g\rangle$ into the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives:

$$(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 \ge |\langle\psi|\Delta\hat{A}\Delta\hat{B}|\psi\rangle|^2 \tag{12}$$

Decompose the operator product $\Delta\hat{A}\Delta\hat{B}$ identically into its symmetric (Hermitian) and antisymmetric (anti-Hermitian) parts:

$$\Delta\hat{A}\Delta\hat{B} = \frac{1}{2}\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\} + \frac{1}{2}[\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}] = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\}}_{\text{Hermitian } \hat{F}} + i\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2i}[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\right)}_{\text{Hermitian } \hat{G}}$$

Because both $\hat{F} = \frac{1}{2}\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\}$ and $\hat{G} = \frac{1}{2i}[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]$ are strictly Hermitian, their expectation values $\langle\hat{F}\rangle$ and $\langle\hat{G}\rangle$ are **purely real** numbers. Therefore, the squared modulus of the complex number $\langle\psi|\Delta\hat{A}\Delta\hat{B}|\psi\rangle = \langle\hat{F}\rangle + i\langle\hat{G}\rangle$ splits cleanly into the sum of two non-negative real squares:

$$|\langle\psi|\Delta\hat{A}\Delta\hat{B}|\psi\rangle|^2 = \langle\hat{F}\rangle^2 + \langle\hat{G}\rangle^2 = \frac{1}{4}\langle\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\}\rangle^2 + \frac{1}{4}\left|\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle\right|^2$$

Substituting into Equation $(12)$ gives the full **Schrödinger Uncertainty Relation**:

$$(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 \ge \frac{1}{4}\langle\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\}\rangle^2 + \frac{1}{4}\left|\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle\right|^2$$

Dropping the non-negative covariance term $\frac{1}{4}\langle\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\}\rangle^2 \ge 0$ and taking the positive square root produces the **Generalized (Robertson) Uncertainty Relation**:

$$\boxed{\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \frac{1}{2}\left|\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{B}]\rangle\right|}$$

#### Sanity Checks and Exact Condition for Minimum-Uncertainty States

1. **Position–Momentum check:** Setting $\hat{A} = \hat{x}$ and $\hat{B} = \hat{p}_x$ with $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}_x] = i\hbar$ yields:
   $$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \ge \frac{1}{2}|\langle i\hbar\rangle| = \frac{\hbar}{2}$$
2. **Minimum-uncertainty states (proving Gaussians are unique!):** Equality in Robertson's bound requires *both* (i) Cauchy–Schwarz saturation $|g\rangle = c|f\rangle$ and (ii) vanishing covariance $\langle\{\Delta\hat{A}, \Delta\hat{B}\}\rangle = 0$. Since $\langle f|g\rangle + \langle g|f\rangle = (c + c^*)\langle f|f\rangle = 0$, the constant $c$ must be purely imaginary: $c = -i\gamma$ with $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$. For $\hat{A} = \hat{x}$ and $\hat{B} = \hat{p}_x$, $(\hat{p}_x - \langle p\rangle)\psi = -i\gamma(\hat{x} - \langle x\rangle)\psi$ becomes the first-order ODE:
   $$-i\hbar\frac{d\psi}{dx} - \langle p\rangle\psi = -i\gamma(x - \langle x\rangle)\psi \implies \frac{d\psi}{\psi} = \left[\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle p\rangle - \frac{\gamma}{\hbar}(x - \langle x\rangle)\right]dx$$
   Integrating gives $\psi(x) = N\exp\left[-\frac{\gamma}{2\hbar}(x - \langle x\rangle)^2 + \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle p\rangle x\right]$ (normalizable for $\gamma > 0$), proving that **only Gaussian wave packets saturate $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x = \hbar/2$**!

### 4.7 The Parity Operator $\hat{\Pi}$

Spatial reflection symmetry simplifies integrals, predicts selection rules, and classifies bound states across atomic and nuclear physics. In quantum mechanics, spatial inversion through the origin is represented by the **parity operator** $\hat{\Pi}$:

$$\hat{\Pi}\psi(\mathbf{r}) \equiv \psi(-\mathbf{r}) \qquad (\text{1D: } \hat{\Pi}\psi(x) = \psi(-x))$$

#### Fundamental Properties (Unabridged Proofs)

1. **Involutory property and eigenvalues ($\pm 1$):** Applying $\hat{\Pi}$ twice restores the original coordinate: $\hat{\Pi}^2\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \hat{\Pi}\psi(-\mathbf{r}) = \psi(\mathbf{r})$, so $\hat{\Pi}^2 = \hat{I}$. If $\hat{\Pi}\psi = \pi\psi$, then $\hat{\Pi}^2\psi = \pi^2\psi = \psi \implies \pi^2 = 1 \implies \boxed{\pi = +1 \text{ (even)} \;\text{ or }\; \pi = -1 \text{ (odd)}}$.
2. **Hermiticity and Unitarity:** Changing integration variable $\mathbf{r}' = -\mathbf{r}$ ($d^3r' = d^3r$, limits cover all $\mathbb{R}^3$):
   $$\langle\phi|\hat{\Pi}|\psi\rangle = \int \phi^*(\mathbf{r})\psi(-\mathbf{r})\,d^3r = \int \phi^*(-\mathbf{r}')\psi(\mathbf{r}')\,d^3r' = \int (\hat{\Pi}\phi(\mathbf{r}'))^*\psi(\mathbf{r}')\,d^3r' = \langle\hat{\Pi}\phi|\psi\rangle$$
   Thus $\hat{\Pi}^\dagger = \hat{\Pi}$ (Hermitian), and combined with $\hat{\Pi}^2 = \hat{I}$ gives $\hat{\Pi}^\dagger\hat{\Pi} = \hat{I}$ (Unitary).
3. **Anticommutation with $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}_x$ (odd-parity operators):** Acting on any test function $f(x)$:
   $$(\hat{\Pi}\hat{x})f(x) = \hat{\Pi}[x f(x)] = -x f(-x) = -\hat{x}\hat{\Pi}f(x) \implies \{\hat{\Pi}, \hat{x}\} = 0 \iff \hat{\Pi}\hat{x}\hat{\Pi} = -\hat{x}$$
   Similarly, using $\frac{d}{dx}f(-x) = -f'(-x)$:
   $$(\hat{\Pi}\hat{p}_x)f(x) = \hat{\Pi}\left[-i\hbar f'(x)\right] = -i\hbar f'(-x) = +i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}f(-x) = -\hat{p}_x\hat{\Pi}f(x) \implies \{\hat{\Pi}, \hat{p}_x\} = 0 \iff \hat{\Pi}\hat{p}_x\hat{\Pi} = -\hat{p}_x$$
4. **Commutation with $\hat{H}$ for symmetric potentials & zero-expectation shortcut:** Because $\hat{\Pi}\hat{p}_x^2\hat{\Pi} = (\hat{\Pi}\hat{p}_x\hat{\Pi})(\hat{\Pi}\hat{p}_x\hat{\Pi}) = (-\hat{p}_x)(-\hat{p}_x) = +\hat{p}_x^2$, kinetic energy is always even under parity. Whenever the potential is symmetric ($V(-\mathbf{r}) = V(\mathbf{r})$), we have $[\hat{\Pi}, \hat{H}] = 0$: **non-degenerate stationary states of any symmetric potential must possess definite parity (either strictly even or strictly odd)**. Moreover, for *any* definite-parity state $\hat{\Pi}|\psi\rangle = \pi|\psi\rangle$ ($\pi = \pm 1$):
   $$\langle\psi|\hat{x}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat{\Pi}^\dagger\hat{\Pi}\hat{x}\hat{\Pi}\hat{\Pi}|\psi\rangle = \pi^2\langle\psi|(-\hat{x})|\psi\rangle = -\langle\psi|\hat{x}|\psi\rangle \implies \boxed{\langle\hat{x}\rangle = 0, \quad \langle\hat{p}_x\rangle = 0}$$
   This symmetry identity eliminates tedious integrations instantly in exam calculations.

### 4.8 Time Evolution of Expectation Values and Ehrenfest's Theorem

How does the classical deterministic world of Newton's laws emerge from a probabilistic wave equation? Paul Ehrenfest (1927) answered this question by proving that the **expectation values** of position and momentum obey equations of identical formal structure to Hamilton's and Newton's equations of classical mechanics.

#### Step 1: General Time-Derivative Formula for Any Observable $\hat{A}(t)$

Consider a normalized state $|\psi(t)\rangle$ evolving under the TDSE with a Hermitian Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \hat{H}^\dagger$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\hat{H}|\psi(t)\rangle, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi(t)| = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\psi(t)|\hat{H}^\dagger = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\psi(t)|\hat{H}$$

Differentiating the expectation value $\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \langle\psi(t)|\hat{A}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle$ via the product rule:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \left(\frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi|\right)\hat{A}|\psi\rangle + \langle\psi|\frac{\partial\hat{A}}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle + \langle\psi|\hat{A}\left(\frac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle\right)$$

Substituting the TDSE and its bra conjugate:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\psi|\hat{H}\hat{A}|\psi\rangle + \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\psi|\hat{A}\hat{H}|\psi\rangle + \left\langle\frac{\partial\hat{A}}{\partial t}\right\rangle$$

Combining the first two terms yields the **Generalized Ehrenfest Equation**:

$$\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle[\hat{A}, \hat{H}]\rangle + \left\langle\frac{\partial\hat{A}}{\partial t}\right\rangle}$$

**Quantum Conservation Law (Immediate Corollary):** Any observable $\hat{A}$ with no explicit time dependence ($\frac{\partial\hat{A}}{\partial t} = 0$) that commutes with the Hamiltonian ($[\hat{A}, \hat{H}] = 0$) has $\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle = 0$—it is a **constant of the motion** (e.g., $\hat{H}$ itself when $V$ is static, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ for a free particle, $\hat{\mathbf{L}}$ for a central potential). Furthermore, in any stationary eigenstate $|\psi_n\rangle$, $\langle\psi_n|[\hat{A}, \hat{H}]|\psi_n\rangle = (E_n - E_n)\langle\psi_n|\hat{A}|\psi_n\rangle = 0$, so expectation values of all time-independent observables are strictly stationary!

#### Step 2: Derivation of Ehrenfest's Two Canonical Equations

Take a 1D particle moving in a static potential $V(\hat{x})$ with Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}_x^2}{2m} + V(\hat{x})$. Neither $\hat{x}$ nor $\hat{p}_x$ depends explicitly on $t$.

1. **First Ehrenfest Equation ($\hat{A} = \hat{x}$):**
   Since $[\hat{x}, V(\hat{x})] = 0$, evaluate $[\hat{x}, \hat{H}]$ using the Leibniz product rule:
   $$[\hat{x}, \hat{H}] = \frac{1}{2m}[\hat{x}, \hat{p}_x^2] = \frac{1}{2m}\left([\hat{x}, \hat{p}_x]\hat{p}_x + \hat{p}_x[\hat{x}, \hat{p}_x]\right) = \frac{1}{2m}(i\hbar\hat{p}_x + i\hbar\hat{p}_x) = \frac{i\hbar}{m}\hat{p}_x$$
   Inserting into the Generalized Ehrenfest Equation:
   $$\frac{d\langle\hat{x}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\left\langle\frac{i\hbar}{m}\hat{p}_x\right\rangle \implies \boxed{\frac{d\langle\hat{x}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{\langle\hat{p}_x\rangle}{m}}$$

2. **Second Ehrenfest Equation ($\hat{A} = \hat{p}_x$):**
   Since $[\hat{p}_x, \hat{p}_x^2] = 0$, evaluate $[\hat{p}_x, V(\hat{x})]$ using a test function $\psi(x)$:
   $$[\hat{p}_x, V(\hat{x})]\psi(x) = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[V(x)\psi(x)\right] - V(x)\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right) = -i\hbar\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\psi(x)$$
   Therefore $[\hat{p}_x, \hat{H}] = -i\hbar\frac{\partial V(\hat{x})}{\partial x}$, and substituting into the Generalized Ehrenfest Equation gives:
   $$\frac{d\langle\hat{p}_x\rangle}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\left\langle -i\hbar\frac{\partial V(\hat{x})}{\partial x}\right\rangle \implies \boxed{\frac{d\langle\hat{p}_x\rangle}{dt} = \left\langle -\frac{\partial V(\hat{x})}{\partial x}\right\rangle = \langle F(\hat{x})\rangle}$$

*(In 3D, the identical steps yield $\frac{d\langle\hat{\mathbf{r}}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{\langle\hat{\mathbf{p}}\rangle}{m}$ and $\frac{d\langle\hat{\mathbf{p}}\rangle}{dt} = \langle -\nabla V(\hat{\mathbf{r}})\rangle$.)*

#### Critical Subtlety: When Does Ehrenfest Truly Recover Classical Newton's Law?

Combining both equations gives $m\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2} = \langle F(x)\rangle$, which is **not** identical to Newton's second law $m\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2} = F(\langle x\rangle)$ because in general $\langle F(x)\rangle \neq F(\langle x\rangle)$! Expanding $F(x) = -V'(x)$ in a Taylor series around the packet centroid $x_0 = \langle x\rangle$ and taking the expectation value (noting $\langle x - \langle x\rangle\rangle = 0$):

$$\langle F(x)\rangle = F(\langle x\rangle) + \frac{1}{2}F''(\langle x\rangle)(\Delta x)^2 + \frac{1}{6}F'''(\langle x\rangle)\langle(x - \langle x\rangle)^3\rangle + \cdots$$

- **Exact classical motion:** Holds for *any* wave packet only when $F''(x) = -V'''(x) = 0$, i.e., when $V(x)$ is at most quadratic in $x$ (**free particle**, **uniform gravitational/electric field**, and **linear harmonic oscillator**).
- **Approximate classical limit:** For anharmonic potentials ($V''' \neq 0$), the centroid follows Newton's trajectory only while the wave packet width $\Delta x$ stays much smaller than the characteristic distance over which the force $F(x)$ varies appreciably. Once dispersive spreading makes $\Delta x$ comparable to the potential's length scale, quantum interference destroys classical trajectories!

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Normalization, Parity Shortcuts, Expectation Values and Involutory Hermitian Operators
> **Problem:** (a) A 1D particle is in the state $\psi(x) = N x \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2a^2}\right)$ ($a > 0$) for $-\infty < x < \infty$. Find the real positive normalization constant $N$, evaluate $\langle\hat{x}\rangle$, $\langle\hat{p}\rangle$, $\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle$, and $\langle\hat{p}^2\rangle$, and verify $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$.
> (b) Evaluate the commutator $[\hat{x}^2, \hat{p}^2]$, and prove that if a Hermitian operator $\hat{A}$ satisfies $\hat{A}^2 = \hat{I}$, its only eigenvalues are $\pm 1$ and $\hat{P}_\pm = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{I} \pm \hat{A})$ are orthogonal projection operators.
>
> **Solution:**
> **(a) Normalization and expectation values:**
> Standard Gaussian integrals ($\alpha > 0$): $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}$, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha^3}}$, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^4 e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx = \frac{3}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha^5}}$.
>
> 1. *Normalization* (here $\alpha = 1/a^2$):
>    $$1 = N^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-x^2/a^2}\,dx = N^2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}a^3 \implies \boxed{N = \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}\,a^3}\right)^{1/2}}$$
> 2. *$\langle\hat{x}\rangle$ and $\langle\hat{p}\rangle$ via Parity Shortcut:* Since $\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)$, $\psi(x)$ is strictly odd ($\pi = -1$). Because $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$ are odd-parity operators:
>    $$\boxed{\langle\hat{x}\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle\hat{p}\rangle = 0}$$
> 3. *$\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle$:*
>    $$\langle\hat{x}^2\rangle = N^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^4 e^{-x^2/a^2}\,dx = \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}\,a^3}\right)\left(\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{4}a^5\right) = \boxed{\frac{3}{2}a^2}$$
> 4. *$\langle\hat{p}^2\rangle$ via Hermitian integration by parts* ($\langle\psi|\hat{p}^2|\psi\rangle = \langle\hat{p}\psi|\hat{p}\psi\rangle = \hbar^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi'(x)|^2\,dx$):
>    $$\psi'(x) = N\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)e^{-x^2/(2a^2)}$$
>    $$\langle\hat{p}^2\rangle = \hbar^2 N^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1 - \frac{2x^2}{a^2} + \frac{x^4}{a^4}\right)e^{-x^2/a^2}\,dx$$
>    $$= \hbar^2\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}\,a^3}\right)\left[a\sqrt{\pi} - \frac{2}{a^2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}a^3\right) + \frac{1}{a^4}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{4}a^5\right)\right] = \frac{2\hbar^2}{a^2}\left(1 - 1 + \frac{3}{4}\right) = \boxed{\frac{3\hbar^2}{2a^2}}$$
> 5. *Uncertainty check:* Since $\langle x\rangle = \langle p\rangle = 0$, $\Delta x = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,a$ and $\Delta p = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,\frac{\hbar}{a}$, giving:
>    $$\Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{3}{2}\hbar > \frac{\hbar}{2} \quad \checkmark$$
>    *(Notice $\psi(x)$ is the $n = 1$ linear harmonic oscillator eigenstate with $a = \sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$, whose exact product is $(n + 1/2)\hbar = \frac{3}{2}\hbar$!)*
>
> **(b) Commutator and involutory Hermitian operator proof:**
> 1. *Commutator $[\hat{x}^2, \hat{p}^2]$:* Apply Leibniz rules repeatedly using $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$ and $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}^2] = 2i\hbar\hat{p}$:
>    $$[\hat{x}^2, \hat{p}^2] = \hat{x}[\hat{x}, \hat{p}^2] + [\hat{x}, \hat{p}^2]\hat{x} = \hat{x}(2i\hbar\hat{p}) + (2i\hbar\hat{p})\hat{x} = \boxed{2i\hbar(\hat{x}\hat{p} + \hat{p}\hat{x})}$$
> 2. *Eigenvalues and projectors of $\hat{A}^2 = \hat{I}$:* Let $\hat{A}|\psi\rangle = \lambda|\psi\rangle$ ($|\psi\rangle \neq 0$). Applying $\hat{A}$ again: $\hat{A}^2|\psi\rangle = \lambda^2|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle \implies (\lambda^2 - 1)|\psi\rangle = 0 \implies \lambda = \pm 1$. Defining $\hat{P}_\pm = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{I} \pm \hat{A})$:
>    - *Hermitian:* $\hat{P}_\pm^\dagger = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{I} \pm \hat{A}^\dagger) = \hat{P}_\pm$.
>    - *Idempotent:* $\hat{P}_\pm^2 = \frac{1}{4}(\hat{I} \pm 2\hat{A} + \hat{A}^2) = \frac{1}{4}(2\hat{I} \pm 2\hat{A}) = \hat{P}_\pm$.
>    - *Orthogonal & complete:* $\hat{P}_+\hat{P}_- = \frac{1}{4}(\hat{I} - \hat{A}^2) = 0$, and $\hat{P}_+ + \hat{P}_- = \hat{I}$. $\blacksquare$

## 5. High-Yield Traps and Exam-Hall Diagram Blueprints

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> - **Sign of $\hat{p}$, $\hat{E}$, and $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}]$:** Always verify from $\psi \sim e^{i(px - Et)/\hbar}$ that $\hat{p} = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ (minus!), $\hat{E} = +i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}$ (plus!), and $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = +i\hbar$ (never $-i\hbar$ and never real $\hbar$). A single flipped sign in $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}]$ wrecks Ehrenfest's theorem and all ladder-operator algebra.
> - **Dropping the Test Function in Differential Commutators:** Writing $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = -i\hbar x\frac{d}{dx} + i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}x$ without acting on $f(x)$ causes candidates to forget the product-rule term $x f'(x)$ and miscalculate $[\hat{p}, V(x)]$. Always introduce $f(x)$ explicitly and strip it only on the final line.
> - **Non-Relativistic Trap for Confined Electrons & MeV Beams:** Whenever kinetic energy $K$ or confinement momentum $pc \gtrsim 0.05\,m_0 c^2$ (for electrons, $K \gtrsim 25\text{ keV}$ or nuclear $pc \sim 20\text{ MeV} \gg 0.511\text{ MeV}$), non-relativistic $K = p^2/2m$ fails catastrophically (giving $380\text{ MeV}$ instead of $19.2\text{ MeV}$ in nuclear confinement and a $+72\%$ error at $2\text{ MeV}$!). Always check $K/m_0 c^2$ before selecting $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2mK}$ vs. $\lambda = hc/\sqrt{K(K + 2m_0c^2)}$.
> - **Gaussian Width Definition Confusion ($\Delta x_0$ vs. $a$):** Some textbooks write $\Psi(x,0) \propto e^{-x^2/(2a^2)}$ (where $\Delta x_0 = a/\sqrt{2}$ and $\tau = ma^2/\hbar$), whereas writing $e^{-x^2/(4\Delta x_0^2)}$ puts the true standard deviation $\Delta x_0$ directly in the exponent with $\tau = 2m\Delta x_0^2/\hbar$. Always prove $\langle x^2\rangle_0 = \Delta x_0^2$ explicitly in your script so your prefactors remain 100% consistent.
> - **Ehrenfest Classical-Limit Overstatement:** Never write "Ehrenfest's theorem proves $\langle x\rangle$ obeys Newton's second law for all potentials." Explicitly state the crucial distinction between $\langle F(x)\rangle$ and $F(\langle x\rangle)$, and note exact equivalence holds only for potentials at most quadratic in $x$.
> - **Constants & Unit-Conversion Shortcuts to Lock Down:**
>   - $1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$; $1\text{ \AA} = 10^{-10}\text{ m}$; $1\text{ fm} = 10^{-15}\text{ m}$.
>   - $hc = 1239.84\text{ eV}\cdot\text{nm} = 12.3984\text{ keV}\cdot\text{\AA} = 1239.84\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm}$.
>   - $\hbar c = 197.33\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm} = 197.33\text{ eV}\cdot\text{nm}$.
>   - Do not mix $h$ and $\hbar$ ($h = 2\pi\hbar$): Compton wavelength is $\lambda_C = \frac{h}{m_e c} = 0.02426\text{ \AA}$, while reduced Compton wavelength is $\bar{\lambda}_C = \frac{\hbar}{m_e c} = 386.2\text{ fm}$.

### 2. Examiner Step-Marking & Numerical Trap Signature

- **Skipping the Hermiticity-to-Completeness Bridge in Abstract Operator Proofs:** On operator proofs such as $\hat{A}^3 = \hat{I} \implies \hat{A} = \hat{I}$, proving $\lambda = 1$ is the only real root of $\lambda^3 = 1$ earns only half marks; capturing full marks requires explicitly stating that a Hermitian operator's eigenstates form a complete basis so $\hat{A}|\psi\rangle = \sum c_n \hat{A}|n\rangle = \sum c_n (1)|n\rangle = |\psi\rangle$ for *every* ket $|\psi\rangle$.
- **Unjustified Substitution in Ehrenfest's Theorem:** Examiners dock step-marks when candidates jump straight from $\frac{d\langle\hat{p}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle[\hat{p}, \hat{H}]\rangle$ to $-\langle V'\rangle$ without demonstrating $[\hat{p}, V(x)]\psi = -i\hbar V'(x)\psi$ explicitly via a test function, or when they forget to state that $\hat{H}$ must be Hermitian to write $\frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi| = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\psi|\hat{H}$.
- **Relativistic vs. Non-Relativistic Blind Spots:** In both relativistic de Broglie calculations ($2\text{ MeV}$ electrons) and nuclear-electron confinement problems, failing to include the $K^2$ term inside $pc = \sqrt{K(K + 2m_0c^2)}$ produces answers off by $70\%$ to $2000\%$.
- **Missing Real-Potential Assumption in the Continuity Equation:** Deriving $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ without explicitly stating "$V(\mathbf{r},t)$ is real, so $V^* = V$ cancels" misses a key conceptual check that distinguishes a textbook script from a rigorous Mains script.

### 3. Unseen Conceptual & Analytical Twists

1. **Complex Optical Potential & Decay Law:** Suppose a particle moves in a static complex potential $V(\mathbf{r}) = V_0(\mathbf{r}) - i\Gamma/2$, where $V_0(\mathbf{r})$ is real and $\Gamma > 0$ is a real positive constant. Modify the probability continuity derivation to prove that the total survival probability decays exponentially in time as $P(t) = P(0)\,e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$, and relate the mean lifetime $\tau = \hbar/\Gamma$ back to the energy–time uncertainty relation.
2. **Ehrenfest Failure in Anharmonic Potentials & Minimum-Uncertainty Evolution:** Using the generalized Ehrenfest equation, compute $\frac{d}{dt}(\Delta x)^2$ for an arbitrary free-particle wave packet ($\hat{H} = \hat{p}^2/2m$) and prove that $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}(\Delta x)^2 = \frac{2(\Delta p)^2}{m^2}$ is a strict positive constant—showing *purely algebraically* (without performing a single Fourier integral!) that *every* free-particle wave packet spreads quadratically in variance as $(\Delta x(t))^2 = (\Delta x_0)^2 + \frac{(\Delta p_0)^2}{m^2}t^2$ whenever $\langle\{\Delta\hat{x}, \Delta\hat{p}\}\rangle_0 = 0$.
