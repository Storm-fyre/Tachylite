# Chapter 3: Magnetic Properties of Solids — Quantum Dia-, Para- & Ferromagnetism

## 1. Microscopic Origins of Magnetism & Langevin Theory of Diamagnetism

Why does bismuth repel a magnet, oxygen cling to pole faces, iron lock permanently magnetized past $1000\text{ K}$, and magnetite stay strongly magnetic despite being an electrical insulator? Every macroscopic magnetic response in condensed matter originates from the orbital motion and intrinsic spin angular momentum of atomic electrons—governed strictly by **quantum mechanics**.

In **SI units**, the magnetic induction $\mathbf{B}$ (in Tesla, $\text{T}$), magnetic field intensity $\mathbf{H}$ ($\text{A m}^{-1}$), and volume magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ (magnetic dipole moment per unit volume, $\text{A m}^{-1}$) obey:

$$\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\left(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}\right), \qquad \mathbf{M} = \chi\mathbf{H}, \qquad \mu_r = \frac{B}{\mu_0 H} = 1 + \chi$$

where $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\text{ T m A}^{-1}$ and $\chi$ is the dimensionless **volume magnetic susceptibility** (molar susceptibility $\chi_m = \chi V_m$ carries units $\text{m}^3\text{ mol}^{-1}$).

### 1.1 Atomic Magnetic Dipoles & the Bohr–van Leeuwen Theorem

An atomic electron carries two distinct sources of angular momentum that generate magnetic dipole moments expressed in units of the **Bohr Magneton** $\mu_B \equiv \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = 9.2740 \times 10^{-24}\text{ A m}^2\text{ (or J T}^{-1})$:

1. **Orbital Magnetic Moment:** $\boldsymbol{\mu}_L = -\dfrac{e}{2m_e}\mathbf{L} = -g_L \mu_B \dfrac{\mathbf{L}}{\hbar}$ (orbital $g$-factor $g_L = 1$).
2. **Spin Magnetic Moment:** $\boldsymbol{\mu}_S = -g_S \dfrac{e}{2m_e}\mathbf{S} = -g_S \mu_B \dfrac{\mathbf{S}}{\hbar}$ (spin $g$-factor $g_S \approx 2.0023 \approx 2$).

In Russell–Saunders ($LS$) coupling, orbital and spin momenta combine into total angular momentum $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}$. Because $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ precess rapidly around $\mathbf{J}$, only the component of $\boldsymbol{\mu}_L + \boldsymbol{\mu}_S$ projected along $\mathbf{J}$ survives time-averaging:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_J = -g_J \mu_B \frac{\mathbf{J}}{\hbar}, \qquad \mu_{\text{eff}} = g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}\,\mu_B$$

where the **Landé $g$-factor** is:

$$\boxed{g_J = 1 + \frac{J(J+1) + S(S+1) - L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}}$$

*(Sanity checks: pure orbital $S = 0 \implies J = L \implies g_J = 1$; pure spin $L = 0 \implies J = S \implies g_J = 2$.)*

**Why Magnetism Is Purely Quantum Mechanical (Bohr–van Leeuwen Theorem):**
In classical statistical mechanics, coupling a charged particle to a magnetic vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ shifts the Hamiltonian to $H = \sum_i \frac{[\mathbf{p}_i + e\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_i)]^2}{2m} + V(\{\mathbf{r}_i\})$. Evaluating the classical canonical partition function $Z = \int e^{-\beta H}\,d^{3N}r\,d^{3N}p$ allows shifting the momentum integration variable to $\mathbf{p}_i' = \mathbf{p}_i + e\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_i)$ over $(-\infty, +\infty)$, eliminating $\mathbf{A}$ (and hence $\mathbf{B}$) **identically** from $Z$ and the free energy $F = -k_B T\ln Z$! Consequently, **$M_{\text{classical}} = -\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial F}{\partial B} \equiv 0$**. All forms of solid-state magnetism—diamagnetism, paramagnetism, and ferromagnetism—exist solely because quantum mechanics discretizes orbits, introduces spin, and enforces the Pauli Exclusion Principle.

### 1.2 Langevin Theory of Diamagnetism (Larmor Precession — Unabridged SI Derivation)

Consider an atom with completely filled electronic shells ($L = S = J = 0$), carrying zero permanent magnetic dipole moment (e.g., $\text{He, Ne, Ar, Na}^+, \text{Cl}^-$, covalent $\text{C}$). Applying an external magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ perturbs every orbiting electron's motion—by Lenz's law, the induced atomic current circulates in the direction that **opposes** the applied flux.

**Physical Setup & Derivation:**
Let an electron of charge $-e$ and mass $m_e$ move in a circular orbit of instantaneous radius $\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ in the plane perpendicular to an applied field $\mathbf{B} = B\hat{z}$ ramped slowly from $0$ to $B$.

**Step 1 — Larmor Precession Frequency $\omega_L$ (Via Faraday Induction / Centripetal Force):**
As $B(t)$ ramps up from $0$ to $B$, Faraday's law induces a tangential azimuthal electric field $E_\phi$ around the circular orbit of area $\pi\rho^2$:

$$\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \implies (2\pi\rho)E_\phi = -\pi\rho^2\frac{dB}{dt} \implies E_\phi = -\frac{\rho}{2}\frac{dB}{dt}$$

The tangential force $F_\phi = (-e)E_\phi = +\frac{e\rho}{2}\frac{dB}{dt}$ changes the electron's azimuthal velocity by $\Delta v_\phi$ over the ramp interval:

$$m_e\frac{dv_\phi}{dt} = \frac{e\rho}{2}\frac{dB}{dt} \implies \Delta v_\phi = \frac{e\rho B}{2 m_e}$$

Dividing by orbit radius $\rho$ yields an additional **uniform Larmor angular velocity** $\omega_L$ superimposed on *every* electron regardless of its original orbital radius or frequency:

$$\boxed{\boldsymbol{\omega}_L = +\frac{e\mathbf{B}}{2 m_e}}$$

*(Independent verification via centripetal balance: without $B$, $m_e\omega_0^2\rho = F_c$; with $B$, $m_e\omega^2\rho = F_c \pm e\omega\rho B \implies (\omega + \omega_0)(\omega - \omega_0) = \pm\frac{eB}{m_e}\omega$. For $\Delta\omega \ll \omega_0$, $2\omega_0\Delta\omega = \pm\frac{eB}{m_e}\omega_0 \implies \Delta\omega = \pm\frac{eB}{2m_e} = \pm\omega_L$. Exact!)*

**Step 2 — Induced Magnetic Moment per Electron:**
Because the electron carries **negative charge** $-e$, a right-handed Larmor precession $+\omega_L$ around $+\mathbf{B}$ produces a circular current in the **opposite** sense:

$$I_{\text{ind}} = (-e)\frac{\omega_L}{2\pi} = -\frac{e^2 B}{4\pi m_e}$$

Multiplying by the projected loop area $\pi\langle\rho^2\rangle$ yields the induced magnetic dipole moment of one electron along $\mathbf{B}$:

$$\Delta\mu_z = I_{\text{ind}}\cdot\left(\pi\langle\rho^2\rangle\right) = -\frac{e^2 B}{4 m_e}\langle\rho^2\rangle$$

**Step 3 — Spherical Averaging over the 3D Atomic Electron Cloud:**
For a spherically symmetric charge distribution around the nucleus, $\langle r^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle + \langle y^2\rangle + \langle z^2\rangle$ with $\langle x^2\rangle = \langle y^2\rangle = \langle z^2\rangle = \frac{1}{3}\langle r^2\rangle$. Hence the mean squared cylindrical radius perpendicular to $\hat{z}$ is:

$$\langle\rho^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle + \langle y^2\rangle = \frac{2}{3}\langle r^2\rangle$$

Substituting $\langle\rho^2\rangle = \frac{2}{3}\langle r^2\rangle$ gives:

$$\Delta\mu_z = -\frac{e^2 B}{6 m_e}\langle r^2\rangle$$

**Step 4 — Total Magnetization $M$ and Susceptibility $\chi_{\text{dia}}$:**
For a solid containing $n = N/V$ atoms per unit volume, each with $Z$ electrons of mean squared distance $\langle r^2\rangle$ from the nucleus:

$$M = n \sum_{i=1}^{Z} \Delta\mu_{z,i} = -\frac{n Z e^2 \langle r^2\rangle}{6 m_e}\,B$$

Because $|\chi_{\text{dia}}| \sim 10^{-5} \ll 1$, internal magnetization barely alters $B$, so $B = \mu_0(H + M) \approx \mu_0 H$. Dividing $M$ by $H$ gives the **Langevin Diamagnetic Susceptibility**:

$$\boxed{\chi_{\text{dia}} = \frac{M}{H} = -\frac{\mu_0 n Z e^2}{6 m_e}\langle r^2\rangle}$$

**Dimensional Check & Quantum Perturbation Verification:**
- *Units:* $\left[\frac{\mu_0 n e^2 r^2}{m_e}\right] = \frac{(\text{kg m C}^{-2})(\text{m}^{-3})(\text{C}^2)(\text{m}^2)}{\text{kg}} = 1$ (pure dimensionless!). Exact!
- *Quantum Exactness:* First-order quantum perturbation theory of the diamagnetic term $\hat{H}_{\text{dia}} = \frac{e^2}{8m_e}\sum_i (\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}_i)^2$ in the atomic Hamiltonian gives energy shift $\Delta E_0 = +\frac{e^2 B^2}{12m_e}\sum_i\langle r_i^2\rangle$, yielding $\chi_{\text{dia}} = -\mu_0 n\frac{\partial^2\Delta E_0}{\partial B^2} = -\frac{\mu_0 n Z e^2}{6m_e}\langle r^2\rangle$—**identically reproducing Langevin's expression** with quantum expectation value $\langle r^2\rangle$!

**Three Universal Signatures of Diamagnetism:**
1. **Strictly Negative ($\chi_{\text{dia}} < 0$):** Diamagnets weaken internal flux and are weakly repelled from strong-field gradients ($|\chi_{\text{dia}}| \sim 10^{-6}\text{–}10^{-5}$).
2. **Independent of Temperature $T$:** Because inner atomic orbital radii $\langle r^2\rangle$ do not depend on $k_B T$, $\chi_{\text{dia}}$ stays flat across temperature.
3. **Universal Property of All Matter:** Every atom contains orbital electrons, so Langevin core diamagnetism exists inside every solid, merely masked whenever permanent atomic dipoles ($J \ne 0$) supply a far larger positive paramagnetic or ferromagnetic contribution.

> [!example]- Worked Archetype: Langevin Diamagnetic Susceptibility of Diamond
> **Problem:** Diamond ($\text{C}$, atomic number $Z = 6$, molar mass $M_A = 12.011\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, mass density $\rho = 3.515 \times 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3}$) has an atomic root-mean-square electronic orbital radius $\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle} = 0.58\text{ Å}$. Calculate its Langevin diamagnetic volume susceptibility $\chi_{\text{dia}}$ and molar susceptibility $\chi_m$ in SI units.
>

>

>    $$\langle r^2\rangle = \left(0.58 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}\right)^2 = 3.364 \times 10^{-21}\text{ m}^2$$

## 2. Paramagnetism — Classical Langevin, Quantum Brillouin & Pauli Spin Models

Whenever constituent atoms or ions possess incomplete inner shells ($J \ne 0$, such as $3d$ transition-metal salts, $4f$ rare-earth compounds, or $\text{O}_2$ molecules), each site carries a **permanent magnetic dipole moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}$**. In the absence of an external field, thermal agitation $k_B T$ randomizes dipole directions so net magnetization vanishes ($M = 0$).

Applying $\mathbf{B}$ pulls dipoles toward alignment (lowering Zeeman potential energy $U = -\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}$), creating a positive magnetization parallel to $\mathbf{B}$ ($\chi > 0$) governed by the competition between magnetic ordering energy ($\mu B$) and thermal disorder ($k_B T$).

### 2.1 Classical Langevin Theory of Paramagnetism

Paul Langevin (1905) modeled a paramagnet as $n = N/V$ identical, **non-interacting** permanent magnetic dipoles of magnitude $\mu$ per unit volume, assuming classically that each dipole can point at **any continuous angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$** relative to $\mathbf{B}$.

1. **Boltzmann Angular Distribution:** A dipole at angle $\theta$ has energy $U(\theta) = -\mu B\cos\theta$. The fraction of dipoles lying within solid angle $d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta$ is proportional to $e^{-U(\theta)/k_B T}\sin\theta\,d\theta$.
2. **Average Aligned Component $\langle\mu_z\rangle$:** Defining dimensionless parameter $\alpha \equiv \frac{\mu B}{k_B T}$:
   $$\langle\mu_z\rangle = \frac{\int_0^\pi (\mu\cos\theta)\,e^{\alpha\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta}{\int_0^\pi e^{\alpha\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta}$$
   Substitute $u = \cos\theta$ ($du = -\sin\theta\,d\theta$, mapping $\theta \in [0,\pi] \to u \in [1, -1]$):
   $$\langle\mu_z\rangle = \mu\,\frac{\int_{-1}^{+1} u\,e^{\alpha u}\,du}{\int_{-1}^{+1} e^{\alpha u}\,du} = \mu\,\frac{d}{d\alpha}\ln\left(\int_{-1}^{+1} e^{\alpha u}\,du\right)$$
   Evaluate the denominator integral explicitly: $\int_{-1}^{+1} e^{\alpha u}\,du = \frac{e^\alpha - e^{-\alpha}}{\alpha} = \frac{2\sinh\alpha}{\alpha}$. Differentiating its logarithm:
   $$\langle\mu_z\rangle = \mu\,\frac{d}{d\alpha}\left[\ln 2 + \ln(\sinh\alpha) - \ln\alpha\right] = \mu\left(\coth\alpha - \frac{1}{\alpha}\right) \equiv \mu\,L(\alpha)$$
3. **Langevin Magnetization & Curie Law:** With saturation magnetization $M_s \equiv n\mu$:
   $$\boxed{M = n\mu\,L(\alpha) = M_s\left(\coth\alpha - \frac{1}{\alpha}\right), \qquad \alpha = \frac{\mu B}{k_B T}}$$
   - **High-Temperature / Weak-Field Limit ($\alpha \ll 1$):** Using $\coth\alpha = \frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{3} - \frac{\alpha^3}{45} + \dots$, we get $L(\alpha) \approx \frac{\alpha}{3}$:
     $$M \approx \frac{n\mu^2 B}{3 k_B T} \approx \frac{\mu_0 n \mu^2}{3 k_B T} H \implies \boxed{\chi_{\text{para}}^{\text{class}} = \frac{M}{H} = \frac{\mu_0 n \mu^2}{3 k_B T} = \frac{C}{T}}$$
     This yields **Curie's Law** ($\chi \propto 1/T$) with classical Curie constant $C = \frac{\mu_0 n \mu^2}{3 k_B}$.
   - **Low-Temperature / High-Field Limit ($\alpha \gg 1$):** $\coth\alpha \to 1$ and $1/\alpha \to 0 \implies L(\alpha) \to 1 \implies M \to M_s = n\mu$ (complete saturation).

### 2.2 Quantum Theory of Paramagnetism — The Brillouin Function $B_J(x)$

In reality, atomic angular momentum $\mathbf{J}$ cannot orient continuously in space: **space quantization** restricts its component along $\mathbf{B}$ to **$(2J + 1)$ discrete projections** $m_J = -J, -J+1, \dots, +J$.

**Step 1 — Discrete Zeeman Levels & Partition Function $Z$:**
The magnetic moment component along $\mathbf{B}$ is $\mu_z = m_J g_J \mu_B$ (summing symmetrically over $m_J \in [-J, +J]$ eliminates sign dependence), giving discrete Zeeman energies $E_{m_J} = -m_J g_J \mu_B B$. Define dimensionless variables:

$$y \equiv \frac{g_J \mu_B B}{k_B T}, \qquad x \equiv J y = \frac{g_J \mu_B J B}{k_B T}$$

The single-ion canonical partition function $Z$ is a finite geometric series of $(2J+1)$ terms with first term $e^{-Jy}$ and ratio $e^y$:

$$Z = \sum_{m_J = -J}^{+J} e^{m_J y} = e^{-Jy}\left(\frac{e^{(2J+1)y} - 1}{e^y - 1}\right)$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $e^{-y/2}$ to symmetrize into hyperbolic sines:

$$Z = \frac{e^{(J + 1/2)y} - e^{-(J + 1/2)y}}{e^{y/2} - e^{-y/2}} = \frac{\sinh\left[\left(J + \frac{1}{2}\right)y\right]}{\sinh\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)}$$

**Step 2 — Magnetization $M$ and Exact Brillouin Function $B_J(x)$:**
The thermal average magnetization $M = n\langle\mu_z\rangle$ follows cleanly from $\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial y}$:

$$M = n g_J \mu_B \frac{\sum_{m_J = -J}^{+J} m_J e^{m_J y}}{\sum_{m_J = -J}^{+J} e^{m_J y}} = n g_J \mu_B \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\ln Z)$$

Differentiating $\ln Z = \ln\sinh\left[\left(J+\frac{1}{2}\right)y\right] - \ln\sinh\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)$:

$$M = n g_J \mu_B \left[\left(J + \frac{1}{2}\right)\coth\left(\left(J + \frac{1}{2}\right)y\right) - \frac{1}{2}\coth\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)\right]$$

Factor out $J$ and substitute $y = x/J$ (defining maximum aligned moment $\mu_{\max} = g_J \mu_B J$ and saturation magnetization $M_s = n g_J \mu_B J$):

$$\boxed{M = n g_J \mu_B J\,B_J(x) = M_s\,B_J(x), \qquad x = \frac{g_J \mu_B J B}{k_B T}}$$

where the **Brillouin Function $B_J(x)$** is:

$$\boxed{B_J(x) = \frac{2J + 1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{2J + 1}{2J}x\right) - \frac{1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2J}\right)}$$

#### Three Exact Limiting Cases (Mandatory Verification)

1. **Two-State Spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ Paramagnet ($J = S = \frac{1}{2}, g_J = 2$):**
   Substituting $2J = 1$ and $2J+1 = 2$ with $x = \frac{\mu_B B}{k_B T}$:
   $$B_{1/2}(x) = 2\coth(2x) - \coth(x) = 2\left(\frac{\coth^2 x + 1}{2\coth x}\right) - \coth x = \frac{1}{\coth x} = \boxed{\tanh(x)}$$
   yielding $M = n\mu_B \tanh\left(\frac{\mu_B B}{k_B T}\right)$—identical to an elementary two-level Boltzmann system!

2. **High-Temperature / Weak-Field Limit ($x \ll 1$) $\to$ Quantum Curie Law:**
   Using $\coth z = \frac{1}{z} + \frac{z}{3} + O(z^3)$ for both arguments of $B_J(x)$:
   $$B_J(x) \approx \frac{2J+1}{2J}\left[\frac{2J}{(2J+1)x} + \frac{(2J+1)x}{6J}\right] - \frac{1}{2J}\left[\frac{2J}{x} + \frac{x}{6J}\right]$$
   The $1/x$ poles cancel identically, leaving:
   $$B_J(x) \approx \frac{(2J+1)^2 - 1}{12 J^2}x = \frac{4J^2 + 4J}{12 J^2}x = \frac{J+1}{3J}x$$
   Substituting $x = \frac{g_J\mu_B J B}{k_B T}$ and $B \approx \mu_0 H$ yields the **Quantum Curie Law**:
   $$\boxed{\chi_{\text{para}}^{\text{quant}} = \frac{C}{T}, \qquad C = \frac{\mu_0 n g_J^2 \mu_B^2 J(J+1)}{3 k_B} = \frac{\mu_0 n \mu_{\text{eff}}^2}{3 k_B}}$$
   with **effective Bohr magneton number $p_{\text{eff}} = g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$**!
   - *Experimental Triumph & Orbital Quenching:* For **$4f$ rare-earth salts** ($\text{Gd}^{3+}, \text{Dy}^{3+}, \text{Er}^{3+}$), deep $4f$ orbitals are shielded by outer $5s^2 5p^6$ shells and obey $p_{\text{eff}} = g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$ with astonishing precision. For **$3d$ iron-group salts** ($\text{Cr}^{3+}, \text{Fe}^{2+}, \text{Co}^{2+}, \text{Ni}^{2+}, \text{Cu}^{2+}$), exposed $3d$ orbitals interact strongly with the asymmetric crystal electric field of neighboring ligands, breaking rotational symmetry and **quenching orbital angular momentum** ($\langle L_z\rangle = 0$), so experiments instead match the **spin-only formula $p_{\text{eff}} = 2\sqrt{S(S+1)}$**!

3. **Classical Limit ($J \to \infty, \mu_B \to 0$ holding $\mu = g_J\mu_B J = \text{const}$):**
   Letting the number of quantized orientations $2J + 1 \to \infty$ at fixed $x = \frac{\mu B}{k_B T} = \alpha$:
   - First term: $\lim_{J\to\infty}\frac{2J+1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{2J+1}{2J}x\right) = 1\cdot\coth(x)$.
   - Second term: Since $\frac{x}{2J} \to 0$, $\coth\left(\frac{x}{2J}\right) \approx \frac{2J}{x}$, so $\lim_{J\to\infty}\frac{1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2J}\right) = \frac{1}{x}$.
   Hence:
   $$\lim_{J\to\infty} B_J(x) = \coth(x) - \frac{1}{x} = \boxed{L(x)}$$
   and simultaneously $g_J^2 \mu_B^2 J(J+1) = (g_J \mu_B J)^2\left(1 + \frac{1}{J}\right) \to \mu^2$, proving rigorously that **Classical Langevin theory is the $J \to \infty$ limiting case of Quantum Brillouin theory**!

### 2.3 Pauli Spin Paramagnetism of Degenerate Conduction Electrons

Simple alkali metals ($\text{Li, Na, K}$) contain $n \sim 10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}$ conduction electrons, each carrying an unpaired spin $S = \frac{1}{2}$. Applying Curie's law naively predicts $\chi = \frac{\mu_0 n \mu_B^2}{k_B T} \sim 10^{-3}$ diverging as $1/T$—yet alkali metals exhibit a tiny, **temperature-independent** paramagnetic susceptibility $\chi \sim +10^{-5}$!

Wolfgang Pauli (1927) resolved this paradox using **Fermi–Dirac degeneracy**. Because the Fermi sea is completely filled up to $E_F \sim 3\text{–}10\text{ eV}$ ($T_F \sim 4 \times 10^4\text{ K} \gg 300\text{ K}$), deep conduction electrons **cannot flip their spins** to align with $\mathbf{B}$ because the matching energy states in the spin-aligned sub-band are already occupied (**Pauli blocking**).

**Unabridged Derivation ($T \ll T_F$):**
1. Split the 3D free-electron density of states $D(E)$ equally into two spin sub-bands: **spin-up ($\uparrow$)** with magnetic moment parallel to $\mathbf{B}$ (energy shifted **down** by $-\mu_B B$) and **spin-down ($\downarrow$)** with moment antiparallel to $\mathbf{B}$ (energy shifted **up** by $+\mu_B B$):
   $$D_\uparrow(E) = \frac{1}{2}D(E + \mu_B B), \qquad D_\downarrow(E) = \frac{1}{2}D(E - \mu_B B)$$
2. In thermodynamic equilibrium at $T = 0\text{ K}$, both spin populations fill up to the **exact same chemical potential (Fermi level $E_F$)**. Consequently, electrons within an energy slice of width $\mu_B B$ near $E_F$ flip from the raised $\downarrow$-band into the lowered $\uparrow$-band!
3. Compute the excess aligned electron density $n_\uparrow - n_\downarrow$ (valid for $\mu_B B \ll E_F$, since even at $B = 10\text{ T}$, $\mu_B B \approx 0.6\text{ meV} \ll E_F \sim 5\text{ eV}$):
   $$n_\uparrow = \frac{1}{2V}\int_{-\mu_B B}^{E_F} D(E + \mu_B B)\,dE = \frac{1}{2V}\int_{0}^{E_F + \mu_B B} D(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon$$
   $$n_\downarrow = \frac{1}{2V}\int_{+\mu_B B}^{E_F} D(E - \mu_B B)\,dE = \frac{1}{2V}\int_{0}^{E_F - \mu_B B} D(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon$$
   Subtracting the two integrals leaves a narrow window of width $2\mu_B B$ centered right at $E_F$:
   $$n_\uparrow - n_\downarrow = \frac{1}{2V}\int_{E_F - \mu_B B}^{E_F + \mu_B B} D(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon \approx \frac{1}{2V}\cdot D(E_F)\cdot(2\mu_B B) = \frac{D(E_F)}{V}\,\mu_B B$$
4. Multiply by $\mu_B$ to obtain magnetization $M = (n_\uparrow - n_\downarrow)\mu_B = \frac{D(E_F)}{V}\mu_B^2 B$. Substituting the 3D Sommerfeld density of states at the Fermi surface $\frac{D(E_F)}{V} = \frac{3n}{2E_F} = \frac{3n}{2k_B T_F}$ and $B \approx \mu_0 H$ yields the **Pauli Spin Susceptibility**:

$$\boxed{\chi_{\text{Pauli}} = \mu_0 \mu_B^2 \frac{D(E_F)}{V} = \frac{3\mu_0 n \mu_B^2}{2 E_F} = \frac{3\mu_0 n \mu_B^2}{2 k_B T_F}}$$

**Physical Insights & Landau Diamagnetism Correction:**
- **Suppression & $T$-Independence:** Comparing $\frac{\chi_{\text{Pauli}}}{\chi_{\text{Curie}}} = \frac{3}{2}\frac{T}{T_F} \approx 0.01$ at $300\text{ K}$ explains *both* the $100\times$ reduction and the **temperature independence** (essentially identical physics to the $T/T_F$ suppression of electronic specific heat $C_{\text{el}}$!).
- **Landau Orbital Diamagnetism:** Conduction electrons also execute quantized cyclotron orbits (**Landau levels**), generating a competing diamagnetic susceptibility $\chi_{\text{Landau}} = -\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{m_0}{m^*}\right)^2\chi_{\text{Pauli}}$. For free electrons ($m^* = m_0$), net conduction-electron susceptibility is $\chi_{\text{el}} = \chi_{\text{Pauli}} + \chi_{\text{Landau}} = \frac{2}{3}\chi_{\text{Pauli}} > 0$. In Bismuth, an extraordinarily light effective mass ($m^* \sim 0.01\,m_0$) magnifies $|\chi_{\text{Landau}}|$ by $10^4\times$, making Bi the most strongly diamagnetic elemental metal!

## 3. Ferromagnetism, Exchange Interaction, Domains & Ordered Spin States

Ferromagnetic materials ($\text{Fe, Co, Ni, Gd, Dy}$) display colossal magnetic susceptibilities ($\chi \sim 10^2\text{–}10^6$), magnetic hysteresis, and **spontaneous magnetization $M_s > 0$ even in strictly zero applied field ($H = 0$)** below a sharp **Curie Temperature $T_c$** ($\text{Fe}: 1043\text{ K}$, $\text{Co}: 1388\text{ K}$, $\text{Ni}: 627\text{ K}$, $\text{Gd}: 293\text{ K}$). Above $T_c$, thermal disorder breaks long-range spin alignment and the crystal transitions smoothly into a paramagnet.

### 3.1 Weiss Molecular Field Theory & Derivation of the Curie–Weiss Law

Pierre Weiss (1907) explained ferromagnetism phenomonologically using two foundational hypotheses:
1. **Internal Molecular (Exchange) Field $\mathbf{B}_E$:** Cooperative interaction among neighboring atomic dipoles acts like an intense effective internal field proportional to the local magnetization $\mathbf{M}$:
   $$\mathbf{B}_{\text{eff}} = \mu_0\left(\mathbf{H} + \lambda_W \mathbf{M}\right)$$
   where $\lambda_W > 0$ is the dimensionless **Weiss molecular field constant** (empirically $\lambda_W \sim 10^3$, generating enormous internal fields $B_E = \mu_0\lambda_W M_s \sim 10^3\text{ T}$!).
2. **Spontaneous Magnetic Domains:** A macroscopic unmagnetized iron bar below $T_c$ consists of microscopic **Weiss domains**, each spontaneously saturated to $M_s(T)$, oriented in differing crystallographic directions so the bulk vector average vanishes until aligned by an external field $\mathbf{H}$.

#### 1. Derivation of the Curie–Weiss Law ($T > T_c$)

In the paramagnetic regime above $T_c$ (weak $H$, linear response $x \ll 1$), substitute the effective field $B_{\text{eff}} = \mu_0(H + \lambda_W M)$ directly into the quantum Curie equation $M = \frac{C}{\mu_0 T} B_{\text{eff}}$:

$$M = \frac{C}{T}\left(H + \lambda_W M\right)$$

Collect all $M$ terms on the left-hand side:

$$M\left(1 - \frac{C\lambda_W}{T}\right) = \frac{C}{T} H \implies M\left(\frac{T - C\lambda_W}{T}\right) = \frac{C}{T} H$$

Defining the **Ferromagnetic Curie Temperature $T_c \equiv C\lambda_W$**:

$$\boxed{\chi = \frac{M}{H} = \frac{C}{T - T_c}, \qquad T_c = C\lambda_W = \frac{\mu_0 n g_J^2 \mu_B^2 J(J+1)\lambda_W}{3 k_B} \qquad (T > T_c)}$$

**Physical Significance:** Plotting inverse susceptibility $1/\chi$ against $T$ yields a straight line with slope $1/C$ intersecting the horizontal temperature axis at **$T = +T_c$**. As $T \to T_c^+$, $\chi \to \infty$, signaling that an infinitesimal perturbation triggers macroscopic spontaneous magnetization!

#### 2. Spontaneous Magnetization Below $T_c$ ($H = 0, T < T_c$)

Setting $H = 0$ inside the exact Brillouin equation $M = M_s B_J(x)$ gives two simultaneous self-consistency equations in reduced magnetization $m \equiv M/M_s$ and dimensionless argument $x = \frac{g_J\mu_B J (\mu_0\lambda_W M)}{k_B T}$:

1. **Quantum Brillouin Curve:** $m = B_J(x)$ (starts from origin with slope $\frac{J+1}{3J}$, curves downward, saturates to $1$).
2. **Molecular-Field Straight Line:** Solving $x$ for $m = M/M_s$ and substituting $T_c = \frac{\mu_0 n g_J^2 \mu_B^2 J(J+1)\lambda_W}{3k_B}$ (noting $M_s = n g_J\mu_B J$):
   $$m = \left(\frac{k_B T}{\mu_0 g_J \mu_B J \lambda_W M_s}\right)x = \left(\frac{J+1}{3J}\frac{T}{T_c}\right)x$$

```
    m = M/Ms ^        Line T > Tc (slope > initial slope)
             |       /       Line T = Tc (tangent at origin!)
          1 -+----- /----.-~====  B_J(x)
             |     /   /   /
             |    /  /   /  Line T < Tc (intersects at m(T) > 0!)
             |   / /   /
             |  //   /
          0 -+--+---+---+-------> x
             0
```

- **For $T > T_c$:** The straight line lies everywhere steeper than $B_J(x)$ $\implies$ only solution is **$m = 0$** (paramagnetic phase).
- **For $T = T_c$:** The line is strictly tangent to $B_J(x)$ at $x = 0$, pinpointing the exact phase transition $T_c = C\lambda_W$.
- **For $T < T_c$:** The straight line cuts $B_J(x)$ at a unique non-zero root **$0 < m(T) \le 1$** (spontaneous magnetization!). Expanding $B_J(x) = \frac{J+1}{3J}x - c_3 x^3$ near $T_c$ yields the universal mean-field critical power law **$M(T) \propto (T_c - T)^{1/2}$**; as $T \to 0$, $M(T) \to M_s$ smoothly. *(At ultra-low $T \ll T_c$, collective quantized spin waves—**magnons**—give the exact **Bloch $T^{3/2}$ Law** $1 - M(T)/M_s \propto T^{3/2}$, superseding Weiss's exponential saturation.)*

### 3.2 Quantum Origin — Heisenberg Exchange Interaction & Why Only Certain Transition Metals and Rare Earths Are Ferromagnetic

Weiss's theory brilliantly predicts $T_c$ and spontaneous magnetization, yet leaves a glaring puzzle: **what physical force generates a $1000\text{ T}$ molecular field?** Ordinary magnetic dipole–dipole interaction between adjacent atomic spins separated by $a \approx 2.5\text{ Å}$ generates only $B_{\text{dip}} \sim \frac{\mu_0 \mu_B}{4\pi a^3} \approx 0.06\text{ T}$ (ordering temperature $\sim 0.04\text{ K}$)—**four orders of magnitude too weak** to account for $T_c = 1043\text{ K}$!

#### 1. The Heisenberg Exchange Hamiltonian

Werner Heisenberg (1928) proved that Weiss's molecular field is **not magnetic at all**—it is a pure consequence of **electrostatic Coulomb repulsion ($e^2 / 4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12}$) coupled with the Pauli Exclusion Principle**!

Consider two neighboring atoms $1$ and $2$ each holding one unpaired electron in orbitals $\phi_a$ and $\phi_b$. By Pauli antisymmetry of the total two-electron wavefunction $\Psi = \psi_{\text{space}}(\mathbf{r}_1,\mathbf{r}_2)\,\chi_{\text{spin}}(1,2)$:
- **Spin Singlet ($S_{\text{tot}} = 0$, antiparallel $\uparrow\downarrow$, antisymmetric spin $\chi_A$):** Requires a **symmetric** spatial wavefunction $\psi_S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2) + \phi_a(2)\phi_b(1)]$, which piles electron charge density *between* nuclei, yielding energy $E_s = E_0 + K_{\text{Coul}} + J_{\text{ex}}$.
- **Spin Triplet ($S_{\text{tot}} = 1$, parallel $\uparrow\uparrow$, symmetric spin $\chi_S$):** Requires an **antisymmetric** spatial wavefunction $\psi_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2) - \phi_a(2)\phi_b(1)]$, which vanishes identically whenever $\mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{r}_2$ (**Pauli exchange hole**), keeping electrons farther apart and lowering Coulomb repulsion to energy $E_t = E_0 + K_{\text{Coul}} - J_{\text{ex}}$.

Here the **Exchange Integral** $J_{\text{ex}} \equiv \frac{1}{2}(E_s - E_t)$ is purely electrostatic ($\sim 10^{-2}\text{–}10^{-1}\text{ eV}$!). Using the spin operator identity $\mathbf{S}_1\cdot\mathbf{S}_2 = \frac{1}{2}[S_{\text{tot}}(S_{\text{tot}}+1) - \frac{3}{2}]$ (which evaluates to $-\frac{3}{4}$ for singlet and $+\frac{1}{4}$ for triplet), the energy splitting is written compactly as the **Heisenberg Exchange Hamiltonian** summed over all nearest-neighbor spin pairs $\langle i, j \rangle$:

$$\boxed{\hat{H}_{\text{ex}} = -2\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} J_{\text{ex}}\,\mathbf{S}_i\cdot\mathbf{S}_j}$$

- **$J_{\text{ex}} > 0$:** Parallel alignment ($\uparrow\uparrow$) minimizes energy $\implies$ **Ferromagnetism**.
- **$J_{\text{ex}} < 0$:** Antiparallel alignment ($\uparrow\downarrow$) minimizes energy $\implies$ **Antiferromagnetism / Ferrimagnetism**.

Comparing the mean exchange field acting on spin $\mathbf{S}_i$ from its $z$ nearest neighbors against Weiss's field $\mathbf{B}_E = \mu_0\lambda_W \mathbf{M}$ links the microscopic and macroscopic theories directly:

$$\boxed{\lambda_W = \frac{2 z J_{\text{ex}}}{\mu_0 n g_J^2 \mu_B^2}, \qquad T_c = \frac{2 z J_{\text{ex}} S(S+1)}{3 k_B}}$$

#### 2. Why Only Specific $3d$ Transition Metals & $4f$ Rare Earths Are Ferromagnetic (Bethe–Slater & Stoner Criteria)

Out of the entire periodic table, only three $3d$ transition metals ($\text{Fe, Co, Ni}$) and a few $4f$ rare earths ($\text{Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er}$) are elemental ferromagnets. Three stringent physical conditions must hold simultaneously:

1. **Incomplete Inner $d$ or $f$ Shells (Hund's Rule Local Moments):** Closed-shell atoms have $J = 0$; simple $s$/$p$ valence metals ($\text{Na, Al, Cu}$) form broad delocalized conduction bands where kinetic-energy cost defeats spin polarization. Only narrow, partially filled **$3d$** and **$4f$** shells retain strong Hund's-rule unpaired spins inside a solid.
2. **Positive Exchange Integral ($J_{\text{ex}} > 0$) — The Bethe–Slater Curve:** Plotting $J_{\text{ex}}$ against the dimensionless geometric ratio $R = a / r_{3d}$ (interatomic spacing $a$ divided by the radius $r_{3d}$ of the unfilled $3d$ shell) reveals a narrow ferromagnetic window:
   - **Too small $a/r_{3d} < 1.5$ ($\text{Cr, Mn}$):** Heavy direct $3d$ orbital overlap acts like covalent bonding where antiparallel singlet pairing lowers energy $\implies J_{\text{ex}} < 0$ (**antiferromagnetic!**). *(Remarkably, expanding the Mn–Mn spacing in **Heusler alloys** like $\text{Cu}_2\text{MnAl}$ pushes $a/r_{3d}$ past $1.5$ and turns Mn ferromagnetic!)*
   - **Optimal window $1.5 < a/r_{3d} < 2.5$ ($\text{Fe}: 1.63, \text{Co}: 1.82, \text{Ni}: 1.97$):** Reduced inter-electronic Coulomb repulsion in the antisymmetric spatial triplet outweighs electron–nucleus covalent attraction $\implies J_{\text{ex}} > 0$ (**ferromagnetic!**).
   - **Too large $a/r_{3d} \gg 2.5$:** Direct wave-function overlap vanishes exponentially $\implies J_{\text{ex}} \to 0$.
3. **Band (Stoner) & Indirect RKKY Mechanisms:**
   - In $3d$ metals ($\text{Fe, Co, Ni}$), $3d$ electrons are partially itinerant (explaining their **non-integer** Bohr magnetons per atom: $\text{Fe}: 2.22\,\mu_B$, $\text{Co}: 1.72\,\mu_B$, $\text{Ni}: 0.61\,\mu_B$). Spontaneous band splitting occurs whenever the **Stoner Criterion** holds: **$I\,D(E_F) > 1$** (high narrow $3d$ density of states $D(E_F)$ times exchange parameter $I$ outweighs the Fermi kinetic energy penalty of polarizing spins).
   - In $4f$ rare earths ($\text{Gd, Dy}$), deeply buried $4f$ shells barely overlap directly; instead, they couple ferromagnetically via an **indirect RKKY (Ruderman–Kittel–Kasuya–Yosida) exchange** mediated by spin-polarized $5d/6s$ conduction electrons!

### 3.3 Magnetic Domains, Bloch Walls & Hysteresis Loop

#### 1. Energy Minimization & Domain Formation

If a ferromagnetic bar formed a single uniformly magnetized domain, strong magnetic poles at its ends would generate a vast external **stray magnetic field**, storing enormous **magnetostatic (demagnetizing) energy** $U_{\text{ms}} = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int B_{\text{stray}}^2\,dV$. By subdividing into alternating antiparallel **magnetic domains** capped by triangular **closure domains**, the crystal traps magnetic flux entirely inside the solid—slashing $U_{\text{ms}} \to 0$ at the far smaller energy cost of forming **domain boundaries (Bloch walls)**.

#### 2. Bloch Wall Structure & Equilibrium Thickness $\delta$

A **Bloch wall** is the transition layer of thickness $\delta = N a$ separating adjacent domains (say $180^\circ$ apart) across which atomic spins rotate smoothly in tiny angular increments $\Delta\phi = \pi/N$ from one domain orientation to the next:
- **Exchange Energy Cost:** Rotating two neighboring spins by small angle $\Delta\phi$ costs $\Delta E_{\text{ex}} = -2 J_{\text{ex}} S^2(\cos\Delta\phi - 1) \approx J_{\text{ex}} S^2(\Delta\phi)^2$. Across $N$ planes along a line of $1/a^2$ chains per unit area, exchange wall energy per unit area is $\sigma_{\text{ex}} = \frac{J_{\text{ex}} S^2 \pi^2}{N a^2} = \frac{\pi^2 J_{\text{ex}} S^2}{a\delta}$ (**favors a thick wall**!).
- **Magnetocrystalline Anisotropy Cost:** Spins inside the wall are forced temporarily away from easy crystallographic axes (e.g., $\langle 100\rangle$ in BCC Fe), costing anisotropy energy per unit area $\sigma_{\text{anis}} \approx K_1 \delta$ (**favors a thin wall**!).

Minimizing total wall energy $\sigma_w(\delta) = \frac{\pi^2 J_{\text{ex}} S^2}{a\delta} + K_1\delta$ with respect to $\delta$ gives:

$$\boxed{\delta = \pi\sqrt{\frac{J_{\text{ex}} S^2}{K_1 a}} \sim 30\text{–}100\text{ nm} \quad (\sim 100\text{–}300\text{ atomic layers in Fe})}$$

#### 3. Magnetization Process & Hysteresis Loop

Applying an increasing magnetic field $H$ to an initially demagnetized ferromagnet drives three sequential microscopic processes:
1. **Weak $H$ (Reversible Wall Displacement):** Bloch walls bow elastically so favorably aligned domains grow at the expense of opposing domains.
2. **Intermediate $H$ (Irreversible Barkhausen Jumps):** Walls snap abruptly past pinning obstacles (grain boundaries, dislocations, non-magnetic inclusions), generating discrete step-like jumps in magnetization (**Barkhausen effect**) that dissipate irreversible Joule/phonon heat.
3. **High $H$ (Domain Rotation to Saturation $M_s$):** Once favorably oriented domains consume the entire crystal, remaining magnetization vectors rotate coherently away from easy axes into alignment with $\mathbf{H}$, reaching saturation $M_s$.

Reducing $H$ back to zero fails to retrace the virgin curve because defects pin Bloch walls in place:
- **Retentivity / Remanence ($B_r$ or $M_r$):** Residual induction remaining at $H = 0$.
- **Coercivity ($H_c$):** Reverse magnetic field required to demagnetize the crystal back to $B = 0$.
- **Hysteresis Energy Loss:** Taking a ferromagnet around one complete $B\text{–}H$ loop dissipates energy per unit volume equal precisely to the enclosed **loop area**:
  $$\boxed{W_{\text{loss}} = \oint \mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\oint \mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{M} \quad (\text{J m}^{-3}\text{ per cycle})}$$
- **Soft vs. Hard Ferromagnets:** *Soft* materials (silicon steel, permalloy: low $H_c$, high $\mu_r$, slim loop) minimize AC hysteresis loss in transformer cores and electromagnets; *Hard* materials (Alnico, $\text{SmCo}_5$, $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{14}\text{B}$: high $B_r$, massive $H_c$, wide rectangular loop) maximize stored energy product $(BH)_{\max}$ for permanent magnets.

### 3.4 Antiferromagnetism (Néel Temperature $T_N$) & Ferrimagnetism (Ferrites)

When the Heisenberg exchange integral is **negative ($J_{\text{ex}} < 0$)**—frequently mediated across an intervening $\text{O}^{2-}$ anion via **superexchange** in transition-metal oxides—adjacent spins lock **antiparallel**, creating two distinct ordered states depending on sublattice symmetry:

#### 1. Antiferromagnetism (Two Identical Antiparallel Sublattices)

The crystal divides into two interpenetrating sublattices $A$ and $B$ carrying **equal magnetic moments** ($|\mathbf{M}_A| = |\mathbf{M}_B|$) coupled negatively by $\lambda_{AB} > 0$:

$$\mathbf{B}_A = \mu_0\left(\mathbf{H} - \lambda_{AB}\mathbf{M}_B\right), \qquad \mathbf{B}_B = \mu_0\left(\mathbf{H} - \lambda_{AB}\mathbf{M}_A\right)$$

- **Below the Néel Temperature $T_N$:** The two sublattices magnetize spontaneously in opposite directions, yielding **zero net spontaneous magnetization ($\mathbf{M}_A + \mathbf{M}_B = \mathbf{0}$)**. Direct proof of antiferromagnetic ordering comes from **neutron diffraction** (which reveals extra half-order magnetic superlattice reflections doubling the chemical unit cell!).
- **Above $T_N$ (Modified Curie–Weiss Law):** Writing $M_A = \frac{C'}{T}(H - \lambda_{AB}M_B)$ and $M_B = \frac{C'}{T}(H - \lambda_{AB}M_A)$ (where total Curie constant $C = 2C'$) and adding both equations gives $(M_A + M_B)\left(1 + \frac{C'\lambda_{AB}}{T}\right) = \frac{2C'}{T}H$:
  $$\boxed{\chi_{\text{AF}} = \frac{M_A + M_B}{H} = \frac{C}{T + \theta_p} \qquad (T > T_N, \;\theta_p = C'\lambda_{AB} > 0)}$$
  *(Note: $1/\chi$ vs. $T$ extrapolates to a **negative** intercept $-\theta_p$! Below $T_N$, $\chi_\parallel \to 0$ as $T \to 0$ while $\chi_\perp \approx \text{const}$, producing a sharp **cusp maximum** in $\chi(T)$ right at $T = T_N$.)* Examples: $\text{MnO}$ ($T_N = 116\text{ K}$), $\text{FeO}$ ($198\text{ K}$), $\text{NiO}$ ($523\text{ K}$), $\text{Cr}$ ($311\text{ K}$).

#### 2. Ferrimagnetism (Two Unequal Antiparallel Sublattices — Ferrites)

In **ferrimagnets**, antiparallel sublattices $A$ and $B$ carry **unequal magnetic moments** ($|\mathbf{M}_A| \ne |\mathbf{M}_B|$), leaving a strong **net spontaneous magnetization $M_{\text{net}} = |M_A - M_B| > 0$** below a ferrimagnetic Curie temperature $T_c$, along with domains and hysteresis loops!

- **Inverse Spinel Ferrites ($\text{MO}\cdot\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$, e.g., Magnetite $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$):** In $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 = \text{Fe}^{3+}_A[\text{Fe}^{2+}\text{Fe}^{3+}]_B\text{O}_4^{2-}$, tetrahedral $A$-sites hold one $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ($3d^5, S=\frac{5}{2}, 5\,\mu_B$) coupled antiparallel to octahedral $B$-sites holding one $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ($5\,\mu_B$) plus one $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ($3d^6, S=2, 4\,\mu_B$). The two $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ moments cancel completely ($5 - 5 = 0$), leaving **$4\,\mu_B$ per formula unit** (experimentally $4.1\,\mu_B$!).
- **High-Temperature Susceptibility & Engineering Advantage:** Above $T_c$, $1/\chi$ vs. $T$ follows a **curved hyperbola** (bending downward toward $+T_c$). Crucially, ceramic ferrites ($\text{MnFe}_2\text{O}_4$, $\text{NiZn}$-ferrite, YIG) are **electrical insulators** ($\rho \sim 10^2\text{–}10^8\,\Omega\text{ m}$), eliminating eddy-current heating in high-frequency MHz–GHz transformers, inductors, and microwave circulators!

> **Problem:** BCC Iron has lattice constant $a = 2.866\text{ Å}$ ($z = 8$ nearest neighbors), Curie temperature $T_c = 1043\text{ K}$, and $0\text{ K}$ saturation moment $\mu_{\text{atom}} = 2.22\,\mu_B$ per atom (take effective $S = 1, g = 2$). Calculate:
> (a) the atomic number density $n$ and $0\text{ K}$ saturation magnetization $M_s$;
> (b) the Weiss molecular field constant $\lambda_W$ and internal molecular field $B_E = \mu_0\lambda_W M_s$ (in Tesla);
> (c) the Heisenberg exchange integral $J_{\text{ex}}$ (in $\text{meV}$), and compare $B_E$ quantitatively against the classical magnetic dipole–dipole field $B_{\text{dip}}$ between nearest-neighbor Fe atoms.
>
> *(Constants: $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\text{ T m A}^{-1}$, $\mu_B = 9.2740 \times 10^{-24}\text{ J T}^{-1}$, $k_B = 1.38065 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J K}^{-1}$, $1\text{ eV} = 1.6022 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$.)*
>
> **Solution:**
> **(a) Atomic Density $n$ & Saturation Magnetization $M_s$:**
> A conventional BCC cube of volume $a^3 = (2.866 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m})^3 = 2.3541 \times 10^{-29}\text{ m}^3$ holds $N_{\text{eff}} = 2$ atoms:
> $$n = \frac{2}{2.3541 \times 10^{-29}\text{ m}^3} = 8.496 \times 10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}$$
> $$M_s = n\mu_{\text{atom}} = (8.496 \times 10^{28})(2.22 \times 9.2740 \times 10^{-24}) = \boxed{1.749 \times 10^6\text{ A m}^{-1}} \quad (\mu_0 M_s = 2.20\text{ T})$$
>
> **(b) Weiss Constant $\lambda_W$ & Internal Exchange Field $B_E$:**
> Using $g = 2, S = 1 \implies g^2 S(S+1) = 4 \times 2 = 8$, the Curie constant $C$ is:
> $$C = \frac{\mu_0 n g^2 \mu_B^2 S(S+1)}{3 k_B} = \frac{(1.2566 \times 10^{-6})(8.496 \times 10^{28})(8)(9.2740 \times 10^{-24})^2}{3(1.38065 \times 10^{-23})} = \frac{7.3457 \times 10^{-23}}{4.1420 \times 10^{-23}} = 1.7735\text{ K}$$
> From $T_c = C\lambda_W$:
> $$\lambda_W = \frac{T_c}{C} = \frac{1043\text{ K}}{1.7735\text{ K}} = \boxed{588}$$
> Internal Weiss exchange field:
> $$B_E = \mu_0 \lambda_W M_s = (1.2566 \times 10^{-6})(588.1)(1.749 \times 10^6) = \boxed{1.29 \times 10^3\text{ T}}$$
>
> **(c) Heisenberg Exchange Integral $J_{\text{ex}}$ & Comparison with Classical Dipole Field:**
> From $T_c = \dfrac{2 z J_{\text{ex}} S(S+1)}{3 k_B}$ ($z = 8, S = 1 \implies 2zS(S+1) = 32$):
> $$J_{\text{ex}} = \frac{3 k_B T_c}{2 z S(S+1)} = \frac{3(1.38065 \times 10^{-23})(1043)}{32} = 1.350 \times 10^{-21}\text{ J} = \boxed{8.43\text{ meV}}$$
> Classical magnetic dipole field at BCC nearest-neighbor separation $d_{\text{NN}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a = 2.482\text{ Å}$:
> $$B_{\text{dip}} \sim \frac{\mu_0 \mu_{\text{atom}}}{4\pi d_{\text{NN}}^3} = \frac{(10^{-7})(2.0588 \times 10^{-23})}{(2.482 \times 10^{-10})^3} = \frac{2.0588 \times 10^{-30}}{1.5290 \times 10^{-29}} = 0.135\text{ T}$$
> Ratio $B_E / B_{\text{dip}} \approx 9.6 \times 10^3$: the quantum electrostatic exchange field is **nearly $10,000\times$ stronger** than classical magnetic dipole–dipole coupling!

> [!warning] Derivation, Sign & Unit-Conversion Traps
> 1. **Missing $\mu_0$ or Mixing CGS ($1/c^2$) into SI Susceptibility Formulas:** Always keep explicit $\mu_0$ in **every** SI volume susceptibility: $\chi_{\text{dia}} = -\frac{\mu_0 n Z e^2 \langle r^2\rangle}{6 m_e}$, $\chi_{\text{para}} = \frac{\mu_0 n g_J^2 \mu_B^2 J(J+1)}{3 k_B T}$, and $\chi_{\text{Pauli}} = \frac{3\mu_0 n \mu_B^2}{2 E_F}$. Dropping $\mu_0$ (or inserting CGS $1/c^2$) makes $\chi$ dimensionally wrong! Remember also CGS-to-SI conversion: $\chi_{\text{SI}} = 4\pi\,\chi_{\text{CGS}}$.
> 2. **Confusing $J^2$ with $J(J+1)$ in Quantum Curie Constant:** Inside the Brillouin function argument $x = \frac{g_J\mu_B J B}{k_B T}$ and saturation magnetization $M_s = n g_J\mu_B J$, the maximum projection is **$J$**, whereas in the small-$x$ Curie constant $C = \frac{\mu_0 n g_J^2 \mu_B^2 J(J+1)}{3 k_B}$, expanding $B_J(x) \approx \frac{J+1}{3J}x$ turns $J \cdot \frac{J+1}{3J} \cdot J$ into **$J(J+1)/3$**! Never write $J^2$ inside $C$.
> 3. **Forgetting the $\frac{2}{3}$ Spherical Averaging Factor in Langevin Diamagnetism:** $\langle\rho^2\rangle = \langle x^2 + y^2\rangle = \frac{2}{3}\langle r^2\rangle$ converts the $4m_e$ denominator into **$6m_e$**! Skipping this explicit line leaves an unexplained jump from $4$ to $6$ in UPSC proofs.
> 4. **Thinking Classical Dipole–Dipole Forces Cause Ferromagnetism:** Never attribute Weiss's molecular field $\lambda_W \mathbf{M}$ to magnetic dipole–dipole attraction—it is **4 orders of magnitude too weak** ($\sim 0.1\text{ T}$ vs. $10^3\text{ T}$). Explicitly cite **Coulomb electrostatic repulsion + Pauli antisymmetry (Heisenberg Exchange Integral $J_{\text{ex}} > 0$)**.
> 5. **Confusing $1/\chi$ Intercepts Across Para, Ferro, Antiferro, and Ferri:** Always keep the $1/\chi$ vs. $T$ signature straight: Paramagnet passes through **$0$**; Ferromagnet intercepts **$+T_c$**; Antiferromagnet extrapolates to **$-\theta_p$** (with a sharp $\chi$ peak at $+T_N$); Ferrimagnet curves **hyperbolically** down to $+T_c$.

### 2. Examiner Step-Marking & Numerical Trap Signature

- **Stopping Short of the Explicit $J \to \infty$ Limit in Quantum Paramagnetism (2010/2017 Trap):** Candidates routinely derive $B_J(x)$ and stop at the small-$x$ Curie limit without explicitly showing the **two-step limit** $\lim_{J\to\infty}\frac{2J+1}{2J}\coth(\frac{2J+1}{2J}x) = \coth x$ and $\lim_{J\to\infty}\frac{1}{2J}\coth(\frac{x}{2J}) = \frac{1}{x}$ to recover $L(x) = \coth x - 1/x$. Missing that explicit limit forfeits 3–4 marks out of 15.
- **Vague Answers on Transition Metals & Rare Earths (2008 Trap):** Writing merely "because they have unpaired electrons" scores poorly since $\text{Cr, Mn, V, Ti}$ also have unpaired $3d$ electrons yet are *not* ferromagnetic! Full marks require explaining **both** the **Bethe–Slater positive exchange window** ($1.5 < a/r_{3d} < 2.5$ separating antiferromagnetic $\text{Cr, Mn}$ from ferromagnetic $\text{Fe, Co, Ni}$) and **indirect RKKY coupling / Stoner criterion $I D(E_F) > 1$**.
- **Omitting Energy Balance in Domain & Hysteresis Questions (2024 Trap):** Simply drawing a generic $B\text{–}H$ loop without linking domains to the competition between **magnetostatic stray-field energy** and **Bloch wall energy** (exchange vs. magnetocrystalline anisotropy), and linking hysteresis irreversibility to **Barkhausen pinning jumps**, caps scores around 9/15.
- **Skipping the Graphical $T < T_c$ Spontaneous Magnetization Proof in Weiss Theory:** Deriving only $\chi = C/(T-T_c)$ above $T_c$ leaves half of Weiss's theory untouched; always draw the intersecting straight-line + $B_J(x)$ plot proving spontaneous magnetization at $H = 0$ below $T_c$.

### 3. Unseen Conceptual & Analytical Twists

1. **Spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ Weiss Mean-Field Critical Exponent & Magnetic Specific Heat Jump:** Using $m = \tanh\left(\frac{T_c}{T} m\right)$ for a spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ ferromagnet at $H = 0$ just below $T_c$ ($\epsilon = 1 - T/T_c \ll 1$), expand $\tanh z \approx z - \frac{1}{3}z^3$ to prove analytically that spontaneous magnetization vanishes as $m(T) = \sqrt{3(1 - T/T_c)}$, and compute the discontinuous **jump in magnetic heat capacity** $\Delta C_M = \frac{3}{2} N k_B$ at $T = T_c$ (demonstrating that the ferromagnetic phase transition is second-order!).
2. **Two-Sublattice Néel Theory of Ferrimagnetism & Compensation Temperature:** Extending Weiss molecular field theory to two unequal antiparallel sublattices ($C_A \ne C_B$, $\mathbf{B}_A = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} - \lambda_{AB}\mathbf{M}_B)$, $\mathbf{B}_B = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} - \lambda_{AB}\mathbf{M}_A)$), derive the exact **hyperbolic inverse susceptibility** $\frac{1}{\chi} = \frac{T}{C_A + C_B} + \frac{1}{\chi_0} - \frac{\sigma_0}{T - \theta}$ above $T_c$, and explain physically how differing temperature decays of $M_A(T)$ and $M_B(T)$ below $T_c$ can cause net spontaneous magnetization to cross zero at a **compensation temperature $T_{\text{comp}} < T_c$**.
