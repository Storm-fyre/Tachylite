# The Complete Chemistry & Engineering of Hydrocarbon Fuels

## I. The Foundation: Fractional Distillation

The fundamental difference between all major fuels—from the gas in your stove to the asphalt on the road—comes down to fractional distillation. As crude oil is heated in a distillation tower, hydrocarbons separate based on their molecular weight, carbon chain length, and boiling points.

---

## II. The Chronological Carbon Chain Spectrum

Hydrocarbons scale from simple, light gases to heavy, complex solids. Here is the precise chronological breakdown of where every carbon chain ends up and why:

### C1: Methane (The Natural Gas Family)
* **Chemistry:** CH₄ (Gas).
* **Application:** Compressed for vehicles as CNG, or piped into homes and industries as Piped Natural Gas (PNG).

### C2: Ethane (The Plastic Building Block)
* **Chemistry:** C₂H₆ (Gas).
* **Application:** Mostly skipped as a combustion fuel because it is far more valuable to the petrochemical industry. It is "cracked" to make ethylene, the foundational building block for most plastics (like polyethylene).

### C3 & C4: Propane & Butane (LPG Family)
* **Chemistry:** C₃H₈ and C₄H₁₀ (Easily liquefied gases).
* **Application:** Fuel for home cooking cylinders and vehicles (known as AutoLPG or Autogas, requiring a pressurized trunk tank).

### C5 & C6: Pentane & Hexane (The "Light Ends" of Petrol)
* **Chemistry:** C₅H₁₂ and C₆H₁₄ (Extremely volatile liquids).
* **Application:** A critical component of petrol. While liquid at standard pressure, they evaporate almost instantly. Refineries blend these into petrol as "light ends" to help engines start on freezing winter mornings (e.g., at 0°C) when heavier C8 molecules remain sluggish. They catch the first spark.

### C7 to C12: The Core of Petrol
* **Chemistry:** Heavier volatile liquids (Heptane, Octane, Nonane).
* **Application:** The bulk of standard petrol. It provides a perfect balance: stable enough to be pumped safely as a liquid, but volatile enough to vaporize entirely in a hot engine cylinder.

### C10 to C25: Diesel and Jet Fuel
* **C10 to C15 (Kerosene/Jet Fuel):** Aviation Turbine Fuel. It has a very low freezing point, critical for airplanes flying at high altitudes where temperatures drop to −50°C.
* **C14 to C25 (Diesel):** Viscous, heavy liquids used for standard trucks, trains, and heavy transport.

### Beyond C25: The Bottom of the Barrel
* **C26 to C40:** Lubricating oils (motor oil) and heavy fuel oils for massive cargo ships.
* **C40 to C50:** Solid waxes and petroleum jelly.
* **C50+:** Bitumen/Asphalt. The thick, black, sticky residue left at the bottom of the distillation tower, used to pave roads.

## III. Detailed Breakdown of Major Fuels

### 1. Natural Gas, CNG, and LNG (The Methane Family)
Unlike petroleum-refined fuels, Natural Gas is extracted directly from deep underground reservoirs. It is predominantly methane, the simplest hydrocarbon with the highest hydrogen-to-carbon ratio, making it the cleanest burning fossil fuel.
* **Chemistry & Composition:** 70% to 90% methane (CH₄), plus ethane, propane, and inert gases (nitrogen, carbon dioxide).
* **CNG (Compressed Natural Gas):** Methane's extremely low critical temperature (−82.6°C, with a normal boiling point of −162°C) means it refuses to liquefy at room temperature regardless of pressure. For vehicles, it is compressed to incredibly high pressures (20 to 25 MPa) but remains a gas.
* **LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas):** For international shipping (since pipelines cannot cross oceans), natural gas is super-cooled to −162°C. At this cryogenic temperature, it condenses into a liquid, shrinking to 1/600th of its gaseous volume.
* **Global Terminology:** Universally called Natural Gas, CNG, or LNG.

### 2. LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas)
* **Chemistry & Composition:** A mixture of light, low-molecular-weight aliphatic hydrocarbons, primarily propane (C₃H₈) and butane (C₄H₁₀), with small amounts of propylene and butylene.
* **Physical Properties:** Gas at standard atmospheric pressure (Propane boils at −42°C; Butane at −1°C). Because their critical temperatures are above room temperature, they condense into a high-density liquid under moderate pressure (500 to 1200 kilopascals), making cylinder transport highly efficient.
* **Combustion Profile:** Cleanest burning petroleum fuel with very low particulate matter. Its small, simple molecules vaporize instantly and mix evenly with oxygen, leaving no soot.
* **Odorant Chemistry (Why Gas Smells):** Pure methane, propane, and butane are 100% colorless and odorless. To make leaks immediately detectable by the human nose at parts-per-billion levels, refineries intentionally add trace organosulfur compounds called **mercaptans** (primarily *ethanethiol* / ethyl mercaptan, C₂H₅SH).
* **Global Terminology:** India, UK, Global: LPG. US: Propane (due to propane-heavy American blends). Vehicles: Autogas.

### 3. Petrol (Gasoline)
* **Chemistry & Composition:** A complex, heavily refined blend of hundreds of hydrocarbons (C4 to C12). Includes straight-chain alkanes, branched alkanes (like isooctane, C₈H₁₈), cycloalkanes, and aromatics (like benzene, C₆H₆, and toluene, C₇H₈).
* **Physical Properties:** Volatile liquid at room temperature with a boiling range of 40°C to 200°C.
* **Combustion Profile:** Moderate soot, requires a spark to ignite.
* **Global Terminology:** India, UK, Australia, Commonwealth: Petrol. US, Canada: Gasoline or Gas. Parts of Europe: Benzin (Germany), Essence (France).

### 4. Diesel
* **Chemistry & Composition:** Heavier, longer-chain hydrocarbons (C10 to C25) and aromatics. A common reference molecule is hexadecane/cetane (C₁₆H₃₄).
* **Physical Properties:** A denser, viscous liquid. Boiling range of 200°C to 350°C. Lower volatility makes it significantly safer to store at room temperature.
* **Combustion Profile:** Higher particulate emissions. Ignites via high compression (no spark required).
* **Global Terminology:** Universal: Diesel (named after inventor Rudolf Diesel). France/Spain: Gasoil or Gazole.

## IV. Comparative Energy and Physical Properties

(Comparing mass vs. volume trade-offs on a standardized Lower Heating Value / LHV baseline)

### CNG (CH₄)
* **State:** High-Pressure Gas
* **Energy by Mass:** ~50.0 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~55.5 MJ/kg (HHV) *(Highest)*
* **Energy by Volume:** ~9.0 MJ/L at 25 MPa *(Lowest)*

### LPG (C₃H₈, C₄H₁₀)
* **State:** Pressurized Liquid
* **Energy by Mass:** ~46.1 MJ/kg (LHV)
* **Energy by Volume:** ~25.3 MJ/L (LHV)

### Petrol (C4 to C12)
* **State:** Liquid
* **Energy by Mass:** ~43.4 MJ/kg (LHV)
* **Energy by Volume:** ~34.2 MJ/L (LHV)

### Diesel (C10 to C25)
* **State:** Viscous Liquid
* **Energy by Mass:** ~42.6 MJ/kg (LHV)
* **Energy by Volume:** ~38.6 MJ/L (LHV) *(Highest)*

> **Note:** CNG has the highest energy by mass due to its hydrogen content, but the lowest by volume because it is a gas. Diesel packs the most energy per liter, resulting in better vehicular fuel economy.

## V. Engine Mechanics and Performance Metrics

Combustion is a rapid, high-temperature exothermic redox chemical reaction between fuel and an oxidant. Petrol and Diesel engines handle this entirely differently.

### 1. The Petrol Engine Environment (Spark Ignition)
In petrol engines, fuel and air are mixed, compressed, and intentionally ignited by a spark plug at the exact perfect millisecond.

**Reference Molecules:**
* **The "Perfect" Molecule (Score 100):** Isooctane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane, C₈H₁₈). Branched, compact, incredibly stable, resists premature detonation.
* **The "Terrible" Molecule (Score 0):** n-Heptane (C₇H₁₆). A straight-chain alkane. Bonds break easily under pressure and heat, auto-igniting almost immediately (e.g., "95 Octane" behaves perfectly like a 95% isooctane / 5% n-heptane mix).

**The Metric — Octane Rating (The Resistance Metric):** Measures a fuel's ability to resist auto-ignition. If fuel gets too hot during compression and auto-ignites before the spark fires, explosive shockwaves crash into each other ("engine knock" or detonation), which can shatter engine parts. Higher octane allows tighter compression for more power safely.
* **Compression Ratio:** 8:1 to 12:1 (limited by octane rating).
* **Air-to-Fuel Ratio:** Operates locked near stoichiometric balance (~14.7:1 by mass) so the exhaust catalyst can simultaneously oxidize and reduce pollutants.
* **Thermal Efficiency:** ~25% to 30% (more waste heat).
* **Power Delivery:** Lower cylinder pressures mean less low-end torque, but high RPM capability yields high horsepower.
* **Emissions:** Carbon Monoxide (CO), unburned hydrocarbons (HC), and Nitrogen Oxides (NOₓ). Easily neutralized together by a standard 3-way catalytic converter (which oxidizes CO and HC while reducing NOₓ).

### 2. The Diesel Engine Environment (Compression Ignition)
In diesel engines, only air is compressed—so intensely that it heats to 500°C – 700°C. Fuel is then sprayed in, and ambient heat causes it to instantly explode.

**The Metric — Cetane Rating (The Willingness Metric):** Measures ignition delay—how quickly and easily fuel auto-ignites when injected. If there is a delay (low cetane), unburned fuel pools up and explodes violently, causing harsh "diesel clatter" and heavy soot. High cetane means a smooth, fast, controlled burn.
* **Compression Ratio:** 14:1 to 25:1 (requires massive compression for auto-ignition heat).
* **Air-to-Fuel Ratio:** Operates "lean" (massive excess of air).
* **Thermal Efficiency:** ~35% to 45%+ (high compression squeezes more kinetic energy out of the fuel).
* **Power Delivery:** Massive cylinder pressures generate immense low-end torque, but heavy components prevent high RPM spinning.
* **Emissions:** The excess air (oxygen-rich exhaust) and localized high heat create Nitrogen Oxides (NOₓ) and Particulate Matter (soot). Because excess oxygen prevents a standard 3-way catalyst from reducing NOₓ, diesel requires complex after-treatment (Diesel Particulate Filters/DPF and AdBlue/urea SCR injection).

*(Rule of Thumb: Octane and Cetane are chemical opposites. A fuel with a high octane rating inherently has a terrible cetane rating, and vice versa).*

## VI. Thermodynamics: Why Hydrogen Dictates Cleanliness and Energy

### 1. High Hydrogen-to-Carbon (H/C) Ratio = Clean
Cleanliness is determined by combustion byproducts. Carbon oxidizes to CO₂; Hydrogen oxidizes to harmless H₂O (water vapor).
* **Methane (CH₄) Stoichiometry:** CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (Ratio: 1 molecule of CO₂ to 2 molecules of water).
* **Octane (C₈H₁₈) Stoichiometry:** 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O (Ratio: Almost 1:1).

**The Soot Problem:** Heavier molecules (like diesel) have long C–C bonds requiring massive, highly dispersed oxygen to burn entirely. Real-world engines have localized oxygen-depleted pockets. Hydrogen easily grabs oxygen to make water, but left-over carbon atoms bind to each other, creating carbon monoxide (CO) and soot (particulate matter). High H/C fuels lack C–C bonds entirely (methane) or have very short carbon chains (propane), vaporize easily, and burn completely soot-free.

### 2. High Hydrogen-to-Carbon (H/C) Ratio = Higher Energy by Mass
Chemical energy measures the heat released (enthalpy of combustion) when fuel oxidizes.
* Oxidizing pure hydrogen releases ~120 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~142 MJ/kg (HHV).
* Oxidizing pure carbon releases only ~33 MJ/kg.

Because hydrogen is lightweight and releases immense energy, higher hydrogen content yields higher specific energy:
* **Hydrogen Gas (H₂):** ~120 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~142 MJ/kg (HHV)
* **Methane (CH₄):** ~50.0 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~55.5 MJ/kg (HHV)
* **Propane (C₃H₈):** ~46.1 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~50.4 MJ/kg (HHV)
* **Diesel (C₁₆H₃₄ average):** ~42.6 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~45.6 MJ/kg (HHV)
* **Coal (Solid carbon):** ~24 to 33 MJ/kg

**The Engineering Caveat:** While high-hydrogen fuels give the most energy per kilogram, they are gases that take up massive physical space. Diesel has less energy per mass, but its high-density liquid state packs far more energy per liter, making it economical for heavy transport without requiring massive, heavy, high-pressure tanks.

## VII. Pushing the Chemical Limits: The Extremes of Hydrocarbons

If you constantly lengthen a hydrocarbon chain, volumetric energy does not "peak and drop"—it either hits a physical wall, or chemists must use extreme structural manipulations.

### 1. The Straight-Chain Asymptote
If you lengthen a standard alkane (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) infinitely, the ends become mathematically irrelevant. The H/C ratio settles exactly at 2:1 (an endless chain of –CH₂– groups).

**State progression:** Gas → Volatile Liquid (Petrol) → Viscous Liquid (Diesel) → Solid Wax → Polyethylene (Plastic).

**The Absolute Limit:** High-density polyethylene maxes out as a solid plastic at:
* **Density:** ~0.97 kg/L
* **Specific Energy (Mass):** ~42.5 MJ/kg (LHV) / ~46.3 MJ/kg (HHV)
* **Volumetric Energy:** ~41.2 MJ/L (LHV) / ~44.9 MJ/L (HHV)

### 2. The True Liquid Global Maxima: Knots, Not Chains
To get the absolute highest energy per volume while remaining a pumpable liquid (critical for aerospace/jet engines), chemists fold molecules into tight, stressed rings instead of making chains longer.

**Examples:** High-Density Liquid Fuels like JP-10 and RJ-5 (used in cruise missiles). RJ-5 hits ~44.9 MJ/L (LHV), vastly beating diesel.
* **Trick 1 — Super-Packing:** Multiple fused cyclic rings nest together perfectly, pushing liquid density above 1.0 kg/L (heavier than water).
* **Trick 2 — Ring Strain:** Carbon naturally "wants" to bond at 109.5-degree angles. Forcing them into tight geometric rings creates massive molecular tension. Burning the fuel releases normal oxidation thermal energy plus a massive bonus from the "spring-loaded" mechanical strain energy snapping open.

### 3. The Theoretical Solid Maximum: Cubane
If liquid state is not required, the absolute global maximum belongs to exotic molecules like Cubane (C₈H₈).
* **Chemistry:** Eight carbon atoms forced into a perfect 90-degree cube.
* **Properties:** The ring strain is astronomical. It packs into a crystal lattice with an absurd hydrocarbon density of ~1.29 kg/L.
* **Application:** Heavily studied for advanced explosives and solid rocket propellants due to its off-the-charts volumetric energy density.
