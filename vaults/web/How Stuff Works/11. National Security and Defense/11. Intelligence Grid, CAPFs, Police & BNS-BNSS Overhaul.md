# Part 11: Intelligence Architectures, CAPF Operational Friction, Police Modernization, and the BNS/BNSS Criminal Justice Overhaul

## I. The National Intelligence Collection, Technical, and Fusion Architecture

### 1. Strategic, Domestic, Military, and Technical Intelligence Agencies (IB, R&AW, NTRO, DIA)

The foundational dilemma of intelligence architecture in a constitutional democracy lies in the structural tension between **clandestine operational agility** (requiring compartmentalized secrecy, deniable human/technical collection, and protection of sources and methods) and **democratic constitutional accountability** (requiring statutory charters, financial auditing, and parliamentary oversight to prevent domestic political surveillance and institutional turf wars). Unlike Western liberal democracies that placed their intelligence agencies under explicit legislative charters following late-20th-century oversight reforms—such as the United States (*National Security Act of 1947*, *Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978*, and permanent Congressional Intelligence Committees) or the United Kingdom (*Security Service Act 1989* for MI5 and *Intelligence Services Act 1994* for MI6 and GCHQ)—India’s apex civilian intelligence agencies operate exclusively through executive notifications and colonial administrative continuities rather than parliamentary statutes.

This extra-statutory design grants the political executive rapid, unfettered tasking flexibility against hybrid sub-conventional threats, cross-border proxy networks, and ethno-secessionist movements. However, it simultaneously generates acute structural pathologies: the absence of a clearly demarcated statutory charter blurs the line between counter-intelligence and domestic political gathering, shields inter-agency coordination failures from legislative scrutiny, and leaves intelligence officers in a legal grey zone regarding covert domestic and extraterritorial operations.

#### The Four Pillars of National Intelligence Collection and Assessment

* **Intelligence Bureau (IB — Domestic Counter-Intelligence and Internal Security):** Tracing its institutional lineage to the **Central Special Branch** established on December 23, 1887, under the British Secretary of State for India, and redesignated after the 1947 transfer of power under the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)**, the IB serves as India’s premier internal security, counter-intelligence, counter-terrorism, and VIP security agency. Constitutionally, the IB derives its nominal anchor from **Entry 8 of the Union List (Seventh Schedule)**, which vests the Union Parliament with exclusive legislative competence over the *"Central Bureau of Intelligence and Investigation."* Despite this explicit constitutional contemplation of legislation, Parliament has never enacted an IB Act. The **L.P. Singh Committee (1978)**, constituted after the Emergency to examine the misuse of intelligence machinery, recommended a formal charter defining the IB's remit, and the **Intelligence Services (Powers and Regulation) Bill, 2011** (introduced as a Private Member's Bill by Manish Tewari) sought to bring the IB, R&AW, and NTRO under a **National Intelligence and Security Oversight Committee** and a **National Intelligence Tribunal**. Both initiatives were shelved due to executive resistance. Operationally, the IB functions through State **Subsidiary Intelligence Bureaus (SIBs)** headed by Joint/Additional Directors, maintaining deep human intelligence (**HUMINT**) networks across Left-Wing Extremism (LWE) corridors, Jammu & Kashmir, the Northeast, and communal/radicalization hotspots, while exercising lawful interception under **Section 69 of the Information Technology Act, 2000** and the **Telecommunications Act, 2023**.
* **Research and Analysis Wing (R&AW, 1968 — External Strategic Intelligence):** Until 1968, the IB handled both internal and external intelligence. Following catastrophic strategic surprise during the **1962 Sino-Indian War** (failure to anticipate PLA winter offensives across the Aksai Chin and NEFA) and tactical operational blind spots during the **1965 Indo-Pakistan War** (failure to detect the raising of Pakistan's 1st Armoured Division infrastructure), the Indira Gandhi administration bifurcated external intelligence on **September 21, 1968**, under founding director **R.N. Kao**. To insulate R&AW from routine right-to-information disclosures (**exempted under Section 24 and the Second Schedule of the RTI Act, 2005**) and parliamentary question-hour scrutiny, it is administratively housed as an "attached office" of the **Cabinet Secretariat**, with its Secretary (Research) reporting directly to the **Prime Minister** and the **National Security Advisor (NSA)**. R&AW commands two specialized technical-operational formations:
  1. **Aviation Research Centre (ARC):** Established in 1962 with US CIA assistance following the Chinese nuclear build-up at Lop Nor, ARC operates fleet assets from **Charbatia (Odisha)**, **Sarsawa (Uttar Pradesh)**, and **Doom Dooma (Assam)**, conducting high-altitude aerial reconnaissance, electronic intelligence (**ELINT**), communications intelligence (**COMINT**), and strategic airlift for special operations.
  2. **Special Frontier Force (SFF):** Raised in November 1962 at **Chakrata (Uttarakhand)** (originally known as **Establishment 22** under Major General Sujan Singh Uban, initially recruiting Tibetan resistance fighters alongside Gorkhas), the SFF (*Vikas* Battalions) operates under the Directorate General of Security (headed by the Secretary, R&AW) and proved decisive during the **August 2020 Kailash Range heights occupation** along the southern bank of Pangong Tso in Eastern Ladakh.
* **National Technical Research Organisation (NTRO, 2004 — Apex TECHINT, Cyber, and Geospatial Hub):** The **1999 Kargil conflict** exposed severe fragmentation in India’s technical intelligence (**TECHINT**): while ARC, R&AW, and the military directorates possessed isolated interception capabilities, there was no apex civilian agency matching the US National Security Agency (NSA) to coordinate high-resolution satellite reconnaissance, cryptanalysis, and cyber-signals intelligence. Acting on the **Kargil Review Committee (1999)** and the **Group of Ministers (GoM) Report on Reforming the National Security System (2001)**, the Union Government established the **NTRO in 2004** directly under the **National Security Advisor (NSA)** in the Prime Minister’s Office. NTRO acts as a "super-feeder" technical agency, managing the **National Institute of Cryptology Research and Development (NICRD)**, high-altitude unmanned aerial surveillance, deep-packet submarine cable and satellite **SIGINT** interception, and the **National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC)**. In **2017**, the government formally notified the NTRO under the **Intelligence Organisations (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1985**, prohibiting its technical personnel from forming associations, communicating with the press, or publishing operational material without clearance.
* **Defence Intelligence Agency (DIA, 2002 — Tri-Service Military Intelligence Integration):** Historically, the three armed services operated isolated intelligence silos—the **Directorate of Military Intelligence (DMI)**, **Directorate of Naval Intelligence (DNI)**, and **Directorate of Air Intelligence (DAI)**—which hoarded tactical battlefield intelligence and refused cross-service dissemination. Established in **March 2002** on the recommendation of the **2001 GoM Report**, the **DIA** operates under the **Headquarters Integrated Defence Staff (HQ IDS)** and the **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)**. Headed by a three-star Director General (rotating among the three services), the DIA exercises direct command over the **Defence Image Processing and Analysis Centre (DIPAC)** (which processes electro-optical and synthetic aperture radar imagery from India’s ** Cartosat** and **RISAT** military satellite constellations), the **Defence Information Warfare Agency (DIWA)**, and all Indian Defence Attachés posted in foreign embassies, while coordinating tactical cross-border HUMINT and SIGINT across the Line of Control (LoC) and Line of Actual Control (LAC).

### 2. The Economic Intelligence Grid (CEIB, DRI, ED, FIU-IND, CBDT)

Modern asymmetric conflict relies on transnational financial plumbing—Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML), hawala clearinghouses, shell-company layering, darknet narcotics proceeds, and Virtual Digital Asset (VDA) mixers—to sustain insurgent logistics and sleeper cells. Because traditional security agencies (IB, R&AW) lack statutory powers to audit corporate balance sheets, inspect customs shipping bills, or attach benami properties, India maintains a specialized **Economic Intelligence Grid** anchored in the **Ministry of Finance (Department of Revenue)** that bridges fiscal enforcement with national security counter-terror financing.

* **Economic Intelligence Council (EIC) and Central Economic Intelligence Bureau (CEIB, 1985):** Established in **1985**, the **CEIB** serves as the nodal secretariat for the **Economic Intelligence Council (EIC)**, which is chaired by the **Union Finance Minister** and includes the Governors of the RBI, Chairman of SEBI, Directors of IB, R&AW, CBI, NIA, and heads of revenue agencies. Below the central EIC, **18 Regional Economic Intelligence Councils (REICs)** coordinate field-level intelligence sharing between income tax, customs, GST, and state police authorities. The CEIB maintains the **National Economic Offences Records (NEOR)** and manages the **Secured Information Exchange Network (SIEN)** for encrypted inter-agency financial dossiers.
* **Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI, 1957):** Operating under the **Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC)**, the DRI is India's apex anti-smuggling intelligence and enforcement agency. Enforcing the **Customs Act, 1962** and the **NDPS Act, 1985**, the DRI intercepts dual-use nuclear/ballistic missile proliferation cargo at maritime ports, dismantles gold and Fake Indian Currency Note (FICN) smuggling syndicates, exposes **Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML)** (where terror financiers over-invoice imports or under-invoice exports to move capital across borders without banking wire transfers), and collaborates with the Indian Coast Guard in deep-sea narcotics interdictions across the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal.
* **Financial Intelligence Unit–India (FIU-IND, 2004):** Established in **November 2004** as an independent body reporting directly to the **Economic Intelligence Council** to fulfill **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** standards, **FIU-IND** is NOT an investigating or arresting agency; rather, it is the central national repository and analytical filter under the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002**. Through its advanced **FINnet 2.0 (Financial Intelligence Network)** architecture, FIU-IND ingests millions of **Cash Transaction Reports (CTRs)**, **Cross-Border Wire Transfer Reports (CBWTRs)**, and **Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs)** from banks, NBFCs, casinos, real-estate registrars, and—since the landmark **March 2023 Ministry of Finance notification**—all domestic and offshore **Virtual Digital Asset (VDA) / Cryptocurrency Exchanges** serving Indian users. Once FIU-IND's algorithmic risk models isolate terror-financing or money-laundering anomalies, it disseminates actionable financial intelligence packages to the **ED**, **NIA**, **IB**, and **CBDT**.
* **Directorate of Enforcement (ED, 1956) and Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT):** While the **CBDT** (through its **Directorates General of Income Tax [Investigation]**) executes search-and-seizure raids under **Section 132 of the Income Tax Act, 1961**, uncovers domestic black-money generation, and enforces the **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Act, 2016**, the **ED** serves as the coercive financial spearhead. Armed with civil adjudicatory powers under the **Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999** (specifically utilized to penalize illegal foreign funding and hawala rackets) and stringent criminal prosecution and provisional property-attachment powers under the **PMLA, 2002** (Sections 5, 17, 19, and 50) and the **Fugitive Economic Offenders Act (FEOA), 2018**, the ED systematically dismantles the economic ecosystems of separatist groups (such as the All Parties Hurriyat Conference and Jamaat-e-Islami in J&K), Maoist extortion cartels, and transnational narco-terror syndicates.

### 3. Post-26/11 Inter-Agency Fusion, NATGRID, and Extraterritorial Counter-Terror Investigation (MAC, NATGRID, NIA)

The **November 26, 2008 (26/11) Mumbai terror attacks** exposed a catastrophic structural failure in India’s domestic security architecture: the **siloization of intelligence, database fragmentation, and federal investigative paralysis**. Prior to 26/11, although R&AW and the IB had intercepted multiple satellite-phone communications and coordinates concerning Lashkar-e-Taiba (LeT) maritime infiltration vessels (*Al-Husseini* and the hijacked Indian trawler *MV Kuber*), the absence of a mandatory, real-time fusion protocol meant that actionable alerts did not translate into synchronized operational interdiction by the Indian Navy, Coast Guard, and Maharashtra Police. Furthermore, because **"Police" and "Public Order" are exclusive State List subjects (Entries 1 and 2 of List II, Seventh Schedule)**, the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI)—governed by the **Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946**—could not investigate pan-Indian or cross-border terror conspiracies without obtaining prior **"general or case-specific consent"** from the concerned State Government under **Section 6 of the DSPE Act**.

To eliminate these three structural blind spots—intelligence hoarding, manual database querying delays, and federal jurisdictional dependence on state consent—the Union Government executed a comprehensive post-26/11 institutional overhaul anchored around three interconnected pillars: the revamped **Multi-Agency Centre (MAC)**, the **National Intelligence Grid (NATGRID)**, and the **National Investigation Agency (NIA)**.

#### Operational Mechanics of the Three Post-26/11 Pillars

1. **Multi-Agency Centre (MAC) and Subsidiary MACs (SMACs):** Originally established at the IB Headquarters in New Delhi in December 2001 following the Kargil Review Committee and 2001 GoM recommendations, MAC remained semi-dormant until the **December 31, 2008 Executive Order** issued by the MHA. This order legally bound **28 central intelligence, military, paramilitary, and revenue organizations** (including IB, R&AW, DIA, DMI, DNI, DAI, NTRO, NIA, CBI, BSF, CRPF, CISF, ITBP, SSB, Assam Rifles, Coast Guard, NCB, ED, DRI, FIU-IND, and Railway Protection Force) to share all terrorism-related intelligence on a mandatory **24x7 real-time basis**. MAC is linked to **Subsidiary Multi-Agency Centres (SMACs)** in all State capitals (integrating State Intelligence Branches and Anti-Terrorism Squads) and over **400 district-level connectivity nodes** across insurgency-affected theatres. Between **2024 and 2026**, the MHA operationalized the **MAC 2.0 Technical Upgradation**, integrating AI-assisted pattern recognition, darknet monitoring nodes, and cross-border drone/narcotics databases directly into the National Memory Bank.
2. **National Intelligence Grid (NATGRID):** Conceptualized in 2009 and fully operationalized across central agencies and State Directors General of Police (DGPs) by 2023–2025, **NATGRID** is an attached office of the MHA designed to eliminate the weeks-long bureaucratic latency required for investigating officers to obtain call records, bank statements, and flight manifests from disparate private and public entities. NATGRID does not store a monolithic centralized copy of citizen data; instead, it functions as a highly encrypted **Application Programming Interface (API) middleware bridge** linking **21+ categories of data providers** (including civil aviation PNR records, railway ticketing, passport/immigration IVFRT databases, telecom Call Detail Records [CDRs], banking Core Banking Solutions, credit card transactions, Income Tax PAN filings, GSTN, SEBI, and Vahan/Sarathi vehicle registries) with **11 authorized central user agencies** (IB, R&AW, NIA, CBI, ED, FIU-IND, NCB, DRI, CBDT, CBIC, and DG GST Intelligence) alongside designated **State Police DGP nodes**. Through NATGRID’s entity-resolution and link-analysis tools (integrated with the **National Automated Fingerprint Identification System - NAFIS**), an investigator tracking a terror suspect's alias can map their travel, telecom, and financial footprint across the entire country in seconds.
3. **National Investigation Agency (NIA — NIA Act, 2008 and the 2019 Amendment):** Enacted by Parliament in December 2008 via the **National Investigation Agency Act, 2008**, the NIA is India’s sole truly federal counter-terrorism law enforcement agency. Under **Section 6 of the NIA Act**, whenever a "Scheduled Offence" (offenses listed in the Schedule to the Act, including the UAPA 1967, Atomic Energy Act, Anti-Hijacking Act, Weapons of Mass Destruction Act, and specified chapters of the BNS/IPC) is committed, the Central Government can direct the NIA to take over the investigation **suo motu** across any State in India **without requiring the consent of the State Government**, exercising all powers and privileges of local police officers across India. To counter evolving transnational, cyber, and syndicate threats, Parliament enacted the landmark **National Investigation Agency (Amendment) Act, 2019**, which introduced three structural expansions:
   * **Extraterritorial Jurisdiction (Section 1(2) & Section 3(2)):** Empowered the NIA to investigate Scheduled Offences committed **outside India** against Indian citizens or affecting the interest of India (subject to international treaties and diplomatic conventions, enabling NIA probes into attacks on Indian diplomatic missions in London, San Francisco, and Ottawa, or terror plots hatched in Pakistan and Afghanistan).
   * **Expansion of Scheduled Offences:** Added four new categories of organized crime directly linked to terror ecosystems into the NIA Schedule: (i) **Human Trafficking** (formerly Sections 370 and 370A of the IPC, now **Sections 143 and 144 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**); (ii) Offenses related to **Counterfeit Currency or Bank Notes (FICN)** (now **Section 178–182 of the BNS**); (iii) Manufacture or sale of **Prohibited Arms** under **Section 25(1AA) of the Arms Act, 1959**; and (iv) **Cyber-Terrorism** under **Section 66F of the Information Technology Act, 2000**, alongside offenses under the **Explosive Substances Act, 1908**.
   * **Designation of Special Courts (Sections 11 & 22):** Empowered both the Central Government and State Governments to designate existing **Courts of Session directly as NIA Special Courts** without going through the lengthy process of notifying new judicial infrastructure in consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court, accelerating trial timelines.

## II. The Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs): Operational Mandates and Structural Friction

### 4. Taxonomy and Operational Architecture of the Seven Central Paramilitary Forces

In Indian administrative parlance, the term "Central Paramilitary Forces" was officially standardized by the Ministry of Home Affairs in **March 2011** to designate the **Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs)**—comprising five primary forces (**BSF, CRPF, CISF, ITBP, and SSB**) alongside the **National Security Guard (NSG)**—while the **Assam Rifles (AR)** is classified separately as a paramilitary force due to its unique dual command structure. Collectively numbering over **10.5 lakh personnel**, these seven forces bridge the critical spectrum between the **State Civil Police** (equipped for routine law and order and crime investigation) and the **Indian Armed Forces** (tasked with external conventional defense against state adversaries).

Structurally, the CAPFs are organized around four distinct functional pillars: (1) **Border Guarding Forces (BGFs)** deployed along India's 15,106 km terrestrial frontier under the post-Kargil **"One Border, One Force"** doctrine; (2) **Internal Security and Counter-Insurgency Forces**; (3) **Critical Infrastructure and Aviation Security Forces**; and (4) **Federal Zero-Error Counter-Terror Contingency Forces**.

#### Functional Deconstruction of the Seven Forces

* **Border Guarding Forces (BSF, ITBP, SSB, and Assam Rifles):**
  * **Border Security Force (BSF, 1965):** Raised on **December 1, 1965**, under K.F. Rustamji following Pakistan’s *Operation Desert Hawk* incursion in the Rann of Kutch (which exposed the inadequacy of state armed police battalions in guarding international borders) and governed by the **BSF Act, 1968**, the BSF (over 2.65 lakh personnel across 193 battalions) guards the **India-Pakistan International Border (IB)** (and segments of the LoC under Army operational control) and the **4,096 km India-Bangladesh Border**. It possesses dedicated **Water Wings** (for Sir Creek and the Sundarbans/Brahmaputra riverine chars), **Air Wings**, **Camel contingents** in the Thar Desert, and its own **Artillery Regiments**. In **October 2021**, the MHA issued a controversial notification under **Section 139 of the BSF Act** standardizing the BSF's territorial jurisdiction to conduct arrest, search, and seizure against trans-border smuggling, narcotics, and illegal entry up to **50 km inside the international border** in **Punjab, West Bengal, and Assam** (increasing it from 15 km in these three states while reducing it from 80 km to 50 km in Gujarat)—a move challenged by Punjab and West Bengal under Article 131 as an encroachment on State Police jurisdiction, though legally restricted solely to central statutes (Passport Act, NDPS Act, Customs Act, Arms Act) with mandatory handover of suspects to local police within 24 hours.
  * **Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP, 1962):** Raised on **October 24, 1962**, during the Sino-Indian War and governed by the **ITBP Force Act, 1992**, the ITBP is a specialized high-altitude mountaineering and ski-warfare force guarding the entire **3,488 km India-China Line of Actual Control (LAC)** across Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, and Arunachal Pradesh at altitudes ranging from 9,000 to 18,700 feet. In **2023–2025**, the Union Cabinet sanctioned **7 new ITBP battalions and a sector headquarters** (9,400 personnel) to man 47 new border outposts and 12 staging camps along the Arunachal Pradesh and Eastern Ladakh frontier in alignment with the **Vibrant Villages Programme**.
  * **Sashastra Seema Bal (SSB, 1963):** Originally raised as the **Special Service Bureau** in early 1963 under R&AW/Cabinet Secretariat for "stay-behind" guerrilla resistance in border populations in the event of another Chinese invasion, the SSB was transferred to the MHA in **2001** following the Kargil GoM Report and designated as the Border Guarding Force for the **1,751 km Open India-Nepal Border** (2001) and the **699 km Open India-Bhutan Border** (2004) under the **SSB Act, 2007**. Because citizens of Nepal and Bhutan enjoy visa-free movement under the 1950 and 1949 treaties, the SSB cannot rely on physical fencing; it relies on intelligence-led human screening, biometric surveillance, and anti-trafficking checkpoints to prevent third-country operatives (ISI modules, Chinese nationals, and Yaba/FICN couriers) from exploiting the open frontier.
  * **Assam Rifles (AR, 1835 — The Dual-Control Paradox):** Originally raised in **1835** as the *Cachar Levy* to protect British tea estates and frontier settlements, and governed by the **Assam Rifles Act, 2006**, the "Sentinels of the North East" (46 battalions) perform a dual role: guarding the **1,643 km India-Myanmar Border** and conducting counter-insurgency operations across the Northeast (most notably in Manipur, Nagaland, and South Arunachal). Uniquely among all central forces, the Assam Rifles operates under a **Dual-Control Structure**: **administrative, budgetary, and infrastructure control** (pay, pensions, clothing, barracks) vests with the **Ministry of Home Affairs**, whereas **operational command and leadership** vests with the **Indian Army (Ministry of Defence)**—with a three-star Army Lieutenant General serving as DG Assam Rifles at Shillong and over 80% of its officer cadre seconded from the regular Indian Army. This dual control has generated persistent institutional tug-of-war between the MHA (which has periodically proposed merging AR with the BSF or ITBP under IPS leadership) and the Indian Army (which insists that the 1,643 km Myanmar jungle frontier and Naga/Manipur counter-insurgency require seamless military integration under the Eastern Command).
* **Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF, 1939/1949 — Internal Security Backbone):** Raised on July 27, 1939, as the *Crown Representative's Police* at Neemuch to assist princely states, and renamed and given statutory backing under the **CRPF Act, 1949** by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, the CRPF is the world’s largest paramilitary force (**over 3.25 lakh personnel across 246+ battalions**). Acting as the Union's primary reserve strike force to assist States in maintaining public order, conducting Parliamentary and State Assembly elections, and prosecuting counter-insurgency campaigns across **Jammu & Kashmir, Left-Wing Extremist (LWE) theatres, and the Northeast**, the CRPF houses three specialized internal wings:
  1. **Commando Battalion for Resolute Action (CoBRA, 2008):** 10 specialized battalions trained in deep-jungle camouflage, long-range reconnaissance patrols, and IED counter-ambushes specifically engineered to dismantle the CPI (Maoist) PLGA in Dandakaranya, Abujhmad, and Jharkhand.
  2. **Rapid Action Force (RAF, 1992):** 15 blue-beret battalions stationed at strategic urban centers across India, equipped with non-lethal crowd dispersal munitions, tear-smoke launchers, polycarbonate riot shields, and psychosocial conflict-de-escalation training to quell communal riots and violent agitations within hours of requisition.
  3. **VIP Security Wing & Mahila Battalions:** The CRPF operates **6 exclusive Mahila (Women) Battalions** (having pioneered the world's first all-women paramilitary battalion in 1986) and commands a dedicated VIP Security Wing providing Z+ and ASL (Advanced Security Liaison) protection to top constitutional functionaries.
* **Central Industrial Security Force (CISF, 1969 — Critical Infrastructure and the 2024 Parliament Security Takeover):** Established under the **CISF Act, 1968** following the Justice B. Mukherjee Commission investigation into the devastating 1964 Heavy Engineering Corporation fire at Ranchi, and converted from a custodial watch-and-ward body into an **"Armed Force of the Union" via the 1983 Amendment**, the CISF (over 1.77 lakh personnel) provides integrated physical and technical security to **over 350 critical industrial and strategic installations**—including Department of Atomic Energy nuclear plants, ISRO space launch centers, defense production units, oil refineries, major seaports, the Delhi Metro, and **68+ domestic and international civil airports** (taken over after the December 1999 IC-814 Kandahar hijacking). Following the **Post-26/11 CISF Amendment Act, 2009**, the CISF was also empowered to provide paid security cover and security consultancy to **private-sector critical infrastructure** (such as Infosys campuses and Reliance Jamnagar refineries). In a historic structural shift triggered by the **December 13, 2023 Parliament security breach** (where intruders leaped into the Lok Sabha chamber with smoke canisters, exposing fragmented coordination between the Parliament Security Service, Delhi Police, and CRPF Parliament Duty Group), the Union Government placed the entire **Parliament House Complex under unified CISF command in May 2024**, deploying over 3,300 CISF personnel with aviation-grade X-ray baggage scanners, facial recognition gates, and unified access control.
* **National Security Guard (NSG, 1984 / NSG Act, 1986 — Federal Zero-Error Contingency Force):** Raised in **1984** following *Operation Blue Star* and the hijacking of Indian Airlines flights, and governed by the **National Security Guard Act, 1986**, the **NSG ("Black Cats")** is a 100% deputation-based federal contingency force modeled on Germany’s **GSG-9** and the UK’s **SAS**. Operating under the philosophy of *"Sarvatra Sarvottam Suraksha"* (Omnipresent, Omnipotent Security) with a "Zero Error" mandate, the NSG has no direct recruitment at the entry level; instead, it comprises two operational components:
  1. **Special Action Group (SAG — 51 SAG for Counter-Terrorism and 52 SAG for Anti-Hijacking):** The offensive strike spearhead, composed **100% of personnel deputed from the Indian Army**.
  2. **Special Ranger Group (SRG — 11, 12, and 13 SRG):** Tasked with outer-cordon sealing, tactical logistics, and high-risk VIP security, composed of personnel deputed from the **CAPFs (BSF, CRPF, ITBP, CISF, SSB)**.
  In addition to maintaining the **National Bomb Data Centre (NBDC)** at Manesar (which archives forensic signatures of every IED detonation across India and conducts post-blast investigations) and the **National Canine Training Centre**, the NSG overcame the critical deployment delay exposed during the 26/11 Mumbai attacks (when taking 9+ hours to airlift commandos from Manesar to Mumbai cost precious lives) by establishing **five permanent Regional Hubs** at **Mumbai (Marol), Chennai (Nedunkundram), Hyderabad (Trimulgherry), Kolkata (Badu)**, and **Gandhinagar (Gujarat)**, alongside positioning permanent strike teams in Srinagar and Ayodhya.

#### Diagnostic Breakdown of the Three Structural Fault Lines

1. **Chronic Operational Overstretch, Rest/Training Deficit, and Attrition:** Under standard counter-insurgency and border management doctrines (and the **214th Report of the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs**), every CAPF battalion requires a structured **"Peace-to-Field" rotation** alongside at least **15% to 20% Reserve Battalions** undergoing mandatory rest, recuperation, and tactical retraining. In practice, because State Governments chronically under-invest in their own State Armed Police battalions and reflexively requisition central forces for every local law-and-order crisis, religious festival, caste agitation, and continuous cycle of Parliamentary, State Assembly, and Panchayat elections, the CAPFs operate at **nearly 100% hard deployment saturation**. Reserve battalions have been cannibalized into active duty, forcing soldiers to move directly from high-altitude LAC posts or Bastar anti-Naxal grids into multi-month election duties in sleeping bags inside school buildings. Although the MHA introduced a policy targeting **100 days of annual leave/family time** for jawans, acute manpower deficits and continuous transit prevent its ground implementation. This relentless operational tempo—compounded by prolonged separation from families, sleep deprivation, and the inability to resolve domestic land/family disputes—has produced an alarming mental health crisis: between 2018 and 2025, **over 800 CAPF personnel died by suicide or fratricide**, vastly exceeding operational combat fatalities in several years, alongside over **50,000 voluntary retirements and resignations** over the past five years.
2. **The OGAS Cadre Stagnation vs. IPS Deputation Monopoly (*Sanjay Prakash v. Union of India*, May 2025):** Unlike the Indian Army, Navy, and Air Force—where an officer commissioned as a Lieutenant rises through the ranks to become Chief of Army Staff without any external bureaucrat parachuting into command—the five primary CAPFs (**CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP, SSB**) suffer from a dual-track leadership schism. At the base of the officer pyramid are **over 13,000 directly recruited Group 'A' Executive Cadre Officers**, selected through the highly competitive **UPSC Central Armed Police Forces (Assistant Commandants) Examination**. These officers lead platoons and companies in lethal ambushes in Abujhmad, Rajouri, and Galwan for decades. However, at the top of the pyramid, archaic MHA Recruitment Rules reserved **100% of Director General (DG) posts**, up to **75% of Additional Director General (ADG) posts**, **50% of Inspector General (IG) posts**, and **20% of Deputy Inspector General (DIG) posts** for **Indian Police Service (IPS) officers on deputation**.
   * **The Stagnation Impact:** Because the top echelons of the pyramid are blocked by lateral IPS deputations (who rotate in for 3-to-5-year tenures without having served a single day as platoon or company commanders in that paramilitary force), directly recruited CAPF Assistant Commandants face catastrophic career stagnation—waiting **14 to 16 years just for their first promotion to Deputy Commandant** (whereas an IPS officer reaches Selection Grade/DIG within 13–14 years), with the vast majority of CAPF cadre officers retiring at the rank of Commandant or DIG without ever commanding their own force.
   * **The 2019–2026 Judicial Arc (*Harananda* to *Sanjay Prakash*):** In **February 2019 (*Union of India v. Harananda*)**, the Supreme Court upheld the 2015 Delhi High Court verdict granting **Organised Group 'A' Service (OGAS)** status and **Non-Functional Financial Upgradation (NFFU)** to CAPF Group 'A' officers. However, the MHA interpreted *Harananda* narrowly, granting only financial pay-scale upgradation (NFFU) while refusing to amend service rules or reduce IPS deputation quotas. Consequently, on **May 23, 2025**, in the landmark verdict of ***Sanjay Prakash & Ors. v. Union of India***, a Supreme Court Bench of **Justice Abhay S. Oka and Justice Ujjal Bhuyan** delivered a decisive structural intervention:
     * The Court held that the five CAPFs are **Organised Group 'A' Services (OGAS) for "all purposes"**, not merely for NFFU, recognizing the sacrifices of cadre officers in safeguarding borders and internal security.
     * It directed that the **number of posts earmarked for IPS deputation up to the Senior Administrative Grade (SAG / Rank of Inspector General) in the CAPFs must be progressively reduced over a period of time, within an outer limit of two years**, so that CAPF cadre officers participate in the decision-making hierarchy of their forces.
     * It mandated the Union Government to complete the long-pending **Cadre Review and revision of existing Service/Recruitment Rules across all CAPFs within six months**, after giving due hearing to CAPF cadre representatives.
     * Following the dismissal of the Centre’s Review Petition on **October 28, 2025**, the Supreme Court initiated stringent **contempt scrutiny in September–October 2026** against the MHA for stalling the six-month cadre review timeline and continuing to induct IPS officers on deputation into SAG-level CAPF posts.
3. **Pension Parity Litigation (OPS vs. NPS vs. UPS) and Ex-Servicemen Status:** Because the regular Indian Armed Forces (Army, Navy, Air Force) were exempted when the Union Government replaced the **Old Pension Scheme (OPS)** with the market-linked **National Pension System (NPS)** from **January 1, 2004**, whereas CAPF personnel joining after 2004 were placed under the NPS despite performing analogous combat duties and being legally defined as *"Armed Forces of the Union"* under their respective Parliamentary Acts, severe resentment and litigation erupted. In **January 2023 (*Pawan Kumar & Ors. v. Union of India*)**, the **Delhi High Court** ruled that since CAPFs are explicitly designated as "Armed Forces of the Union," they are entitled to the Old Pension Scheme on par with the military (a verdict subsequently stayed/appealed before the Supreme Court). To defuse this fiscal-structural friction across central services, the Union Government notified the **Unified Pension Scheme (UPS)** (effective **April 1, 2025**), guaranteeing an assured pension of **50% of the average basic pay drawn in the last 12 months prior to superannuation** (for a minimum qualifying service of 25 years) with full dearness relief indexing, while granting the Welfare and Rehabilitation Board (**WARB**) enhanced financial allocations for "Ex-Central Armed Police Force Personnel" (**Ex-CAPF**).

## III. Police Institutional Modernization: From Colonial Regime-Maintenance to SMART Policing

### 6. The Colonial Architecture of the Police Act of 1861 vs. The Soli Sorabjee Model Police Act (2006)

The foundational crisis of domestic law enforcement across India's 28 States stems from an unredressed historical anomaly: **over seven decades after the adoption of a democratic Republican Constitution, the primary organizational statute governing state policing remains the colonial Police Act of 1861 (or state statutes modeled directly on its template).** Enacted by the British Imperial Legislature in the immediate aftermath of the **1857 Revolt** on the recommendations of the **1860 Police Commission (headed by M.H. Court)** and patterned directly after the **Royal Irish Constabulary**, the **Police Act of 1861** was engineered with a single overriding strategic objective: to create an inexpensive, politically subservient, paramilitary **regime-maintenance force** designed to intimidate, surveil, and subjugate a colonized population at the behest of the colonial executive, while insulating the ruling administration from any accountability to the local citizenry.

#### Structural Pathologies of the 1861 Colonial Template vs. The Sorabjee Architecture

* **The Trap of "Superintendence" (Section 4 of the Police Act, 1861):** Section 4 of the 1861 Act declares that the *"superintendence of the police throughout a general police-district shall vest in and shall be exercised by the State Government."* Because the 1861 Act deliberately left the word **"superintendence"** undefined, post-independence state political executives weaponized this ambiguity to extend legitimate policy oversight into illegitimate **micro-management of operational investigations**—dictating whom to arrest, whom to shield, which First Information Reports (FIRs) to register or bury, and using the power of arbitrary, overnight transfers and postings as a coercive lever to enforce political compliance. Even when the Supreme Court in ***Vineet Narain v. Union of India* (1997)** clarified that "superintendence" must stop at the threshold of statutory crime investigation (where an investigating officer is answerable solely to the CrPC/BNSS and the courts, following the UK *Lord Denning dictum* in *R v. Metropolitan Police Commissioner ex parte Blackburn* [1968]), state politicians continued to subvert operational autonomy through transfer-posting regimes.
* **The District Dual-Control Friction (Section 4 Paragraph 2):** Outside cities equipped with the **Police Commissionerate System** (where powers of executive magistracy under CrPC/BNSS regarding preventive arrests, Section 144/163 prohibitory orders, licensing, and riot control are vested directly in the Commissioner of Police), the 1861 Act places the District Superintendent of Police (SP) under the *"general control and direction"* of the **District Magistrate (DM / Collector)**. Born from colonial distrust of native police officers and the concentration of imperial authority in the Collector, this dual control creates operational delays during communal riots and blurs accountability between the IAS District Magistrate and the IPS Superintendent of Police.
* **The Inverted Constabulary Pyramid and Fiscal Starvation:** Over **85% to 88% of India's state police strength** consists of the **Constabulary** (Constables and Head Constables), who serve as the first point of contact for citizens yet were designed under the 1861 Act as unskilled "lathi-wielding" orderlies rather than investigative professionals, suffering from 12-to-16-hour workdays, lack of weekly offs, and severe housing shortages. Compounding this, India’s **sanctioned police-to-population ratio stands at roughly 196 per 100,000 citizens**, while the **actual working strength hovers around 152–155 per 100,000** (compared to the **United Nations recommended benchmark of 222 per 100,000**), with over **5.3 lakh vacancies** persisting across State Police forces and less than **3% to 5% of state police budgets** available for capital modernization after meeting salary and pension commitments.
* **The Soli Sorabjee Model Police Act (2006):** Following the eight exhaustive reports of the **National Police Commission (NPC, 1977–1981)** chaired by Dharma Vira, the **Ribeiro Committee (1998)**, and the **Padmanabhaiah Committee (2000)**—all of which were ignored by State Governments—the Ministry of Home Affairs constituted the **Police Act Drafting Committee (PADC) in 2005 chaired by former Attorney General Soli Sorabjee**. Submitting the **Model Police Act in October 2006**, the Sorabjee Committee sought to replace the 1861 colonial architecture with a modern statute anchored in four principles:
  1. **Transition from "Police Force" to "Police Service":** Redefining the police as a professional public service agency upholding constitutional rights, protecting vulnerable groups (women, SC/STs, minorities), and practicing democratic policing.
  2. **Functional Autonomy Balanced by Democratic Accountability:** Vesting operational command strictly in the DGP while limiting the State Government's "superintendence" to laying down broad policy guidelines and Strategic Policing Plans via a bipartisan **State Police Board**.
  3. **Separation of Crime Investigation from Law and Order:** Creating dedicated, forensically trained Crime Investigation Units in urban and rural police stations that cannot be diverted to VIP bandobast or riot control.
  4. **Upgrading the Constabulary and Working Conditions:** Eliminating orderly duties, mandating maximum 8-hour shift cycles, establishing transparent merit-based promotions from Constable to Assistant Sub-Inspector, and creating an independent **State Police Accountability Commission**.

### 7. Judicial Constitutionalism vs. Executive Defiance: Prakash Singh v. Union of India (2006) and the Seven Directives

Because State Legislatures systematically refused to enact the recommendations of the National Police Commission (1979–81), former BSF and Assam DGP **Prakash Singh** filed a historic Public Interest Litigation under Article 32 in 1996. On **September 22, 2006**, in the landmark judgment of ***Prakash Singh & Ors. v. Union of India* [(2006) 8 SCC 1]**, a three-judge Supreme Court Bench headed by Chief Justice Y.K. Sabharwal invoked **Article 142 of the Constitution** (to do "complete justice" in a legislative vacuum) and issued **Seven Binding Directives** to the Union and all State Governments—designed to operate as the law of the land until appropriate legislation was enacted.

However, the post-2006 trajectory of the *Prakash Singh* judgment offers the most striking empirical case study of **executive resistance and "malicious compliance" in Indian federalism**. Rather than openly defying the Supreme Court and risking contempt proceedings, **17+ States hastily enacted new State Police Acts (or amended existing ones) between 2007 and 2018 whose explicit purpose was to institutionalize political control under a veneer of statutory compliance**—packing State Security Commissions with ruling-party ministers, inserting vague clauses allowing premature transfers on "administrative grounds," and appointing pliant officers as **"Acting DGPs"** on the eve of their retirement to bypass UPSC empanelment and fixed two-year tenures.

## IV. The July 2024 Criminal Justice Overhaul (BNS, BNSS, BSA), Mandatory Forensics, and Digital ICJS

### 8. Statutory Codification of Asymmetric Threats: Terrorism (BNS Section 113) and Organized Crime (BNS Section 111)

Alongside the **SMART Policing** framework articulated at the **November 2014 Guwahati DGP/IGP Conference**—envisioning a police force that is **S**trict and **S**ensitive, **M**odern and **M**obile, **A**lert and **A**ccountable, **R**eliable and **R**esponsive, and **T**echno-savvy and **T**rained—the most profound structural transformation of India's internal security legal architecture occurred on **July 1, 2024**, with the enforcement of three foundational criminal statutes replacing the colonial-era triad:
1. The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** (replacing the **Indian Penal Code [IPC], 1860**);
2. The **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (replacing the **Code of Criminal Procedure [CrPC], 1973**); and
3. The **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023** (replacing the **Indian Evidence Act [IEA], 1872**).

From a national security and counter-insurgency standpoint, the **Indian Penal Code of 1860** suffered from a glaring structural vacuum: drafted by Lord Macaulay in the mid-19th century, **the IPC contained zero definition or substantive offense for either "Terrorism" or "Organized Crime."** Consequently, for over seven decades, Indian law enforcement had to rely either on special central anti-terror laws (TADA 1987, POTA 2002, and the **Unlawful Activities [Prevention] Act, 1967 [UAPA]**) or a patchwork of asymmetric state-level organized crime statutes (such as the **Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act [MCOCA], 1999**, GUJCTOC in Gujarat, KCOCA in Karnataka, and UPCOCA in Uttar Pradesh), leaving states without a dedicated MCOCA-style law forced to prosecute transnational narco-extortion syndicates, contract-killing networks, and cyber-slavery cartels under weak, generic IPC "criminal conspiracy" (Section 120B) or "extortion" (Section 384) provisions.

The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** closed this structural vacuum by directly embedding **"Organised Crime" (Section 111)**, **"Petty Organised Crime" (Section 112)**, **"Terrorist Act" (Section 113)**, and **"Acts Endangering Sovereignty, Unity and Integrity of India" (Section 152)** into the general penal code of the Republic.

#### Granular Statutory Analysis of BNS Sections 111, 112, 113, and 152

* **BNS Section 113 ("Terrorist Act") and the Dual-Track Constitutional Debate:** Section 113(1) of the BNS imports the exact statutory definition of a "Terrorist Act" from **Section 15 of the UAPA, 1967** into ordinary criminal law: punishing anyone who does any act in India or in any foreign country with the intent to threaten or likely to threaten the **unity, integrity, sovereignty, security, or economic security of India**, or with the intent to strike terror in the people, by using bombs, dynamite, firearms, lethal weapons, poisonous/biological/radioactive/nuclear substances, or by damaging **public property / critical infrastructure**, or by smuggling or circulating **High-Quality Counterfeit Indian Paper Currency, coin, or any other material** (destabilizing monetary stability), or detaining any person to compel the Government of India or a State Government to do or abstain from doing any act.
  * **Punishment Matrix (Sub-sections 2 to 6):** Committing a terrorist act resulting in death carries the **death penalty or life imprisonment**; otherwise, **5 years to life imprisonment**. Section 113(3)–(6) criminalizes conspiracy, terror training camps, membership in a terrorist organization, harboring terrorists, and holding proceeds of terrorism.
  * **The Section 113(7) SP-Rank Gatekeeper vs. Article 14 Arbitrariness Concerns:** Because both **BNS Section 113** and **UAPA Section 15** now criminalize the exact same substantive conduct using virtually identical statutory text, yet operate under drastically different **procedural regimes**, Section 113(7) inserts a procedural filter: *"An officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police shall decide whether to register the case under this section or under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967."* While this allows district police to prosecute localized terror modules under BNS Section 113 without awaiting central/state government prosecution sanction required under **Section 45 of the UAPA**, constitutional jurists point out a severe **competing-forces tension between investigative speed and Article 14 equal protection**: if an SP registers an offense under **UAPA**, the accused faces up to **180 days of pre-charge detention**, trial by a **Special NIA Court**, and the near-insurmountable statutory bail bar of **Section 43D(5) of the UAPA** (*Union of India v. K.A. Najeeb* / *Watali* doctrine); whereas if the SP registers the identical act under **BNS Section 113**, the case proceeds under standard **BNSS** bail provisions and regular Sessions Courts without requiring independent Review Committee scrutiny under Section 45(2) of the UAPA. Furthermore, whereas **BNSS Section 187(2)-(3)** permits police custody (up to 15 days in the whole or in parts) to be sought in non-continuous tranches across the **first 40 or 60 days** of the total 60/90-day detention period (upending the 1992 *CBI v. Anupam J. Kulkarni* rule that barred police custody after the initial 15 days), pairing BNS Section 113 with staggered BNSS police remand significantly expands state interrogation leverage.
* **BNS Section 111 ("Organised Crime") and Section 112 ("Petty Organised Crime"):** Section 111 establishes India's first uniform, pan-national statutory weapon against organized crime syndicates. It defines **Organised Crime** as any **"continuing unlawful activity"**—including kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing (*supari*), **economic offence**, **cyber-crimes** (specifically targeting ransomware, financial fraud, and digital arrest syndicates), trafficking of persons, drugs, weapons, or illicit goods/services, or human trafficking racketeering for prostitution or ransom—carried out by an individual or group acting either singly or jointly as a member of, or on behalf of, an **organised crime syndicate**, by use of violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion, or other unlawful means to obtain direct or indirect material/financial benefit.
  * An **Organised Crime Syndicate** is defined as a group of two or more persons who, acting either singly or collectively as a syndicate or gang, indulge in any continuing unlawful activity, where **"continuing unlawful activity"** requires that **more than one charge-sheet** has been filed before a competent court within the **preceding period of ten years** and the court has taken cognizance of such offense.
  * **Section 112 (Petty Organised Crime)** bridges the street-level enforcement gap by punishing organized gangs that commit snatching, shoplifting, theft, unauthorized ticket selling, illegal betting/gambling, ATM theft, or **public examination paper-leak rackets** with imprisonment from **1 to 7 years**.
* **BNS Section 152 (Replacing IPC Section 124A Sedition with Sovereignty Protection):** Following the Supreme Court’s **May 2022 interim order in *S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India*** putting colonial **Section 124A IPC (Sedition)** in abeyance, the BNS omitted the word "sedition" entirely—shifting the statutory anchor from protecting the "Government established by law" against disaffection to protecting the **Sovereignty, Unity, and Integrity of the Indian State**. **Section 152 of the BNS** penalizes whoever, purposely or knowingly, by words (spoken or written), signs, visible representation, **electronic communication**, or by **use of financial means**, excites or attempts to excite **secession, armed rebellion, or subversive activities**, or encourages **feelings of separatist activities**, or endangers the sovereignty or unity and integrity of India, with **imprisonment for life or up to 7 years**. By explicitly incorporating *electronic communication* and *financial means*, Section 152 directly targets cross-border cognitive warfare, Khalistani/insurgent diaspora crowdfunding, and encrypted secessionist propaganda networks.

### 9. Scientific Investigation by Statute: BNSS Section 176(3), the National Forensic Infrastructure Enhancement Scheme (NFIES 2024), and NFSU

Historically, the Achilles' heel of India's internal security and counter-terrorism prosecutions has been a dismal **conviction rate** in complex cases—driven not by lack of intelligence, but by **corrupted crime scenes, broken chains of custody, delayed forensic laboratory reports, and over-reliance on ocular witnesses (who turn hostile under syndicate intimidation) and custodial confessions**. To force a structural transition from *"third-degree custodial interrogation"* to *"scientific forensic-led prosecution,"* the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** and the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023** enacted mandatory statutory forensics backed by a multi-thousand-crore physical infrastructure build-out.

#### Statutory and Infrastructure Mechanics of the Forensic Overhaul

* **Mandatory Forensic Crime-Scene Investigation (BNSS Section 176(3)):** Under **Section 176(3) of the BNSS**, on receipt of information relating to the commission of an **offense which is made punishable for seven years or more**, the officer-in-charge of a police station **shall** cause the **forensic expert to visit the crime scene to collect forensic evidence** in the offense and also **cause videography of the process on a mobile phone or other electronic device**. Recognizing that several remote districts initially lacked full forensic laboratory coverage in July 2024, the statute provided a maximum five-year phased transition window while authorizing States to utilize forensic facilities of another State until local capacity is saturated.
* **Mandatory Audio-Video Recording of Searches and Seizures (BNSS Sections 105 & 185):** A historic vulnerability in counter-insurgency, UAPA, and Arms Act prosecutions has been defense allegations that police/security forces "planted" weapons, IEDs, or seditious literature during unrecorded midnight raids, leading to acquittals when independent *panch* witnesses turn hostile. **Section 105 and Section 185(2) of the BNSS** eliminate both false implication by rogue officers and bogus retraction by accused operatives: the entire process of conducting any search of a place or taking possession of any property, article, or thing **shall be recorded through any audio-video electronic means (preferably by mobile phone)**, including the preparation of the seizure list, and the police officer **shall without delay forward such recording to the District Magistrate, Sub-divisional Magistrate, or Judicial Magistrate of the first class**.
* **Specimen Signatures, Voice Samples, and Fingerprints (BNSS Section 349):** Expanding the investigative toolkit beyond the **Criminal Procedure (Identification) Act, 2022** (which empowered police to collect iris scans, retina scans, biological samples, and behavioral attributes to be stored by the **NCRB for 75 years**), **Section 349 of the BNSS** empowers a Magistrate of the First Class to order **any person** (even a suspect who has not been previously arrested in connection with the investigation) to provide **specimen signatures, handwriting, voice samples, or finger impressions** for forensic comparison.
* **The National Forensic Infrastructure Enhancement Scheme (NFIES, June 2024) and NFSU (2020):** Because enacting mandatory forensic visits under BNSS Section 176(3) without expanding laboratory capacity would paralyze the criminal justice system (given that India previously faced a 40%+ vacancy rate across State FSLs and an acute deficit of trained forensic scientists), the Union Cabinet on **June 19, 2024**, approved the **National Forensic Infrastructure Enhancement Scheme (NFIES)** as a **100% centrally funded Central Sector Scheme** under the Ministry of Home Affairs with a total financial outlay of **₹2,254.43 crore from FY 2024–25 to FY 2028–29**. Anchored around the **National Forensic Sciences University (NFSU)** (established as an Institution of National Importance at Gandhinagar under the **NFSU Act, 2020**), NFIES comprises three core infrastructure verticals:
  1. **Establishment of 9 New Off-Campus Centres of the NFSU** (allocated ₹1,309.13 crore across states including Maharashtra/Nagpur, Chhattisgarh/Raipur, Odisha/Khordha, Tamil Nadu/Chengalpattu, Rajasthan/Jaipur, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh/Varanasi, Andhra Pradesh, and West Bengal) to train **over 36,000 forensic specialists annually** in cyber-forensics, ballistic fingerprinting, DNA profiling, drone forensics, and crypto-tracing;
  2. **Establishment of 7 New Central Forensic Science Laboratories (CFSLs)** (allocated ₹860.30 crore across Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Tamil Nadu, Rajasthan, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, and Kerala, largely co-located with the new NFSU campuses) to clear the national forensic casework backlog; and
  3. **Upgradation of the existing NFSU Delhi Campus** (₹85 crore) alongside the deployment of **Mobile Forensic Vans (MFVs)** equipped with 14+ on-the-spot forensic testing kits across every district in India.

#### Operational Architecture of CCTNS 2.0 and the ICJS Digital Applications

* **CCTNS 2.0 (Crime and Criminal Tracking Network and Systems) and NAFIS:** Operating under the **Nationalweg Crime Records Bureau (NCRB)**, **CCTNS** connects **100% of India’s 17,130+ sanctioned Police Stations** across all 28 States and 8 Union Territories into a unified national core database containing over 32 crore crime and criminal records. Under **CCTNS 2.0**, police stations are linked in real time with the **National Automated Fingerprint Identification System (NAFIS)**—a searchable database of over **1.06 crore 10-digit fingerprint records** assigning every arrested offender a unique **10-digit National Fingerprint Number (NFN)** for life—and **Cri-MAC (Crime Multi-Agency Centre)**, which flashes instant alerts across state borders regarding human trafficking rings, inter-state robbery syndicates, and absconding extremists.
* **The Three Flagship Mobile & Cloud Platforms Enforcing the Sanhitas (`e-Sakshya`, `NyayShruti`, and `e-Forensics`):** Developed by the **National Informatics Centre (NIC)** and scaled nationwide in 2024–2026 under **ICJS Phase II** (operating on the principle of *"One Data, One Entry"* so that data entered at a police station flows seamlessly to courts, prosecutors, prisons, and forensic labs without manual re-typing), three specialized digital systems eliminate legacy bottlenecks:
  1. **`e-Sakshya` (Electronic Evidence Capture & Cloud Chain-of-Custody Application):** Engineered specifically to execute **BNSS Section 105, Section 176(3), and Section 185**, the `e-Sakshya` mobile app enables any investigating officer at a crime scene to record continuous videography of the site, photograph physical clues, record the video testimony of witnesses, and upload a selfie confirming the officer's physical presence. Crucially, the moment the recording stops, `e-Sakshya` embeds immutable **GPS coordinates, date-time stamps, and a cryptographic SHA-256 hash value**, uploading the encrypted packet directly to the **NIC National Cloud** and generating a standardized **BSA Section 63 Certificate** that magistrates and trial judges can pull directly into the court docket using the FIR number—completely eliminating SD-card tampering or memory-stick loss in police *malkhanas*.
  2. **`NyayShruti` (Audio-Video Electronic Trial and Testimony Platform):** Anchored in **Section 530 of the BNSS** (which mandates that all trials, inquiries, and proceedings may be held in electronic mode) and **Sections 254, 308, and 355**, `NyayShruti` creates a secure virtual courtroom bridge linking Judicial Magistrates and Sessions Judges with police stations, hospitals, forensic laboratories, and prisons.
