# Part 4: Ethno-Nationalist Insurgencies in the Northeast: The Identity-Territory Paradox, Peace Accords, Manipur, and AFSPA

## I. Colonial Frontier Cartography, The Identity-Territory Paradox, and the Naga Insurgency Matrix

### 1. Colonial Isolationism and the Structural Genesis of the Identity-Territory Paradox

The security architecture of India’s Northeast is governed by a structural friction between **Westphalian territoriality**—which demands hard, mutually exclusive administrative boundaries and an undivided monopoly over legitimate violence—and **pre-colonial ethno-spatial fluidity**, wherein hundreds of distinct tribal and sub-national communities historically occupied overlapping ecological niches across hill ridges and alluvial valleys. Unlike Left-Wing Extremism in the central Indian heartland, which seeks the ideological overthrow of the parliamentary state, insurgencies in the Northeast have historically operated along a spectrum ranging from outright secessionist sovereignty (**NNC**, **ULFA**) to irredentist homeland consolidation (**Greater Nagalim**, **Zale’n-gam**) and sub-state constitutional autonomy (**Bodoland**, **Karbi Anglong**, **Frontier Nagaland**).

This structural fault line was institutionalized by British colonial frontier engineering, which bifurcated the region into revenue-generating plains (tea plantations, timber, oil, and wet-rice valleys) and non-revenue-yielding, topographically hostile hill tracts. Rather than extending uniform civil and criminal administration across the hills, the colonial state erected legal barriers designed to insulate commercial plains investments from tribal raids while governing the hills through indirect rule and customary chieftainships.

The legal and institutional mechanics of this colonial-to-constitutional transition operate through four foundational mechanisms:

*   **The Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation (BEFR), 1873 and the Inner Line Permit (ILP):** Enacted to protect British commercial monopolies in tea, oil, and elephant trade in the Assam plains, Section 2 of the **BEFR 1873** empowered the executive to prescribe an **"Inner Line"** beyond which no non-indigenous British subject could pass without a formal license (**Inner Line Permit**), nor could any outsider acquire land or forest produce beyond that line. In independent India, this colonial instrument was repurposed as a protective demographic shield for indigenous hill tribes against plains migration, currently operating across **Arunachal Pradesh**, **Nagaland**, **Mizoram**, and—following the **December 2019** Presidential Order alongside the Citizenship (Amendment) Act—**Manipur**.
*   **Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas (Government of India Act, 1935):** Building upon the "Backward Tracts" classification of the Montague-Chelmsford Reforms (1919), the **Government of India Act, 1935** designated frontier hill zones into two tiers: **Excluded Areas** (including the Naga Hills District, Lushai Hills District, North Cachar Hills, and the Northeast Frontier Tracts), which were completely removed from the purview of the provincial legislature and placed under the direct personal discretion of the Governor; and **Partially Excluded Areas** (such as the Garo Hills, Khasi and Jaintia Hills, and Mikir Hills), where provincial ministers exercised limited authority subject to the Governor’s overriding veto. This administrative segregation prevented the emergence of a shared political consciousness between the plains and the hills during the Indian national movement.
*   **The Sixth Schedule and Article 371 Asymmetric Architecture:** To integrate these alienated frontier populations without triggering violent cultural assimilation, the Constituent Assembly—guided by the **Bordoloi Sub-Committee (North-East Frontier Tribal and Excluded Areas Sub-Committee)**—crafted the **Sixth Schedule** (**Articles 244(2) and 275(1)**), creating **Autonomous District Councils (ADCs)** in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram with legislative, executive, and judicial powers over land, non-reserved forests, village administration, and customary law. Simultaneously, **Article 371A** (Nagaland) and **Article 371G** (Mizoram) stipulate that no Act of Parliament relating to religious or social practices, customary law and procedure, administration of civil and criminal justice involving customary law, and ownership and transfer of land and its resources shall apply to these states unless approved by a resolution of the respective State Legislative Assembly.

#### Overlapping Ethnic Homelands vs. Westphalian Territoriality

While asymmetric federalism resolved first-order secessionist pressures in several pockets, it inadvertently incentivized the **Identity-Territory Paradox**. Because statehood and **Sixth Schedule** Autonomous Councils link political power, central fiscal transfers, and land ownership to exclusive ethnic territoriality, every micro-ethnic group has been structurally incentivized to carve out an exclusive homeland. However, demographic settlement patterns in the Northeast are deeply interspersed rather than cartographically segregated. Consequently, the territorial maximalism of one insurgent movement directly encroaches upon the existential living space of neighboring communities—Turning every bilateral peace negotiation between New Delhi and an insurgent group into a potential trigger for horizontal inter-ethnic warfare.

#### A.Z. Phizo’s NNC, the 1975 Shillong Accord, and the NSCN Fragmentation

The operational and political evolution of the Naga insurgency unfolded through distinct structural phases:

1.  **The NNC Secessionist Phase and the 1963 Statehood Compromise:** Formed in **1946** out of the colonial-era Naga Club (1918), the **Naga National Council (NNC)** fell under the militant leadership of **Angami Zapu Phizo**, who Rejected the June 1947 **Hydari Nine-Point Agreement** over the interpretation of Clause 9 (whether Nagas had the right to choose independence after a 10-year interim period). The NNC declared unilateral independence on **August 14, 1947**, organized an extra-constitutional "plebiscite" in **1951**, and in **1956** established an underground parallel government—the **Federal Government of Nagaland (FGN)**—alongside an armed wing, the Naga Safe Guards/Naga Home Guards, prompting New Delhi to deploy the Indian Army and enact the **Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958**. While the Union Government successfully co-opted moderate Naga leaders via the **16-Point Agreement (1960)** with the Naga People's Convention—culminating in the creation of the **State of Nagaland on December 1, 1963**, protected by **Article 371A**—Phizo’s underground faction continued kinetic guerrilla operations with Chinese and Pakistani material support.
2.  **The Shillong Accord (1975) and the Birth of the NSCN (1980):** Sustained counter-insurgency pressure and the loss of East Pakistani sanctuaries after the 1971 liberation of Bangladesh forced a section of the NNC/FGN leadership to sign the **Shillong Accord on November 11, 1975**, wherein the underground signatories unconditionally accepted the Constitution of India and agreed to deposit their arms. However, a transnational delegation returning from China—led by **Thuingaleng Muivah** (a Tangkhul Naga from Ukhrul, Manipur), **Isak Chishi Swu** (a Sumi Naga from Nagaland), and **S.S. Khaplang** (a Hemi Naga from Myanmar)—denounced the **Shillong Accord** as a capitulation and formed the **National Socialist Council of Nagaland (NSCN)** on **January 31, 1980**, blending evangelical Christian nationalism (*"Nagaland for Christ"*) with Maoist one-party revolutionary socialism.
3.  **The 1988 Tribal-Ideological Split (NSCN-IM vs. NSCN-K):** On **April 30, 1988**, deep inter-tribal suspicions and disagreements over entering dialogue with New Delhi fractured the NSCN into two violent rivals: **NSCN (Isak-Muivah)**, heavily anchored among the Tangkhul Nagas of Manipur’s hill districts and southern Nagaland tribes, and **NSCN (Khaplang)**, anchored among the Konyak Nagas of eastern Nagaland and trans-border Naga tribes residing in Myanmar’s Sagaing region. While **NSCN-IM** signed a formal **Ceasefire Agreement with the Government of India in July 1997** (effective August 1, 1997), **NSCN-K** entered a ceasefire in 2001 only to unilaterally abrogate it in **March 2015** (executing the deadly June 2015 Chandel ambush on the 6 Dogra Regiment), after which it splintered into the anti-talk **NSCN-K (Yung Aung)** faction based in Myanmar and pro-talk factions led by **Niki Sumi** and **Khango Konyak** that re-entered ceasefire agreements with the Union Government.

#### Greater Nagalim, the 2015 Framework Agreement, and the 2017 NNPG Agreed Position

The core cartographic driver of the **NSCN-IM** is the irredentist demand for **Greater Nagalim**—the administrative unification of all Naga-inhabited contiguous areas across four Indian states (**Nagaland**, the hill districts of **Manipur** [Ukhrul, Senapati, Tamenglong, Chandel], the **Tirap, Changlang, and Longding [TCL]** districts of **Arunachal Pradesh**, and parts of **Assam** [Karbi Anglong, Dima Hasao, Sivasagar, Jorhat]) alongside Naga-inhabited tracts of **northwestern Myanmar** ( encompassing an estimated 1,20,000 sq km compared to Nagaland's current 16,579 sq km). Because any physical redrawing of state boundaries would trigger catastrophic counter-insurgencies in Manipur, Assam, and Arunachal Pradesh, the Union Government has sought to replace physical territorial integration with non-territorial cultural and pan-Naga statutory bodies.

*   **The 2015 Naga Framework Agreement (August 3, 2015):** Signed between the Government of India’s Interlocutor (R.N. Ravi) and **NSCN-IM** general secretary Thuingaleng Muivah, the **Framework Agreement** acknowledged the *"unique history and position of the Nagas"* and introduced the concept of **"shared sovereignty"**—envisioning a division of competencies within the Indian constitutional framework alongside pan-Naga cultural councils across state boundaries without physically altering the territorial boundaries of Manipur, Assam, or Arunachal Pradesh.
*   **The 2017 Agreed Position with NNPGs (November 17, 2017):** Recognizing that **NSCN-IM** (dominated by Manipur-born Tangkhul cadres) did not exclusively represent the tribes residing within the actual state of Nagaland, the Union Government broadened the peace architecture by signing the **Agreed Position (Preamble)** on **November 17, 2017** with the Working Committee of the **Naga National Political Groups (NNPGs)**—a conglomerate of seven (later expanded) Naga underground factions led by N. Kitovi Zhimomi (**GPRN/NSCN**, NNC factions, NSCN-Reformation, etc.). Unlike the NSCN-IM, the **NNPGs** represent primarily indigenous Nagaland tribes and have declared their readiness to sign a final peace accord immediately within the Indian Constitution, leaving unresolved symbolic issues to be pursued democratically post-settlement.

#### The Yehzabo/Flag Deadlock and the 2024–2025 ENPO Frontier Nagaland Autonomy Architecture

Despite nearly three decades of ceasefire and over 80 rounds of negotiations, the final Naga settlement remains deadlocked over two non-negotiable demands asserted by **NSCN-IM**: a separate **Naga National Flag** and a separate **Naga Constitution (Yehzabo)**. Following the August 2019 abrogation of Article 370 and the separate flag/constitution in Jammu & Kashmir, the Union Government has maintained an absolute red line that while a cultural Naga flag may be used for non-governmental/social functions and local governance adjustments can be incorporated into **Article 371A**, India cannot constitutionally sanction a separate sovereign flag or a parallel **Yehzabo**. Meanwhile, within Nagaland, the protracted ceasefire has birthed an entrenched **"ceasefire economy"** characterized by institutionalized parallel "taxation" (extortion) by competing underground factions across commercial towns like Dimapur and Kohima.

Simultaneously, internal developmental and political marginalization within Nagaland has generated a major sub-regional fracture led by the **Eastern Nagaland People’s Organisation (ENPO)**, representing seven tribes (**Konyak, Khiamniungan, Chang, Sangtam, Tikhir, Phom, and Yimkhiung**) across six eastern districts (**Mon, Tuensang, Longleng, Kiphire, Noklak, and Shamator**) bordering Myanmar. Citing severe socio-economic neglect by the Kohima-centric state administration since 1963, the **ENPO** boycotted the April 2024 Lok Sabha elections demanding a separate **Frontier Nagaland Territory (FNT)**. Through intensive tripartite negotiations across **2024–2025** between the Ministry of Home Affairs, the Nagaland State Government, and the **ENPO**, the Centre formulated a customized **Frontier Nagaland Territory** autonomy framework—granting direct financial allocation, executive powers, and legislative autonomy to an eastern council within the state of Nagaland without requiring formal statehood bifurcation.

## II. Sub-Regional Peace Engineering: The Mizo Benchmark, Assamese Sub-Nationalism, and Tripartite Accords

### 3. The 1986 Mizo Peace Accord and the Architecture of Political Co-optation

Within comparative counter-insurgency theory, the **Mizo Peace Accord of June 30, 1986** remains the definitive Indian benchmark for transforming a violent secessionist insurgency into one of the most peaceful, high-literacy states in the Union, achieving a total and irreversible dissolution of the underground armed apparatus.

#### Counter-Insurgency Grouping to Constitutional Integration (Article 371G)

Triggered by the catastrophic administrative failure of the Assam government during the **1959 *Mautam* (bamboo-flowering rat famine)** in the Lushai Hills District, **Laldenga** converted the Mizo National Famine Front into the secessionist **Mizo National Front (MNF)** in **1961**. On **March 1, 1966**, the MNF launched **Operation Jericho**, simultaneously overrunning Aizawl, Lunglei, and Champhai, prompting the Union Government to deploy the Indian Air Force for strafing runs in Aizawl and initiate a controversial counter-insurgency doctrine of **"Protected and Progressive Villages" (PPVs)**—the forced regrouping of over 80% of the rural Mizo population along the Vairengte-Aizawl-Lunglei highway corridor to sever the MNF guerrilla *fish* from the civilian *water*.

While kinetic grouping and the **1971 creation of Bangladesh** (which eliminated the MNF’s Chittagong Hill Tracts sanctuaries) degraded the MNF militarily, lasting peace was engineered through two political steps: first, elevating the Mizo Hills to a **Union Territory in 1972** to build an indigenous democratic leadership class; and second, signing the **Mizo Peace Accord on June 30, 1986** between the Rajiv Gandhi administration, the Mizoram administration, and **Laldenga**. The structural permanence of the **1986 Mizo Peace Accord** rested on four pillars:

*   **Total Disarmament and Monopoly of Violence:** Unlike the Naga ceasefire where cadres retained weapons in designated camps, the **MNF** brought all underground cadres and weapons overground prior to constitutional elevation, permanently dismantling its armed wing (the Mizo National Army).
*   **Power-Sharing Sacrifice by the Incumbent Political Elite:** Sitting Congress Chief Minister **Lal Thanhawla** voluntarily stepped down to become Deputy Chief Minister, allowing **Laldenga** to assume office as Chief Minister of an interim coalition government before winning the 1987 state elections—demonstrating that political accommodation required genuine power transfer rather than mere patronage.
*   **Constitutional Protection under the 53rd Amendment (Article 371G):** Mizoram was granted full statehood in **February 1987** alongside **Article 371G**, guaranteeing constitutional immunity for Mizo religious/social practices, customary law, civil/criminal justice administration, and land ownership from central parliamentary override, while preserving the **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** under the **BEFR 1873**.

### 4. Assamese Sub-Nationalism and the ULFA Bifurcation

Unlike the tribal hill insurgencies, insurgency in the Brahmaputra Valley originated from linguistic-cultural sub-nationalism, demographic anxiety over unabated illegal migration from East Pakistan/Bangladesh, and economic grievances regarding "colonial resource extraction" of Assam’s tea, crude oil, and timber without commensurate industrial reinvestment. Founded on **April 7, 1979** at the historic **Rang Ghar in Sivasagar** by **Paresh Baruah**, **Arabinda Rajkhowa**, **Anup Chetia**, and **Bhimkanta Buragohain** alongside the beginning of the **Assam Agitation (1979–1985)**, the **United Liberation Front of Asom (ULFA)** rejected the **1985 Assam Accord** and launched a violent armed campaign for a *"Swadhin Asom"* (Sovereign Socialist Assam).

#### The December 2023 ULFA Tripartite Peace Accord vs. Paresh Baruah’s ULFA (Independent)

Throughout the late 1980s and 1990s, **ULFA** established an extensive network of extortion, political assassinations, oil-pipeline bombings, and external training sanctuaries across **Bangladesh** (under DGFI patronage), **Bhutan** (Samdrup Jongkhar camps), and **Myanmar** (Kachin Independence Army and NSCN-K camps). The state countered through successive kinetic campaigns—**Operation Bajrang (1990)**, **Operation Rhino (1991)**, and crucially the **December 2003 Royal Bhutan Army’s "Operation All Clear"**, which demolished 30 insurgent camps inside Bhutan, followed by the **2009–2010 Sheikh Hasina administration’s crackdown in Bangladesh**, which resulted in the arrest and handover of top ULFA political ideologues including Chairman **Arabinda Rajkhowa** and later General Secretary **Anup Chetia**.

This strategic decapitation split ULFA into the **Pro-Talk Faction (led by Arabinda Rajkhowa)**, which entered ceasefire negotiations in **2011**, and **ULFA-Independent (ULFA-I)**, led by Commander-in-Chief **Paresh Baruah**, who rejected talks and retreated to the **Taga sanctuary in Myanmar’s Sagaing region** and along the Sino-Myanmar border in Yunnan.

*   **The ULFA Tripartite Peace Accord (December 29, 2023):** Signed in New Delhi between the Ministry of Home Affairs, the Government of Assam, and the **Arabinda Rajkhowa-led Pro-Talk ULFA faction**, this landmark accord formally dissolved the original ULFA organization, oversaw the surrender of over **700 cadres** and their weaponry, and vacated all designated camps. In exchange for abandoning secessionism, the accord institutionalized three structural guarantees:
    1.  **Political & Delimitation Safeguards:** Protection of indigenous Assamese political representation through the **2023 Election Commission delimitation exercise**, reserving **96 to 97 out of 126 Assembly constituencies** culturally/demographically for indigenous populations alongside enhanced reservation for Scheduled Tribes and Scheduled Castes.
    2.  **Land and Cultural Protection:** Statutory restrictions preventing the sale of agricultural land to non-indigenous buyers in heritage zones (such as around **Majuli** and **Batadrava**), eviction of encroachments on *Satra* (Vaishnavite monastery) lands, and constitutional processing of Scheduled Tribe status for six communities (Tai Ahom, Chutia, Moran, Matak, Koch-Rajbongshi, and Tea Tribes).
    3.  **₹1.5 Lakh Crore Economic & Infrastructure Package:** Time-bound central capital investment across oil refineries, flood-mitigation reservoirs, railways, and higher educational institutions, monitored by a joint MHA-Assam-ULFA committee.
*   **The Residual Threat of Paresh Baruah’s ULFA-I:** While the **December 2023 Accord** neutralized ULFA's domestic mass base, **Paresh Baruah’s ULFA-I** continues to operate as a residual, externalized threat vector from Myanmar, periodically attempting low-cost improvised explosive device (IED) placements (such as the August 2024 Independence Day inert-IED propaganda stunt across Assam) and coordinating with anti-talk Naga and Meitei outfits in Myanmar, though suffering severe attrition due to the Myanmar civil war and continuous desertions.

#### Evolution of Bodo Peace Accords (1993 BAC, 2003 BTC, 2020 BTR Accord dissolving NDFB)

As the largest plains tribe in Assam, the Bodos launched the *"Divide Assam 50-50"* movement in 1987 under **Upendranath Brahma’s All Bodo Students’ Union (ABSU)**, alongside two rival armed insurgencies: the secessionist **National Democratic Front of Bodoland (NDFB)** (founded in 1986 as Bodo Security Force by **Ranjan Daimary**) and the autonomy-seeking **Bodo Liberation Tigers (BLT)** (1996). The state’s three successive peace accords illustrate the institutional learning curve of **Sixth Schedule** engineering:

*   **The 1993 BAC Accord and 2003 BTC Accord:** The **1993 Bodo Accord** created a statutory **Bodoland Autonomous Council (BAC)** without **Sixth Schedule** constitutional backing or fixed territorial boundaries, collapsing immediately over village-inclusion criteria. The **February 2003 Second Bodo Accord** with the **BLT** (led by Hagrama Mohilary) amended the **Sixth Schedule** to create the **Bodoland Territorial Area Districts (BTAD)** governed by the **Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC)** across Kokrajhar, Baksa, Chirang, and Udalguri. However, because Bodos constituted only ~30% of the BTAD population while holding 30 of the 46 council seats, and because the **NDFB** remained underground, the region witnessed severe anti-settler and inter-ethnic violence in 2008, 2012, and 2014.
*   **The 2020 BTR Peace Accord (January 27, 2020):** To achieve total insurgency closure, the Union Government signed a comprehensive tripartite accord with **ABSU**, the **United Bodo People’s Organisation (UBPO)**, and **all four factions of the NDFB** (led by **Ranjan Daimary**, **Gobinda Basumatary**, **Dhirendra Boro**, and **B. Saoraigwra**). The **2020 Accord** renamed the area the **Bodoland Territorial Region (BTR)**, expanded the council to **60 seats**, recognized **Bodo (in Devanagari script) as an Associate Official Language of Assam**, established a **Bodo-Kachari Welfare Council** for Bodos living outside the BTR, and created a boundary commission to include contiguous Bodo-majority villages into the BTR while excluding contiguous non-Bodo-majority border villages—culminating in the formal dissolution of the **NDFB** and the surrender of over 1,615 cadres.

#### Karbi Anglong (2021), Adivasi (2022), and Dimasa/DNLA (2023) Tripartite Settlements

Following the BTR template, the Union Government systematically dismantled the remaining ethnic insurgencies across Assam’s hill and plains districts:

*   **The Karbi Anglong Peace Accord (September 4, 2021):** Signed with **five Karbi insurgent groups** (**KLNLF, PDCK, UPLA, KPLT, and KLF**), leading to the surrender of over 1,000 armed cadres and 300+ weapons. Rather than conceding a separate state under Article 244A, the accord upgraded the **Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council (KAAC)** with enhanced legislative and executive autonomy, greater devolution of state subjects, protection of Karbi customary law, and a **₹1,000 crore special development package** over five years.
*   **The Adivasi Peace Accord (September 15, 2022):** Signed with **eight Adivasi/Tea Tribe militant outfits** (**BCirsa Commando Force [BCF], Adivasi Cobra Military of Assam [ACMA], All Adivasi National Liberation Army [AANLA], Santhal Tiger Force [STF]**, and four splinter factions), which had formed in the 1990s to counter Bodo militant attacks in western Assam. Over 1,182 cadres laid down arms in exchange for the creation of an **Adivasi Welfare and Development Council**, expediting Scheduled Tribe (ST) status processing, social infrastructure in tea gardens, and a **₹1,000 crore central-state development package**.
*   **The Dimasa / DNLA Peace Accord (April 27, 2023):** Following the earlier 2012 settlement with the DHD (Dima Halam Daogah), the newly formed **Dimasa National Liberation Army (DNLA)** and its political wing (DPSC) had revived armed extortion in Dima Hasao and Karbi Anglong. Under the **April 2023 Tripartite Accord**, all **179 DNLA cadres** surrendered their weapons and dissolved the outfit in exchange for a **Dimasa Welfare Council** for Dimasas residing outside Dima Hasao, institutional strengthening of the **North Cachar Hills Autonomous Council (NCHAC)**, and a **₹1,000 crore development package**.

#### The Bru-Reang Quadripartite Settlement (2020) and the September 2024 Tripura Peace Accord (NLFT & ATTF)

In the Mizoram-Tripura corridor, the state resolved two decades-old humanitarian and insurgent crises:

*   **The Bru-Reang Quadripartite Agreement (January 16, 2020):** In 1997, violent ethnic clashes in Mizoram triggered by Bru demands for an Autonomous District Council forced over **37,000 Bru (Reang) tribals**—a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group (PVTG)—to flee into squalid relief camps in North Tripura, where nine previous repatriation attempts failed due to security fears in Mizoram. Breaking the 23-year deadlock, the Union Government, the Governments of **Tripura** and **Mizoram**, and Bru representatives signed the **January 2020 Quadripartite Agreement**, permitting the permanent settlement of all displaced Bru families inside **Tripura** with voting rights, ST certificates, a **40x30 sq ft residential plot**, **₹4 lakh fixed deposit**, **₹1.5 lakh housing assistance**, and free rations/monthly stipends for two years under a **₹600 crore central package**.
*   **The Tripura Peace Accord with NLFT and ATTF (September 4, 2024):** Historically, indigenous Tripuri tribal alienation resulting from post-1947 and 1971 Bengali refugee demographic inversion fueled two lethal insurgencies: the **National Liberation Front of Twipra (NLFT)** (founded in 1989) and the **All Tripura Tiger Force (ATTF)** (founded in 1990 by Ranjit Debbarma). While previous accords (1993 ATTF accord and 2019 NLFT-SD accord) weakened the movement, residual factions continued operating from Bangladesh sanctuaries. On **September 4, 2024**, the Ministry of Home Affairs, the Government of Tripura, and representatives of **NLFT** and **ATTF** signed a historic tripartite memorandum of settlement in New Delhi. Under this accord, **328+ armed cadres** surrendered their weapons and formally dissolved both **NLFT** and **ATTF**, backed by a **₹250 crore special development package** for the tribal areas of Tripura (**TTAADC**), officially rendering Tripura entirely free of armed insurgency.

## III. The Manipur Security Collapse (2023–2026): Topographical Bifurcation, Narco-Insurgency, and Constitutional Intervention

### 6. The Structural Hill-Valley Dichotomy: Geography, Land Tenure, and Article 371C

Unlike single-vector insurgencies, the Manipur crisis is rooted in a rigid **spatial-demographic-legal inversion** wherein topography, land ownership statutes, and constitutional schedules operate as mutually reinforcing zero-sum traps.

#### Imphal Alluvial Basin vs. Surrounding Hill Ranges: Demographic-Spatial Asymmetry

Manipur’s physical geography resembles a central amphitheater: the oval-shaped **Imphal Valley** constitutes merely **10% of the state’s total landmass (~2,238 sq km)** yet houses approximately **57% to 60% of the state’s population** (predominantly Hindu/Sanamahi **Meiteis**, alongside **Meitei Pangal** Muslims) and controls **40 out of the 60 seats** in the Manipur Legislative Assembly. Surrounding this central basin on all sides are the rugged **Hill Districts**, which encompass **90% of Manipur’s geographical area (~20,089 sq km)** but are inhabited by roughly **40% to 43% of the population**, divided between **Naga tribes** (mainly in the north and northeast: Senapati, Ukhrul, Tamenglong, Kamjong, Noney) and **Kuki-Zo-Chin-Mizo tribes** (concentrated in the south, southwest, and surrounding foothills: Churachandpur, Kangpokpi, Tengnoupal, Pherzawl, Chandel), who together hold only **20 reserved ST Assembly seats**.

#### The MLR&LR Act (1960) Land Market Asymmetry and Article 371C Hill Areas Committee Friction

This spatial-political asymmetry is locked into constant conflict by two intersecting legal structures:

*   **The Manipur Land Revenue and Land Reforms (MLR&LR) Act, 1960:** By statutory design, Section 1(2) of the **MLR&LR Act, 1960** applies strictly to the valley districts and excludes the hill areas (save where the state government extends it by notification). Crucially, **Section 158 of the MLR&LR Act** prohibits the transfer of land belonging to a member of a Scheduled Tribe to any non-Scheduled Tribe person without the explicit permission of the Deputy Commissioner and the District Council. Because **Nagas and Kukis** are constitutionally recognized as **Scheduled Tribes (ST)** whereas **Meiteis** are classified under General/OBC/SC categories, hill tribals are legally permitted to purchase property and settle permanently in the overcrowded **Imphal Valley**, while Meiteis are legally barred from purchasing land in the **90% Hill Areas**. Consequently, when the **Manipur High Court issued an order on March 27, 2023** (subsequently modified by the High Court in February 2024 following severe Supreme Court censure) directing the state government to recommend **Scheduled Tribe (ST) status for the Meitei community**, the **Kuki-Zo** and **Naga** populations viewed it as an existential legal maneuver by the politically dominant Meiteis to capture both ST job quotas and ancestral tribal hill lands, triggering the May 3, 2023 **All Tribal Student Union Manipur (ATSUM)** solidarity marches and the ensuing violent conflagration.
*   **Article 371C and the Defunct Autonomous District Councils (ADCs):** Conversely, from the hill tribal perspective, the valley-dominated legislature (40 of 60 MLAs) has systematically starved the hills of autonomous governance. Unlike Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram, Manipur’s hill districts were **excluded from the Sixth Schedule** of the Constitution. Instead, they are governed by **Article 371C** (which establishes a **Hill Areas Committee [HAC]** comprising all 20 tribal MLAs) and the **Manipur (Hill Areas) District Councils Act, 1971**—an ordinary state statute under which the **Autonomous District Councils (ADCs)** lack legislative and judicial powers and remain financially dependent on the Imphal secretariat. Kuki-Zo grievances were further inflamed across 2022–2023 by the state government’s unilateral eviction drives in Reserved and Protected Forests (such as Kangchup Chiru and Churachandpur-Khoupum), which Kuki-Zo communities perceived as an attempt to bypass the **Article 371C Hill Areas Committee** and dispossess hereditary Kuki village chiefs (*Haosa*).

### 7. Dual Insurgent Ecosystems: Valley-Based Meitei Outfits vs. Hill-Based Kuki-Zo SoO Groups

When civil violence erupted in May 2023, it was immediately militarized by two pre-existing, heavily armed insurgent ecosystems operating on opposite sides of the topographical divide, supplemented by radicalized civilian militias.

#### Valley-Based Meitei Insurgent Groups (UNLF, PLA, KYKL, PREPAK, KCP) and Radical Civil Militias

Insurgency in the Imphal Valley originated from grievances over the **September 1949 Merger Agreement** signed by Maharaja Bodhchandra Singh at Shillong and the 23-year delay in granting Manipur full statehood (achieved only in **1972**). Beginning in the 1960s and 1970s, a constellation of **Valley-Based Insurgent Groups (VBIGs)** emerged, espousing a mix of Maoist revolutionary ideology and pre-Hindu Meitei cultural revivalism (*Sanamahism*), demanding the restoration of Manipur's pre-1949 sovereignty while fiercely defending Manipur’s territorial integrity against Naga or Kuki partition:

*   **United National Liberation Front (UNLF):** Founded on **November 24, 1964** by Arambam Samarendra, the **UNLF** (along with its armed wing, the Manipur People’s Army - MPA) is the oldest Meitei insurgent outfit. In a major strategic split, while the hardline **Koireng faction** remained hostile, the **Pambei-led faction of the UNLF** signed a historic **Tripartite Peace Agreement with the MHA and the Government of Manipur on November 29, 2023**, bringing over 1,000 armed cadres into designated camps—though reports of armed UNLF cadres moving across valley buffer zones persisted during the 2024–2025 clashes.
*   **People’s Liberation Army (PLA), PREPAK, KCP, and KYKL:** Founded in **1978** by Chinese-trained **N. Bisheswar Singh**, the **PLA** (political wing: Revolutionary People’s Front - RPF) operates alongside the **People’s Revolutionary Party of Kangleipak (PREPAK, 1977)**, the **Kangleipak Communist Party (KCP, 1980)**, and the **Kanglei Yawol Kanna Lup (KYKL, 1994)**. Operating from sanctuaries across the **Kabaw Valley in Myanmar’s Sagaing region** under an umbrella coalition known as **CorCom (Coordination Committee)**, these banned **UAPA** terrorist organizations took advantage of the 2023–2025 ethnic crisis to re-infiltrate the Imphal Valley, extort businesses, and train radicalized Meitei youth militias—specifically **Arambai Tenggol** and **Meitei Leepun**—converting civil vigilante groups into heavily armed paramilitary formations.

#### Hill-Based Kuki-Zo Armed Groups and the Collapse of the 2008 Suspension of Operations (SoO) Ground Rules

In the surrounding hills, **Kuki-Zo** militant groups originally formed in the late 1980s and early 1990s in response to violent **NSCN-IM** ethnic cleansing campaigns against Kuki villages during the **1992–1997 Naga-Kuki clashes** (in which over 1,000 Kukis were killed and 350 villages uprooted), demanding a separate Kuki state (**Zale’n-gam** or **Kukiland**).

*   **The 2008 Suspension of Operations (SoO) Architecture:** Following an initial bilateral ceasefire with the Indian Army in 2005, **25 Kuki-Zo armed groups** organized under two umbrella coalitions—the **Kuki National Organisation (KNO)** (17 outfits led by the Kuki National Army - KNA) and the **United People’s Front (UPF)** (8 outfits led by the Zomi Revolutionary Army - ZRA and Kuki National Front - KNF)—signed a formal **Tripartite Suspension of Operations (SoO) Agreement on August 22, 2008** with the Union Ministry of Home Affairs and the Government of Manipur.
*   **Breakdown of SoO Ground Rules (2023–2025):** Under the **SoO Ground Rules**, approximately 2,200 vetted cadres were required to remain confined within **14 designated camps**, with their weapons locked inside double-key armories monitored jointly by security forces, in exchange for monthly government stipends and political negotiations toward a **Sixth Schedule Territorial Council**. During the 2023–2025 conflict, however, the **SoO** containment mechanism fractured: state authorities accused **KNA** and **ZRA** cadres of violating ground rules, deserting camps, recruiting cross-border Chin-Kuki militants from Myanmar, and engaging in sniper/drone attacks along the foothills. While the Manipur state cabinet demanded the unilateral abrogation of the **SoO Agreement** (with the Centre allowing the agreement with two groups, KNA and ZRA, to lapse under review in early 2024 before re-imposing strict camp consolidations and buffer zone rules in 2025), **KNO** and **UPF** hardened their political stance, joined by the newly formed ** Indigenous Tribal Leaders' Forum (ITLF)** and **Kuki-Zo Council**, demanding a **Union Territory with a Legislature (Separate Administration)** under Article 239A on the grounds that demographic coexistence in the Imphal Valley had been permanently destroyed.

#### Looted State Armories, the Poppy-Cultivation Narco-Insurgency Nexus, and Militarized Buffer Zones

Three operational multipliers sustained the conflict for nearly two years:

1.  **Looting of State Police Armories and Force Bifurcation:** In the opening weeks of May 2023 (and in recurring raids throughout 2023–2024), mobs and militia cadres overran or were handed keys to **Manipur Rifles**, **India Reserve Battalion (IRB)**, and district police armories across the Imphal Valley and hill districts. Over **6,000 sophisticated service weapons**—including **INSAS rifles, AK-47s, Self-Loading Rifles (SLRs), Light Machine Guns (LMGs), carbines, and 2-inch mortars**—along with **over 5,00,000 rounds of ammunition** were looted. Simultaneously, the Manipur Police Force split completely along ethnic lines, with Meitei police commandos unable to enter Kuki-Zo hills and Kuki-Zo police personnel unable to serve in the Imphal Valley.
2.  **The Poppy-Cultivation Narco-Insurgency Nexus:** Following the 2021 military coup in Myanmar, transnational narcotics syndicates shifted illicit **opium poppy cultivation** into the unpoliced hill tracts of Manipur (particularly across Kangpokpi, Ukhrul, Churachandpur, Tengnoupal, and Chandel, where satellite surveys by the Manipur Remote Sensing Applications Centre detected over **15,400 acres of illicit poppy fields** between 2017 and 2023 before large-scale state destruction drives). The windfall profits from raw opium and cross-border **methamphetamine (Yaba)** and heroin trafficking from Myanmar’s **Golden Triangle** created a self-sustaining war economy, enabling hill-based and valley-based armed groups to purchase smuggled M16/M4 carbines, sniper rifles, and mortar shells across the porous **1,643-km Indo-Myanmar border**.
3.  **Militarized Buffer Zones and Partitioned Geography:** By mid-2023, Manipur underwent near-total spatial ethnic cleansing: virtually all Kuki-Zo residents were driven out of the Imphal Valley, and virtually all Meitei residents were driven out of Churachandpur, Kangpokpi, and the border town of Moreh, leaving over **60,000 internally displaced persons (IDPs)** in relief camps and over **260 dead**. To prevent mutual annihilation, over **60,000 Indian Army, Assam Rifles, and CAPF personnel** established fortified **"Buffer Zones"** along the 1-to-3-kilometer foothills interface separating the valley from the hills (such as Phougakchao Ikhai, Bishnupur-Churachandpur border, and Kangpokpi foothills)—zones that became active trench-warfare frontlines subjected to women-led vigilante roadblocks (*Meira Paibis* in the valley and Kuki Women’s Human Rights groups in the hills) impeding military convoys.

#### From Article 355 Security Takeover to the February 2025 Imposition of President’s Rule (Article 356)

The Union Government’s constitutional response evolved in two distinct stages as the crisis exposed the paralysis of the partisan state executive:

*   **May 2023 Invocation of Article 355 Principles & Unified Command Restructuring:** Under **Article 355 of the Constitution**, the Union is duty-bound *"to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance."* Immediately in May 2023, the Centre appointed former CRPF Director General **Kuldiep Singh as Security Advisor** and restructured the **Unified Command** to place operational security coordination under central oversight, while the **Supreme Court** constituted an all-woman three-former-High-Court-Judge committee led by **Justice Gita Mittal** for humanitarian relief and transferred dozens of heinous sexual-assault and murder probes to the **CBI** and **NIA**.
*   **The February 13, 2025 Imposition of President’s Rule (Article 356):** Following escalating violence in late 2024 (including the Jiribam killings and rocket/drone strikes), the re-imposition of **AFSPA** across six Imphal valley police stations in November 2024, and an impending constitutional crisis in the State Assembly, Chief Minister N. Biren Singh resigned on **February 9, 2025**. On **February 13, 2025**, the President of India issued a proclamation under **Article 356 of the Constitution**, imposing **President’s Rule in Manipur** and placing the 60-member Manipur Legislative Assembly under **suspended animation**. Operating directly through Governor **Ajay Kumar Bhalla**, the Union administration immediately issued strict **disarmament ultimatums** (recovering over 1,000 looted weapons including Arambai Tenggol stockpiles within weeks), enforced **free movement along National Highway-2 (Imphal–Dimapur) and NH-37 (Imphal–Jiribam)**, consolidated **SoO camps** away from buffer zones, and accelerated physical fencing of the **Indo-Myanmar border**.

#### Section 3 "Disturbed Area" Notifications, Section 4 Kinetic Powers, and Section 6 Sovereign Immunity

The operational potency—and constitutional controversy—of **AFSPA, 1958** stems from the interaction of four specific statutory sections:

*   **Section 3 (Declaration of "Disturbed Area"):** If the **Governor of a State** or the **Central Government** (a concurrent power added by the **1972 Amendment**, overriding state government resistance) is of the opinion that the whole or any part of such State is in such a *"disturbed or dangerous condition that the use of armed forces in aid of the civil power is necessary,"* either authority may, by official Gazette notification, declare the whole or specific police station jurisdictions of that State to be a **"Disturbed Area."**
*   **Section 4 (Special Powers of the Armed Forces):** Within a notified **Disturbed Area**, any commissioned officer, warrant officer, non-commissioned officer, or any other person of equivalent rank in the armed forces may, after giving such due warning as he may consider necessary:
    1.  **Section 4(a) [Lethal Force]:** Fire upon or otherwise use force, **even to the causing of death**, against any person who is acting in contravention of any law or order for the time being in force in the disturbed area prohibiting the assembly of **five or more persons** or the carrying of weapons or of things capable of being used as weapons or of fire-arms, ammunition or explosive substances.
    2.  **Section 4(b) [Destruction of Structures]:** Destroy any arms dump, prepared or fortified position or shelter from which armed attacks are made or are likely to be made, or any structure used as a training camp or hide-out by armed gangs or absconders.
    3.  **Section 4(c) [Preventive Warrantless Arrest]:** Arrest, without warrant, any person who has committed a cognizable offence or against whom a reasonable suspicion exists that he has committed or is **about to commit** a cognizable offence.
    4.  **Section 4(d) [Warrantless Search and Seizure]:** Enter and search without warrant any premises to make any such arrest as aforesaid or to recover any person believed to be wrongfully restrained or confined or any property or arms, ammunition or explosive substances.
*   **Section 5 (Handover to Civil Police) and Section 6 (Central Sanction Immunity):** While **Section 5** mandates that any person arrested and taken into custody under this Act shall be made over to the officer in charge of the nearest police station with the *"least possible delay"* together with a report of the circumstances occasioning the arrest, **Section 6** erects a formidable shield of sovereign immunity: no prosecution, suit, or other legal proceeding shall be instituted against any person in respect of anything done or purported to be done in exercise of the powers conferred by this Act, **except with the previous sanction of the Central Government**. In practice, this de facto immunity has created a persistent structural tension between the **Armed Forces’ operational requirement for legal protection in asymmetric combat zones** (where soldiers cannot operate under peacetime policing liabilities) and the **citizens' Fundamental Rights under Articles 14, 21, and 22 of the Constitution**, particularly when high-profile aberrations—such as the **2000 Malom massacre**, the **2004 custodial death of Thangjam Manorama in Manipur** (which triggered Irom Sharmila's 16-year fast), and the **December 2021 Oting, Mon (Nagaland) botched ambush** in which 21 Para (SF) personnel fatally mistook coal miners for NSCN-K insurgents—alienate the civilian population.

### 10. Committee Jurisprudence and Supreme Court Restraints on Counter-Insurgency Impunity

To reconcile the operational imperatives of counter-insurgency with constitutional rule of law, both executive review committees and the Supreme Court of India have progressively circumscribed the unbridled execution of **AFSPA**.

#### The Justice Jeevan Reddy Committee (2005) and Second ARC Recommendations

Following widespread public protests in Manipur after the death of Thangjam Manorama in 2004, the Union Ministry of Home Affairs constituted a five-member committee chaired by former Supreme Court judge **Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy in November 2004** to review the provisions of **AFSPA, 1958**. Submitting its report in **June 2005**, the **Justice Jeevan Reddy Committee** delivered a landmark critique:

*   **Recommendation for Total Repeal of AFSPA:** The Committee concluded that **AFSPA, 1958** had become *"a symbol of oppression, an object of hate and an instrument of discrimination and high-handedness,"* recommending its **complete repeal**.
*   **Insertion of a New Chapter VI-A into the UAPA, 1967:** Recognising that the Union Government still required legal power to deploy the Armed Forces in extreme internal crises without a State Government's request, the **Jeevan Reddy Committee** (subsequently endorsed by the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission [5th Report on Public Order, 2007]** headed by Veerappa Moily and the **Justice Santosh Hegde Commission [2013]**) recommended inserting appropriate provisions directly into the **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA), 1967**. This proposed framework stipulated clear limits on deployment duration, strict legal guidelines on the use of force, and the mandatory creation of **Grievance Cells** in every district where armed forces are deployed to inquire into allegations of missing persons and human rights violations. However, due to staunch opposition from the **Ministry of Defence** and the **Indian Army**—which argued that diluting **Section 4** and **Section 6** would expose soldiers to motivated litigation by insurgent front organizations and paralyze tactical initiative—the repeal recommendation was never implemented.

#### Naga People's Movement of Human Rights (1997) to the Landmark EEVFAM (2016) Judgment

Where executive reform stalled, the **Supreme Court of India** intervened through two foundational constitutional judgments that redefined the legal boundaries of **AFSPA**:

1.  **Naga People’s Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India (1997, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** While upholding the **constitutional validity of AFSPA, 1958** as falling within Parliament’s legislative competence and not being a disguised proclamation of Emergency under Article 352, the Constitution Bench laid down binding operational constraints:
    *   A **Section 3 "Disturbed Area" declaration** cannot be indefinite; there must be a mandatory **periodic review every six months**.
    *   Under **Section 4(a)**, the power to use lethal force is strictly conditional upon an existing prohibitory order and must adhere to the **doctrine of minimal force and proportionality**.
    *   Under **Section 5**, *"least possible delay"* means the arrested person must be handed over to the nearest police station within **24 hours** (excluding travel time) in strict compliance with **Article 22(2)** of the Constitution; the Armed Forces have **zero statutory power to interrogate or retain custody** of arrested civilians.
    *   The **List of Do’s and Don’ts** issued by Army Headquarters is legally binding on all personnel, and any refusal by the Central Government to grant prosecution sanction under **Section 6** must be a speaking order subject to judicial review.
2.  **Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association (EEVFAM) v. Union of India (July 8, 2016):** adjudicating a Public Interest Litigation seeking investigation into **1,528 alleged fake encounters and extrajudicial killings** in Manipur by the Army, Assam Rifles, and Manipur Police between 1979 and 2012, a Supreme Court Bench of **Justices Madan B. Lokur and Uday Umesh Lalit** dismantled the legal myth of absolute immunity in Disturbed Areas:
    *   **Termination of the "Enemy" Doctrine in Domestic Counter-Insurgency:** The Court ruled that an internal disturbance is not a war against an external sovereign enemy, and even if an armed militant enters a Disturbed Area, he remains a criminal under Indian law unless engaged in active lethal combat; **retaliatory or excessive force resulting in death is unconstitutional and constitutes culpable homicide/murder under the IPC/BNS**.
    *   **Piercing Section 6 Immunity:** The Supreme Court held that there is **no concept of absolute sovereign immunity** under **Section 6 of AFSPA** for personnel who commit criminal offenses or staged encounters. Every death caused by the armed forces in a Disturbed Area—whether of a civilian or a suspected insurgent—must be thoroughly investigated, and in July 2017, the Court constituted a **CBI Special Investigation Team (SIT)** to register FIRs and prosecute dozens of verified extrajudicial execution cases in Manipur.

### 11. Phased Spatial Reduction of Disturbed Areas and the Post-AFSPA Policing Transition

Driven by an over **70% to 80% statistical decline in insurgent violence**, security force casualties, and civilian deaths across the Northeast between 2014 and 2026 following the series of tripartite peace accords, the Union Ministry of Home Affairs has executed a calibrated **phased spatial de-notification of AFSPA**, transitioning the region from military counter-insurgency back to regular civilian policing.

#### Police Station-Level De-Notification Across Assam, Nagaland, Manipur, and Arunachal Pradesh (2022–2026)

Historically, **Section 3 notifications** were issued as blunt instruments covering entire states at a stroke. Since **April 2022**, the Ministry of Home Affairs shifted to a micro-spatial methodology—evaluating threat levels at the **individual Police Station (PS) jurisdiction level** during every six-month review cycle:

*   **Complete Revocation States (Mizoram, Tripura, Meghalaya):** While **Mizoram** has remained free of AFSPA operationalization since the **1986 Peace Accord**, **Tripura** completely revoked AFSPA in **May 2015**, and the Union Government completely withdrew AFSPA from **Meghalaya on March 31, 2018** (where a 20-km belt along the Assam border had previously been notified).
*   **Assam (85%+ Spatial Withdrawal):** Having remained entirely under AFSPA since November 1990, Assam saw a dramatic rollback between **April 2022 and October 2023**. By **2025–2026**, AFSPA has been completely withdrawn from **all districts of Assam except four Upper Assam districts** (**Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Charaideo, and Sivasagar**), where it is retained solely to allow the Army and Assam Rifles to intercept **Paresh Baruah’s ULFA-I** and **NSCN-K (Yung Aung)** cadres infiltrating from Myanmar via the Patkai hills.
*   **Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh:** Following the recommendations of a high-level committee constituted after the December 2021 Oting incident in Mon district, the Centre began a phased rollback in **Nagaland**, progressively removing AFSPA from over **21 Police Stations across 8 districts** while retaining it in **8 districts and specific sensitive police stations across 5 other districts** where extortion and factional **NSCN** turf wars persist. In **Arunachal Pradesh**, AFSPA remains restricted strictly to the **Tirap, Changlang, and Longding (TCL)** districts and **three police stations in Namsai district** bordering Assam and Myanmar.
*   **Manipur’s Bidirectional Trajectory:** Between April 2022 and April 2023, the Centre had progressively lifted AFSPA from **19 Police Stations across seven valley districts** in Manipur. However, following the escalation of ethnic violence, weaponized drone strikes, and the Jiribam massacres in late 2024, the Ministry of Home Affairs **re-imposed AFSPA on November 14, 2024 across six specific valley/foothill Police Stations** (**Sekmai and Lamsang** in Imphal West, **Lamlai** in Imphal East, **Jiribam** in Jiribam district, **Leimakhong** in Kangpokpi, and **Moirang** in Bishnupur). Under the post-**February 2025 President’s Rule** extensions, the entire state of Manipur—**excluding only 13 urban/core Police Stations in the Imphal Valley**—operates under a **Section 3 Disturbed Area notification** to empower joint Army-CAPF disarmament and buffer-zone enforcement operations.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 5: From Internal Ethno-Insurgency to External Frontier Hardening

Every persistent ethno-nationalist insurgency and narco-militia crisis examined in the Northeast—whether **Paresh Baruah’s ULFA-I**, **NSCN-K (Yung Aung)**, the **Valley-Based Meitei Insurgent Groups** operating from the Kabaw Valley, or the proliferation of **Golden Triangle methamphetamine and NATO-grade small arms** fueling the **Manipur security collapse**—exposes a fundamental strategic reality: domestic counter-insurgency cannot be permanently decoupled from the physical and technological hardening of India’s external terrestrial borders. Just as the closure of sanctuaries in Bhutan (2003) and Bangladesh (2009) forced Assamese, Bodo, and Tripuri insurgents to sign tripartite peace accords, neutralizing residual insurgencies and cross-border asymmetric warfare in Manipur, Nagaland, Jammu, and Punjab requires dismantling porous frontier regimes. This operational imperative directly drives the structural overhaul analyzed in **Part 5**: the management of India’s **15,106 km land borders** under the **"One Border, One Force"** doctrine, the **2024–2026 termination of the 16-km Free Movement Regime (FMR) and full fencing of the 1,643-km Indo-Myanmar border**, the deployment of **CIBMS (BOLD-QIT)** smart fencing, anti-tunneling **GPR/geophone grids**, indigenous counter-drone architectures (**D4, Indrajaal, Bhargavastra**), **BRO strategic tunnels**, the **Vibrant Villages Programme**, and the statutory consolidation of sovereign border control under the **Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025**.
