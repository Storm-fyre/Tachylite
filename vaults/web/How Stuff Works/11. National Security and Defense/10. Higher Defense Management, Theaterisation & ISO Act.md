# Part 10: Higher Defense Management, the ISO Act, Military Theaterisation, and Integrated Warfighting Commands

## I. Historical Evolution, Operational Silos, and the Trajectory of Higher Defense Reforms (1947–2018)

### 1. Pathologies of Single-Service Silos and Civil-Military Friction: Empirical Failures in 1962, 1965, and 1999 Kargil

#### Constitutional Civil-Military Architecture and the "Attached Office" Anomaly

The architecture of Indian higher defense management post-1947 was shaped by a structural tension between two competing imperatives: the constitutional necessity of **civilian political supremacy** over the armed forces to insulate the fledgling republic from praetorian intervention, and the operational necessity of **integrated tri-service warfighting** across contested continental and maritime frontiers. Following independence, the **Lord Ismay institutional blueprint (1947)** and the **Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961** abolished the colonial post of a unified Commander-in-Chief and established three co-equal Service Chiefs for the **Indian Army**, **Indian Navy**, and **Indian Air Force**. Crucially, until December 2019, the three Service Headquarters (**Service HQs**) were legally classified not as integral departments of the **Ministry of Defence (MoD)**, but as outside **"Attached Offices."**

Under this bureaucratic arrangement, the civilian **Defence Secretary** functioned as the administrative head of the **Department of Defence (DoD)**, creating a layer of generalist civil-service scrutiny between the military leadership and the political executive (**Defence Minister** and **Cabinet Committee on Security - CCS**). Because every proposal originating from a Service HQ regarding force structure, capital procurement, or doctrinal revision had to traverse dual scrutiny by the generalist bureaucracy of the DoD and the financial advisors of the **Ministry of Finance (MoF)** without uniformed staff representation inside the ministry itself, strategic planning suffered from protracted file-clearance delays, institutional distrust, and acute disconnectedness between national political objectives and military operational readiness.

* **Fragmentation of the Chiefs of Staff Committee (CoSC):** Under the pre-2020 framework, inter-service coordination rested on the **Chiefs of Staff Committee (CoSC)**, chaired on a rotational basis by the longest-serving Service Chief. Functioning purely as a *primus inter pares* (first among equals) without statutory authority to override single-service parochialism, the Chairman CoSC could neither enforce joint procurement priorities nor compel asset sharing, reducing tri-service planning to a lowest-common-denominator consensus where the **Army**, **Navy**, and **Air Force** guarded their respective capital budgets and doctrinal autonomy.
* **Absence of Joint Operational Planning:** Each service formulated its own five-year and fifteen-year perspective plans in isolation. The **Indian Army** prioritized attrition-based continental corps formations; the **Indian Air Force (IAF)** prioritized independent strategic air campaigns and deep-penetration strike squadrons; and the **Indian Navy** pursued blue-water carrier task forces, resulting in duplicated logistics supply chains, incompatible communication waveforms, and zero unified command accountability during multi-front crises.
* **Civil-Military Information Asymmetry:** Because generalist civil servants rotated out of the **Ministry of Defence** every three to five years without specialized training in military strategy, defense economics, or weapons systems engineering, bureaucratic oversight defaulted to procedural compliance rather than capability outcome auditing. Conversely, the uniformed services operated in an operational vacuum, frequently excluded from apex strategic deliberations until kinetic hostilities had already commenced.

#### Operational Silos in the 1962 Sino-Indian War, 1965 Indo-Pak War, and 1999 Kargil Conflict

The operational costs of single-service silos and civil-military disconnects manifested repeatedly across India’s post-independence conflicts, demonstrating that fragmented higher defense management directly degrades battlefield deterrence and combat effectiveness.

* **1962 Sino-Indian War (Politicized Micromanagement and Withholding of Combat Airpower):** During the **1962 conflict**, the political leadership and the civilian Ministry of Defence bypassed institutional military advice to pursue the **"Forward Policy"** without matching logistical build-up or winter acclimatization. Most critically, due to the absence of an integrated joint planning staff and an institutional failure to assess People's Liberation Army Air Force (PLAAF) logistical constraints in Tibet, the **Indian Air Force’s** offensive combat squadrons were completely withheld from providing **Close Air Support (CAS)** and interdiction strikes against exposed Chinese supply lines in **NEFA** and **Aksai Chin**—a strategic miscalculation rooted in unverified intelligence fears of Chinese retaliatory bombing against Indian cities.
* **1965 Indo-Pak War (Inter-Service Disconnect and Naval Exclusion):** In the **1965 war**, the **Chief of Army Staff** and **Chief of Air Staff** conducted parallel operations with minimal tactical synchronization. When Pakistan launched **Operation Grand Slam** in the Chhamb sector on September 1, 1965, urgent Army requests for emergency IAF air support suffered delays and heavy losses of outdated **Vampire** jets due to a lack of joint air-land battle drills. Simultaneously, the **Indian Navy** was not formally briefed by the political executive or the Army leadership on the impending outbreak of hostilities across the International Border, leaving naval assets strategically underutilized while the **Pakistan Navy** executed a hit-and-run raid on **Dwarka (Operation Somnath)**.
* **1999 Kargil Conflict (Operation Vijay & Operation Safed Sagar):** Following the covert winter intrusion of **Pakistan’s Northern Light Infantry (NLI)** across the **Line of Control (LoC)** in the **Kargil**, **Dras**, **Batalik**, and **Mushkoh** sectors, acute friction emerged between **Army HQ** and **Air HQ**. Initially, the **Indian Army** requested the deployment of **Mi-25/35 attack helicopters** for localized close support without integrating the IAF into the broader operational plan. The **Indian Air Force** rightly objected that low-slow rotary platforms were fatally vulnerable to Pakistani **Stinger** and **Anza MANPADS** at altitudes exceeding 15,000–18,000 feet, insisting instead on high-altitude fixed-wing precision strikes (**Mirage-2000s** equipped with **Litening laser-designation pods** under **Operation Safed Sagar**). Only after weeks of inter-service debate and CCS intervention were joint targeting protocols improvised on the fly, exposing the absence of a permanent **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)** and unified **Northern Theater Command**.

### 2. The Reform Trajectory: From the Kargil Review Committee (1999) to the Shekatkar Committee (2016)

#### Kargil Review Committee (K. Subrahmanyam, 1999) and the Group of Ministers (GoM) Report (2001)

The systemic intelligence, border management, and higher command failures exposed during the **1999 Kargil conflict** catalyzed the most exhaustive institutional audit of Indian national security since independence. Appointed in July 1999 under the chairmanship of strategic affairs doyen **K. Subrahmanyam**, the **Kargil Review Committee (KRC)** diagnosed that India was the only major democracy where the Armed Forces Headquarters remained outside the apex governmental ministry, and where no single military point-person existed to provide unified, impartial tri-service operational advice to the Prime Minister and Defence Minister.

Following the submission of the **Kargil Review Committee Report (December 1999 / tabled February 2000)**, the Union Government constituted a high-powered **Group of Ministers (GoM) in April 2000**, chaired by Deputy Prime Minister **L.K. Advani** and supported by four specialized Task Forces (on Intelligence Apparatus led by **G.C. Saxena**, Internal Security led by **N.N. Vohra**, Border Management led by **Madhav Godbole**, and Management of Defence led by **Arun Singh**). The resulting **GoM Report on "Reforming the National Security System" (2001)** laid the foundational institutional stepping stones toward tri-service integration:

* **Creation of the HQ Integrated Defence Staff (HQ IDS, October 2001):** Because political consensus could not be achieved in 2001 on the Arun Singh Task Force's recommendation to appoint a four-star **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)**—owing to reservations from the **Indian Air Force**, bureaucratic resistance, and political opposition—the government established the **Headquarters Integrated Defence Staff (HQ IDS)** headed by a three-star **Chief of Integrated Defence Staff to the Chairman Chiefs of Staff Committee (CISC)**. HQ IDS was mandated to synchronize perspective planning, joint doctrine formulation, and defense acquisition prioritization across the three services.
* **Establishment of the Defence Intelligence Agency (DIA, 2002):** To cure the pathology where the **Directorates of Military Intelligence (DMI)**, **Naval Intelligence (DNI)**, and **Air Intelligence (DAI)** hoarded tactical intelligence in silos and failed to correlate Pakistani artillery dumps and troop movements prior to Kargil, the GoM Report created the tri-service **Defence Intelligence Agency (DIA)** under a three-star Director General reporting to the CISC and CDS, integrating defence attachés and technical military intelligence.
* **Creation of the Andaman and Nicobar Command (ANC, October 2001):** As an experimental template for geographic theaterisation, the GoM Report established India’s first and—for over two decades—only unified geographic tri-service theater command at **Port Blair (ANC)**, placing Army amphibious brigades, Naval surface combatants, Air Force squadrons, and Coast Guard assets under a single rotational three-star **Commander-in-Chief Andaman and Nicobar Command (CINCAN)** to guard the **Six Degree Channel** and the western approaches to the **Malacca Strait**.
* **Establishment of the Strategic Forces Command (SFC, January 2003):** Alongside ANC, a functional tri-service command—the **Strategic Forces Command (SFC)**—was operationalized under the **Nuclear Command Authority (NCA)** to exercise unified operational control over India’s land-, air-, and sea-based nuclear delivery vectors.

#### Naresh Chandra Task Force (2012) and the Lt. Gen. D.B. Shekatkar Committee (2016) on Tooth-to-Tail Optimization

A decade after the GoM Report, the Union Government appointed the 14-member **Naresh Chandra Task Force on National Security (July 2011 – May 2012)** to review the implementation gaps of the 2001 reforms. Recognizing that the rotational Chairman CoSC remained toothless while political reluctance toward an all-powerful single-point CDS persisted, the **Naresh Chandra Task Force (2012)** recommended an intermediate institutional compromise: the creation of a four-star **Permanent Chairman of the Chiefs of Staff Committee (PC-CoSC)** who would enjoy a fixed two-year tenure, relinquish command of his parent service, oversee the **HQ IDS**, and coordinate long-term tri-service procurements without stripping the three Service Chiefs of direct operational command over their respective forces. Crucially, the Naresh Chandra Committee also recommended establishing three functional tri-service commands to address emerging non-kinetic and asymmetric warfighting domains: a **Special Operations Command**, an **Aerospace Command**, and a **Cyber Command**, alongside cross-posting civil officers into Service HQs and military officers into the Ministry of Defence.

While the PC-CoSC proposal languished between 2012 and 2018, the burgeoning fiscal burden of military revenue and pension expenditure prompted the Ministry of Defence in May 2016 to constitute an 11-member Committee of Experts chaired by **Lt. Gen. D.B. Shekatkar (Retd.)** to recommend measures to **"Enhance Combat Capability and Rebalance Defence Expenditure"** by optimizing the military’s **tooth-to-tail ratio** (the proportion of frontline combat soldiers to rear-area logistical, administrative, and maintenance support personnel). Submitted in December 2016 with **99 major recommendations** (of which the MoD initially accepted 65 relating to the Indian Army for phased implementation), the **Shekatkar Committee Report** systematically targeted colonial-era administrative redundancies that drained capital modernization budgets:

* **Redeployment of 57,000 Uniformed Personnel via Logistics & Static Pruning:** The **Shekatkar Committee** identified severe bloat in rear-echelon static establishments and recommended structural pruning to release approximately **57,000 officers, Junior Commissioned Officers (JCOs), and Other Ranks (ORs)** into frontline combat and cyber/intelligence formations, yielding direct recurring savings exceeding **₹25,000 crore** over a five-year cycle.
* **Abolition of Colonial-Era Non-Core Establishments:** The committee catalyzed the complete closure of all **39 Military Farms** across India (which held over 20,000 acres of prime defense land for cattle rearing despite the ubiquity of commercial dairy cooperatives), the downsizing of **Army Postal Establishments** in peace stations, the corporatization/outsourcing of **8 Advance Base Workshops (ABWs)** under the Corps of Electronics and Mechanical Engineers (EME), and the amalgamation of multiple **Central Ordnance Depots (CODs)** and Vehicle Depots into modernized, automated regional logistics hubs.
* **Restructuring of Signals, Engineering, and National Cadet Corps (NCC):** It mandated the merger of separate **Signal Regiments** at Corps and Command levels, optimization of the **Military Engineer Services (MES)** by outsourcing routine civil maintenance works, and utilizing retired armed forces personnel to staff **National Cadet Corps (NCC)** instructional cadres so that active-duty officers could return to combat units.
* **Explicit Blueprint for Three Integrated Theater Commands:** Beyond fiscal and logistical rebalancing, the **Shekatkar Committee (2016)** explicitly recommended replacing India’s fragmented 17 single-service commands with **Three Integrated Theater Commands**—a **Western Theater Command** facing Pakistan, a **Northern Theater Command** facing China along the LAC, and a **Southern/Maritime Theater Command** securing the Indian Ocean Region—Directly shaping the structural blueprint adopted by the **Department of Military Affairs (DMA)** between 2020 and 2026.

## II. The 2019–2020 Institutional Restructuring: Chief of Defence Staff (CDS), Department of Military Affairs (DMA), and Tri-Service Agencies

### 3. The Chief of Defence Staff (CDS) and the Dual-Track Ministry of Defence Architecture (DMA vs. DoD)

#### Tri-Hat Institutional Mandate of the Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)

On **December 24, 2019**, the **Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS)** executed the single most consequential structural reform in Indian higher defense management since 1947 by approving the creation of the four-star post of **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)** and establishing a fifth department within the **Ministry of Defence**: the **Department of Military Affairs (DMA)**. Assuming office on **January 1, 2020** (initially held by **General Bipin Rawat**, followed by **General Anil Chauhan** in September 2022, and **General N.S. Raja Subramani** in May 2026), the **CDS** dismantled the 72-year-old "Attached Office" barrier by embedding uniformed leadership directly inside the civil-military hierarchy of the Union Government.

The institutional design of the **Chief of Defence Staff** vests a single four-star officer (ineligible to hold any government office after demitting the post, and barred from returning to command a parent service) with three interlocking statutory and executive hats:

1. **Permanent Chairman of the Chiefs of Staff Committee (PC-CoSC):** The CDS functions as the permanent, non-rotational head of the **CoSC** (comprising the CDS, COAS, CNS, and CAS), supported by the **HQ Integrated Defence Staff (HQ IDS)**. In this capacity, the CDS adjudicates inter-service capability trade-offs, assigns inter-se priority to capital acquisition proposals across the Army, Navy, and Air Force under the **Integrated Capability Development Plan (ICDP)**, and serves as the Military Adviser to the **Nuclear Command Authority (NCA)**.
2. **Principal Military Adviser to the Defence Minister on All Tri-Service Matters:** While the three Service Chiefs retain the institutional right to advise the **Defence Minister (Raksha Mantri)** exclusively on operational matters pertaining to their individual services, the **CDS** serves as the single-point **Principal Military Adviser** on all tri-service doctrines, joint operational readiness, theaterisation, and multi-domain resource allocation.
3. **Secretary of the Department of Military Affairs (DMA):** By designating the uniformed **CDS** as the administrative **Secretary of the DMA** within the Ministry of Defence—enjoying full bureaucratic parity with the civilian **Defence Secretary**—the December 2019 amendment to the **Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961** eliminated the double-handling of military administrative files. Under the DMA, Joint Secretaries, Deputy Secretaries, and Under Secretaries comprise a fused cadre of both civilian officers and uniformed military officers (Major Generals/Brigadiers, Rear Admirals/Commodores, Air Vice Marshals/Air Commodores), institutionalizing genuine civil-military integration at the policy-drafting level.

#### Structural Division of Labor: Department of Military Affairs (DMA) vs. Department of Defence (DoD)

A critical feature of India’s higher defense governance is the statutory demarcation between the **Department of Military Affairs (DMA)** headed by the **CDS** and the **Department of Defence (DoD)** headed by the civilian **Defence Secretary**. Rather than merging the entire Ministry of Defence under uniformed leadership—which would violate constitutional norms of civilian administrative accountability—the **Allocation of Business Rules (December 30, 2019)** bifurcated responsibilities along a functional fault line separating **force generation, joint restructuring, and revenue administration** (assigned to DMA) from **macro-defense policy, capital weapons acquisition, and territorial defense coordination** (retained by DoD):

* **Jurisdiction of the Department of Military Affairs (DMA under the CDS):**
  * **Force Structure & Theaterisation:** Facilitation of the restructuring of the **17 single-service commands** for optimal utilization of resources by bringing about jointness in operations, including the establishment of **Integrated Joint/Theater Commands**.
  * **Service-Specific Administration & Revenue Procurement:** Administrative governance of the **Armed Forces of the Union** (Army, Navy, and Air Force), the **Integrated Headquarters of the MoD** (Army HQ, Naval HQ, Air HQ, and HQ IDS), the **Territorial Army**, and all **revenue procurement** (ammunition, spares, fuel, rations, and maintenance contracts) exclusive to the Defence Services.
  * **Promotions, Training, & Indigenous Equipment Mandates:** Oversight of senior military promotions, deputation, joint training academies (**NDA**, **DSSC**, **CDM**, **NDC**), and promoting the use of indigenous equipment across the armed forces.
* **Jurisdiction of the Department of Defence (DoD under the Civil Defence Secretary):**
  * **Supreme Statutory Responsibility for "Defence of India":** Under the Allocation of Business Rules, the civilian **Defence Secretary** remains explicitly responsible for the **"Defence of India and every part thereof including preparation for defence and all such acts as may be conducive in times of war to its prosecution and after its termination to effective demobilization."**
  * **Exclusive Control Over Capital Acquisitions:** While the **CDS** and **DMA** determine *what* weapons systems are prioritized across the three services based on operational threat assessments, the actual financial negotiation, tendering, contract execution, and vendor management for all **Capital Acquisitions** (under **DAP 2020**) remain vested in the **Acquisition Wing** under the **Department of Defence (DoD)**. This institutional separation ensures an independent civilian check against procurement conflict-of-interest while freeing the uniformed military leadership to focus on operational readiness and capability definition.
  * **Non-DMA Security & Infrastructure Agencies:** The DoD retains direct administrative control over the **Indian Coast Guard (ICG)**, the **Border Roads Organisation (BRO)**, **Defence Estates / Cantonment Boards**, the **National Cadet Corps (NCC)**, and defense diplomacy/bilateral treaties.

* **Armed Forces Special Operations Division (AFSOD):** Headquartered initially at **Agra/New Delhi** and commanded by a two-star **Major General** (or equivalent), **AFSOD** integrates elite special forces elements from all three services: the **Indian Army’s Parachute Regiment (Special Forces - Para SF)**, the **Indian Navy’s Marine Commandos (MARCOS)**, and the **Indian Air Force’s Garud Commando Force**. Prior to AFSOD, each service deployed its special forces largely for tactical service-specific missions (e.g., Garuds for airbase protection, MARCOS for maritime boarding). Under AFSOD, tri-service task forces train, equip, and deploy jointly using dedicated IAF special-operations transport platforms (**C-130J Super Hercules** and **Chinook** heavy-lift helicopters) to execute strategic cross-border direct action, high-value terrorist leadership neutralization, hostage rescue, and behind-enemy-lines reconnaissance in denied environments.
* **Defence Space Agency (DSA):** Headquartered in **Bengaluru** and commanded by a two-star **Air Vice Marshal** (or equivalent), the **DSA** absorbed the **Defence Imagery Processing and Analysis Centre (DIPAC)** in New Delhi and the **Defence Satellite Control Centre (DSCC)** in Bhopal. Operating alongside the **Defence Space Research Organisation (DSRO)**, the DSA manages India’s military space situational awareness, anti-satellite (**ASAT**) kinetic deterrence validated under **Mission Shakti (March 2019)**, electronic counter-space operations, and real-time geospatial intelligence (GEOINT) distribution from dedicated military satellites (**GSAT-7 Rukmini** for the Navy, **GSAT-7A Angry Bird** for the Air Force/Army, **EMISAT** electronic intelligence platform, and the **₹26,968-crore Space-Based Surveillance Phase-III [SBS-3]** constellation of 52 low-earth and geostationary defense satellites approved by the CCS in October 2024).
* **Defence Cyber Agency (DCyA):** Headquartered in **New Delhi** and commanded by a two-star **Rear Admiral** (or equivalent), the **DCyA** coordinates with the **National Cyber Security Coordinator (NCSC)** and **NCIIPC** while commanding cyber-warfare cells across all three services. Unlike civilian agencies restricted to defensive network patching, the **DCyA** is doctrinally mandated to conduct both defensive hardening of military **Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (C4ISR)** grids (including the transition to the indigenous Ubuntu-based **Maya OS** across Ministry of Defence terminals) and **active offensive cyber operations**—capable of degrading adversary air-defense radar networks, military satellite uplinks, and battlefield command nodes during kinetic hostilities.

Note that while **AFSOD**, **DSA**, and **DCyA** were constituted as two-star "Agencies/Divisions" rather than full three-star "Commands" (as originally envisaged by the Naresh Chandra Task Force), this graduated phasing allowed the CDS to operationalize multi-domain doctrinal synergy and validate tri-service staffing tables before scaling them into full-fledged functional commands alongside geographic theaterisation.

#### Supply-Chain Integration: Joint Logistics Nodes (JLNs) at Mumbai, Guwahati, and Port Blair

In high-intensity multi-domain warfare, tactical maneuver is dictated by the velocity and resilience of the logistics tail. Historically, an Indian Army brigade, an Indian Navy base, and an Indian Air Force station operating within the exact same geographic sector maintained three completely segregated supply chains, separate fuel dumps, separate civil-hired transport contracts, and isolated inventory databases for identical items such as rations, general engineering stores, construction material, and common-caliber small-arms ammunition.

To dismantle this logistical duplication, the **Chief of Defence Staff** operationalized three **Joint Logistics Nodes (JLNs)** between **January and April 2021** under the overarching control of the **Joint Operations Division (HQ IDS)**:

1. **JLN Mumbai (Operationalized January 1, 2021):** Anchors tri-service logistical pooling across the Western seaboard, integrating supply lines for **Western Naval Command**, **Southern Army Command** formations in Maharashtra/Gujarat, and **South Western Air Command** bases.
2. **JLN Guwahati (Operationalized January 1, 2021):** Serves as the critical multimodal logistics hub for the **Eastern Theater**, pooling aviation fuel, winter clothing, heavy engineering bridging equipment, rations, and civil-hired rail/road transport for Army Mountain Divisions and IAF Advanced Landing Grounds (ALGs) facing the **Line of Actual Control (LAC)** in **Arunachal Pradesh** and the **Siliguri Corridor**.
3. **JLN Port Blair (Operationalized April 1, 2021):** Directly supports the **Andaman and Nicobar Command (ANC)**, eliminating the absurdity where three services chartered separate merchant vessels from the mainland to ferry identical fuels, lubricants, rations, and spares to littoral island outposts from **Narkondam** in the north to **Campbell Bay (INS Baaz)** in the south.

By integrating service-specific digital inventory portals—the Army’s **Computerised Inventory Control Project (CICP)**, the Navy’s **Integrated Logistics Management System (ILMS)**, and the Air Force’s **Integrated Materials Management Online System (IMMOLS)**—into a unified **Tri-Service Logistics IT Application**, the **JLNs** enable **"cross-servicing."** Under cross-servicing, an Army forward formation can draw fuel, rations, or common spares directly from the nearest Naval depot or Air Force base without inter-ministerial financial billing bottlenecks, compressing turnaround times and generating substantial economies of scale in bulk defense procurement.

## III. Statutory Foundations of Jointness: The Inter-Services Organisations (Command, Control and Discipline) Act, 2023 and ISO Rules, 2025

### 5. Pre-2023 Legal Fragmentation vs. Statutory Unification under the ISO Act, 2023

#### The Tri-Act Disciplinary Bottleneck (Army Act 1950, Air Force Act 1950, Navy Act 1957)

While the creation of the **CDS** and **DMA** in 2019–2020 solved the *institutional* apex problem, the operationalization of **Integrated Theater Commands** faced a formidable *statutory* roadblock embedded in India’s military justice architecture. Under Article 33 of the Constitution, Parliament enacted three completely separate disciplinary codes for the armed forces: the **Army Act, 1950**, the **Air Force Act, 1950**, and the **Navy Act, 1957**. Under these legacy statutes, legal command, administrative jurisdiction, and summary disciplinary authority were strictly service-specific: only an officer subject to the **Army Act, 1950** could exercise statutory disciplinary powers over Army personnel; only a Naval officer under the **Navy Act, 1957** could discipline sailors; and only an Air Force officer under the **Air Force Act, 1950** could exercise legal authority over airmen.

In existing tri-service establishments—such as the **Andaman and Nicobar Command (ANC)**, **Strategic Forces Command (SFC)**, **HQ IDS**, **National Defence Academy (NDA)**, or **AFSOD**—this legal fragmentation created severe administrative paralysis. If an Army Major General commanding a tri-service formation needed to initiate disciplinary proceedings or summary trial against an errant Air Force corporal or Naval lieutenant serving directly under his operational command, the Army Commander possessed **zero statutory jurisdiction** to punish or court-martial that individual. Instead, the offender had to be physically repatriated or administratively reverted to his parent service unit (often thousands of kilometers away), where a parent-service Commanding Officer unfamiliar with the operational context had to conduct the inquiry de novo. In a high-tempo **Integrated Theater Command** comprising 1,50,000+ personnel from all three services, such legal fragmentation would render unified command impossible.

#### Anatomy of the Inter-Services Organisations (Command, Control and Discipline) Act, 2023

To demolish this statutory barrier without repealing or rewriting the three foundational Service Acts (which protect unique service traditions, promotion boards, and specialized military offenses), Parliament passed the **Inter-Services Organisations (Command, Control and Discipline) Act, 2023** in August 2023. Brought into force via Gazette Notification on **May 10, 2024**, and supplemented by the notification of existing tri-service bodies as ISOs on **December 27, 2024**, the **ISO Act, 2023** serves as the legal keystone for military theaterisation:

* **Section 3 (Constitution of Inter-Services Organisations and Joint Services Commands):** Empowers the Central Government, by notification in the Official Gazette, to constitute an **Inter-Services Organisation (ISO)**—which expressly includes a **Joint Services Command (Theater Command)**—comprising personnel belonging to at least two of the three services (Army, Navy, and Air Force), placed under the command of a **Commander-in-Chief (C-in-C)** or an **Officer-in-Command (O-i-C)**. Under Section 3(2), existing tri-service entities (**ANC**, **SFC**, **NDA**, **DSSC**, **HQ IDS**, **AFSOD**, **DSA**, **DCyA**) were deemed and notified as ISOs.
* **Section 4 & Section 5 (Vesting of Full Disciplinary and Administrative Control):** This is the core enabling mechanism of the statute. **Section 4** overrides the jurisdictional silos of the 1950/1957 Service Acts by stipulating that the **Commander-in-Chief** or **Officer-in-Command** of an ISO shall exercise complete command, disciplinary, and administrative control over every person appointed, deputed, posted, or attached to that ISO, **irrespective of the service to which the person belongs**. Crucially, while the *substantive offense, punishment scale, and pension/service conditions* of an individual remain governed by their respective parent **Service Act** (so a sailor is still tried for offenses defined under the Navy Act, 1957), the **C-in-C of the Theater Command** (even if he is an Army General or Air Marshal) is legally vested with all the powers exercisable by the **Chief of the Naval Staff** or **Flag Officer Commanding-in-Chief** under that Act to convene Courts-Martial, confirm sentences, dispose of summary trials, and issue administrative orders.
* **Section 7 (Empowerment of the Commanding Officer of an Inter-Services Unit):** Extends unified authority down to the tactical battalion/squadron/ship level by empowering the **Commanding Officer (CO)** of a composite Inter-Services Unit to initiate all disciplinary and administrative actions over multi-service personnel serving within that unit.
* **Section 8 & Section 9 (Central Government Superintendence and Expedition of Proceedings):** Places all ISOs under the overarching superintendence of the **Central Government**, which may issue binding policy directions on grounds of national security or public interest, while eliminating the requirement to reconvene separate Courts of Inquiry across parent services.

### 6. Operationalizing Unified Command: The Inter-Services Organisations Rules, 2025

#### Statutory Mechanics of the ISO Rules, 2025 (Sections 3 to 11 Execution)

While the **ISO Act, 2023** established the overarching statutory skeleton when enforced in **May 2024**, day-to-day summary trials, leave sanctions, chain-of-command succession during a Commander’s absence, and procedural rules of evidence across mixed-service formations required granular subordinate legislation. Framed by the Ministry of Defence under **Section 11 of the ISO Act, 2023**, the **Inter-Services Organisations (Command, Control and Discipline) Rules, 2025** were notified in the Official Gazette and came into force on **May 27, 2025**, rendering the statutory architecture for **Integrated Theater Commands** 100% operational:

* **Clear Chain of Command & Officiating Succession:** The **ISO Rules, 2025** lay down explicit protocols for the continuity of command when the substantive **Commander-in-Chief (C-in-C)**, **Officer-in-Command (O-i-C)**, or **Commanding Officer (CO)** of an Inter-Services Organisation is absent on operational duty, leave, or temporary incapacity. Previously, acting/officiating officers from a different service could be legally challenged in Armed Forces Tribunals (AFT) if they signed charge sheets or administrative orders; the 2025 Rules vest the notified officiating incumbent with seamless statutory authority.
* **Harmonized Disciplinary Disposal & Non-Duplication of Inquiries:** The Rules codify the procedural mechanics through which a mixed-service **Court of Inquiry (CoI)** or **Board of Officers** convened by an ISO head is legally valid across all three Service Acts simultaneously. A single investigation into an operational lapse, logistics irregularity, or disciplinary breach inside a **Joint Logistics Node** or **Theater Command** can now be directly acted upon by the ISO Commander to award summary punishments, order attachments, or convene a General/District Court Martial without referring the file back to parent Service HQs.
* **Residuary Powers Clause:** To prevent any unforeseen statutory vacuum when reconciling differences between the **Army Rules (1954)**, **Navy Regulations**, and **Air Force Rules (1969)** inside a joint command, the **ISO Rules, 2025** incorporate a **Residuary Powers** provision specifying that any procedural or administrative ambiguity not explicitly covered under the delegated powers of the C-in-C, O-i-C, or CO shall be referred to the **Central Government (Department of Military Affairs)** for final binding determination.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Governing Statutes** | **Army Act (1950)**, **Air Force Act (1950)**, **Navy Act (1957)** operating in strict mutual exclusion. | **ISO Act, 2023** (enforced **May 10, 2024**) + **ISO Rules, 2025** (enforced **May 27, 2025**) overlying the 3 Service Acts. |
| **Disciplinary Jurisdiction of Joint Commander** | Restricted strictly to personnel belonging to the Commander’s own parent service. | **Full disciplinary and administrative authority** over all attached **Army, Navy, and IAF** personnel regardless of uniform. |
| **Handling of Offenders from Other Services** | Mandatory repatriation/reversion of the offender back to parent Service unit for de novo inquiry and trial. | Direct disposal, summary trial, and Court-Martial convening within the **Inter-Services Organisation / Theater Command**. |

| **Impact on Theaterisation** | Major legal bottleneck preventing a Theater Commander from commanding tri-service troops in combat. | Complete statutory enabler for **Western, Northern, and Maritime Integrated Theater Commands**. |

## IV. Military Theaterisation and Integrated Warfighting Commands: From 17 Single-Service Silos to Multi-Domain Synergy

### 7. The Architecture of Integrated Theater Commands: Western, Northern, and Maritime Commands

#### Spatial De-Confliction Crisis of the 17 Single-Service Commands

Prior to the rollout of theaterisation, the Indian Armed Forces operated through **17 Single-Service Commands** (alongside the two tri-service commands, **ANC** and **SFC**, making 19 total commands):
* **Indian Army (7 Commands):** Northern Command (**Udhampur**), Western Command (**Chandimandir**), South Western Command (**Jaipur**), Central Command (**Lucknow**), Eastern Command (**Kolkata**), Southern Command (**Pune**), and Army Training Command (**ARTRAC, Shimla**).
* **Indian Air Force (7 Commands):** Western Air Command (**New Delhi**), South Western Air Command (**Gandhinagar**), Central Air Command (**Prayagraj**), Eastern Air Command (**Shillong**), Southern Air Command (**Thiruvananthapuram**), Training Command (**Bengaluru**), and Maintenance Command (**Nagpur**).
* **Indian Navy (3 Commands):** Western Naval Command (**Mumbai**), Eastern Naval Command (**Visakhapatnam**), and Southern Naval Command (**Kochi**—Training).

This 17-command layout suffered from a fatal geographical pathology: **not a single operational Command Headquarters of the Army, Navy, and Air Force was co-located in the same city**, nor were their geographical boundaries coterminous. Similarly, along the Pakistan frontier, three Army Commands (Northern, Western, South Western) overlapped awkwardly with two Air Force Commands (Western and South Western) and the Western Naval Command.

#### Blueprint of the Three Integrated Theater Commands (MoD’s 2025 "Year of Reforms")

On **January 1, 2025**, Defence Minister **Rajnath Singh** officially declared **2025 as the "Year of Reforms"** in the Ministry of Defence, placing the rollout of **Integrated Theater Commands (ITCs)** at the center of India’s military modernization mandate. Moving away from earlier fragmented proposals that contemplated separate Air Defence, Peninsular, and multiple Land Commands, the **Department of Military Affairs (DMA)** and the three Service Chiefs converged on an adversary-centric **"One Border, One Threat, One Theater Commander"** architecture comprising **Three Integrated Geographic Theater Commands**:

1. **Western Theater Command (Headquartered at Jaipur — Pakistan-Facing):**
   * **Geographic & Operational Scope:** Covers the entire western terrestrial frontier against **Pakistan**, stretching from the **Saltoro Ridge / Siachen Glacier** and the **Line of Control (LoC)** in Jammu & Kashmir, down through the **International Border (IB)** across Punjab, Rajasthan, and the **Sir Creek** marshlands in Gujarat.
   * **Force Composition:** Integrates the strike and holding corps of the **Army’s Western Command (Chandimandir)**, **South Western Command (Jaipur)**, Pakistan-facing elements of **Northern Command**, and desert formations of **Southern Command**, fused with the offensive air assets and air-defense grids of the **IAF’s Western Air Command** and **South Western Air Command**, alongside tactical **Border Security Force (BSF)** battalions under operational control during hostilities.
2. **Northern Theater Command (Headquartered at Lucknow — China LAC-Facing):**
   * **Geographic & Operational Scope:** Directly counters China’s **PLA Western Theater Command** across the entire **3,488-km Line of Actual Control (LAC)**—spanning **Eastern Ladakh (Depsang, Galwan, Pangong Tso, Demchok)**, the **Central Sector (Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand)**, and the **Eastern Sector (Sikkim, the Chumbi Valley/Siliguri Corridor, and Arunachal Pradesh)**—while also encompassing the northern/eastern land frontiers with **Nepal, Bhutan, Myanmar, and Bangladesh**.
   * **Force Composition:** Integrates the **XIV Corps (Leh)**, **Central Army Command (Lucknow)**, **Eastern Army Command (Kolkata)**, and the **XVII Mountain Strike Corps (Panagarh)** with the high-altitude air superiority, transport, and deep-strike assets of the **IAF’s Central Air Command (Prayagraj)** and **Eastern Air Command (Shillong)**, plus operational integration of the **Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP)** and **Assam Rifles**.
3. **Maritime Theater Command (Headquartered at Karwar / Thiruvananthapuram — Indian Ocean Domain):**
   * **Geographic & Operational Scope:** Encompasses the entire **Indian Ocean Region (IOR)**, guarding India’s **11,098-km coastline**, **2.02 million sq km Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)**, island territories (**Lakshadweep** and **Andaman & Nicobar**), and critical maritime choke points from the **Strait of Hormuz** and **Bab-el-Mandeb** in the west to the **Six Degree Channel / Malacca Strait** in the east.
   * **Force Composition:** Integrates the **Western Naval Command (Mumbai)** and **Eastern Naval Command (Visakhapatnam)**, subsumes the existing tri-service **Andaman and Nicobar Command (ANC, Port Blair)**, and absorbs dedicated **IAF maritime strike squadrons** (**Su-30MKI** aircraft armed with air-launched **BrahMos** supersonic cruise missiles and **Jaguar DARIN-III** maritime strike aircraft), **Army amphibious brigades** (such as the **91 Infantry Brigade** at Thiruvananthapuram), and the **Indian Coast Guard (ICG)**.

Under this theaterised command division, a fundamental separation of powers is established between **Operational Command** and **Force Generation ("Raise, Train, and Sustain")**:
* **The Three Service Chiefs (COAS, CNS, CAS):** Relinquish direct operational command of wartime combat formations and focus on the **"Raise, Train, and Sustain" (RTS)** function—managing recruitment, specialized service training, technical maintenance, equipment induction, and administrative welfare.
* **The Four-Star Integrated Theater Commanders:** Exercise direct wartime and peacetime operational command over all land, air, sea, space, cyber, and special-operations assets allocated to their geographic theater, reporting operationally to the **Chiefs of Staff Committee (CoSC)** chaired by the **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)** and the political executive (**Defence Minister / CCS**).

### 8. Resolving Structural Friction: Asset Indivisibility, Promotion Parity, and Network-Centric Multi-Domain Operations

#### Indian Air Force Doctrine of Asset Indivisibility vs. Theaterised Allocation

The most intense doctrinal debate during the design of India’s Integrated Theater Commands centered on the **Indian Air Force’s (IAF)** core strategic principle of **"Asset Indivisibility"** and centralized control of airpower. Operating with approximately **31 fighter squadrons** against a sanctioned establishment of **42.5 squadrons** (prior to the full induction of **83 Tejas Mk-1A** and **97 additional Tejas Mk-1A** aircraft), the IAF leadership argued that aerospace power possesses unique characteristics of **speed, range, and flexibility (swing-role capability)** that allow the same squadron of **Rafale** or **Su-30MKI** multi-role fighters to strike high-altitude targets along the **Ladakh LAC** in the morning and swing 1,200 km south to execute deep interdiction in the **Rajasthan/Sindh sector** by afternoon.

The IAF cautioned that permanently parceling out its limited fighter squadrons, **Airborne Warning and Control Systems (AWACS / Netra)**, **Flight Refuelling Aircraft (FRA / IL-78 tankers)**, and long-range **S-400 Triumf** surface-to-air missile squadrons into geographically rigid, Army-dominated continental theaters would reduce a strategic instrument into penny-packets of tactical "flying artillery," leaving individual theaters under-resourced while preventing rapid strategic concentration of airpower across theatres.

To resolve this structural friction between **continental geographic permanence** and **aerospace flexibility**, India’s theaterisation model incorporates four institutional safeguards:

* **Core Theater Allocation + Centralized Strategic Swing Pool:** While dedicated tactical air assets (attack helicopters, tactical airlift, point air defense, and baseline multi-role squadrons) are assigned to the **Western**, **Northern**, and **Maritime Theater Commands** for seamless joint integration, strategic force-multipliers—such as **Flight Refuelling Aircraft (IL-78)**, **AEW&C/AWACS**, strategic heavy-lift (**C-17 Globemaster III**), and a central reserve of swing-role fighter squadrons (**Rafale / Su-30MKI**)—are managed via a dynamic inter-theater allocation mechanism overseen by the **CoSC (CDS and Service Chiefs)**. This enables rapid re-tasking of airpower from a dormant theater to a hot theater within hours without bureaucratic friction.
* **Integrated Air Defence & Sensor-to-Shooter Fusion (IACCS – Akashteer – Trigun):** Instead of splitting air defense into isolated geographical pockets, theaterisation fuses the **Indian Air Force’s Integrated Air Command and Control System (IACCS)** with the **Indian Army’s Akashteer** automated air defense control and reporting system and the **Indian Navy’s Trigun** maritime domain awareness network. Demonstrated with lethal efficacy during **Operation Sindoor (May 2025)**—where the **IACCS-Akashteer** integrated grid neutralized waves of cross-border loitering munitions, drones, and cruise missiles while coordinating stand-off precision strikes across Pakistan and PoK—this unified digital backbone ensures that every radar (whether Army, Navy, or IAF) feeds a single composite air situational picture to prevent fratricide and optimize interceptor engagement (**S-400**, **MR-SAM/Barak-8**, **Akash-NG**).

#### Cross-Service Promotion Parity, Common Data-Links, and Multi-Domain Network-Centric Warfighting

Beyond air-asset allocation, transitioning 1.4 million personnel from three distinct organizational cultures into unified warfighting commands required resolving deep-seated personnel, technological, and career-progression asymmetries:

* **Cross-Service Promotion Parity and Rotational Command Architecture:** The Indian Army constitutes roughly 85% of India’s uniformed manpower, raising legitimate concerns within the **Indian Navy** and **Indian Air Force** that geographic Theater Commands might become permanent Army fiefdoms. To guarantee inter-service institutional equity:
  1. **Four-Star Parity & Rotational Eligibility:** Theater Commanders are appointed at the four-star rank (**General / Admiral / Air Chief Marshal**), and eligibility to command the **Western** and **Northern Theater Commands** is kept open across the **Army** and **Indian Air Force** on a rotational/merit basis (while the **Maritime Theater Command** is anchored by a Naval four-star Commander-in-Chief with an Air Force or Army three-star Deputy Commander).
  2. **Mandatory Cross-Service Deputy Commanders:** Whenever an Army General heads a continental Theater Command, the **Deputy Theater Commander** is mandatorily drawn from the **Indian Air Force** (and vice versa), ensuring that operational planning incorporates specialized aerospace and land-warfare competence at the highest command tier.
  3. **Harmonized Annual Confidential Reports (ACRs) & Cross-Postings:** Because the three services historically used incompatible grading scales in their **Annual Confidential Reports (ACRs)** (causing inflation or deflation bias when an Army general appraised an Naval or Air Force staff officer), the **DMA** instituted a standardized **Common Tri-Service Appraisal System** for officers posted in joint establishments, alongside cross-posting over **100+ officers** (Majors to Colonels and equivalents) directly into sister-service field units, warships, and airbases, and unifying curricula at the **National Defence Academy (NDA)**, **Defence Services Staff College (DSSC, Wellington)**, **College of Defence Management (CDM, Secunderabad)**, and **National Defence College (NDC, New Delhi)**.
* **Common Communication Data-Links and Network-Centric Warfighting:** Historically, the three services procured separate, proprietary tactical data-links: the Navy deployed **Link-II** (backed by **GSAT-7 Rukmini**), the Air Force deployed the **Operational Data Link (ODL / AFNET)**, and the Army developed the **Tactical Communications System (TCS)** and **Project Sitaara**. Under the DMA’s jointness reforms, these disparate networks are bridged via the tri-service optical-fiber and satellite **Defence Communication Network (DCN)** and **Software Defined Radios (SDRs)** operating on common encrypted waveforms. In a multi-domain engagement, a **Naval P-8I Neptune** maritime patrol aircraft or a **DSA SBS-3 satellite** detecting a target can transmit target coordinates instantaneously across a common data-link to an **Army BrahMos land-attack battery** or an **IAF Su-30MKI** without voice-relay delays.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 11: From Military Command Integration to Internal Security and Intelligence Fusion

While **Higher Defense Management**, the **ISO Act, 2023 / Rules 2025**, and **Integrated Theater Commands** optimize the Indian Armed Forces to defeat external state adversaries across land, sea, air, space, and cyber domains, modern national security threats do not stop at the Line of Control or the Exclusive Economic Zone. External military aggression is continuously preceded and accompanied by covert intelligence penetration, transnational terror financing, sub-conventional sabotage, and domestic law-and-order destabilization.
