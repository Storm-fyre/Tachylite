# Part 6: Maritime and Coastal Security Architecture: Littoral Defense, Island Territories, Anti-Piracy, and Post-26/11 Overhaul

## I. Geopolitical Bathymetry, Littoral Vulnerability, and the Post-26/11 Structural Rupture

### 1. Hydrographic Exposure, Critical Offshore Infrastructure, and the Artisanal-Security Friction

Maritime security across the Indian peninsula is governed by a structural tension between two competing state imperatives: maximizing frictionless commercial maritime throughput and artisanal livelihood across an expansive open oceanic frontier, versus enforcing high-resolution positive identification and territorial denial against sub-conventional infiltration. Unlike terrestrial borders—where physical obstacles, concertina wire, and thermal-laser fences (**CIBMS**) can channelize movement—the maritime domain is a fluid, three-dimensional commons governed by the **United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982)**. Under UNCLOS, foreign vessels enjoy the right of "innocent passage" even within a coastal state's **12-nautical-mile (nm) Territorial Sea**, and freedom of navigation within the **200-nm Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)**. This legal and physical fluidity creates an asymmetric advantage for non-state actors, transnational narco-syndicates, and state-sponsored proxy operatives who hide within the dense commercial and fishing clutter of the Indian Ocean Region (IOR).

India’s hydrographic geometry presents a formidable surveillance and interdiction challenge. Bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, the Bay of Bengal to the east, and the Indian Ocean proper to the south, the Indian state must secure a standard hydrographic baseline coastline of **7,516.6 km** (comprising **5,422.6 km** across nine mainland coastal states and four Union Territories, and **2,094 km** across island archipelagos), encompassing **1,382 islands** and rocks, and an **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) of 2.02 million sq km** (projected to expand toward 3 million sq km with extended continental shelf claims). Along this littoral ribbon sits the industrial and economic spine of the republic, creating high-value stationary targets exposed to sea-borne sabotage, stand-off loitering munitions, and amphibious commando raids.

#### Critical Littoral and Offshore Asset Exposure

* **Port Infrastructure Asymmetry (12 Major vs. 200+ Non-Major Ports):** Maritime trade constitutes **95% of India’s total trade volume** and **70% by value**. While the **12 Major Ports** (operating under the Major Port Authorities Act, 2021) maintain rigorous compliance with the International Maritime Organization’s **International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code**, automated container radiation scanners, and armed **Central Industrial Security Force (CISF)** cover, India’s **200+ Non-Major (Intermediate and Minor) Ports** fall under the jurisdiction of state maritime boards and private concessionaires. Fewer than a third of operational non-major ports maintain comprehensive multi-layered biometric access control, underwater hull-inspection sonars, or CISF cover, turning minor ports and captive jetties into soft entry nodes for contraband, Dual-Use goods, and arms consignments.
* **Offshore Energy and Single Point Moorings (SPMs):** Offshore hydrocarbon complexes—most notably **Mumbai High**, Bassein, and Panna-Mukta on the western continental shelf, and the **Krishna-Godavari (KG) Basin** on the eastern seaboard—produce over half of India's domestic crude oil and natural gas. Alongside dozens of offshore **Single Point Moorings (SPMs)** and coastal Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) regasification terminals (Dahej, Hazira, Dabhol, Kochi, Ennore), these unmanned or lightly crewed steel platforms sit tens of nautical miles offshore, highly vulnerable to fast-attack craft, frogman sabotage, or Unmanned Surface Vessels (USVs).
* **Coastal Nuclear, Space, and Defense Complexes:** Geographic necessity for coolant water and launch azimuth safety places India’s premier strategic assets directly on the littoral edge. These include the **Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC)** at Trombay, nuclear power stations at **Tarapur** (Maharashtra), **Kalpakkam** and **Kudankulam** (Tamil Nadu), the **Satish Dhawan Space Centre (SHAR)** at Sriharikota, the **Integrated Test Range (ITR)** at Chandipur/Abdul Kalam Island (Odisha), and strategic naval shipyards and submarine bases at Mumbai, Karwar (**Project Seabird**), Visakhapatnam, and Rambilli (**Project Varsha**).
* **Submarine Cable Landing Stations (CLS):** Over **99% of India’s international internet, SWIFT financial clearing, and sovereign cloud data traffic** transits through undersea fiber-optic cables making landfall at high-density choke points in **Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Tuticorin, and Digha**. In the shallow continental shelf waters (depths under 100 meters), these cables are vulnerable to deliberate anchor-dragging sabotage by commercial shadow fleets and autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs).
* **The Artisanal Clutter Dilemma:** Over **4 million Indian fisherfolk** operate roughly **2.5 lakh to 3 lakh fishing vessels** out of more than **1,500 designated and undesignated fish landing centers**. Because over **80% of this fleet consists of sub-20-meter wooden or fiberglass-reinforced plastic (FRP) boats** with minimal radar cross-sections (RCS) that historically lacked transponders, security agencies face an acute signal-to-noise problem: distinguishing a single rogue vessel carrying explosives or narcotics from 50,000 legitimate fishing craft operating simultaneously at sea.

### 2. Anatomy of the November 2008 (26/11) Sea-Borne Infiltration and Pre-2008 Pathologies

Prior to November 2008, India’s coastal security architecture operated on a reactive, terrestrial-centric mindset, despite the unmistakable warning of the **March 1993 Mumbai serial blasts**, in which RDX explosives and AK-56 rifles supplied by Pakistan’s **Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI)** and the **D-Company** syndicate were landed completely unchallenged across the Raigad coast (Shekhadi and Dighi jetties) in Maharashtra. Although the government initiated **Operation Swan** in April 1993—establishing a rudimentary joint coastal patrolling mechanism involving the Navy, Coast Guard, State Police, and Customs across Maharashtra and Gujarat—the system suffered from chronic budgetary neglect, lack of statutory role clarity, and the absence of fused electronic surveillance.

The structural collapse of this fragmented arrangement occurred during the **November 2008 (26/11) Mumbai terror attacks**, when ten **Lashkar-e-Taiba (LeT)** *fidayeen* operatives, trained in amphibious navigation and high-altitude marine survival at Azizabad and Muzaffarabad under the operational supervision of the ISI and LeT’s naval wing, executed a clinical maritime infiltration across the Arabian Sea.

#### Systemic Institutional Failures Exposed by 26/11

1. **Identity Camouflage via Unmonitored Fishing Trawlers:** By hijacking the Porbandar-registered Indian fishing trawler ***MV Kuber*** near the International Maritime Boundary Line (IMBL), the LeT assault team neutralized visual and hull-profile suspicion. Because Indian fishing vessels lacked mandatory **Automatic Identification System (AIS)** transponders, satellite tracking beacons, or tamper-proof biometric crew manifests, *MV Kuber* traversed over **550 nautical miles** through India’s EEZ and territorial waters without triggering any electronic anomaly alert.
2. **Absence of a Coastal Radar Chain and Fused Picture:** In 2008, India possessed no integrated chain of coastal surface-search radars or electro-optic sensors. The **Vessel Traffic Management System (VTMS)** operated by the Mumbai Port Trust and Jawaharlal Nehru Port Trust (JNPT) was calibrated solely to guide large commercial merchant vessels (>300 GT) through shipping lanes, routinely filtering out small fishing boat radar returns as sea clutter. Furthermore, intelligence inputs generated by the **Research and Analysis Wing (R&AW)** and **Intelligence Bureau (IB)** regarding a potential LeT maritime threat against South Mumbai hotels were never correlated with real-time vessel movements because no central maritime fusion center existed.
3. **Jurisdictional Fragmentation Across 15+ Agencies:** Before the post-2008 overhaul, maritime governance was fractured across **more than 15 central and state ministries and departments**—including the Ministry of Defence (Navy and Coast Guard), Ministry of Home Affairs (State Police, BSF Water Wing, CISF), Ministry of Shipping (DG Shipping, Major Ports, Lighthouses), Ministry of Finance (Customs Preventive), Ministry of Agriculture (Department of Fisheries), and Ministry of Petroleum (ONGC offshore security). In the shallow littoral zone (0 to 12 nm), the Indian Navy viewed coastal policing as a constabulary task outside its blue-water warfighting doctrine, the Indian Coast Guard lacked the vessel density to monitor every creek and landing site, and the Maharashtra Police lacked seaworthy interceptor boats, night-vision equipment, and marine navigation training.
4. **Porous and Unregulated Fish Landing Points:** When the terrorists transferred from *MV Kuber* (4 nm offshore) into a high-speed inflatable Zodiac dinghy and made landfall at **Badhwar Park** (an urban fishermen's colony in Cuffe Parade, South Mumbai), they exploited a complete vacuum in physical jetty security. Even though local fishermen noticed armed strangers disembarking with heavy rucksacks and alerted local police stations, the absence of a dedicated **Joint Operations Centre (JOC)** or coastal quick-reaction protocol allowed the operatives to disperse into the metropolis to strike Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, the Taj Mahal Palace, the Oberoi Trident, Leopold Cafe, and Nariman House.

Following the Mumbai attacks, the **Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS)** in February 2009 executed a foundational structural intervention, dismantling the legacy siloed model and replacing it with a statutory, sensor-fused, and hierarchically accountable **Three-Tier Coastal and Maritime Defense Architecture**.

## II. The Three-Tier Coastal Defense Architecture and Apex Command Integration

### 3. Jurisdictional Zonation: Indian Navy, Indian Coast Guard, and State Marine Police

To eliminate the jurisdictional ambiguity that allowed *MV Kuber* to slip between blue-water naval patrols and terrestrial police stations, the February 2009 CCS directives mapped India’s domestic security forces directly onto the maritime spatial zones defined by **UNCLOS (1982)** and India’s **Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and other Maritime Zones Act, 1976**. However, rather than treating these zones as rigid, mutually exclusive walls, the architecture establishes **concentric, overlapping rings of responsibility** under unified operational command.

#### Tier 1: Outer Oceanic Rim and Overall Command (Indian Navy)

* **Overall Lead Mandate:** Under the post-26/11 cabinet mandate, the **Indian Navy** is designated as the **overall authority responsible for all facets of maritime security**, encompassing both offshore and coastal security. The Navy provides outer oceanic surveillance, deep-water interdiction beyond the territorial waters toward the **200-nm EEZ rim and high seas**, and high-intensity kinetic deterrence against state-backed maritime threats.
* **Command Integration:** To operationalize this mandate, the Commanders-in-Chief (C-in-Cs) of the three mainland Naval Commands (**Western Naval Command** at Mumbai, **Eastern Naval Command** at Visakhapatnam, and **Southern Naval Command** at Kochi) and the tri-service **Andaman and Nicobar Command** (at Port Blair / Sri Vijaya Puram) are concurrently designated as the **Commanders-in-Chief, Coastal Defence (C-in-C Coastal Defence)**. In a maritime emergency or active infiltration scenario, all Coast Guard and State Marine Police assets within that theatre come under the operational control of the respective C-in-C Coastal Defence.

#### Tier 2: Intermediate Territorial Waters and EEZ Constabulary (Indian Coast Guard)

* **Statutory Anchor and Lead Territorial Mandate:** Established on the recommendations of the **K.F. Rustamji Committee** and governed by the **Coast Guard Act, 1978**, the **Indian Coast Guard (ICG)** functions as a non-military armed force of the Union under the **Ministry of Defence (MoD)**. In the post-26/11 architecture, the ICG is designated as the **lead agency for coastal security in India's Territorial Waters (0 to 12 nm)**, explicitly including areas patrolled by the State Marine Police, while continuing its primary statutory responsibilities across the **Contiguous Zone (12 to 24 nm)** and **EEZ (up to 200 nm)**—namely protection of offshore installations (such as Mumbai High), anti-smuggling, marine environmental protection, and Search and Rescue (SAR).
* **Commander Coastal Command:** The **Director General of the Indian Coast Guard (DG ICG)** is designated as the **Commander Coastal Command** and is responsible for overall coordination between central and state agencies in all matters relating to coastal security. Operating over **155+ ships/hovercraft** and **75+ aircraft/helicopters** across five Coast Guard Regions (North-West at Gandhinagar, West at Mumbai, East at Chennai, North-East at Kolkata, and Andaman & Nicobar at Port Blair), the ICG also serves as the nodal agency for operating the **Coastal Surveillance Network (CSN)** radar chain.

#### Tier 3: Shallow Littoral Waters up to 12 nm (State Coastal/Marine Police)

* **The Coastal Security Scheme (CSS Phases I–III):** Because large naval frigates and Coast Guard Offshore Patrol Vessels (OPVs) have deep drafts that prevent them from entering shallow tidal creeks, estuaries, and mudflats (below 3 to 5 meters depth), the **0 to 12 nm shallow littoral zone** (and functionally the **0 to 5 nm inshore belt**) is assigned to the **State Coastal/Marine Police** of the nine coastal states and four Union Territories. Funded by the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)** under the **Coastal Security Scheme (CSS)**:
  * **CSS Phase I (2005–2011)** and **CSS Phase II (2011–2020):** Established **204 Coastal Police Stations (CPS)** equipped with over **200+ fast interceptor boats** (5-ton and 12-ton classes), **60+ jetties**, four-wheelers, and motorcycles, alongside a **National Academy of Coastal Policing (NACP)** established at **Okha (Devbhumi Dwarka, Gujarat)** under the BSF to provide standardized marine tactical and navigation training.
  * **CSS Phase III (2020–2026+):** Shifted focus from brick-and-mortar station construction to coastal mapping of vulnerable landing points, lifecycle maintenance contracts for interceptor boats, CCTV surveillance at fish landing centers, and integration of Coastal Police Stations into the digital **IMAC/NMDA** network.
* **Structural Bottlenecks in Tier 3:** Despite capital grants under CSS, Tier 3 remains the weakest link in the triad due to chronic federal-state administrative friction: (a) high attrition and reluctance of regular state police personnel to serve in marine wings due to seasickness and lack of sea-duty allowances; (b) severe non-availability of state police interceptor boats (often 30% to 40% out of commission) due to the inability of state police headquarters to manage specialized marine engine maintenance and dry-docking; and (c) the lack of dedicated captive jetties, forcing marine police to berth their boats at commercial or Coast Guard harbours miles away from their coastal police stations.

#### Specialized Harbour and Creek Force Multipliers

* **Sagar Prahari Bal (SPB):** Raised by the Indian Navy immediately post-26/11 (March 2009), the **Sagar Prahari Bal** is a specialized naval force comprising over **1,000 personnel** operating **80+ Fast Interceptor Crafts (FICs)** and Immediate Support Vessels (ISVs). Deployed across all major naval bases, dockyards, and adjoining commercial ports (Mumbai, Visakhapatnam, Kochi, Karwar, Chennai, Kolkata, Port Blair), the SPB provides round-the-clock high-speed waterborne perimeter security against suicide boat attacks and underwater saboteurs.
* **CISF and ISPS Port Security:** The **Central Industrial Security Force (CISF)** provides armed counter-terror security, perimeter access control, and cargo/vessel screening across all **12 Major Ports** in alignment with the **ISPS Code** supervised by the **Directorate General of Shipping**. In parallel, the **Border Security Force (BSF) Water Wing** deploys floating Border Outposts (BOPs) and swamp-capable All-Terrain Vehicles (ATVs) across the labyrinthine marshlands of **Sir Creek / Harami Nala** in Gujarat and the **Sundarbans** riverine delta in West Bengal.

#### The National Committee for Strengthening Maritime and Coastal Security (NCSMCS)

* **Composition and Mandate:** Constituted in **August 2009**, the **NCSMCS** is the highest inter-ministerial and federal-state deliberative body for coastal security, chaired directly by the **Cabinet Secretary**. Its membership comprises the Secretaries of Defence, Home Affairs, Shipping, Petroleum & Natural Gas, Fisheries, Finance (Revenue/Customs), the Chairman of the National Technical Research Organisation (NTRO), the Director of the Intelligence Bureau (IB), the Chief of the Naval Staff (CNS), the DG Coast Guard, and the **Chief Secretaries of all nine coastal states and Administrators of the four coastal Union Territories**.
* **Structural Function:** Meeting periodically to audit implementation bottlenecks, the NCSMCS uses the administrative authority of the Cabinet Secretary to compel coastal states to fill Marine Police vacancies, operationalize biometric card readers at fish landing centers, enforce security audits at non-major ports, and expedite land acquisition for **Coastal Surveillance Network (CSN)** radar towers.

#### The National Maritime Security Coordinator (NMSC, 2022)

* **Resolving the 20-Year Institutional Vacuum:** Although the **Group of Ministers (GoM) Report on Reforming the National Security System (2001)**—prepared in the wake of the Kargil War—had recommended creating an apex maritime coordinator, bureaucratic turf wars between the Ministry of Defence, Ministry of Home Affairs, and Ministry of Shipping stalled its creation for over two decades. While the NCSMCS met only episodically under an overburdened Cabinet Secretary, India lacked a dedicated, full-time domain specialist at the strategic apex.
* **Appointment and Charter (February 2022):** In **February 2022**, the Government of India appointed the country's first **National Maritime Security Coordinator (NMSC)** (a retired Vice Admiral of the Indian Navy) embedded within the **National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS)**, reporting to the **National Security Advisor (NSA)**. The NMSC functions as the permanent institutional bridge across military, para-military, and civilian maritime stakeholders—synchronizing the Indian Navy, Coast Guard, State Marine Police, DG Shipping, Ports, Fisheries, and intelligence agencies, overseeing the upgrade of **IMAC into the National Maritime Domain Awareness (NMDA)** project, and assessing holistic threats ranging from gray-zone maritime militias to undersea cable security and blue-economy vulnerabilities.

#### Joint Operations Centres (JOCs)

* **Tactical Fusion Nodes:** To translate apex directives into real-time coastal interdiction, four **Joint Operations Centres (JOCs)** were established post-26/11 at **Mumbai, Visakhapatnam, Kochi, and Port Blair (Sri Vijaya Puram)** under the command of the respective C-in-Cs Coastal Defence.
* **24x7 Multi-Agency Watch:** Each JOC is jointly manned round-the-clock by liaison officers from the **Indian Navy, Indian Coast Guard, State Marine Police, Customs, Intelligence Bureau, and Major/Non-Major Port Authorities**. Receiving live radar, AIS, and satellite feeds from **IMAC (Gurugram)** alongside human intelligence (HUMINT) from the **Multi-Agency Centre (MAC)** and fishermen watch groups (*Sagar Rakshaks*), the JOC can instantly vector the nearest naval warship, Coast Guard interceptor, or State Marine Police boat to intercept a suspect target without waiting for bureaucratic clearances across parent ministries.

## III. Technological Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA): From Radar Chains to Space-Based Fusion

### 5. The Sensor-to-Decision Loop: CSN, NC3I, IMAC, and the NMDA Transition

In modern littoral warfare, physical patrol vessels cannot achieve continuous spatial coverage across a 7,516.6 km coastline and 2.02 million sq km EEZ without an overarching electronic sensor grid. **Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA)** is defined as the effective understanding of anything associated with the maritime domain that could impact the security, safety, economy, or environment of a nation. Technically, achieving littoral MDA requires overcoming two physical constraints: first, the **radar horizon limit** caused by the curvature of the Earth (which restricts a sea-level radar to ~15–25 nm unless elevated on tall towers or lighthouses); and second, the **data-silo problem**, where coastal radar tracks, merchant vessel transponders, and customs manifests exist on incompatible software networks.

India systematically solved these engineering and network constraints through a four-layered technological architecture: **CSN -> NC3I -> IMAC -> NMDA**.

#### Coastal Surveillance Network (CSN Phases I & II)

* **CSN Phase I:** Operated by the **Indian Coast Guard** and developed indigenously in partnership with **Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL)**, Phase I established a chain of **46 static radar stations** (**36 along the mainland coast** and **10 across the Andaman & Nicobar and Lakshadweep islands**), strategically mounted atop existing **Directorate General of Lighthouses and Lightships (DGLL)** towers to maximize elevation and radar horizon.
* **CSN Phase II:** To eliminate blind spots between Phase I nodes and integrate commercial port traffic, Phase II added **38 static radar stations** and **4 Mobile Surveillance Systems (MSS)** (truck-mounted radar/electro-optic units for dynamic deployment along vulnerable creeks and beaches), bringing the total network to **84+ static and mobile radar stations**, alongside full electronic integration with the **Vessel Traffic Management Systems (VTMS)** at the Gulf of Kutch, Gulf of Khambhat, and major ports.
* **Multi-Sensor Payload:** Each CSN station is an automated, unmanned sensor suite comprising solid-state **Frequency Diversity Radar** (capable of detecting small wooden/FRP fishing boats in high sea states up to 25 nm away), **Electro-Optical / Infrared (EO/IR) long-range thermal cameras** (for visual day/night target classification up to 10–15 nm), **Automatic Identification System (AIS)** receivers, **VHF distress/communication transceivers**, and meteorological sensors. Furthermore, India has exported this indigenous CSN radar architecture to littoral neighbors including the **Maldives, Mauritius, Seychelles, and Sri Lanka**, creating an extended Indo-Pacific sensor shield.

#### NC3I Network and the Information Management and Analysis Centre (IMAC)

* **NC3I Transport Layer:** Commissioned in **2014**, the **National Command Control Communication and Intelligence (NC3I)** network is a high-bandwidth, encrypted terrestrial fiber-optic and satellite communication backbone linking **51 operational nodes** of the Indian Navy and Indian Coast Guard across the mainland and island territories.
* **IMAC (Gurugram):** Located at **Gurugram (Haryana)** and jointly operated by the Indian Navy and Coast Guard, **IMAC** serves as the nerve center of the NC3I network. IMAC ingests real-time feeds from the **84+ CSN coastal radars**, space-based and coastal **AIS**, **Long-Range Identification and Tracking (LRIT)** systems, **Indian Navy maritime patrol aircraft**, and **White Shipping Agreements** (concluded with over 22 nations and multinational centers to exchange advance commercial vessel movement data), generating a single, unified **Common Operational Picture (COP)** of the entire Indian Ocean Region. Co-located within the IMAC campus since December 2018 is the **Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (IFC-IOR)**, which hosts international liaison officers (from the US, UK, France, Japan, Australia, etc.) to share non-classified maritime safety, anti-piracy, and contraband tracking data across the Indo-Pacific.

#### Transition to the National Maritime Domain Awareness (NMDA) Project

* **From Visual Monitoring to Predictive Multi-Agency AI Fusion:** While IMAC successfully fused military and Coast Guard sensor tracks, civilian maritime data—such as customs shipping bills, port entry logs, fishing boat registries, and narcotics intelligence—remained in separate ministerial databases. Under the oversight of the **NMSC**, the government initiated the ₹2,500+ crore **National Maritime Domain Awareness (NMDA)** upgrade (developed with BEL and C-DAC).
* **Architecture of NMDA:** NMDA expands the **IMAC** backbone from a bi-service (Navy/ICG) network into a **15+ agency national grid**, directly connecting the operational nodes of the **State Marine Police, Directorate General of Shipping, Major and Non-Major Ports, Customs (CBIC), Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB), Department of Fisheries, Petroleum & Natural Gas, and Intelligence Bureau**. Utilizing indigenous **Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML)** algorithms, NMDA automates threat triage across tens of thousands of simultaneous vessel tracks—instantly flagging **"dark ships"** (vessels that turn off AIS transponders while still appearing on Synthetic Aperture Radar [SAR] satellite imagery or CSN radars), suspicious loitering near submarine cables or offshore oil rigs, and mid-sea mother-ship-to-trawler rendezvous indicative of narcotics or arms transshipment.

#### Solving the Sub-20m Tracking Bottleneck: AIS vs. NavIC Transponders

* **The AIS Limitation (>20m Vessels):** Under SOLAS conventions and DG Shipping mandates, **Automatic Identification System (AIS)** Class A and Class B transponders are compulsory for all merchant vessels over **300 Gross Tonnage (GT)** and all mechanically propelled fishing vessels **above 20 meters in length**. However, standard VHF-based AIS transponders are prohibitively expensive (₹30,000–₹50,000+), consume heavy battery power that drains small fishing boat alternators, and have line-of-sight range limitations unless picked up by space-based AIS receivers. Consequently, India’s **2+ lakh sub-20-meter fishing boats** remained electronically invisible.
* **ISRO’s NavIC-Based Satellite Transponders (<20m Vessels):** To solve this engineering and economic constraint, the **Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)**, in collaboration with the Department of Fisheries, Indian Navy, and Coast Guard, developed an indigenous **Mobile Satellite Service (MSS) S-band transponder** powered by India’s sovereign **NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation)** positioning system and **GSAT-6 / GSAT-N** communication satellites. Rolled out across **1,00,000+ sub-20m fishing vessels** (funded at zero cost to fishermen under the *Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana* [PMMSY] and state subsidies), this compact terminal provides:
  1. **Continuous Automated Tracking:** Broadcasts the boat's encrypted NavIC coordinates directly via satellite to **IMAC/NMDA** and fisheries monitoring centers far beyond coastal VHF range.
  2. **IMBL Geofencing and Distress Alerts:** Triggers automatic visual/audio alarms if a fisherman inadvertently approaches the **International Maritime Boundary Line (IMBL)** with Pakistan or Sri Lanka, and includes one-touch emergency buttons for **medical distress, fire, or piracy/hijacking**.
  3. **Two-Way Messaging via Bluetooth:** Connects to the fisherman’s smartphone via Bluetooth to deliver cyclone warnings (from INCOIS) and potential fishing zone (PFZ) advisories, creating a strong economic incentive for fishermen to keep the transponder switched on rather than tampering with it.

#### Biometric Identity Cards, Hull Colour-Coding, and ReALCraft

* **Biometric Identity Cards for Fishermen:** Following the 26/11 attacks, the Ministry of Home Affairs (via the Registrar General of India and coastal UTs) and the Ministry of Agriculture (Department of Fisheries) executed a massive drive to issue tamper-proof **Biometric Identity Cards** and **QR-coded Aadhaar-linked Fishery Cards** to over **2 million marine fishermen**. Boarding teams from the Indian Coast Guard and State Marine Police carry ruggedized **offline handheld card readers** at sea to verify the thumbprints and identity of crew members against the vessel's manifest instantly.
* **Colour-Coding and ReALCraft:** Every coastal state and UT enforces a mandatory **uniform colour-coding scheme** for the hull and wheelhouse cabin of its fishing boats (e.g., distinct colour combinations for Gujarat, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, etc.), allowing naval helicopters and Coast Guard patrol vessels to visually spot an out-of-state or unregistered vessel at a glance. Simultaneously, all manual state boat registries have been unified onto the **ReALCraft (Registration and Licensing of Fishing Craft)** national web portal, directly accessible to **JOCs** and **NMDA**.

#### Operational Stress-Testing: Exercise Sea Vigil and TROPEX

* **Biennial Exercise Sea Vigil:** Initiated in **January 2019** (marking a decade after 26/11) and conducted biennially (2019, 2021, 2022, 2024, and 2026), **Exercise Sea Vigil** is India’s largest pan-national coastal defense readiness audit. Spanning the entire **7,516.6 km coastline** and **2.02 million sq km EEZ** simultaneously, it mobilizes all **nine coastal states, four UTs, and 15+ central ministries/agencies**. During *Sea Vigil*, covert "Red Teams" (comprising **Marine Commandos [MARCOS]**, **NSG**, and intelligence personnel disguised as fishermen or merchants) attempt to hijack trawlers, smuggle simulated explosives through minor ports, infiltrate coastal nuclear/oil installations, and land at unguarded beaches—testing whether **CSN radars**, **IMAC/NMDA**, **JOCs**, and **State Marine Police** can detect and intercept the threat in real time.
* **Synchronization with TROPEX:** *Sea Vigil* is structurally designed as the coastal precursor to the Indian Navy’s premier biennial **TROPEX (Theatre Level Operational Readiness Exercise)**. While *Sea Vigil* validates the peacetime-to-crisis transition across the littoral constabulary grid, *TROPEX* immediately follows to test the seamless escalation from **coastal defense and anti-terror interdiction** into **full-spectrum high-intensity multi-domain warfighting** across the Indian Ocean, integrating carrier battle groups, nuclear/conventional submarines, the Indian Coast Guard, the Indian Army, and the Indian Air Force.

## IV. Strategic Island Fortifications, SLOC Chokepoints, and Blue-Water Anti-Piracy Operations

### 7. Forward Island Bastions: Andaman & Nicobar Command (ANC) and Lakshadweep (INS Jatayu)

India’s maritime security architecture does not terminate at the 12-nm territorial sea or the 200-nm mainland EEZ; its strategic depth is anchored by two asymmetric island archipelagos—the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** in the east and **Lakshadweep** in the west—which sit directly astride the world’s busiest **Sea Lines of Communication (SLOCs)**. Historically treated as remote administrative outposts where ecological conservation and tribal protection regulations restricted dual-use infrastructure, both island chains have undergone an accelerated militarization and infrastructure transformation between 2019 and 2026 to counter the rapid expansion of the **Chinese People’s Liberation Army Navy (PLAN)**, dual-use Chinese acoustic/hydrographic research vessels (*Yuan Wang* and *Xiang Yang Hong* classes), and shifting geopolitical alignments in the Indian Ocean.

#### The Andaman and Nicobar Command (ANC): Gatekeeper of the Malacca Strait

* **Tri-Service Jointness Anchor:** Spanning a 750-km north-south arc across **836 islands/islets** in the Bay of Bengal, the Andaman and Nicobar archipelago lies over 1,200 km from the Indian mainland but sits a mere **90 miles from Indonesia (Aceh)** and **22 nm from Myanmar’s Coco Islands**. Acting on the recommendations of the **Kargil Review Committee (1999)** and the **Group of Ministers Report (2001)**, the Government of India established the **Andaman and Nicobar Command (ANC)** at Port Blair (renamed **Sri Vijaya Puram** in September 2024) in **October 2001**. ANC remains **India’s first and sole operational geographical tri-service theater command**, placing naval warships, amphibious infantry brigades, IAF fighter/transport squadrons, and Coast Guard vessels under a single rotational three-star **Commander-in-Chief (CINCAN)**.
* **Dominating the Six Degree Channel:** The southernmost island, **Great Nicobar (Indira Point)**, directly overlooks the **Six Degree Channel**—the 160-km-wide international shipping funnel connecting the Indian Ocean to the **Strait of Malacca**. Over **60,000 to 90,000 commercial vessels annually**—carrying roughly a third of global traded goods and **nearly 80% of China’s seaborne crude oil imports** (the strategic *"Malacca Dilemma"*)—must pass within visual and radar range of Great Nicobar before entering the Malacca Strait.
* **Runway Extensions at INS Kohassa and INS Baaz:** To establish unassailable air and anti-submarine warfare (ASW) dominance across the entire island chain, India upgraded its northernmost and southernmost naval air stations:
  * **INS Kohassa (NAS Shibpur, North Andaman):** Commissioned in **January 2019** near Diglipur, directly monitoring the **Preparis Channel** and Myanmar’s Coco Islands. Its runway is being extended from 3,000 feet to **10,000 feet** to enable round-the-clock operations of **Boeing P-8I Poseidon** long-range ASW aircraft, **MQ-9B SeaGuardian** HALE drones, and **Su-30MKI / Rafale** fighter detachments.
  * **INS Baaz (Campbell Bay, Great Nicobar):** Commissioned in **2012** as India's southernmost air station overlooking the **Six Degree Channel**. Alongside upgraded hangarage and fuel storage, its runway is similarly undergoing extension to **10,000 feet** to support heavy strategic airlift (C-17 Globemaster III), maritime strike fighters, and ASW aircraft, supported by a projected subsea optical fiber and SOSUS-style seabed acoustic sensor array connecting the Andaman Sea.
* **The Great Nicobar Island (GNI) / Galathea Bay Project:** To transform Great Nicobar from a lightly defended outpost into a self-sustaining strategic hub, the government approved the ₹72,000+ crore holistic infrastructure project centered on **Galathea Bay** (designated a **"Major Port"** in late 2024). The project integrates four strategic pillars: (1) an **International Container Transshipment Port (ICTP)** with a natural deep draft of **over 20 meters** capable of handling ultra-large container vessels bypassing Colombo and Singapore; (2) a **greenfield dual-use international and military airport** capable of sustaining full fighter squadrons and naval aviation; (3) a **450 MVA gas and solar power plant**; and (4) a forward naval staging and logistics turnaround base.

#### Lakshadweep and the Commissioning of INS Jatayu (March 2024)

* **The Nine Degree Channel Imperative:** Mirrors to the Andaman chain in the east, the **36 islands of Lakshadweep** guard India’s western maritime approaches in the Arabian Sea. Specifically, the **Nine Degree Channel**—a 200-km-wide deep-water passage separating **Minicoy Island** from the main Lakshadweep group (Kalpeni/Suheli Par)—serves as the primary east-west commercial highway for virtually all merchant shipping transiting between **Europe, the Suez Canal, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and East/Southeast Asia**. Just south of Minicoy lies the **Eight Degree Channel** separating India from the Maldives.
* **Commissioning of INS Jatayu at Minicoy (March 2024):** While the Indian Navy had commissioned **INS Dweeprakshak** at the capital island of **Kavaratti** in **2012** to serve as its principal logistics and coastal security base in Lakshadweep, the southernmost island of **Minicoy** operated only a minimal *Naval Detachment* established in the 1980s. In **March 2024**, the Indian Navy commissioned **INS Jatayu** at **Minicoy Island** as India’s second full-fledged naval base in Lakshadweep.
* **Strategic Capabilities of INS Jatayu:** Situated directly on the **Nine Degree Channel** and just 70 nm north of the Maldives (at a time when Maldivian domestic politics temporarily tilted toward Beijing), **INS Jatayu** is being equipped with long-range coastal surveillance radars, electronic intelligence (ELINT) arrays, jetty infrastructure for large warships and submarines, and a **planned greenfield dual-use airfield** at Minicoy capable of operating **P-8I maritime patrol aircraft, fighter jets, and unmanned combat aerial vehicles (UCAVs)**. Together with *INS Dweeprakshak* and the Western Naval Command at Mumbai/Karwar, *INS Jatayu* creates an overlapping anti-submarine and anti-piracy surveillance umbrella across the central and western Arabian Sea.

### 8. High-Seas Jurisdiction and Expeditionary Security: The Maritime Anti-Piracy Act (2022) and Operation Sankalp (2024–2026)

As India’s economic footprint expanded globally, maritime security could no longer be confined to defensive coastal patrolling; it required expeditionary blue-water protection of Indian-flagged merchant vessels, Indian seafarers (who constitute roughly 10–12% of the global merchant marine workforce), and critical energy supply chains across the western Indian Ocean. However, for over three decades after ratifying **UNCLOS (1982)** in 1995, the Indian state suffered from a glaring statutory paradox: while the Indian Navy possessed the kinetic capability to intercept pirates thousands of miles out on the high seas, the Indian legal system lacked a dedicated domestic statute codifying **universal jurisdiction over high-seas piracy**.

When the Indian Navy and Coast Guard captured pirates—such as during the 1999 hijacking of the Japanese vessel *MV Alondra Rainbow* or the 2011 capture of over 100 Somali pirates in the Arabian Sea—prosecutors were forced to charge them under conventional provisions of the **Indian Penal Code (IPC, 1860)** (such as armed robbery, kidnapping, and attempt to murder) read with colonial-era British Admiralty statutes. Because IPC jurisdiction normally required the offense to be committed within India’s **12-nm Territorial Sea**, targeting an Indian citizen, or aboard an Indian-flagged ship, prosecuting foreign pirates who attacked a foreign-flagged vessel on the **high seas (beyond 12 nm and beyond the 200-nm EEZ)** triggered complex jurisdictional challenges and prolonged evidentiary deadlocks.

#### Statutory Architecture of the Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022

Enacted by Parliament in December 2022 and brought into force in **January 2023**, the **Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022** permanently closed this legal vacuum by incorporating the anti-piracy framework of **UNCLOS (Articles 100–107)** directly into Indian municipal law:

* **Definition of Piracy (Section 2(1)(h)):** Mirrors Article 101 of UNCLOS, defining piracy as any illegal act of violence, detention, or depredation committed for private ends by the crew or passengers of a private ship or aircraft on the **high seas** against another ship, aircraft, or persons/property on board, including voluntary participation in the operation of a "pirate ship" (such as a hijacked mother dhow) or inciting/intentionally facilitating such acts.
* **Spatial Jurisdiction Across the EEZ and High Seas (Section 1(2) & Section 2(1)(f)):** For the purposes of this Act, the **"high seas"** is explicitly defined to include the **Exclusive Economic Zone (12 nm to 200 nm)** and **all international waters beyond the EEZ**. This ensures seamless seamless legal coverage from the outer edge of India's 12-nm territorial waters (within 12 nm, domestic criminal law—now the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita [BNS]**—applies directly) across the entire global ocean commons.
* **Punishment and Constitutional Nuance (Section 3):** Whoever commits an act of piracy is punishable with **imprisonment for life**, or with **death or imprisonment for life** if the act of piracy includes or results in death. (By framing the capital provision as *"death or imprisonment for life"*, Parliament ensured the statute complies with the Supreme Court’s landmark constitutional ruling in *Mithu v. State of Punjab* [1983], which struck down mandatory death sentences under Article 21). Attempting, aiding, abetting, or procuring piracy carries up to **10 to 14 years rigorous imprisonment** and a fine.
* **Powers of Arrest, Seizure, and Designated Courts (Sections 5, 8 & 10):** Explicitly empowers **"authorized personnel"** (commanders and crews of warships/military aircraft of the **Indian Navy** and ships/aircraft of the **Indian Coast Guard**) to board, search, seize pirate vessels/property, and arrest suspects on the high seas. To expedite trials, the Central Government, in consultation with the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court, notifies **Sessions Courts as Designated Courts** (such as the **Yellow Gate Police Station / Mumbai Sessions Court** jurisdiction). Crucially, the Designated Court can try offenses committed by any person in the custody of the Indian Navy or Coast Guard **regardless of their nationality** (barring foreign warships/government ships enjoying sovereign immunity).
* **Presumption of Guilt and Extradition (Sections 11 & 14):** Under **Section 11**, if firearms, explosives, or hijack hostages are recovered from the possession of the accused on board the intercepted vessel, the Designated Court **shall presume** that the accused committed the offense unless proved otherwise. Furthermore, all offenses under the Act are deemed **extraditable** under the Extradition Act, 1962, with reciprocal treaty states.

#### Operation Sankalp (2024–2026): First-Responder Net Security Provider in the Red Sea and Arabian Sea

Originally launched in **June 2019** to escort Indian-flagged oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman following mine attacks on commercial shipping, **Operation Sankalp** underwent a dramatic strategic expansion between **December 2023 and 2026** into the **Indian Navy’s largest and longest-sustained expeditionary maritime security operation since independence**.

This surge was triggered by a dual asymmetric crisis in the western Indian Ocean: first, the **Houthi campaign in Yemen**, which unleashed anti-ship ballistic missiles (ASBMs), cruise missiles, and long-range one-way attack drones (such as the strike on the chemical tanker ***MV Chem Pluto*** just 217 nm off Porbandar in December 2023, and the missile strike on ***MV Marlin Luanda*** in the Gulf of Aden in January 2024); and second, an opportunistic **resurgence of Somali piracy**, as Somali syndicates exploited the diversion of Western naval coalitions (*Operation Prosperity Guardian* and *EUNAVFOR Aspides*) into the Red Sea to hijack commercial vessels and Iranian/Yemeni fishing dhows ( using them as long-range "motherships") up to **600 to 1,000 nm east of the Somali coast**, deep toward the Lakshadweep Basin.

* **Force Posture and Scale:** Under the expanded **Operation Sankalp**, the Indian Navy maintained a continuous forward deployment of **10 to 12 frontline guided-missile destroyers and stealth frigates** (including *INS Kolkata, INS Kochi, INS Chennai, INS Visakhapatnam, INS Mormugao, INS Tarkash, INS Trishul, INS Talwar,* and *INS Sumedha*), backed by continuous **P-8I Poseidon** and **MQ-9B SeaGuardian** aerial surveillance, **Dornier** patrols, and embarked **Marine Commandos (MARCOS)** detachments across three critical maritime boxes: the **Gulf of Aden**, the **North and Central Arabian Sea**, and off the **East Coast of Somalia**.
* **The Landmark *MV Ruen* Interception (March 2024):** The definitive operational validation of both the Indian Navy’s blue-water expeditionary capability and the **Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022** occurred in **March 2024**. Somali pirates had hijacked the Maltese-flagged bulk carrier ***MV Ruen*** in December 2023 and were deploying it back out into the Indian Ocean as an armed pirate mothership to hijack larger merchant tankers.
  1. **Detection and Interdiction:** Located **1,400 nm (2,600 km) from the Indian mainland** off the Somali coast by an Indian Navy **P-8I** and **SeaGuardian UAV**, *MV Ruen* was intercepted by the guided-missile destroyer ***INS Kolkata*** (assisted by patrol vessel *INS Subhadra*). When pirates on board *MV Ruen* opened heavy gun-fire and shot down a ship-launched spotter drone, *INS Kolkata* executed calibrated kinetic fire to disable the bulk carrier’s steering gear and navigational bridge.
  2. **Long-Range Airborne MARCOS Insertion:** In a historic joint-service operation, an **Indian Air Force C-17 Globemaster III** flew 10 hours non-stop from India to execute a precision mid-ocean para-drop of two **Combat Rubberised Raiding Crafts (CRRCs)** and an additional assault team of **MARCOS** directly into the sea alongside *INS Kolkata*.
  3. **Surrender and Statutory Prosecution:** After a **40-hour siege**, the **35 Somali pirates** surrendered unconditionally, and all **17 hostage crew members** (from Bulgaria, Angola, and Myanmar) were rescued unharmed. Crucially, instead of releasing the pirates at sea ("catch and release"), *INS Kolkata* transported all **35 Somali pirates** to Mumbai and handed them over to the **Mumbai Police (Yellow Gate Police Station)** for formal prosecution before the Designated Court under the **Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022**. Weeks later, in late March 2024, *INS Sumedha* and *INS Trishul* intercepted the hijacked Iranian fishing vessel ***FV Al-Kambar***, rescuing 23 Pakistani crew members and capturing **9 additional Somali pirates**, cementing India’s status as the premier **First Responder and Preferred Security Partner** in the Indian Ocean Region.

### 9. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Littoral Interdiction to Transnational Narco-Financial Pipelines

While the **Three-Tier Coastal Security Architecture**, the **CSN–IMAC–NMDA** surveillance grid, forward island bases like **INS Jatayu**, and **Operation Sankalp** hardened India’s maritime borders against kinetic commando raids and high-seas piracy, this very hardening forced external adversaries and criminal syndicates to adapt their maritime modus operandi. Rather than sending armed *fidayeen* teams directly onto urban beaches as in 26/11, Pakistan’s **ISI**, transnational organized crime syndicates (**D-Company** and **Khalistani-gangster networks**), and Golden Crescent/Golden Triangle cartels now utilize deep-sea mother dhows departing the **Makran coast (Gwadar/Pasni)** and the **Myanmar littoral** to execute mid-ocean drops of multi-thousand-kilogram consignments of high-purity heroin and synthetic methamphetamine (*Yaba* and *Ice*).

Countered at sea through joint **Indian Navy–Coast Guard–Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB)** operations such as **Operation Samudragupta** (which seized over 40,000+ kg of narcotics across the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal between 2022 and 2026, including a single record 3,300-kg seizure off the Gujarat coast in February 2024), these maritime contraband shipments represent only the physical front end of a vastly more complex threat matrix. Once narcotics, counterfeit currency, or smuggling proceeds make landfall or clear customs through **Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML)**, they immediately enter an invisible financial ecosystem of **Hawala networks, shell companies, mule bank accounts, and Virtual Digital Asset (VDA / cryptocurrency) mixers**. Deconstructing how these maritime and terrestrial smuggling corridors converge with terrorist financing—and how the Indian state combats them through the **NDPS Act**, **NCORD**, the **Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND)**, the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA, 2002)** jurisprudence, and the **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** regime—forms the core focus of **Part 7**.
