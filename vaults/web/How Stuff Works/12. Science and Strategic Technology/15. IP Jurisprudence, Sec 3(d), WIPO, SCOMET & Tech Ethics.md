## I. Statutory Taxonomy of Indian Intellectual Property Rights and the Historical Trajectory of the Patents Act, 1970

### 1. The Eight-Pillar Indian IPR Statutory Architecture and Institutional Governance

#### First-Principles Economics of Non-Rivalrous Knowledge Goods vs. Statutory Monopoly Rents
In neoclassical microeconomics and information theory, scientific discoveries, molecular formulas, source code, and semiconductor mask topologies are **non-rivalrous** and **non-excludable** public goods. Once a novel pharmaceutical molecule is synthesized and clinically validated across Phases I–III—requiring an average capital outlay exceeding $1.5 to $2.5 billion and a decade of high-attrition screening—the marginal cost of reverse-engineering its chemical structure via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and mass spectrometry is negligible. In an unregulated market without statutory exclusion, competitive imitators would immediately copy the molecule at marginal manufacturing cost ($MC$), driving market prices down to $P = MC$ and preventing the original innovator from recovering fixed R&D sunk costs. Conversely, granting an unrestricted, perpetual statutory monopoly allows the innovator to restrict output and price at the profit-maximizing monopoly equilibrium ($MR = MC$), generating severe **deadweight welfare losses**, pricing life-saving therapeutics beyond the reach of low-income populations, and blocking sequential follow-on innovation.

Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) jurisprudence is therefore an institutional optimization mechanism designed to balance two competing structural forces: **dynamic efficiency** (incentivizing high-risk private R&D investment across the Technology Readiness Level "Valley of Death" through temporary, state-enforced monopoly rents) versus **static allocative efficiency** (maximizing immediate public access, technology diffusion, and competitive pricing). In exchange for a time-bound right to exclude competitors (**jus excludendi alios**), the state extracts a mandatory **quid pro quo** from the inventor: full, enabling public disclosure of the technical architecture of the invention so that upon expiry of the statutory term, the technology enters the public domain permanently.

#### Granular Deconstruction of the Eight IPR Regimes
Following the administrative reorganization of India's intellectual property governance and the abolition of the Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB) via the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** (which transferred appellate and revocation jurisdiction directly to specialized Intellectual Property Divisions [IPDs] in the High Courts of Delhi, Madras, and Calcutta), seven of India's eight core IPR regimes are centrally administered or coordinated through the **Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT)** under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, executed by the **Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM)**:

1. **Patents (**Patents Act, 1970**, amended in 1999, 2002, and 2005)**: Grants an exclusive statutory right for a uniform term of **20 years from the date of filing** (or priority date under the Patent Cooperation Treaty) for an invention—either a product or a process—that satisfies the strict tripartite threshold under **Section 2(1)(j)** and **Section 2(1)(ja)**:
   * **Novelty (Absolute Global Standard)**: The invention must not have been anticipated by publication anywhere in the world or prior public knowledge/use in India before the priority date.
   * **Inventive Step (Non-Obviousness)**: Defined under **Section 2(1)(ja)** as a feature of an invention that involves **technical advance** as compared to the existing knowledge or having **economic significance** or both, and that makes the invention not obvious to a **Person Skilled in the Art (PSITA)**.
   * **Industrial Applicability (Utility)**: Defined under **Section 2(1)(ac)** as the capacity of the invention to be made or used in an industry.
2. **Trademarks (**Trade Marks Act, 1999**, read with the Trade Marks Rules, 2017)**: Protects distinctive visual or sensory signs capable of graphical representation that distinguish the goods or services of one enterprise from those of others, preventing consumer confusion and commercial passing-off. It encompasses word marks, device logos, three-dimensional packaging shapes, color combinations, and **non-traditional sound marks** (represented via musical notation). Registered for an initial term of **10 years**, it is **endlessly renewable** in perpetuity for successive 10-year blocks, provided the mark remains in bona fide commercial use and does not degenerate into a generic term.
3. **Copyrights (**Copyright Act, 1957**, amended in 2012)**: Protects the original **expression of an idea** (never the underlying idea, mathematical concept, or physical fact itself—the **Idea-Expression Dichotomy**) across literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, as well as cinematograph films and sound recordings. Crucially, under **Section 2(o)**, **computer programmes** (both human-readable **source code** and machine-executable **object code**) and compilations of data are statutorily classified and protected as **literary works**. For literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, the term is the **lifetime of the author plus 60 years** from the calendar year following death; for cinematograph films, sound recordings, photographs, and posthumous/corporate works, it is a flat **60 years** from the year of publication.
4. **Industrial Designs (**Designs Act, 2000**)**: Protects exclusively the new or original **aesthetic features** of shape, configuration, surface pattern, ornament, or composition of lines or colors applied to any two-dimensional or three-dimensional article by an industrial process, judged solely by the eye. It explicitly excludes any mode or principle of construction or anything which is in substance a **mere mechanical device** (which must seek patent protection) or artistic works covered under copyright (**Section 15(2) of the Copyright Act** terminates copyright in an unregistered industrial design once the article is reproduced more than **50 times** by an industrial process). The term of protection is **10 years**, extendable once for an additional **5 years** (maximum **15 years**).
5. **Geographical Indications (**Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999**)**: Confers a **collective community right** (incapable of individual private assignment, transmission, or licensing to non-producers outside the demarcated geographical territory) over agricultural, natural, or manufactured/handicraft goods whose definite quality, reputation, or other characteristic is essentially attributable to their geographical origin (**terroir** or localized traditional craftsmanship). Registered via the GI Registry in Chennai for an initial **10-year term**, a GI registration is **endlessly renewable** every 10 years. India has crossed **600+ registered Geographical Indications** (spanning **Darjeeling Tea** [India's first GI in 2004], **Basmati Rice**, **Kanchipuram Silk**, **Kashmir Pashmina**, **Alphonso Mango**, and regional tribal handicrafts).
6. **Plant Varieties (**Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights [PPV&FR] Act, 2001**)**: Enacted to fulfill India's obligation under **Article 27.3(b) of the WTO TRIPS Agreement**—which requires members to protect plant varieties either by patents, by an effective **sui generis** system, or by a combination thereof—this statute is uniquely administered by the **Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare** through the **PPV&FR Authority**. Unlike the Western **UPOV (International Union for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants) 1991 Convention** (which restricts farmers from saving or exchanging protected seeds), India's sui generis statute balances formal plant breeders' rights (granted on the **DUS criteria**: **Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability**, plus Novelty) with statutory **Farmers' Rights** under **Section 39(1)(iv)**. An Indian farmer is statutorily entitled to **save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share, or sell** his farm produce, including seed of a variety protected under the Act, in the same manner as he was entitled before the Act, with the sole proviso that the farmer cannot sell **branded seed** packaged under the breeder's registered denomination. The protection term is **18 years** for trees and vines, and **15 years** for extant varieties and other field crops.
7. **Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design (**Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Design [SICLD] Act, 2000**)**: Protects the three-dimensional disposition of transistors, interconnects, and active/passive elements (**mask works** or photolithography layout topologies) prepared for manufacturing a semiconductor integrated circuit. The layout-design must be original, not commercially exploited for more than 2 years prior to registration, and inherently distinctive. The term of protection is **10 years** from the date of filing or from the date of first commercial exploitation anywhere in the world, whichever is earlier.
8. **Trade Secrets (Common Law Equity & The 2024 Draft Protection of Trade Secrets Bill)**: Unlike the seven statutory registration regimes above, trade secrets—encompassing confidential chemical process parameters, bioreactor cell-line fermentation temperatures, mRNA lipid-nanoparticle mixing ratios, proprietary commercial datasets, and unpatented AI model weights—do not require state registration or public disclosure. Historically protected in India only through fragmented common-law principles of **breach of confidence**, **Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872** (non-disclosure agreements), and **Section 405/408 of the IPC / Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (criminal breach of trust)**, this vacuum left Indian deep-tech firms without dedicated civil injunctive remedies against corporate espionage. Addressing this structural deficit, the Law Commission of India (Report No. 289 of the 22nd Law Commission, carried forward into the **23rd Law Commission** tenure in **2024**) released the **Draft Protection of Trade Secrets Bill, 2024**. The Bill codifies a three-part statutory test aligned with **TRIPS Article 39(2)** (information that is secret, has commercial value because of its secrecy, and has been subject to reasonable steps to keep it secret), while embedding critical public-interest exceptions: **independent discovery**, **reverse engineering** of lawfully acquired products, **whistleblower immunity** for exposing illegal acts, and a pioneering **Compulsory Licensing / Government Use provision** empowering the central government to mandate the disclosure and licensing of trade secrets (such as vaccine manufacturing know-how) during a national emergency or extreme public health crisis.

### 2. Evolution of Indian Patent Jurisprudence: From Colonial Monopoly to the "Pharmacy of the Global South" and TRIPS Compliance

#### The Justice Ayyangar Committee Report (1959) and the Process-Patent Revolution (1970–2005)
At independence in 1947, India inherited the colonial **Indian Patents and Designs Act, 1911**, which granted broad product patents without requiring local manufacturing in India. Multinational pharmaceutical corporations held over 85% to 90% of all active Indian patents, used those patents purely as **import monopolies** rather than establishing domestic chemical synthesis plants, and blocked local firms from producing essential antibiotics, antimalarials, and sulfa drugs. Consequently, a 1961 US Senate Subcommittee investigation chaired by **Senator Estes Kefauver** documented that drug prices in India were among the highest in the world, directly inversely correlated with India's per-capita income.

To dismantle this structural dependency, the Government of India appointed the **Patents Enquiry Committee (1948–1950)** under **Dr. Bakshi Tek Chand**, followed by the landmark **Justice N. Rajagopala Ayyangar Committee Report on the Revision of the Patent Law (September 1959)**. Justice Ayyangar conducted an empirical juridical and economic audit of the global patent system, concluding that patent regimes are not natural moral rights, but deliberate instruments of national industrial policy whose optimal calibration depends strictly on a nation's stage of technological and industrial development. He demonstrated that foreign patentees were exploiting product patents to block domestic chemical engineering while starving the Indian public of affordable medicines and food agrochemicals.

Directly implementing the **Justice Ayyangar Committee Report**, Parliament enacted the **Patents Act, 1970** (brought into force on **April 20, 1972**), which executed three transformative structural reforms that birthed the modern Indian generic pharmaceutical industry:

* **Abolition of Product Patents in Food, Pharmaceuticals, and Agrochemicals (Former Section 5)**: Under the original **Section 5 of the Patents Act, 1970**, for any substance intended for use, or capable of being used, as **food, medicine, or drug**, or for any substance prepared or produced by **chemical processes** (including alloys, optical glass, semiconductors, and intermetallic compounds), **no patent could be granted in respect of claims for the substances themselves**. Patents were strictly restricted to the specific **methods or processes of manufacture**.
* **Drastic Truncation of Pharmaceutical Process Patent Terms (Former Section 53)**: While general mechanical and engineering inventions received a 14-year patent term under the 1970 Act, process patents relating to food, medicines, or drugs were curtailed to just **5 years from the date of sealing of the patent, or 7 years from the date of filing of the complete specification**, whichever period was shorter.
* **Chemical Reverse-Engineering and the "Pharmacy of the Global South"**: Because organic molecules can almost always be synthesized through multiple alternative reaction pathways (e.g., substituting a palladium-catalyzed Suzuki coupling with an alternative Grignard reagent or Friedel-Crafts acylation sequence), public-sector **CSIR laboratories** (such as **CSIR-CDRI Lucknow** and **CSIR-IICT Hyderabad**) partnered with emerging domestic firms (Cipla, Ranbaxy, Dr. Reddy's, Lupin, Sun Pharma) to reverse-engineer patented Western drugs via novel, non-infringing chemical processes within months of their global launch. Between 1970 and 2004, the foreign share of the Indian pharmaceutical market plummeted from >80% to <25%, while India emerged as the **"Pharmacy of the Global South"**—supplying over **20% of global generic medicines by volume**, **60% of global vaccines**, and **>80% of the antiretroviral (ARV) drugs** used to treat HIV/AIDS across sub-Saharan Africa and developing nations (most notably when Cipla broke the global antiretroviral cartel in **2001** by offering the triple-drug cocktail of **Stavudine + Lamivudine + Nevirapine** at **$350 per patient per year** against the Western originator price of **$10,000–$15,000 per year**).

#### The 1995 WTO TRIPS Agreement and the 2005 Product-Patent Transition
The geopolitical equilibrium shifted dramatically during the **Uruguay Round of GATT (1986–1994)**, where advanced economies led by the United States, the European Union, and Japan successfully linked international trade market access to uniform global intellectual property enforcement, culminating in the **Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS)** upon the establishment of the **World Trade Organization (WTO)** on **January 1, 1995**:

* **The TRIPS Article 27.1 Non-Discrimination Mandate**: **Article 27.1 of TRIPS** mandated that patents shall be available for **any inventions, whether products or processes, in all fields of technology**, without discrimination as to the place of invention, the field of technology, and whether products are imported or locally produced, for a minimum uniform term of **20 years from the filing date** (**Article 33**).
* **Three-Stage Statutory Transition (1995–2005)**: As a developing country that did not grant pharmaceutical or agrochemical product patents on January 1, 1995, India availed itself of the maximum **10-year transition period** under **Articles 65.2 and 65.4 of TRIPS** (until **January 1, 2005**), executed across three statutory amendments:
  1. **Patents (Amendment) Act, 1999** (retrospectively effective from January 1, 1995, following the WTO Dispute Settlement Body ruling against India in *India – Patent Protection for Pharmaceutical and Agricultural Chemical Products*): Established the **"Mailbox" Facility** under **Article 70.8 of TRIPS** (allowing applicants to file pharmaceutical/agrochemical product patent applications to establish a priority date, which remained sealed until 2005) and granted **Exclusive Marketing Rights (EMRs)** for up to 5 years under **Article 70.9**.
  2. **Patents (Amendment) Act, 2002**: Extended the uniform term of all Indian patents to **20 years from the date of filing**, updated the definition of "invention" to include **inventive step**, and overhauled the compulsory licensing chapter.
  3. **Patents (Amendment) Act, 2005** (effective January 1, 2005): Deleted **Section 5** entirely, thereby re-introducing **Product Patents for pharmaceuticals, food, and agrochemicals** after a 33-year hiatus. However, to prevent the return of the pre-1970 import monopoly crisis, Parliament consciously utilized the sovereign regulatory space affirmed by the **November 2001 WTO Doha Declaration on the TRIPS Agreement and Public Health**—which explicitly declared that the TRIPS Agreement *"can and should be interpreted and implemented in a manner supportive of WTO Members' right to protect public health and, in particular, to promote access to medicines for all"*—to embed the world's most rigorous statutory anti-evergreening and compulsory licensing safeguards directly into the 2005 Act.

## II. Public-Health TRIPS Flexibilities, Solid-State Polymorphism in Section 3(d), and Compulsory Licensing Mechanics

### 3. Subject-Matter Exclusions: Section 3(d) Anti-Evergreening Jurisprudence, Section 3(k) Algorithms, and Section 3(j) Biological Exclusions

#### Physicochemical Polymorphism vs. Therapeutic Efficacy: *Novartis AG v. Union of India* (2013) and the Imatinib Mesylate ($\beta$-Crystalline) Dispute
In pharmaceutical R&D, once an originator corporation discovers a **New Chemical Entity (NCE)**—the primary pharmacophore that binds to a biological target receptor—it seeks to extend its 20-year monopoly far beyond expiry without discovering a new drug. This commercial strategy, termed **Evergreening**, relies on filing sequential secondary patents on minor solid-state or chemical modifications of the known NCE: converting a free base into an acid-addition **salt**, synthesizing **esters** or **ethers**, isolating specific solid-state crystalline **polymorphs** (different three-dimensional crystal packing arrangements of the exact same molecule), creating **isomers** or **metabolites**, altering **particle size** (micronization), or combining two known drugs into a fixed-dose combination. While these modifications routinely alter **physicochemical properties**—such as thermodynamic stability, hygroscopicity (moisture absorption), tableting flowability, aqueous solubility, and **bioavailability** (the fraction of an administered dose that reaches systemic blood circulation unchanged)—they rely on the exact same active molecular moiety at the cellular target site.

To erect an impenetrable statutory firewall against pharmaceutical evergreening while remaining compliant with TRIPS Article 27, the **Patents (Amendment) Act, 2005** fundamentally reconstructed **Section 3(d) of the Patents Act, 1970**. Under **Section 3(d)**, the following are statutorily declared **"not inventions"** within the meaning of the Act:
> *"The mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy of that substance or the mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance or of the mere use of a known process, machine or apparatus unless such known process results in a new product or employs at least one new reactant."*
> **Explanation to Section 3(d)**: *"For the purposes of this clause, salts, esters, ethers, polymorphs, metabolites, pure form, particle size, isomers, mixtures of isomers, complexes, combinations and other derivatives of known substance shall be considered to be the same substance, unless they differ significantly in properties with regard to efficacy."*

This statutory architecture was subjected to its definitive constitutional and scientific test in the landmark Supreme Court judgment of ***Novartis AG v. Union of India*** **(April 1, 2013)**:

* **The Biochemical Chain of Synthesis**: In 1992–1993, Jürg Zimmermann invented **Imatinib**, a revolutionary small-molecule **tyrosine kinase inhibitor** that selectively binds to the ATP-binding site of the abnormal **BCR-ABL fusion protein** generated by the **Philadelphia chromosome** translocation $t(9;22)$ in **Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML)**. Zimmermann's **1993 US Patent (US 5,521,184)** explicitly disclosed both the **Imatinib free base** and its pharmaceutically acceptable acid-addition salts, including **Imatinib Mesylate**, and pre-dated India's 1995 TRIPS mailbox window (making Imatinib a "known substance" in the public domain in India). In **1998**, Novartis filed an Indian mailbox application claiming a specific solid-state crystalline polymorph: the **$\beta$-crystalline form of Imatinib Mesylate** (marketed globally as **Glivec / Gleevec** at ₹1,20,000 per patient per month, whereas Indian generic manufacturers were supplying it at ₹8,000–₹10,000 per month).
* **The Competing Scientific Arguments**: Novartis argued that converting the Imatinib free base into the $\beta$-crystalline polymorph of Imatinib Mesylate yielded a **30% increase in oral bioavailability** alongside superior thermodynamic stability, reduced hygroscopicity, and improved flow properties for tablet compression, which Novartis contended satisfied the "enhanced efficacy" threshold of **Section 3(d)**.
* **The Supreme Court's Holding on "Therapeutic Efficacy"**: A two-judge bench led by **Justice Aftab Alam** rejected Novartis's appeal on both **Section 2(1)(ja)** (lack of inventive step over the 1993 Zimmermann patent and 1996 *Cancer Research* publications) and **Section 3(d)**. The Court laid down three foundational legal principles:
  1. **Strict Definition of Efficacy**: Because the sole function of a drug or medicine is to cure disease or alleviate pathology in the human body, the word **"efficacy"** in **Section 3(d)** in the context of pharmaceuticals means **strictly and exclusively "therapeutic efficacy"**—the clinical functional capacity of the drug to produce a superior healing or pharmacodynamic effect at the physiological target.
  2. **Physicochemical Properties vs. Therapeutic Efficacy**: Improved solid-state properties such as thermodynamic stability, reduced moisture absorption, and better flowability merely facilitate industrial storage and tablet formulation; they have zero bearing on the drug's therapeutic efficacy once dissolved in the bloodstream.
  3. **Bioavailability is Not Synonymous with Therapeutic Efficacy**: While **bioavailability** measures the pharmacokinetic rate and extent to which the active moiety enters systemic circulation, a 30% increase in bioavailability does not automatically translate into enhanced **therapeutic efficacy** (for example, higher blood absorption might simply require a slightly lower milligram dose or, worse, trigger heightened dose-dependent toxicity without improving clinical remission rates). For increased bioavailability to qualify under **Section 3(d)**, the applicant must demonstrate through **rigorous empirical clinical data** that the polymorphic modification directly produces a **significant enhancement in clinical therapeutic outcome** over the known substance.

#### Algorithmic & Software Exclusions under Section 3(k): "Computer Programme Per Se" vs. Technical Effect
In the computational domain, the Indian legislature recognized that allowing broad software and algorithmic patents—as permitted in the United States following *State Street Bank* and partially constrained by *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank*—creates dense **"patent thickets"** over foundational mathematical logic, sorting routines, and neural-network architectures, stifling India's software services and startup ecosystem. Consequently, **Section 3(k) of the Patents Act, 1970** statutorily excludes from patentability:
> *"A mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms."*

The critical statutory qualifier inserted by the Joint Parliamentary Committee during the **2002 Amendment** is the Latin phrase **"per se"** ("by itself" or "standing alone"), which applies solely to **"a computer programme per se"** while mathematical methods, business methods, and pure algorithms remain **absolutely excluded** under all circumstances:

* **Pure Algorithms and Mathematical Methods (Absolute Bar)**: Abstract mathematical formulas, backpropagation equations, loss-function optimization routines, cryptographic number-theoretic equations, and commercial business workflows (e.g., an automated e-commerce dynamic pricing method) are unpatentable regardless of how novel or commercially lucrative they are.
* **Computer Programme *Per Se* vs. Technical Effect (Conditional Eligibility)**: While standalone source code is protected under **Section 2(o) of the Copyright Act, 1957**, a software-implemented invention is **not** barred by **Section 3(k)** if it transcends being a mere "computer programme per se." As crystallized by the Delhi High Court in ***Ferid Allani v. Union of India*** **(2019)**, ***Microsoft Technology Licensing v. Assistant Controller of Patents*** **(2023)**, and ***Lava International v. Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson*** **(2024)**, if a computer program, when executed on hardware, demonstrates a **"technical effect"** or provides a **"technical contribution"** that solves a real-world engineering problem—such as optimizing memory management in a processor cache, compressing radio-frequency bandwidth in a 5G MIMO antenna array, or controlling fuel-injection timing in an automotive Electronic Control Unit (ECU)—it is patent-eligible under Indian law.

#### Biological and Agricultural Exclusions under Section 3(j): Microorganisms vs. Higher Life Forms and Essentially Biological Processes
Aligned with **Article 27.3(b) of the TRIPS Agreement**, **Section 3(j) of the Patents Act, 1970** establishes a strict boundary between patentable industrial biotechnology and unpatentable biological nature:
> *"Plants and animals in whole or any part thereof other than micro-organisms but including seeds, varieties and species and essentially biological processes for production or propagation of plants and animals."*

Furthermore, this is reinforced by **Section 3(c)** (which excludes the discovery of any living thing or non-living substance occurring in nature, such as simply isolating a naturally occurring human gene sequence or wild bacterial strain without structural modification), **Section 3(b)** (which excludes inventions whose primary or intended use or commercial exploitation is contrary to *ordre public* or morality or causes serious prejudice to human, animal, or plant life or health or to the environment—invoked to scrutinize germline editing and terminator seed technology), **Section 3(h)** (excluding methods of agriculture or horticulture), and **Section 3(i)** (excluding any process for the medicinal, surgical, curative, prophylactic, diagnostic, therapeutic, or other treatment of human beings or animals to render them free of disease or to increase their economic value). Unpacking the molecular mechanics of **Section 3(j)** reveals three operational rules:

* **Exclusion of Naturally Occurring Microorganisms vs. Patentability of Genetically Modified Microorganisms**: Following the 2002 Amendment and the Calcutta High Court ruling in ***Dimminaco A.G. v. Controller of Patents*** **(2002)**, **microorganisms** are an explicit statutory exception to the **Section 3(j)** exclusion. However, when read harmoniously with **Section 3(c)**, a **naturally occurring microorganism** merely isolated from soil or marine sediment cannot be patented; only **genetically modified or human-engineered microorganisms** (e.g., a recombinant *Escherichia coli* or *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* strain engineered with synthetic plasmid vectors to express human insulin or degrade petroleum hydrocarbons, originally pioneered globally in *Diamond v. Chakrabarty*, 1980) are eligible for product patents in India.
* **Absolute Bar on Plants, Seeds, and Transgenic Plant Varieties**: Even if a biotechnology company uses **CRISPR-Cas9** or *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*-mediated transformation to insert a synthetic insecticidal *Bacillus thuringiensis* (**Bt**) *cry1Ac* gene into a cotton or brinjal genome, once the recombinant DNA construct is integrated into a **plant cell, seed, or whole plant**, the resulting transgenic seed or plant variety is **categorically barred from patentability under Section 3(j)** (as adjudicated in the landmark ***Monsanto Technology LLC v. Nuziveedu Seeds Ltd.*** litigation regarding Bollgard-II Bt Cotton). While the laboratory-synthesized **recombinant DNA construct, chimeric gene sequence, or biotechnological transformation process** may be patented as a biochemical product/process prior to integration into the plant, any protection over the resulting **seed or plant variety** must be sought exclusively under the **PPV&FR Act, 2001** (where it becomes subject to the **Section 39** Farmers' Right to save and exchange seeds).
* **Exclusion of Essentially Biological Processes**: Cross-breeding, selective hybridization, back-crossing, and Mendelian selection of plants or animals are excluded as **"essentially biological processes,"** ensuring that traditional agricultural breeding remains entirely outside the patent monopoly system.

### 4. Statutory Remedies Against Monopoly Abuse: Compulsory Licensing (Sections 84 & 92), the Section 107A Bolar Exemption, and the 2024 Patent Rules Amendments

#### Compulsory Licensing Mechanics under Section 84: *Natco Pharma v. Bayer Corporation* (2012) and the Section 92 Sovereign Emergency Override
Even when a pharmaceutical or strategic technology legitimately clears the **Section 3(d)** threshold and obtains a 20-year product patent, the **Patents Act, 1970** rejects the notion that a patent confers an absolute right to starve the domestic market or charge extortionate prices. Under **Section 83** (General Principles Applicable to Working of Patented Inventions), Parliament explicitly codified that patents are granted **to encourage inventions and to secure that the inventions are worked in India on a commercial scale**, that they are **not granted merely to enable patentees to enjoy a monopoly for the importation of the patented article**, and that they must **not impede protection of public health** or nutrition, declaring that the patented invention must be made available at **reasonably affordable prices to the public**.

To enforce these principles in accordance with **Article 31 of the TRIPS Agreement** and **Paragraph 5(b) of the 2001 Doha Declaration**, **Chapter XVI of the Patents Act** establishes a multi-tiered **Compulsory Licensing (CL)** architecture—an involuntary statutory license granted by the state to a third-party domestic manufacturer to produce and market a patented product without the consent of the patent holder, in exchange for a state-determined reasonable royalty:

1. **Section 84 (General Compulsory License upon Application after 3 Years)**: At any time after the expiration of **three years from the date of the grant of a patent**, any person interested may make an application to the **Controller of Patents** for the grant of a compulsory license on any one or more of **three independent statutory grounds** under **Section 84(1)** (provided the applicant has first made efforts to obtain a voluntary license from the patentee on reasonable terms and conditions within a statutory period of **6 months** under **Section 84(6)(iv)**):
   * **Ground (a) — Unmet Public Demand**: That the **reasonable requirements of the public** with respect to the patented invention have **not been satisfied**; or
   * **Ground (b) — Unaffordable Pricing**: That the patented invention is **not available to the public at a reasonably affordable price**; or
   * **Ground (c) — Non-Working in India**: That the patented invention is **not worked in the territory of India** on a commercial scale.
2. **Landmark Invocation — *Natco Pharma Ltd. v. Bayer Corporation* (March 9, 2012)**: India issued its first—and benchmark—post-TRIPS Compulsory License under **Section 84** for **Sorafenib Tosylate** (marketed by German pharmaceutical giant Bayer as **Nexavar**), a life-extending multi-kinase inhibitor targeting RAF/MEK/ERK signaling and VEGFR/PDGFR angiogenesis in advanced **Renal Cell Carcinoma (kidney cancer)** and **Hepatocellular Carcinoma (liver cancer)**:
   * **Satisfaction of All Three Section 84(1) Prongs**: The Controller of Patents (affirmed by the IPAB in 2013 and the Bombay High Court / Supreme Court in 2014) found that Bayer failed all three statutory criteria simultaneously:
     * Under **Section 84(1)(a)**, while over **8,842 liver and kidney cancer patients** in India required the drug annually, Bayer's imports supplied **less than 2% (~200 patients)** of the needy patient population.
     * Under **Section 84(1)(b)**, Bayer priced a one-month course of 120 tablets of Nexavar at **₹2,80,428 per month** (~₹33.6 lakh per year), far exceeding the annual income of >99% of Indian households.
     * Under **Section 84(1)(c)**, despite holding the Indian patent since 2008 and possessing manufacturing facilities in India, Bayer manufactured **zero units locally**, relying entirely on import shipments.
   * **The Compulsory License Terms**: The Controller granted a non-exclusive, non-assignable compulsory license to Hyderabad-based generic manufacturer **Natco Pharma** to manufacture Sorafenib Tosylate in India for the domestic market at a capped price of **₹8,800 per month** (a **97% price reduction**), mandating that Natco supply the drug completely free of cost to at least 600 indigent patients annually and pay Bayer a **6% royalty** (later revised to **7% by the IPAB**) on net sales.
3. **Section 92 (Sovereign Suo Motu Notification for National Emergency or Public Non-Commercial Use)**: Unlike **Section 84**, which requires a 3-year waiting period after grant and a 6-month prior voluntary negotiation attempt, **Section 92** empowers the Central Government—at **any time after the sealing of a patent**—to issue an official Gazette notification declaring that compulsory licenses should be granted in circumstances of **(i) national emergency**, **(ii) extreme urgency**, or **(iii) public non-commercial use** (including public health crises relating to **AIDS, HIV, tuberculosis, malaria, or other epidemics** under **Section 92(3)**). Once notified by the Central Government, the Controller grants licenses immediately to capable domestic manufacturers **without any prior negotiation with the patentee**.
4. **Section 92A (Compulsory License for Export of Patented Pharmaceuticals to Nations with Insufficient Manufacturing Capacity)**: Enacted in 2005 to operationalize the **Paragraph 6 Decision of the Doha Declaration (now Article 31bis of the TRIPS Agreement)**, **Section 92A** allows the Controller to grant a compulsory license solely for the **manufacture and export of patented pharmaceutical products** to any Least Developed Country (LDC) or developing country that has insufficient or no domestic pharmaceutical manufacturing capacity to address a public health problem.
5. **Section 100 & Section 66 (Government Use & Public-Interest Revocation)**: **Section 100** empowers the Central Government to authorize any company or agency to use a patented invention **"for the purposes of Government"** (invoked discussions during the 2021 COVID-19 Delta wave regarding *Tocilizumab*, *Remdesivir*, and *Baricitinib*), while **Section 66** empowers the Central Government to **revoke a patent completely** if it is of the opinion that the patent or the mode in which it is exercised is *"mischievous to the State or generally prejudicial to the public."*

#### The Section 107A Bolar Exemption, Section 25 Dual-Tier Opposition, and the Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024 (Form-27 Dilution)
Beyond compulsory licensing, the Indian patent regime incorporates three procedural mechanisms that govern the timing of generic competition and patent validity policing—two of which remain global models of public-health safeguarding, while the third underwent controversial deregulation in March 2024:

* **Section 107A(a) — The Statutory "Bolar Exemption" (Regulatory Review Exception)**: In the absence of a statutory safe harbor, a generic manufacturer would have to wait until the exact day a 20-year product patent expires before synthesizing a single gram of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) to conduct stability testing, human bioequivalence trials, and regulatory dossier submissions to the **Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO)** or the **US FDA**—effectively granting the originator an automatic **2-to-3-year de facto patent term extension** after the 20-year expiry while generic regulatory approvals are processed. To eliminate this post-expiry monopoly delay, **Section 107A(a) of the Patents Act, 1970** codifies India's expansive **Bolar Exemption** (named after the US legislative reversal of *Roche Products v. Bolar Pharmaceutical*, 1984):
  > *"Any act of making, constructing, using, selling or importing a patented invention solely for uses reasonably related to the development and submission of information required under any law for the time being in force, in India, or in a country other than India, that regulates the manufacture, construction, use, sale or import of any product shall not be considered as an infringement of patent rights."*
  In the landmark judgment of ***Bayer Corporation v. Union of India & Natco Pharma*** **(Delhi High Court Full Bench, 2019)**, the Court held that India's **Section 107A(a)** is broader than its North American counterparts: it explicitly permits Indian generic firms not only to manufacture a patented API for domestic CDSCO trials, but also to **sell and export commercial research quantities of the patented API to foreign generic firms** during the life of the Indian patent, provided the export is reasonably related to generating regulatory approval data in that foreign jurisdiction. Simultaneously, **Section 107A(b)** legalizes the **Parallel Importation** of patented products into India based on the **Doctrine of International Exhaustion of Patent Rights** (allowing an importer to buy a patented drug from any country where it is sold more cheaply by a person duly authorized under the law of that country and import it into India without the patentee's permission).
* **Section 25 — Dual-Tier Pre-Grant and Post-Grant Opposition**: India is one of the very few major patent jurisdictions globally to maintain a bifurcated administrative opposition filter under **Section 25**:
  1. **Pre-Grant Opposition (**Section 25(1)**)**: At any time after a patent application is published (typically at 18 months) but **before a patent is granted**, **"any person"**—a deliberately expansive locus standi that allows cancer patient advocacy networks, public-health NGOs, academic scientists, and generic competitors—can file a representation opposing the grant without paying exorbitant court fees, invoking any of the 11 exhaustive grounds in **Section 25(1)(a)–(k)** (including anticipation, lack of inventive step, **Section 3(d)** evergreening, failure to disclose the source or geographical origin of biological material used in the invention, or anticipation by **Traditional Knowledge**).
  2. **Post-Grant Opposition (**Section 25(2)**)**: Within **one year** from the date of publication of the grant of a patent, any **"person interested"** (defined under **Section 2(1)(t)** as a person engaged in, or in promoting, research in the same field to which the invention relates) may give notice of opposition, triggering an inquiry by a three-member **Opposition Board** appointed by the Controller.
* **The Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024 and the Form-27 Working-Statement Controversies**: On **March 15, 2024**, DPIIT notified the **Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024**, which introduced significant administrative streamlining alongside a controversial dilution of public-health transparency:
  * **Streamlining & Inventor Recognition**: The 2024 Rules reduced the timeline for filing a **Request for Examination (RFE)** under **Section 11B** from 48 months to **31 months** from the priority date (accelerating patent examination), simplified foreign filing disclosures under **Section 8 (Form-3)**, and created a new **"Certificate of Inventorship" (Form 8A)** under **Rule 70A** to provide official state recognition to actual human inventors whose names otherwise remain subordinate to corporate assignees on the patent grant certificate.
  * **Dilution of Form-27 (Statement of Commercial Working under Section 146(2))**: Historically, **Section 146(2)** read with **Rule 131** required every patentee and licensee to file an **annual verified statement on Form-27** detailing the extent to which the patented invention had been commercially worked in India—specifically requiring disclosure of the exact **quantum and monetary value** of the patented product manufactured inside India versus imported from abroad, the retail price charged, and whether public demand had been met at a reasonable price. This annual Form-27 database was the primary empirical evidentiary source used by generic firms and patient groups (including Natco in the *Nexavar* case) to prove non-working and unaffordability under **Section 84(1)**. Following the **2020 Amendment Rules** (which removed the requirement to specify exact unit quantities) and culminating in the **Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024**, the mandatory filing frequency of **Form-27** was relaxed from **once every financial year to once every three financial years**, and the revised Form-27 allows patentees merely to check a binary box indicating whether the patent is "worked" or "not worked" (and whether it is available for licensing), without disclosing domestic production volumes, import ratios, or pricing data—raising concern among public-health jurists that this creates an evidentiary asymmetry for future **Section 84** compulsory licensing applicants.

## III. Telecommunications Standard Essential Patents (SEPs), Biopiracy Counter-Architectures, and the WIPO 2024 Genetic Resources Treaty

### 5. Standard Essential Patents (SEPs) and FRAND Licensing Jurisprudence in 5G/6G and Wi-Fi Ecosystems

#### Technical Standardization vs. Patent Hold-Up and Hold-Out in Cellular Physical Layers
In modern information and communications technology (ICT), cellular networks (**4G LTE, 5G NR, and emerging 6G**), short-range wireless protocols (**Wi-Fi 6/7 under IEEE 802.11**), and video compression codecs (**HEVC / H.265**) cannot function unless billions of heterogeneous devices manufactured by competing companies—smartphones, baseband modems, base stations, and IoT sensors—adhere strictly to identical, non-negotiable physical and protocol standards. These global interoperability standards are formulated through consensus by **Standard Setting Organizations (SSOs)** such as the **3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)**, the **European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI)**, and the **Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE)**.

When an SSO adopts a specific technical modulation scheme—such as **Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access (OFDMA)**, **Polar Codes** for control channels, or **Low-Density Parity-Check (LDPC)** error-correcting codes in 5G—any patent covering a mandatory component of that technical specification becomes a **Standard Essential Patent (SEP)**. By physical and engineering definition, **it is impossible to manufacture a standard-compliant 5G smartphone or Wi-Fi router without infringing a Standard Essential Patent**, because no non-infringing design-around alternative is permitted by the interoperability standard. This creates a structural conflict between two opposing market failures:

* **Patent Hold-Up and Royalty Stacking (Upstream Monopoly Extraction)**: Once an industry has invested hundreds of billions of dollars in wafer fabs and assembly lines locked into the 3GPP 5G standard, a single SEP owner acquires immense hold-up leverage. By threatening to obtain a court **injunction** blocking the import or sale of an implementer's entire smartphone lineup over a single patented wireless protocol step, the SEP holder can extract extortionate royalties far exceeding the intrinsic ex-ante technical value of the invention (**Patent Hold-Up**). Furthermore, because a single 5G smartphone implicates **tens of thousands of declared SEPs** held by dozens of different patent holders (Qualcomm, Ericsson, Nokia, Huawei, InterDigital, Samsung), if every SEP holder demands a 1% to 2% royalty, the cumulative royalty burden (**Royalty Stacking**) would exceed 30% to 40% of the handset's total selling price, rendering manufacturing economically unviable.
* **Patent Hold-Out / Reverse Hold-Up (Downstream Free-Riding)**: Conversely, because patents are not self-enforcing and invalidity/infringement trials take years, a downstream handset implementer can immediately embed the standardized 5G protocols into millions of smartphones, capture market share, and systematically delay or refuse licensing negotiations (**Patent Hold-Out** or acting as an **"Unwilling Licensee"**), forcing the SEP owner to litigate patent-by-patent across multiple jurisdictions while collecting zero revenue.
* **The FRAND Commitment**: To neutralize both pathologies, SSOs require patent holders participating in standardization to submit an irrevocable contractual undertaking to license their SEPs to all willing implementers on **FRAND (Fair, Reasonable, and Non-Discriminatory)** terms:
  * **Fair**: The licensing terms must not include anti-competitive tying of non-essential patents, coercive grant-backs, or restrictive non-challenge clauses.
  * **Reasonable**: The royalty rate must reflect the intrinsic technical contribution of the patented technology before standardization, ensuring the aggregate royalty stack across the entire standard remains commercially reasonable.
  * **Non-Discriminatory**: Similarly situated implementers competing in the same market tier must be offered comparable effective royalty rates without discriminatory favoritism.

#### Indian Judicial Evolution on SEPs, Injunctions, and Royalty Bases (SSPPU vs. End-Device Valuation)
As India transformed under the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme into the world's second-largest mobile handset manufacturing hub, the **Delhi High Court Intellectual Property Division (IPD)** and the **Competition Commission of India (CCI)** emerged as premier global battlegrounds for SEP-FRAND jurisprudence:

* **The Valuation Base Controversy (SSPPU vs. Net End-Device Selling Price)**: A central economic clash in SEP litigation is the **royalty base**:
  * **Implementers' Position (SSPPU)**: Handset manufacturers argue that because cellular communication protocols are completely executed inside a discrete **$15 to $25 baseband processor chip**, FRAND royalties must be calculated strictly on the price of the **Smallest Saleable Patent-Practicing Unit (SSPPU)**—the baseband chip—rather than the retail price of an ₹80,000 smartphone whose value is driven by OLED displays, multi-lens cameras, Titanium enclosures, and brand equity unrelated to the cellular SEP.
  * **SEP Holders' Position (End-Device Level)**: SEP owners counter that established global telecommunications industry practice prices portfolio licenses as a small percentage (typically **0.5% to 1.5%**) of the **net selling price of the end-user device** (often subject to a dollar cap), arguing that the economic value of 5G connectivity scales across the entire device functionality.
* **Landmark Delhi High Court Precedents (*Intex v. Ericsson* [2023], *Nokia v. Oppo* [2023], and *Lava International v. Ericsson* [March 2024])**:
  1. **Indirect Proof of Infringement via Standard Mapping**: In SEP litigation, instead of physically dismantling a phone to trace electron flows, Indian courts validate **Two-Step Indirect Infringement Mapping**: **(i)** mapping the patent claim elements onto the mandatory sections of the 3GPP technical standard specification, and **(ii)** demonstrating that the implementer's device is certified as compliant with that 3GPP standard.
  2. **Portfolio-Wide Global FRAND Licensing and *Pro-Tem* Deposits**: In ***Intex Technologies v. Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson*** **(Division Bench, 2023)** and ***Nokia Technologies OY v. Guangdong Oppo Mobile*** **(2023)**, the Delhi High Court held that evaluating thousands of SEPs individually is impossible; therefore, offering a **global portfolio license** is fully consistent with FRAND, and an implementer who engages in **Hold-Out** (stalling negotiations while selling infringing phones) can be ordered to furnish immediate ***pro-tem* (interim) financial security deposits** or face an interim injunction aligned with the European Court of Justice's ***Huawei v. ZTE***willing-licensee framework.
  3. ***Lava International Ltd. v. Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson*** **(March 28, 2024)**: Delivering India's most comprehensive post-trial final judgment on SEPs, the Delhi High Court held Lava liable as an unwilling licensee for infringing Ericsson's 2G/EDGE and 3G SEPs, upheld the validity of the patents over **Section 3(k)** algorithm objections because the speech-coding and AMR (Adaptive Multi-Rate) inventions operated at the physical hardware layer to increase radio spectrum efficiency, endorsed **Comparable Licenses** over top-down chip-level SSPPU calculations as the primary market benchmark, and awarded historic damages exceeding **₹244 crore ($29+ million)** plus 5% annual interest.
  4. **Jurisdictional Clash Between the Patents Act and the Competition Act, 2002**: When Indian handset makers (Micromax, Intex) approached the **Competition Commission of India (CCI)** alleging that Ericsson abused its dominant position under **Section 4 of the Competition Act, 2002** by demanding end-device royalties, a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court in ***Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson v. Competition Commission of India*** **(July 2023)** held that the **Patents Act, 1970** (specifically **Chapter XVI** on Compulsory Licensing and reasonable licensing terms) is a **special statute (*lex specialis*)** when examining the exercise of patent rights and licensing royalties, thereby overriding general antitrust inquiries by the CCI into patent licensing terms (presently under final constitutional scrutiny before the Supreme Court of India).

### 6. Biopiracy, the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), and the May 2024 WIPO Treaty on Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge

#### Historical Biopiracy Mechanisms (Turmeric, Neem, Basmati) and the CSIR-AYUSH TKDL Defensive Prior-Art Architecture
**Biopiracy** is the unauthorized commercial appropriation and monopolization—via Western product or process patents—of biological and **Genetic Resources (GRs)** (medicinal plants, landrace seeds, marine organisms, microbial strains) and **Associated Traditional Knowledge (ATK)** developed, conserved, and passed down over centuries by Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities (IPLCs) in biodiversity-rich developing nations, without obtaining **Prior Informed Consent (PIC)** or executing **Fair and Equitable Benefit-Sharing (ABS)** agreements.

In the 1990s, a structural flaw in international patent law fueled rampant biopiracy against India. Under the domestic patent statutes of major jurisdictions (such as **pre-America Invents Act 35 U.S.C. § 102** in the United States and the **European Patent Convention**), while foreign **written publications** defeated novelty globally, **unwritten prior public use or oral knowledge** existing solely outside the US/Europe was often not recognized as novelty-destroying prior art, or, even when written down in ancient **Sanskrit, Urdu, Persian, Tamil, or Pali** classical texts (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Yoga), remained completely invisible and unsearchable to patent examiners at the **USPTO** and **EPO**. This information asymmetry resulted in three notorious biopiracy shocks that forced India to spend millions of dollars in multi-year foreign revocation battles:

1. **The Turmeric (*Curcuma longa*) Wound-Healing Patent (USPTO, 1995–1997)**: In March 1995, the USPTO granted **US Patent No. 5,401,504** to two researchers at the University of Mississippi Medical Center for the *"use of turmeric in wound healing."* **CSIR** (led by Dr. R.A. Mashelkar) filed a formal re-examination challenge producing **32 documented prior-art references** in Sanskrit, Urdu, and Hindi—including an ancient Ayurvedic text and a 1953 paper in the *Journal of the Indian Medical Association*—forcing the USPTO to **revoke all six claims** in **August 1997** for lack of novelty.
2. **The Neem (*Azadirachta indica*) Fungicide Patent (EPO, 1994–2005)**: In 1994, the European Patent Office granted **EP Patent No. 0436257** to the US Department of Agriculture and multinational corporation **W.R. Grace** for a method of controlling fungi on plants using a hydrophobic extracted **Neem oil** formulation. After a 10-year legal opposition proving that Indian farmers and Ayurvedic texts had extracted Neem seed emulsions for agricultural pest and fungal control for millennia, the EPO revoked the patent in **2000** (upheld on final appeal in **March 2005**) for lack of novelty and inventive step.
3. **The Basmati Rice Lines & Grains Patent (USPTO, 1997–2001)**: In September 1997, Texas-based corporation **RiceTec Inc.** obtained **US Patent No. 5,663,484** claiming *"Basmati rice lines and grains"* bred by crossing Indian Basmati germplasm (obtained from international gene banks) with semi-dwarf American long-grain varieties. Following aggressive prior-art intervention by the Government of India (APEDA and CSIR), RiceTec was forced in **2001** to surrender or narrow 15 of its 20 sweeping claims, stripping the patent of the right to monopolize the generic name "Basmati" and confining it strictly to three specific hybrid lines bred by RiceTec.

Realizing that litigating individual biopiracy patents after grant in Western courts was prohibitively expensive (~$1–$5 million per challenge), India pioneered a systemic, preventative technological solution in **2001**: the **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)**, jointly established by **CSIR** and the **Ministry of AYUSH**:

* **Linguistic and Taxonomic Translation Engine**: The TKDL systematically digitized over **4.5 lakh (450,000+) traditional medicinal formulations** transcribed from ancient Sanskrit, Urdu, Persian, Arabic, and Tamil treatises across **Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Yoga**. To bridge the vocabulary gap between ancient humoral nomenclature and modern molecular pharmacology, TKDL created the **Traditional Knowledge Resource Classification (TKRC)**—comprising over 27,000 subgroups mapped directly onto the **World Intellectual Property Organization's (WIPO) International Patent Classification (IPC)** system—and translated every scientific botanic name, disease pathology, and extraction method into **five major international patent examination languages: English, German, French, Spanish, and Japanese**.
* **Defensive Prior-Art Interdiction**: Under Non-Disclosure Access Agreements signed with **16+ major International Patent Offices** (including the **EPO, USPTO, JPO, UKIPO, CIPO, and IP Australia**)—and expanded by the **Union Cabinet in August 2022** to allow access to domestic Indian startups, academic researchers, and R&D laboratories for drug discovery—patent examiners worldwide search the TKDL before granting any herbal or natural-product patent. Whenever a foreign patent application claims a formulation already documented in the TKDL, the examiner cites the TKDL record to reject the application at the threshold for lack of novelty—preventing or forcing the withdrawal of **over 300+ biopiracy patent applications globally at zero litigation cost**.
* **Domestic Statutory Counterparts (Patents Act Sec 3(p) & Biological Diversity Act, 2002 [Amended 2023])**: Domestically, **Section 3(p) of the Patents Act, 1970** bars patents on *"an invention which, in effect, is traditional knowledge or which is an aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known component or components,"* while **Section 10(4)(d)(ii)** and **Section 25(1)(j)/(k)** mandate that every Indian patent applicant must disclose the exact **source and geographical origin of any biological material** used in the invention. Concurrently, **Section 6 of the Biological Diversity Act, 2002** (as updated by the **Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023**, enforced from April 2024) mandates that no person shall obtain any intellectual property right in or outside India for any invention based on research or information on a biological resource obtained from India without obtaining the prior approval of the **National Biodiversity Authority (NBA)**, which imposes **Access and Benefit-Sharing (ABS)** royalties directed back to local **Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs)** (while the 2023 Amendment exempted registered AYUSH practitioners, local communities, and cultivated medicinal plants from prior NBA commercial utilization approval to promote domestic bio-manufacturing).

#### The May 2024 WIPO Treaty (Geneva): Mandatory Patent Disclosure Requirements, Sanctions, and the CBD-Nagoya-TRIPS Convergence
While the **TKDL** serves as a powerful **defensive shield** for already-codified written knowledge, it could not prevent a foreign biotech company from collecting a rare medicinal plant or uncodified oral ethnobotanical knowledge from an Indian forest tribe, isolating a novel bioactive alkaloid or modifying its chemical structure slightly to pass the novelty test, and filing a patent in the US, Switzerland, or Japan **without disclosing where the genetic resource came from**—thereby evading the **1992 UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)** and its **2010 Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing (ABS)**. For over **24 years** inside WIPO's Intergovernmental Committee (IGC) and the WTO TRIPS Council, India, Brazil, South Africa, and the **Like-Minded Megadiverse Countries (LMMC)** fought to make **origin disclosure** a mandatory global condition of patent law.

This quarter-century diplomatic battle culminated on **May 24, 2024**, with the historic consensus adoption of the **WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge** at the WIPO Diplomatic Conference in Geneva (alongside the companion November 2024 **Riyadh Design Law Treaty**, marking 2024's twin Riyadh/Geneva WIPO multilateral milestones). It is the **first WIPO treaty in history** to address the interface between intellectual property, genetic resources, and traditional knowledge, and the first to explicitly include provisions specifically for **Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities**:

* **Article 3 — The Legally Binding Patent Disclosure Requirement (PDR)**: Whenever a claimed invention in a patent application is **"based on"** (defined in **Article 2** as meaning that the genetic resources and/or associated traditional knowledge must have been necessary or **material to the development of the claimed invention**, and that the claimed invention must depend on the specific properties of the genetic resources and/or associated traditional knowledge):
  1. **Genetic Resources Disclosure (**Article 3.1**)**: Contracting Parties **shall require** patent applicants to disclose the **Country of Origin** of the genetic resources, or, if the Country of Origin is not known to the applicant (or does not apply), the **source of the genetic resources** (such as a specific botanical garden, microbial culture collection, or international gene bank).
  2. **Associated Traditional Knowledge Disclosure (**Article 3.2**)**: Where the claimed invention is based on Associated Traditional Knowledge, Contracting Parties **shall require** applicants to disclose the **Indigenous Peoples or Local Community**, as applicable, who provided the Associated Traditional Knowledge, or, if not known, the **source** of the Associated Traditional Knowledge.
  3. **Declaration of Unknown Origin (**Article 3.3**)**: Only if neither the origin nor the source is known to the applicant despite diligent inquiry may the applicant make a formal declaration to that effect, affirming under legal penalty that the declaration is true to the best of the applicant's knowledge.
* **Article 4 & Article 5 — Non-Retroactivity, Administrative Sanctions, and the Fraudulent Intent Exception**:
  * **Non-Retroactivity (**Article 4**)**: The disclosure obligation applies prospectively only to patent applications filed after the Treaty enters into force for a Contracting Party (which occurs three months after **15 eligible parties** deposit their instruments of ratification or accession).
  * **Pre-Grant Rectification & Post-Grant Sanctions (**Article 5**)**: Contracting Parties must provide applicants an opportunity to rectify an inadvertent failure to disclose the required origin information before a patent is rejected. Post-grant, **Article 5.3** establishes a carefully negotiated compromise between biodiversity-rich nations and pharmaceutical-exporting nations: as a general baseline, **no Contracting Party shall revoke, invalidate, or render unenforceable the conferred patent rights solely on the basis of an applicant's failure to disclose the information specified in Article 3**; however, **Article 5.4** carves out a crucial teeth-bearing exception—each Contracting Party **may provide for post-grant sanctions or remedies, including revocation/invalidation, where there has been fraudulent intent** in regard to the disclosure requirement, alongside administrative fines and criminal/civil penalties under domestic law.
* **Article 6 & Article 7 — Interoperable Information Systems and Relationship with CBD/Nagoya**: **Article 6** empowers Contracting Parties to establish centralized digital databases of genetic resources and traditional knowledge (explicitly validating the global expansion of architectures modeled on India's **TKDL**) accessible to patent examiners worldwide, while **Article 7** anchors the treaty in mutual supportiveness with the **CBD**, the **Nagoya Protocol**, and the **FAO International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (ITPGRFA)**—transforming global patent offices into transparent compliance checkpoints that alert national biodiversity authorities whenever domestic genetic resources are commercialized abroad.

## IV. Dual-Use Strategic Export Controls (WMD Act & SCOMET) and Frontier Techno-Legal Ethics (AI Authorship & Neurorights)

### 7. Strategic Dual-Use Export Control Architecture: The WMD Act, 2005 and DGFT's SCOMET Categories 0 to 8

#### Multilateral Export Control Regimes (NSG, MTCR, Australia Group, Wassenaar) and the WMD Act Statutory Mandate
At the intersection of frontier science, international trade, and national security lies the governance of **Dual-Use Technologies**—advanced materials, chemical precursors, pathogenic microorganisms, precision CNC machine tools, cryogenic propulsion systems, semiconductor lithography equipment, quantum sensors, and artificial intelligence accelerators that possess legitimate civilian and commercial applications, but can simultaneously be diverted to fabricate **Weapons of Mass Destruction (WMDs—nuclear, biological, and chemical weapons)**, ballistic/hypersonic delivery vehicles, or advanced conventional military systems.

Globally, dual-use proliferation is policed by **four Multilateral Export Control Regimes (MECRs)**:
1. **The Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG, 1974)**: Founded specifically in response to India's May 1974 *Smiling Buddha* peaceful nuclear explosion, the 48-nation NSG regulates nuclear-specific items (Trigger List) and nuclear dual-use items. While India received a historic **India-specific clean waiver from the NSG in September 2008** (enabling international civil nuclear commerce despite not signing the NPT), China has repeatedly blocked India's formal consensus admission as a full NSG member.
2. **The Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR, 1987)**: Regulates unmanned delivery systems (ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, space launch vehicles, and long-range UAVs) capable of delivering a **500 kg payload to a range of at least 300 km** (Category I items). **India became a full member of the MTCR in June 2016**—a milestone that directly lifted the 290-km range ceiling on the **BrahMos** supersonic cruise missile, enabling DRDO to develop the **450 km to 800+ km extended-range BrahMos** variants and acquire predator-class HALE drones.
3. **The Australia Group (AG, 1985)**: An informal forum of countries that harmonizes export controls on **chemical weapon precursors** (e.g., thionyl chloride, nerve agent precursors) and **biological pathogens/toxins** (e.g., *Bacillus anthracis*, Ebola virus, ricin, and dual-use fermenters) to support the Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC) and Biological Weapons Convention (BWC). **India became a full member of the Australia Group in January 2018**.
4. **The Wassenaar Arrangement (WA, 1996)**: Succeeding the Cold War COCOM regime, the Wassenaar Arrangement regulates export controls on **Conventional Arms and Dual-Use Goods and Technologies** across nine advanced technology categories (electronics, computers, telecommunications/encryption, sensors/lasers, navigation/avionics, marine, and aerospace propulsion). **India became a full participating state of the Wassenaar Arrangement in December 2017**.

To translate these multilateral non-proliferation obligations and **UN Security Council Resolution 1540 (2004)** into binding domestic criminal and trade law, Parliament enacted the **Weapons of Mass Destruction and their Delivery Systems (Prohibition of Unlawful Activities) Act, 2005 (WMD Act, 2005)**, read alongside **Section 14A to 14E of the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 (FTDR Act)**. The **WMD Act, 2005** imposes strict criminal penalties (up to life imprisonment) for any unauthorized manufacture, acquisition, possession, development, transport, or transfer of nuclear, biological, or chemical weapons, their means of delivery, or related dual-use materials, equipment, and **intangible technology transfers** (such as transmitting CAD blueprints or software source code electronically to a foreign national). Furthermore, the **Weapons of Mass Destruction and their Delivery Systems (Prohibition of Unlawful Activities) Amendment Act, 2022** inserted **Section 12A**, explicitly prohibiting the **financing of any prohibited WMD activity** and empowering the Central Government to immediately freeze, seize, or attach the financial assets and economic resources of any person or entity engaged in WMD proliferation financing, aligning India's statutory regime with **Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendation 7**.

#### Granular Deconstruction of SCOMET Categories 0–8 and the 2023–2026 GAEC/GAICT Liberalizations (Drones, AI, Quantum, Cryogenics)
Operationally, India's dual-use export control list is codified under Appendix 3 of Schedule 2 of the ITC (HS) Classification of Export and Import Items, administered by the **Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT)** under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, and titled **SCOMET (Special Chemicals, Organisms, Materials, Equipment and Technologies)**. Every export of a SCOMET-listed tangible item, software package, or intangible technology transfer requires a prior statutory authorization evaluated by an **Inter-Ministerial Working Group (IMWG)** chaired by DGFT (incorporating representatives from MEA, MoD/DRDO, DAE, DoS/ISRO, MeitY, and intelligence agencies), except for **Category 0** (licensed directly by the **Department of Atomic Energy**) and **Category 6** (licensed by the **Department of Defence Production, Ministry of Defence**):

* **Category 0**: **Nuclear materials, nuclear-related other materials, equipment and technology**—covering source materials (natural/depleted Uranium, Thorium), special fissionable materials ($^{235}\text{U}$, $^{239}\text{Pu}$, $^{233}\text{U}$), nuclear reactors, calandria vessels, heavy water ($\text{D}_2\text{O}$) production plants, spent-fuel reprocessing plants, and gas-centrifuge uranium enrichment cascades (aligned with the **NSG Trigger List Part 1**).
* **Category 1**: **Toxic chemical agents and other chemicals**—subdivided into Category 1A (banned Chemical Weapons Convention Schedule 1 nerve and blister agents like Sarin, VX, and Mustard gas), Category 1B (CWC Schedule 2 chemicals like Thiodiglycol), Category 1C (CWC Schedule 3 dual-use industrial chemicals like Phosgene, Hydrogen Cyanide, Phosphorus Trichloride, and Triethanolamine), and Category 1D (additional **Australia Group** chemical precursors).
* **Category 2**: **Micro-organisms, toxins**—covering human, animal, and plant pathogens including bacteria (*Bacillus anthracis*, *Yersinia pestis*, *Francisella tularensis*), viruses (Ebola, Marburg, Monkeypox/Mpox, Variola, Lassa, SARS-related coronaviruses, Avian Influenza), biological toxins (Botulinum toxins, Ricin, Saxitoxin, Aflatoxins), **genetically modified organisms / synthetic genetic elements** coding for pathogenicity, and dual-use BSL-3/BSL-4 biocontainment fermenters, centrifugal separators, and aerosol inhalation chambers (aligned with the **Australia Group**).
* **Category 3**: **Materials, Materials Processing Equipment, and related technologies**—covering ultra-high-tensile **maraging steels**, titanium-aluminide alloys, high-modulus **carbon fibers** (used in solid rocket motor casings and uranium gas-centrifuge rotors), zirconium tubes, and multi-axis CNC flow-forming machines.
* **Category 4**: **Nuclear-related other equipment, assemblies, and components**—covering dual-use industrial items usable in nuclear weapons or fuel cycles, such as high-speed flash X-ray generators, krytron/sprytron pulsed-power triggers, mass spectrometers, and beryllium/hafnium components (aligned with the **NSG Dual-Use List Part 2**).
* **Category 5**: **Aerospace systems, equipment, including production and test equipment, related technology and specially designed components and accessories**—covering complete rocket systems, space launch vehicles, sounding rockets, **Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs / Drones)**, solid and liquid rocket propulsion engines, **Scramjet / Ramjet air-breathing hypersonic engines**, carbon-carbon re-entry thermal protection nosecones, and inertial navigation ring-laser gyroscopes (aligned with the **MTCR Annex**).
* **Category 6**: **Munitions List**—covering purely military armaments including small arms, artillery, main battle tanks, warships, submarines, combat aircraft, military explosives/propellants, and **Directed-Energy Weapons (DEWs)** (aligned with the **Wassenaar Arrangement Munitions List**).
* **Category 7**: **[Reserved]**—Historically covered electronics, computers, and information security, which were consolidated and upgraded into **Category 8** to mirror the exact 9-category numbering structure of the **Wassenaar Arrangement Dual-Use List**.
* **Category 8**: **Special Materials and Related Equipment, Material Processing, Electronics, Computers, Telecommunications, Information Security, Sensors and Lasers, Navigation and Avionics, Marine, Aerospace and Propulsion**—the primary frontier technology category governing:
  * **8A3 / 8D3 / 8E3 (Electronics & Semiconductors)**: GAAFET/FinFET Integrated Circuits, Wide-Bandgap **Gallium Nitride (GaN)** and **Silicon Carbide (SiC)** MMICs, **EUV photolithography** equipment, and Electronic Design Automation (EDA) software.
  * **8A4 (High-Performance & Quantum Computers)**: Exascale/PetaFLOP AI accelerator clusters and **Quantum Computers** (systems supporting $\ge 34$ to $100+$ physical qubits with controlled two-qubit gate error rates, alongside **Cryogenic Dilution Refrigerators** operating below $100\text{ mK}$ and **Cryogenic CMOS** electronics added in recent Wassenaar/SCOMET updates).
  * **8A5 Part 2 (Information Security & Post-Quantum Cryptography)**: Cryptographic hardware and software implementing symmetric/asymmetric encryption and **Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)** networks.

To ensure that stringent non-proliferation controls do not strangle India's booming commercial aerospace, chemical, IT/semiconductor design, and civilian drone export industries, DGFT executed a series of landmark **General Authorization (Bulk Open-Licensing)** reforms across **2023–2026** for trusted Indian exporters possessing a certified **Internal Compliance Programme (ICP)** or **Authorized Economic Operator (AEO)** status:

* **GAEC (General Authorization for Export of Chemicals and Related Equipment)**: Replaces consignment-by-consignment IMWG approvals with a single **5-year bulk authorization** for exporting specified Category 1C, 1D, and 3A dual-use industrial chemicals to partner countries in the multilateral export control regimes, subject solely to post-export quarterly reporting.
* **GAICT (General Authorization for Export of Information and Communication Technology / Intra-Company Transfers)**: Enables Indian R&D subsidiaries and Global Capability Centers (GCCs) working on **Category 8** semiconductor design, **AI architectures**, **5G/6G telecom encryption**, and **aerospace avionics** to seamlessly transfer source code, chip layouts, and encrypted technical data to their foreign parent or sister companies without waiting 45–60 days for individual SCOMET licenses.
* **GAED (General Authorization for Export of Drones / UAVs, notified 2023–2024)**: Recognizing that all civilian agricultural, survey, and delivery drones technically fell under **Category 5B** or **8A9**, DGFT introduced **GAED**, granting a one-time **3-year bulk export authorization** for civilian UAVs/drones (having a range $\le 25\text{ km}$ and payload $\le 25\text{ kg}$, excluding military combat drones and autonomous swarm munitions), unlocking global market access for Indian drone startups.
* **Catch-All Controls**: Even if an item is **not explicitly listed** in SCOMET Categories 0 to 8, under the statutory **Catch-All Clause** of the FTDR Act and Foreign Trade Policy 2023, an Indian exporter is legally obligated to apply for a SCOMET license if notified by DGFT, or if the exporter knows or has reason to suspect that the unlisted item (such as commercial AI software or dual-use laboratory glassware) is intended entirely or in part for a WMD program or military end-use in a sanctioned destination.

1. **Can an AI Machine be Named a Statutory "Inventor" on a Patent? (The Global *DABUS* Rulings)**:
   * **The DABUS Test Case**: Computer scientist **Dr. Stephen Thaler** engineered a neural network system named **DABUS (Device for the Autonomous Bootstrapping of Unified Sentience)** and filed coordinate patent applications across the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Patent Office, Australia, South Africa, and **India (Indian Patent Application No. 202017019068)** claiming two inventions autonomously generated by DABUS without human engineering direction: a **fractal-profile food container** improving thermal conduction and robotic grip, and a **neural-flame emergency beacon**. Thaler explicitly listed **DABUS as the sole statutory inventor**, claiming ownership of the patent for himself as the owner of the machine (invoking the ancient property-law doctrine of **accession**, just as the owner of an orchard owns the fruit produced by its trees).
   * **Global Judicial Rejection (2021–2024)**: With the sole exception of South Africa (which operates a purely formal depository system without substantive examination), every major appellate court and patent office in the world rejected the DABUS applications:
     * **United States (*Thaler v. Vidal*, US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, 2022; cert. denied by US Supreme Court, April 2023)**: Held that the US Patent Act (**35 U.S.C. § 100(f)**) defines an "inventor" as an **"individual,"** which in statutory construction unambiguously means a **natural human being**. Subsequently, the **USPTO issued its February 2024 Inventorship Guidance for AI-Assisted Inventions**, ruling that while wholly AI-generated inventions are unpatentable, **AI-assisted inventions are patentable if and only if a natural human person made a "significant contribution"** to the conception of the claimed invention (merely presenting a general problem prompt to an LLM or running an automated screen does not confer human inventorship).
     * **United Kingdom (*Thaler v. Comptroller-General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks*, UK Supreme Court, December 20, 2023)**: Unanimously held that under **Sections 7 and 13 of the UK Patents Act 1977**, an inventor must be a **natural person**, and rejected the doctrine of accession on the ground that an autonomous machine has no legal personality and therefore possesses no property rights in an intangible invention that it could transfer or assign to its human owner.
     * **European Patent Office (EPO Legal Board of Appeal J 8/20, 2021)** and **High Court of Australia (*Commissioner of Patents v. Thaler*, 2022)**: Reached identical conclusions that statutory inventorship requires human legal capacity.
   * **The Statutory Position under the Indian Patents Act, 1970**: Under **Section 6(1)(a)** of the Indian Patents Act, an application for a patent can be made only by the **"true and first inventor"** or their legal assignee. While **Section 2(1)(p)** allows a "person" (the patentee/owner) to include a corporate juristic entity or government agency, **Section 2(1)(y)** defines the "true and first inventor" and **Section 6** read with the **Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024 (Form 8A Certificate of Inventorship)** requires the inventor to be a **natural human person** capable of executing a legal assignment contract and possessing moral rights. The Indian Patent Office raised formal objections against Thaler's DABUS application under **Sections 2 and 6**, confirming that under existing Indian statutory law, an AI system cannot be designated as an inventor.
2. **Generative AI Training on Copyrighted Works vs. Section 52 "Fair Dealing" and Output Authorship**:
   * **The Upstream Training Data Clash (Text and Data Mining vs. Infringement)**: Training a frontier multimodal foundation model requires scraping and tokenizing trillions of words, images, and audio clips—much of which is actively copyrighted material (books, journalism, source code, and artistic portfolios). Copyright holders globally (**The New York Times v. OpenAI** in the US, **Getty Images v. Stability AI** in the UK, and **Asian News International [ANI] v. OpenAI** filed before the **Delhi High Court in November 2024**, joined by the Digital News Publishers Association and Indian book publishers in 2025) contend that copying complete copyrighted articles into training servers, tokenizing them during pre-training, or fetching them in real-time via **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** violates the exclusive right of reproduction and adaptation under **Section 14 of the Indian Copyright Act, 1957**, while creating a commercial substitute that diverts web traffic from original creators.
   * **US "Fair Use" vs. India's Exhaustive "Fair Dealing" (**Section 52(1)(a)**)**: A critical doctrinal divergence separates US and Indian copyright law:
     * The United States operates an open-ended, flexible four-factor **"Fair Use"** defense (**17 U.S.C. § 107**), allowing AI developers to argue that ingesting text to extract abstract statistical token correlations is highly **"transformative"** (analogous to *Authors Guild v. Google*, 2015), provided the LLM does not regurgitate verbatim passages due to overfitting.
     * By contrast, **Section 52(1)(a) of the Indian Copyright Act, 1957** codifies a closed, exhaustive **"Fair Dealing"** exception restricted specifically to: **(i) private or personal use, including research**; **(ii) criticism or review**; and **(iii) the reporting of current events and current affairs**. Unlike the European Union (which enacted explicit **Text and Data Mining [TDM] exceptions** under **Articles 3 and 4 of the 2019 EU Digital Single Market Copyright Directive**, allowing commercial AI training unless the copyright holder explicitly opts out via machine-readable protocols like `robots.txt`) or Japan (**Article 30-4 of the Japanese Copyright Act**, permitting non-enjoyment data analysis), India's Copyright Act lacks an explicit commercial TDM exception—leaving Indian courts to adjudicate whether commercial LLM training qualifies as "research" under **Section 52(1)(a)(i)** or requires mandatory collective licensing / opt-out remuneration frameworks.
   * **Downstream AI Output Copyright (**Section 2(d)(vi)**)**: Under **Section 2(d)(vi) of the Indian Copyright Act, 1957** (inserted in 1994), in relation to any literary, dramatic, musical, or artistic work which is **"computer-generated,"** the **"author"** is defined as *"the person who causes the work to be created."* While a purely mechanical reading might suggest that typing a 10-word text prompt into Midjourney or ChatGPT makes the human user the statutory author, both the **US Copyright Office (2023–2025 Guidance, *Zarya of the Dawn* and *Thaler v. Perlmutter*)** and Indian copyright jurisprudence (anchored in the Supreme Court's standard of **"skill, judgment, and minimum modicum of human creativity"** in ***Eastern Book Company v. D.B. Modak***, 2008) establish that purely autonomous AI outputs generated from simple prompts lack human authorship over the expressive elements and fall into the public domain, whereas works involving **substantial human creative selection, iterative arrangement, and post-generation modification** remain eligible for copyright over the human-contributed expression.

#### Invasive Brain-Computer Interfaces (Neuralink) and the Five Foundational Neurorights (Cognitive Liberty, Mental Privacy, Mental Integrity, Psychological Continuity, Algorithmic Bias Protection)
At the final frontier of biomedical engineering and artificial intelligence lies the direct physical coupling of silicon microelectronics with the living human cerebral cortex via **Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs)**. Operating on the biophysical principle that human motor intentions, sensory perceptions, and internal speech are encoded as spatiotemporal patterns of **extracellular action potentials ("spikes")** and **local field potentials (LFPs)** generated by voltage-gated $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$ ion channels across neuronal membranes, BCIs are classified along an invasiveness gradient:

* **Non-Invasive BCIs**: Scalp **Electroencephalography (EEG)** and functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS), which suffer from severe spatial blurring and skull attenuation of high-frequency gamma oscillations ($>30\text{ Hz}$).
* **Endovascular Semi-Invasive BCIs**: Stent-electrode arrays (**Synchron Stentrode**) threaded via the jugular vein into the superior sagittal sinus adjacent to the motor cortex without open craniotomy.
* **Invasive Intracortical BCIs (**Neuralink N1 "Telepathy" Implant**, **Blackrock Neurotech Utah Array**, **Precision Neuroscience Layer 7 Cortical Interface**)**: Exemplified by **Neuralink's January 2024 to 2026 human clinical trials (PRIME Study)**, a robotic sewing system (**R1 Robot**) uses micron-scale optical coherence tomography to avoid cortical blood vessels while inserting **64 ultra-flexible polyimide polymer threads** (each thinner than a human hair, $\sim 4\text{–}6\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ thick, minimizing glial scar tissue encapsulation) carrying **1,024 recording/stimulating microelectrodes** directly into the hand-knob area of the primary **motor cortex (M1)**. The hermetically sealed coin-sized **N1 implant** amplifies, digitizes, and wirelessly transmits neural spike trains via Bluetooth to a deep-learning decoder, enabling a quadriplegic or ALS patient to control computer cursors, play video games, and synthesize speech purely by imagining motor movements—while next-generation visual prostheses (**Neuralink "Blindsight"**, granted US FDA Breakthrough Device Designation in **September 2024**) stimulate the primary visual cortex (V1) directly to restore vision even in patients who have lost both eyes and optic nerves.

While bidirectional BCIs represent a medical triumph for paralysis, locked-in syndrome, and blindness, coupling high-bandwidth cortical read/write electrodes to commercial cloud AI models dissolves the final physical boundary protecting human autonomy. Historically, constitutional and human rights law (including **Article 18 of the ICCPR** and **Article 21 of the Constitution of India** as interpreted in ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India***, 2017) protected external speech, bodily integrity, and digital communications while taking for granted that the **forum internum**—the unexpressed inner sanctum of human thought, subconscious emotion, and memory inside the skull—was physically inaccessible to the state or corporations. When AI decoders trained on functional MRI and intracortical electrophysiology can reconstruct inner speech, semantic imagery, P300 recognition potentials, and emotional valence before a person consciously chooses to express them, traditional data-privacy statutes prove structurally inadequate.

To close this constitutional gap, neurobiologist **Dr. Rafael Yuste** and the **Morningside Group / NeuroRights Initiative** formulated **Five Foundational Neurorights**—pioneered into binding national law by **Chile's October 2021 Constitutional Amendment (Article 19, No. 1)** and enforced in the **Chilean Supreme Court's landmark August 2023 judgment in *Girardi v. Emotiv Inc.*** (ordering the deletion of commercial EEG brainwave recordings), followed by **Colorado and California's 2024 Neural Data Privacy Acts** and **UNESCO's 2024–2025 Global Standard-Setting Instrument on the Ethics of Neurotechnology**:

1. **The Right to Mental Privacy**: Raw and decoded neural data must be legally classified not as ordinary commercial browsing metadata, but as an inviolable biological extension of the human organ system (subject to the same prohibition against commercial sale as human organs). Any non-consensual extraction, retention, or commercial monetization of neural spike/EEG signals to infer subconscious preferences, political beliefs, or deception is strictly prohibited.
2. **The Right to Cognitive Liberty (Mental Self-Determination)**: Every individual possesses the sovereign right to alter their own mental states using neurotechnology and, equally, the absolute right **to refuse coercive neurotechnological intervention or neural surveillance** in workplaces (e.g., mandatory attention-monitoring EEG headbands), schools, military service, or forensic criminal interrogations (reinforcing the Indian Supreme Court's landmark ruling in ***Selvi v. State of Karnataka*** **(2010)**, which held that involuntary **Narcoanalysis, Polygraph, and Brain Electrical Activation Profile [BEAP / P300] tests** violate the **Article 20(3)** right against self-incrimination and **Article 21** substantive due process).
3. **The Right to Mental Integrity**: Protects individuals from unauthorized, malicious, or negligent physical or electromagnetic interference with their neural circuitry—specifically guarding **bidirectional (read/write) closed-loop implants** against neuro-cyberattacks ("brainjacking") that could hijack motor prostheses, induce pathological depression, or erase memories.
4. **The Right to Psychological Continuity (Personal Identity)**: Preserves the coherence of an individual's personal identity, sense of agency, and authentic decision-making continuity against closed-loop AI stimulation algorithms that subtly alter personality traits, impulse control, or moral judgment without the patient's conscious awareness (creating the **Agency & Criminal Liability Paradox**: if a closed-loop BCI algorithm misinterprets a fleeting subconscious impulse and actuates a physical harm, does legal mens rea attach to the human host or product liability to the BCI manufacturer?).
5. **The Right to Fair Access to Mental Augmentation and Protection from Algorithmic Bias**: Ensures that as neurotechnologies transition from therapeutic restoration to cognitive enhancement, they do not fracture society into a biologically augmented cognitive caste versus an unaugmented underclass, while mandating that the machine-learning weights embedded inside neural decoders are audited against systemic algorithmic bias.
