# Part 21: The Maratha Swarajya, the Ahom Paik State, 18th-Century Courtly Decentralization, Regional Miniature Schools, and Classical Performing Arts Lineages (1630–1761)

## I. The Maratha Swarajya and the Peshwa Confederacy: Basaltic Orogeny, Fiscal Predation, and Feudal Decentralization (1630–1761)

### 1. Deccan Trap Geomorphology, Mavali Peasant Mobilization, and Fortress Warfare

The geopolitical emergence of the **Maratha Swarajya** under **Chhatrapati Shivaji (1630–1680)** was fundamentally conditioned by the structural geology and orographic climatology of the Western Ghats (**Sahyadri** range). Formed by Late Cretaceous continental flood basalts (**Deccan Traps**), the **Sahyadri** escarpment consists of horizontally bedded, alternating strata of hard amygdaloidal basalt and softer vesicular lava flows. Differential sub-aerial weathering and monsoonal fluvial incision carved these formations into flat-topped mesas with near-vertical scarp faces, narrow defiles, and heavily forested valleys known as the **Maval** tract. Situated along the steep rain-shadow gradient between the hyper-humid **Konkan** littoral and the semi-arid **Desh** plateau, the **Maval** valleys yielded low, precarious cereal surpluses compared to the alluvial Indo-Gangetic plain. Consequently, imperial **Mughal** and **Adilshahi** heavy cavalry armies—dependent on vast grain-baggage trains, slow-moving siege parks, and flat alluvial battlefields—experienced severe logistical paralysis when attempting to penetrate the broken basaltic terrain of the **Sahyadris**.

**Chhatrapati Shivaji** operationalized this topographic asymmetry by bypassing the entrenched, high-caste **Deshmukh** magnates of the open plateau and directly mobilizing the **Mavali** (and **Hetkari**) peasant-pastoralists of the western valleys. Accustomed to seasonal scarcity, steep hill-climbing, and forest survival, the **Mavali** infantry and light **Bargir** cavalry formed an agile military instrument capable of living off parched grain and operating across vertical gradients where **Mughal** **Mansabdars** could not deploy.

*   **Asymmetric Kinetics of Ganimi Kava:** Maratha operational doctrine (**Ganimi Kava**) weaponized thermodynamic and logistical exhaustion against imperial field armies. Rather than engaging in set-piece linear shock battles against **Mughal** artillery and armored cavalry, Maratha light horsemen (mounted on hardy, low-maintenance **Bhima-thadi** mares) severed imperial forage lines, ambushed baggage trains in narrow ghat passes, and retreated instantaneously into fortified basalt summits before heavy imperial columns could deploy.
*   **Orogenic Fortress Engineering (Raigad and Pratapgad):** **Shivaji** converted the natural basalt mesas of the **Sahyadris** into an interlocking chain of over 240 hill forts (**Gad** / **Kot**), which functioned as the primary administrative nodes of the state. **Pratapgad** (constructed **1656** astride the Par pass overlooking the Koyna valley) was engineered with double-tiered curtain walls hugging the sheer basalt cliff to control trans-ghat communications, serving as the tactical trap where **Afzal Khan**’s **Adilshahi** army was annihilated in **1659**. **Raigad** (formerly **Rairi**, seized in **1656** and designated the imperial capital) sat atop an isolated, wedge-shaped basalt massif rising 820 meters above sea level, accessible only via a single narrow ridge fortified by the curved, twin-bastioned **Maha Darwaza**, topped by the **Balekilla** citadel and rock-cut water cisterns (**Ganga Sagar** lake) that rendered the summit hydrologically autonomous during prolonged sieges.
*   **The Treaty of Purandar (1665):** When the veteran **Mughal** commander **Mirza Raja Jai Singh I** launched a systematic siege campaign using heavy artillery and financial subversion against **Purandar** fort, **Shivaji** recognized the temporary vulnerability of static defenses against concentrated imperial resources. Under the **Treaty of Purandar (June 1665)**, **Shivaji** calibrated his strategic retreat through precise fiscal-territorial accounting: he surrendered **23 hill forts** yielding an annual revenue of **400,000 Hons** (gold pagodas) to the **Mughal** empire, while retaining **12 core forts** (including **Raigad**, **Torna**, and **Rajgad**) yielding **100,000 Hons**, alongside conditional rights to collect revenue from the **Adilshahi** **Konkan** and **Balaghat** districts in exchange for remittance of 40 lakh **Hons** in installments and a **Mansab** of 5,000 for his son **Sambhaji**.
*   **Littoral Bathymetry, Sindhudurg, and the Maratha Navy under Kanhoji Angre:** Recognizing that agrarian sovereignty in the **Konkan** required control over maritime customs and protection against the **Siddis of Janjira**, the **Portuguese**, and the **English East India Company**, **Shivaji** established a state-funded blue- and brown-water fleet (**1657–1659**). At **Sindhudurg (1664–1667)** off **Malvan**, he selected a low-lying rocky island surrounded by submerged basalt reefs that prevented deep-draft European men-of-war from approaching within cannon range. The zig-zag ramparts of **Sindhudurg** were anchored directly into the intertidal basalt bedrock using molten lead mixed with lime-mortar (**Chuna**). In the early 18th century, **Sarkhel** (Admiral) **Kanhoji Angre (1698–1729)** perfected littoral naval warfare from fortified island and estuary bases (**Kolaba**, **Vijaydurg** / **Gheriah**, and **Suvarnadurg**). **Kanhoji Angre** deployed shallow-draft, two-masted **Gurabs** (150–300 tons, mounting heavy prow-chaser guns) and swift, oar-and-sail **Galbats** (40–70 tons) that maneuvered inside shallow tidal creeks during land-sea breeze transitions, enforcing Maratha sovereign passes (**Dastaks**) and defeating joint **English-Portuguese** naval expeditions (such as the **1721** assault on **Kolaba**).

### 2. The 1674 Raigad Coronation, the Ashtapradhan Bureaucracy, and the Kathi Agrarian Settlement

Prior to **1674**, **Shivaji** occupied an ambiguous constitutional status within the **Deccan** legal order—technically an insurgent **Adilshahi** **Jagirdar**’s son and a rebellious **Mughal** **Zamindar**. Without formal Vedic consecration (**Rajyabhisheka**), his royal charters (**Sanads**), treaties, and revenue demands lacked juridical parity with sovereign Islamic sultans or Rajput **Rajas**, and entrenched Maratha clan chiefs (**Mores**, **Nimbalkars**, **Ghorpades**) viewed the **Bhonsle** lineage as mere social equals rather than a paramount monarch.

To resolve this institutional deficit, **Shivaji** staged his grand **Raigad Coronation (June 6, 1674)**, officiated by the renowned Banaras pandit **Gaga Bhatta**. By tracing the **Bhonsle** genealogy to the solar **Sisodia** **Kshatriya** lineage of **Mewar**, **Gaga Bhatta** performed the Vedic **Upanayana** and **Abhisheka** rituals, elevating **Shivaji** to **Chhatrapati** ("Lord of the Royal Umbrella"), initiating the sovereign **Rajyabhisheka Shak** calendar era, and legitimizing a new, centralized fiscal-bureaucratic apparatus.

*   **Numismatic Sovereignty and Epigraphic Idiom:** Coinciding with the **1674 Raigad coronation**, **Shivaji** established a royal mint at **Raigad** issuing gold **Shivarai Hons** (~2.7–2.8 grams, bearing the Nagari legend *Sri Raja Siva* on the obverse and *Chhatrapati* on the reverse) and copper **Shivarai** coins (~9–10 grams). Concurrently, he commissioned **Raghunath Pandit Hanmante** to compile the **Rajavyavaharakosha**, a Sanskrit lexicon that systematically purged Persian administrative terminology from state chancery records in favor of Sanskritized Marathi equivalents.
*   **The Ashtapradhan Council:** To prevent the emergence of autonomous baronial fiefdoms, **Shivaji** instituted the **Ashtapradhan**—an advisory council of eight central ministers appointed directly by the **Chhatrapati** on strict merit, paid exclusively via cash salaries from the treasury, and forbidden from holding hereditary office or territorial **Jagirs**. With the sole exception of the **Panditrao** and **Nyayadhish**, all ministers were required to command military campaigns:
    1.  **Peshwa (Mukhya Pradhan):** Prime Minister overseeing general administration, state seals, and royal correspondence.
    2.  **Amatya (Majumdar):** Finance Minister auditing all central and provincial income-expenditure accounts (**Prant** ledgers).
    3.  **Sachiv (Shurunavis):** Secretary of the royal secretariat insuring the textual accuracy and dispatch of royal charters (**Sanads**).
    4.  **Mantri (Waqia-Navis):** Minister of Intelligence, court chronicles, and household security.
    5.  **Senapati (Sar-i-Naubat):** Commander-in-Chief managing recruitment, training, and deployment of cavalry (**Paga**) and infantry.
    6.  **Sumant (Dabir):** Foreign Minister handling diplomatic relations with the **Mughals**, **Deccan Sultanates**, and European factories.
    7.  **Nyayadhish:** Chief Justice adjudicating civil and criminal appeals based on **Dharmashastra** and customary law.
    8.  **Panditrao (Danadhyaksha):** Minister of Religious Endowments, royal charities, and learned Brahmanical assemblies.
*   **Tripartite Fortress Checks and Balances:** Inside every hill fort, **Shivaji** abolished single-commander autonomy by dividing authority among three co-equal officers of distinct caste backgrounds: the **Havaldar** (a Maratha managing security and the opening/closing of fort gates), the **Sabnis** (a Brahmin managing the muster rolls and civil accounts), and the **Karkhanis** (a **Prabhu** / **Kayastha** managing commissariat grain stores, gunpowder magazines, and building repairs).
*   **Annaji Datto’s Kathi Land Revenue Settlement:** Within the core **Swarajya** territories, **Shivaji** systematically dismantled the fiscal stranglehold of hereditary rural intermediaries—the **Deshmukhs**, **Deshpandes**, **Patils**, and **Kulkarnis** (collectively **Watandars** / **Mirasdars**)—who historically extorted unregulated cesses from the peasantry while remitting minimal fixed tribute to the state. Guided by **Malik Ambar**’s precedent, **Sachiv Annaji Datto** executed a comprehensive cadastral survey (**1670–1678**) using a standardized measuring rod called the **Kathi** (5 cubits and 5 fists in length, roughly 8.25 feet). Twenty **Kathis** squared formed a **Bigha** (400 square **Kathis**), and 120 **Bighas** constituted a **Chavar**. Salaried state revenue clerks (**Karkuns**) under provincial **Subahdars** (**Mamladars**) and district **Tarfdars** (**Kamaishdars**) assessed crop yields directly from the cultivating peasant (**Kunbi**), fixing the state demand initially at **33%** and subsequently raising it to **40%** once miscellaneous petty cesses (**Pattis**) were consolidated. **Watandars** were stripped of their armed retainers, forbidden from building fortified **Gadhis**, and prohibited from collecting revenue directly from peasants, receiving instead a strictly audited fractional stipend from the state treasury.
*   **Predatory External Extraction (Chauth and Sardeshmukhi):** Because the rocky, rain-shadow soils of the **Swarajya** could not generate sufficient agrarian surplus to sustain a standing army of 40,000 cavalry (**Bargirs** equipped by the state and **Shiledars** supplying their own mounts) and 60,000 infantry, the Maratha state institutionalized external fiscal predation (**Mulukhgiri**) across surrounding **Mughlai** territories:
    *   **Chauth (25% of assessed revenue):** Originating from pre-existing protection tributes in **Daman** and **Ramnagar**, **Chauth** was levied as an external military tax guaranteeing immunity from Maratha cavalry raids.
    *   **Sardeshmukhi (an additional 10% surcharge):** Claimed by **Shivaji** in his legal capacity as the hereditary **Sardeshmukh** (paramount head of all **Deshmukhs**) of Maharashtra, which flowed directly into the monarch’s personal treasury.

### 3. The Peshwa Ascendancy, Saranjam Feudalization, and the Panipat Logistics Crisis

The prolonged **War of 27 Years (1680–1707)** against **Aurangzeb**’s massive **Mughal** expeditionary force shattered the centralized **Kathi** bureaucracy erected by **Shivaji**. Following the execution of **Chhatrapati Sambhaji (1689)** and the fall of **Raigad**, his successor **Rajaram** (retreating to **Gingee**) and Queen Regent **Tarabai** lacked a functioning central treasury to pay cash salaries. To sustain decentralized guerrilla resistance across the **Deccan**, **Rajaram** was compelled to abandon **Shivaji**’s prohibition on land assignments, granting Maratha cavalry commanders (**Sardars**) hereditary military fiefs (**Saranjams** / **Mokasas**) and the right to retain whatever **Chauth** they could independently extract from **Mughal** territories.

When **Aurangzeb**’s successors released **Sambhaji**’s son **Shahu (r. 1707–1749)** to ignite a Maratha civil war against **Tarabai** (who established a rival court at **Kolhapur**), **Shahu** lacked both military regiments and liquid capital. His ascent depended entirely on the financial and diplomatic engineering of **Balaji Vishwanath**, a **Chitpavan Brahmin** revenue official whom **Shahu** appointed **Peshwa** in **1713**.

*   **Balaji Vishwanath (Peshwa, 1713–1720) and the 1719 Mughal Settlement:** **Balaji Vishwanath** mobilized the credit networks of **Chitpavan** and **Deshastha** bankers (**Sahukars**) to finance **Shahu**’s cause and co-opted powerful **Sardars** (such as **Kanhoji Angre**). In **1719**, **Balaji Vishwanath** marched to Delhi and concluded a landmark treaty with the **Sayyid Brothers** (verified by **Emperor Rafi-ud-Darajat**), securing imperial **Mughal** **Farmans** recognizing **Shahu**’s **Swarajya** plus the legal right to collect **Chauth** and **Sardeshmukhi** across all **six Mughal Subas of the Deccan** (Khandesh, Berar, Gondwana, Hyderabad, Bidar, and Bijapur) in exchange for maintaining 15,000 cavalry for the Emperor and paying an annual tribute of 10 lakh rupees. To bind the fractious **Sardars** to the central court while checking their autonomy, **Balaji Vishwanath** partitioned the **Chauth** revenue stream into complex, overlapping accounting fractions: **25% (Babti)** went to the **Chhatrapati**; **6% (Sahotra)** to the **Pant Sachiv**; **3% (Nargaunda)** to the king's discretionary disposal; and the remaining **66% (Mokasa)** was assigned directly to the **Saranjamdar** military commanders to maintain their troops. Because revenue clerks appointed by the **Peshwa** audited the **Babti** inside every **Sardar**’s **Mokasa** district, the **Peshwa** at **Pune** became the indispensable fiscal clearinghouse of the empire.
*   **Baji Rao I (Peshwa, 1720–1740) and the Maratha Confederacy:** Succeeding his father at age twenty, **Baji Rao I** articulated the strategic doctrine of striking directly at the withered trunk of the **Mughal** empire so the provincial branches would fall of their own accord. Masterfully executing high-velocity **Ganimi Kava** cavalry campaigns, he outmaneuvered **Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah I** at the **Battle of Palkhed (1728)** and **Battle of Bhopal (1737)**, conquered **Malwa** and **Bundelkhand**, and raided the suburbs of **Delhi (1737)**. To counter the entrenched hostility of older **Deshastha** Maratha aristocratic clans (such as the **Dabhades** and **Senapati** factions, whom he defeated at **Dabhoi** in **1731**), **Baji Rao I** elevated a new generation of loyal, non-aristocratic commanders, assigning them vast hereditary **Saranjam** territories across conquered provinces. This policy birthed the pentarchic **Maratha Confederacy**:
    *   **The Peshwas at Pune:** Controlling the **Konkan-Desh** core and acting as the supreme executive head (institutionalized permanently via the **Sangola Agreement of 1750** following **Shahu**’s death).
    *   **The Scindias (Shindes) of Gwalior:** Founded by **Ranoji Scindia**, dominating **Malwa** and Rajasthan.
    *   **The Holkars of Indore:** Founded by **Malhar Rao Holkar** (from the pastoral **Dhangar** community), controlling western **Malwa** and trans-Chambal trade routes.
    *   **The Gaekwads of Baroda:** Founded by **Pilaji** and **Damaji Gaekwad**, extracting the commercial wealth of **Gujarat**.
    *   **The Bhonsles of Nagpur:** Founded by **Raghuji Bhonsle**, expanding eastward across **Berar**, **Gondwana**, **Odisha**, and raiding **Bengal**.
*   **Balaji Baji Rao / Nana Saheb (Peshwa, 1740–1761) and the Structural Contradictions of Expansion:** Under **Balaji Baji Rao**, Maratha light cavalry pushed the fiscal frontier to its maximum geographic limits—forcing **Alivardi Khan** of **Bengal** to cede **Odisha** and pay 12 lakh rupees in annual **Chauth (1751)**, assuming the formal protection of the **Mughal** throne via the **Ahadnama-i-Wazarat (1752)**, and planting Maratha garrisons at **Attock** and **Peshawar** in the **Punjab (1758)** under **Raghunath Rao**. However, this rapid territorial overextension contained fatal structural contradictions:
    *   *Military Hybridization Failure:* Under **Balaji Baji Rao** and his cousin **Sadashivrao Bhau**, the Maratha army abandoned the rapid, foraging mobility of **Shivaji** and **Baji Rao I**. Captivated by European-style regular infantry and heavy artillery commanded by the French-trained **Ibrahim Khan Gardi** (who deployed 8,000 disciplined **Telinga** musketeers and heavy field guns), the Marathas tethered their agile cavalry to slow-moving cannon parks and massive, non-combatant pilgrimage camps.
    *   *Diplomatic Alienation of Northern Agrarian Powers:* Because Maratha expansion in the **Indo-Gangetic Doab**, **Rajputana**, and **Punjab** operated as predatory **Chauth** extraction rather than institutional administrative integration, the Marathas alienated indigenous anti-Mughal forces. Heavy succession tributes extorted from **Jaipur**, **Marwar**, and the **Jat** ruler **Suraj Mal of Bharatpur** left the Marathas without a single reliable Hindu ally in North India, whereas the invading Afghan ruler **Ahmad Shah Abdali (Durrani)** successfully forged an ideological and strategic coalition with the **Rohilla Afghans** (**Najib-ud-Daulah** and **Hafiz Rahmat Khan**) and the **Nawab of Awadh (Shuja-ud-Daulah)**, securing the fertile grain-basket of the **Doab**.
*   **The Third Battle of Panipat (January 14, 1761):** Marching north in **1760** to expel **Ahmad Shah Abdali**, **Sadashivrao Bhau** and the young **Vishwasrao** encamped at **Panipat** with a sprawling, top-heavy host encumbered by tens of thousands of non-combatant pilgrims and camp followers. **Abdali** crossed the **Yamuna** at **Baghpat**, severing **Bhau**’s supply lines to **Delhi** and enclosing the Maratha camp in a 60-kilometer logistical siege. Once the Maratha horses and troops were starving and incapacitated by the bitter winter cold, **Bhau** launched a desperate frontal breakout on **January 14, 1761**. While **Ibrahim Khan Gardi**’s artillery initially shattered the **Rohilla** right wing, **Bhau**’s cavalry charged forward, masking **Gardi**’s static guns. **Abdali** countered by deploying 2,000 mobile camel-mounted swivel guns (**Zamburaks** / **Shahins**) that fired deadly grape-shot into the massed Maratha center, followed by a reserve strike of 10,000 fresh **Durrani** heavy cavalry. The tactical abandonment of the field by **Malhar Rao Holkar**, the death of **Vishwasrao** and **Sadashivrao Bhau**, and the slaughter of an estimated 40,000 to 70,000 Maratha soldiers and non-combatants shattered the **Peshwa**’s central authority (**Balaji Baji Rao** died of shock months later), permanently transforming the Maratha state into a loose, polycentric confederacy and clearing the geopolitical vacuum of North India for the **English East India Company**.

## II. The Ahom Paik State of the Brahmaputra Valley: Fluvial Thermodynamics, Non-Monetized Corvée, and Sacred Mound Architecture (1228–1826)

### 4. Brahmaputra Alluvial Reclamation and the Non-Monetized Paik-Khel Mobilization Grid

While the **Maratha Swarajya** adapted to the semi-arid, basaltic rain-shadow of the **Deccan**, the **Ahom Kingdom (1228–1826)** of the **Brahmaputra Valley** was engineered around the opposite ecological extreme: a hyper-humid, tectonically active alluvial trough flanked by the Eastern Himalayas, the Patkai hills, and the Meghalaya plateau. Founded in **1228** when the **Tai-Shan** prince **Sukaphaa** crossed the **Patkai** mountain passes from **Mong Mao** (Upper Burma/Yunnan) into the upper **Brahmaputra** basin (**Soumarpith**), the **Ahom** state introduced wet-rice hydraulic technology (**Sali** winter rice transplantation) into an ecology previously dominated by shifting **Jhum** cultivation and marshland foraging.

The **Brahmaputra** river system—fed by both Himalayan snowmelt and intense Southwest Monsoon precipitation exceeding 2,500 mm annually—subjected the valley floor to violent seasonal flooding, lateral channel avulsion, and dense jungle regrowth, while remaining geographically isolated from the silver-bullion trade networks of the Indo-Gangetic plain. Faced with high land-to-labor ratios, acute bullion scarcity, and an existential need for massive collective labor to build flood-control embankments (**Mathauris** / **Garhs**), the **Ahom** monarchy bypassed monetization altogether. Under King **Pratap Singha (r. 1603–1641)** and his chief administrator **Momai Tamuli Borbarua (1609)**, the state institutionalized a totalizing, non-monetized corvée labor and military mobilization apparatus: the **Paik** and **Khel** system.

*   **Anatomy of the Paik and Got Unit:** Every non-noble, non-servile adult male subject between the ages of **15 and 50** was registered as a **Paik**. Four **Paiks** (reduced to three **Paiks** per unit under King **Rajeswar Singha**, **r. 1751–1769**, due to demographic pressure and religious exemptions) were grouped into a foundational rotational squad called a **Got**—designated sequentially as the **Mul** (first), **Dewal** (second), and **Tewal** (third) **Paik**. At any given moment, one **Paik** from each **Got** served the state directly for four months (providing military service, building roads/embankments, dredging tanks, or manufacturing war materials), while the remaining two or three **Paiks** stayed in the village to cultivate the agricultural plots of the entire **Got**, thereby guaranteeing continuous agrarian production during wartime or public works campaigns without paying a single coin in wages.
*   **Agrarian Tenures (Ga-mati vs. Ubar-mati):** Because the crown claimed absolute proprietorship over all land and human labor, each **Paik** was allotted **two Puras** (approximately **2.66 acres**) of prime wet-rice land (**Rupit**) completely free of rent, known as **Ga-mati** ("body-land"), in direct exchange for his rotational corvée labor. A **Paik**’s hereditary homestead and garden plot (**Bari**) was exempt from state confiscation, and skilled artisans or higher-caste subjects (**Chamua Paiks**) could commute physical labor into specialized craft goods or cash tributes. Any land cultivated beyond the statutory **Ga-mati** allocation was classified as **Ubar-mati** (surplus land) and taxed at the low rate of one rupee per **Pura** per annum, reverting to the crown upon the **Paik**’s death or superannuation at age fifty.
*   **The Khel Functional Guild-Regiment Hierarchy:** For administrative and military command, **Paiks** were organized across village boundaries into functional corporate divisions called **Khels** (such as the **Nao-soliya Khel** of boat-builders, **Kharghariya Khel** of gunpowder makers, or **Dhenu-chocha Khel** of archers), restructured systematically by **Momai Tamuli Borbarua**. Each **Khel** operated under a strict decimal officer hierarchy: a **Bora** commanded 20 **Paiks**; a **Saikia** commanded 100; a **Hazarika** commanded 1,000; a **Rajkhowa** commanded 3,000; and a **Phukan** (or civil **Barua**) commanded 6,000 **Paiks**. At the apex stood the **Patra Mantris** (Great Council of State)—comprising the **Burhagohain**, **Borgohain**, and **Borpatrogohain**—alongside the two supreme executive and military viceroys: the **Borbarua** (governing Upper Assam east of Kaliabor) and the **Borphukan** (governing Lower Assam west of Kaliabor and defending the **Mughal** frontier from **Guwahati**). Nobles received no salaries; instead, they were granted temporary office lands (**Man-mati**) and personal quotas of attached **Paiks** (**Likchous**) that reverted to the state upon removal from office.
*   **Buranji Historical Chronicles:** Unlike the mythological-genealogical **Puranic** tradition of mainland India, the **Ahom** court maintained a rigorous, secular historiographical archive known as the **Buranjis** (literally, "a storehouse that teaches the ignorant"). Written initially in the **Tai-Ahom** language on strips of **Sanchi-pat** (cured bark of the *Aquilaria agallocha* / agarwood tree) by hereditary priestly scribes (**Deodhais**, **Bailungs**, and **Mohans**) and later in **Assamese** prose from the 16th century onward, the **Buranjis** recorded dated diplomatic correspondence (**Lekha**), military campaigns, cadastral censuses, omen interpretations, and administrative misconduct (such as the **Padshah Buranji**, **Kamarupar Buranji**, and **Tungkhungia Buranji**). Conducted under the strict state supervision of the **Likhakar Barua**, the **Buranjis** served as an institutional memory bank guiding the **Patra Mantris** in constitutional precedent and foreign policy.

### 5. Riverine Asymmetric Warfare at Saraighat and Sivasagar-Charaideo Monumentalism

The structural resilience of the **Paik-Khel** mobilization grid was tested to its limit during the 17th-century **Ahom-Mughal Wars**. In **1662**, **Aurangzeb**’s general **Mir Jumla** invaded Assam, capturing the capital **Garhgaon** during the dry winter season. However, the onset of the **Southwest Monsoon** transformed the **Brahmaputra** floodplain into a vast inland sea, severing **Mir Jumla**’s supply lines, spreading malaria and dysentery through the **Mughal** camp, and enabling **Ahom** **Khel** militias to encircle the invaders. Although King **Jayadhwaj Singha** agreed to the humiliating **Treaty of Ghilajharighat (January 1663)**—ceding **Guwahati** and paying a massive war indemnity—his successor **Chakradhwaj Singha (r. 1663–1670)** appointed the brilliant strategist **Lachit Borphukan** as Commander-in-Chief to reclaim Lower Assam.

*   **The Riverine Battle of Saraighat (March 1671):** After **Lachit Borphukan** recaptured **Guwahati** in **1667**, **Aurangzeb** dispatched the veteran Rajput general **Raja Ram Singh of Amber** at the head of 30,000 infantry, 18,000 cavalry, 15,000 archers from **Koch Bihar**, and a fleet of heavy river warships (**Koshas** and **Ghurabs**). Recognizing that **Ahom** infantry would be crushed by **Ram Singh**’s **Mughal-Rajput** heavy cavalry on open plains, **Lachit Borphukan** engineered a monumental geomorphic trap around **Guwahati**. Utilizing thousands of **Paiks** overnight (famously executing his own maternal uncle for delaying construction of the **Amjuri** embankment), **Lachit** linked the rocky hills flanking **Guwahati** with continuous mud-and-bamboo ramparts (**Garhs**) studded with anti-cavalry bamboo spikes (**Panji**), forcing **Ram Singh** out of the plains (following a planned disaster at the battle of **alaboi** in **1669**) and funneling the **Mughal** offensive directly into the narrow **1.2-kilometer riverine bottleneck of Saraighat** (flanked by **Kamakhya/Pandu** on the south bank and **Amingaon/Agiathuri** on the north bank). In **March 1671**, when the **Mughal** admiral **Munnawar Khan** launched a massive naval assault against the **Andharubali** sandbank while **Ahom** troops were demoralized, a critically ill **Lachit Borphukan** boarded his command vessel with seven boats, declaring that he would rather die in the river than abandon his king’s trust. **Ahom** **Bachari** war-boats—built from buoyant **Chama** (*Artocarpus chaplasha*) timber without iron nails (which rusted in humid conditions), capable of accommodating 60–80 oarsmen, and equipped with twin sterns that allowed instantaneous reversal in turbulent currents without turning around—converged from both banks, ramming the cumbersome **Mughal** vessels, decimating **Ram Singh**’s fleet, and permanently fixing the western boundary of the **Ahom** state at the **Manas River** (confirmed after the final **Battle of Itakhuli** in **1682**).
*   **Sivasagar Masonry Architecture (Rang Ghar and Talatal Ghar):** During the 18th century under the **Tungkhungia** royal line—specifically **Rudra Singha (r. 1696–1714)**, **Siva Singha (r. 1714–1744)**, **Pramatta Singha (r. 1744–1751)**, and **Rajeswar Singha (r. 1751–1769)**—the **Ahom** court relocated to **Rangpur** (modern **Sivasagar**) and transitioned from biodegradable bamboo-and-timber architecture to monumental burnt-brick masonry. Because the alluvial **Brahmaputra** valley lacked lime-bearing kankar nodules, **Ahom** royal engineers (**Changrung Phukan**) formulated an indigenous organic structural mortar called **Karhal**: a highly adhesive, seismic-resistant cement compounded from sticky rice (**Bora Chaul**), duck eggs (**Hanh-koni**), black gram (**Mati-mah**), snail-shell lime (**Chun**), brick dust, and resin from the **Bael** (*Aegle marmelos*) fruit, mixed with fish glue (**Karatoya** / **Rahu** fish extracts):
    *   **Rang Ghar (built in bamboo by Rudra Singha; rebuilt in brick by Pramatta Singha, 1744–1751):** Situated outside the **Rangpur** palace enclosure, the **Rang Ghar** is a two-story, oval-plan royal sports pavilion (measuring 27 meters by 11 meters) from which the **Swargadeo** (king) and nobility viewed buffalo fights, elephant combats, and **Bihu** festivities at the **Rupahi Pathar** arena. Its ground floor features a series of multifoil, cusped arches supporting an upper viewing gallery, capped by an inverted, boat-shaped parabolic roof adorned with sculpted stone crocodile (**Makara**) finials that channeled heavy monsoon rain rapidly off the structure.
    *   **Talatal Ghar (initiated by Rudra Singha, 1702; expanded in brick by Rajeswar Singha, 1751–1769):** Serving as the fortified military headquarters and royal secretariat at **Rangpur**, the **Talatal Ghar** was engineered as a seven-story palatial complex built along an east-west axis with octagonal corner shrines (**Puja Ghar**). Four stories rose above ground (**Kareng**), while **three subterranean stories** (**Talatal**) descended beneath the earth, housing emergency armories, provisions, and concealed escape tunnels leading toward the **Dikhow River** and **Garhgaon** palace (subsequently sealed during the colonial era).
*   **Royal Mound-Burials of the Charaideo Maidams:** Situated in the foothills of the **Patkai** range near **Sukaphaa**’s first capital at **Charaideo** (28 km east of **Sivasagar**), the **Charaideo Maidams** represent the sacred monumental necropolis of the **Tai-Ahom** royalty and high nobility (inscribed as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in July 2024**). Prior to the complete adoption of Hindu cremation rites under **Rajeswar Singha** and **Lakshmi Singha** in the late 18th century, deceased **Swargadeos** were entombed in intact timber coffins (**Rung-dang**, carved from **Uriam** wood) alongside royal regalia (**Hengdang** swords, ivory ornaments, silver vessels, and wooden furniture) inside hemispherical earthen tumuli known as **Maidams**. Constructed by specialized **Maidamiya Khels** under the **Changrung Phukan**, a **Maidam** comprises three distinct structural tiers:
    1.  **Subterranean Vault (Garbha):** One or more vaulted masonry chambers constructed from kiln-fired bricks bonded with **Karhal** mortar and reinforced by massive stone slabs and concentric brick arches to bear the immense overburden pressure.
    2.  **Earthen Hemispherical Mound:** Layers of compacted clay, boulders, and pebbles piled over the brick vault to form an artificial sacred hillock symbolizing the cosmic mountain (**Meru** / **Mung-Phi** celestial ascent), planted with grass to prevent monsoonal sheet erosion.
    3.  **Surface Superstructure (Chao-Chali and Garh):** An octagonal, open-arched brick pavilion (**Chao-Chali**) perched directly at the apex of the mound for annual ancestral offerings (**Me-Dam-Me-Phi**), while the base of the entire tumulus is enclosed by a low octagonal perimeter brick wall (**Garh**) pierced by an arched western gateway (**war-dwar**).

## III. Eighteenth-Century Courtly Decentralization and Regional Miniature Painting Economies

### 6. Centrifugal Patronage Migration and the Rajasthani Miniature Ateliers

The artistic efflorescence of the **Regional Miniature Painting Schools** across 17th- and 18th-century **Rajputana** and the **Western Himalayas** was the direct fiscal and demographic consequence of imperial **Mughal** decentralization. During the reigns of **Akbar**, **Jahangir**, and **Shah Jahan**, the imperial **Tasvir Khana** absorbed the subcontinent’s premier master painters (**Ustads**), financing costly mineral pigments (Badakhshan lapis lazuli, cinnabar, gold leaf, and **Peori** Indian yellow) through the surplus of the **Khalisa** treasury. However, two structural ruptures dismantled this centralized monopoly: first, **Aurangzeb**’s **1668** curtailment of courtly figurative painting and subsequent **27-year absence in the Deccan**, which dried up imperial stipends; and second, the sack of **Delhi** by **Nadir Shah (1739)** and **Ahmad Shah Abdali (1757)**, which triggered a mass flight of unemployed master artists (**Chitaras** / **Musawwirs**) from **Shahjahanabad** toward autonomous regional courts.

Concurrently, as **Rajput** rulers disengaged from costly imperial military expeditions in **Balkh**, **Qandahar**, and the **Deccan**, they retained a higher proportion of their hereditary **Watan Jagir** revenues and trans-regional caravan tolls internally. To assert sovereign cultural autonomy, validate their **Suryavanshi** / **Chandravanshi** lineages, and sacralize their rule through **Pushtimarg** and **Ramanandi** **Vaishnava** devotion, Rajput courts established state painting workshops (**Surat Khanas**), synthesizing refined **Mughal** technical naturalism with indigenous **Apabhramsa** flat color planes, rhythmic linearity, and **Bhakti** poetic aesthetics (**Ragamala**, **Baramasa**, **Gita Govinda**, and **Rasikapriya**).

*   **The Mewar School (Chawand and Udaipur) and Sahibdin:** Resisting complete **Mughal** political subordination longest among the Rajput houses, **Mewar** preserved the purest early synthesis of indigenous Western Indian manuscript vitality and **Sultanate** idioms. Following the **1605 Chawand Ragamala** painted by **Nisaradi** during the reign of **Amar Singh I**, the **Mewar** atelier reached its classical zenith at **Udaipur** under **Maharana Jagat Singh I (r. 1628–1652)** and his master Muslim artist **Sahibdin**. **Sahibdin** transformed the **Mewar** style by introducing vertical spatial registers, unmodulated patches of brilliant vermilion, saffron, and lapis blue, and passionate narrative compression across monumental royal commissions: the **1628 Ragamala**, the **Gita Govinda (1629)**, the **Rasikapriya**, the **Bhagavata Purana (1648)**, and the collaborative seven-volume **Jagat Singh Ramayana (1649–1653)** (co-executed with the scribe **Mahatma Hirananda** and painter **Manohar**). In the 18th century under **Sangram Singh II** and **Jagat Singh II**, **Mewar** painting shifted from mythological texts toward large-scale, documentary-style courtly reportage (**Tamasha** paintings) depicting royal hunts, polo matches, and seasonal festivals around the **Lake Palace (Jag Niwas)** with bird’s-eye cartographic precision.
*   **The Bundi and Kota Schools (Hadoti Clan):** Ruled by the **Hada Chauhan** branches who served as frontline **Mughal** **Mansabdars** since **Rao Surjan**’s submission in **1569**, **Bundi** and **Kota** absorbed **Mughal** illusionistic shading early while infusing it with the lush, sub-tropical ecology of the **Chambal River** valley.
    *   **Bundi:** Initiated by the **1591 Chunar Ragamala** (painted for **Rao Bhoj Singh**), 17th- and 18th-century **Bundi** paintings (flourishing under **Rao Chattar Sal** and **Rao Umed Singh**, featuring the celebrated **Chitrashala** murals at **Garh Palace**) are distinguished by reddish-brown female facial shading, receding architectural pavilions, turbulent night skies lit by golden lightning snakes, and dense groves of plantain, mango, and date palms teeming with waterfowl and peacocks.
    *   **Kota (separated from Bundi in 1631):** Under **Rao Ram Singh I**, **Maharao Bhim Singh**, and especially **Maharao Umed Singh I (r. 1770–1819)** alongside his regent **Zalim Singh**, the **Kota** atelier elevated the **royal tiger and lion hunt (Shikar)** into a supreme visual genre. **Kota** artists utilized calligraphic, double-curved brush lines (**Kalam**) and overlapping diagonals of dense bamboo, rocky crags, and scrub jungle to depict the visceral muscular tension of wild beasts locked in mortal combat alongside the ruler.
*   **The Marwar (Jodhpur) and Amber-Jaipur (Dhundhar) Schools:**
    *   **Marwar (Jodhpur):** Operating across the arid **Thar** desert and controlling the lucrative **Pali** trade corridor, **Marwar** progressed from early folk-inflected **Ragamalas (Pali, 1623)** to sophisticated court portraiture under **Jaswant Singh** and **Ajit Singh**, culminating in an exuberant stylistic revival under **Maharaja Man Singh (r. 1803–1843)**. Dedicated to the **Nath Sampradaya** (following **Jallandharnath**), **Man Singh**’s atelier produced hundreds of large-format folios of the **Nath Charita**, **Shiva Purana**, and **Dhola-Maru**, characterized by towering, spiral-pleated turbans (**Pagris**), elongated upturned eyes, dynamic equestrian portraits of **Rathore** nobles on prancing Marwari steeds, and swirling, rhythmic cloud bands.
    *   **Amber-Jaipur:** As the closest **Rajput** allies of the **Mughals**, the **Kachhwaha** rulers of **Amber** maintained a heavily Mughalized atelier until **Sawai Jai Singh II (r. 1699–1743)** founded the planned grid city of **Jaipur (1727)** and reorganized the royal **Suratkhana** into one of the thirty-six state departments (**Chhattis Karkhanas**). Under **Sawai Ishwari Singh** and **Sawai Pratap Singh (r. 1778–1803)**, master artists like **Sahibram** produced monumental **life-size portraits** on joined paper (using traced perforated **Charba** pounces) alongside the famous colossal canvas of the **Raslila**, enriched with heavy gold embossing, pearl inlays, and luminous greens.
*   **The Kishangarh School (Sawant Singh, Nihal Chand, and Bani Thani):** Carved out of **Marwar** in **1609** by **Kishan Singh**, the small principality of **Kishangarh** achieved aesthetic immortality between **1735 and 1757** through the spiritual and romantic collaboration of three figures: the prince-poet **Raja Sawant Singh (r. 1748–1764)**, who wrote devotional **Braj Bhasha** poetry under the pen-name **Nagari Das** inside the **Vallabhacharya Pushtimarg** tradition; his beloved singer and poetess **Vishnupriya**, immortalized as **Bani Thani** ("Lady of Fashion"); and the master Delhi-trained painter **Nihal Chand** (**Suryadhwaj**). Transmuting his passionate romance with **Bani Thani** into the divine union of **Radha and Krishna**, **Sawant Singh** directed **Nihal Chand** to create an idealized, hyper-mannerist facial archetype exemplified in the iconic portrait of **Bani Thani (c. 1750)**: an extraordinarily elongated facial profile with a high, sloping forehead, a sharp aquiline nose, an arched eyebrow like a taut bow, a deeply curved lotus-petal eye tinged with pink (**Khajjak** shape), a pointed chin, a translucent **Odhani** veil held delicately between tapered fingers, and a lock of spiral black hair resting against the cheek, set against panoramic backdrops of the white marble pavilions and vermilion sunset waters of **Gundalao Lake**.

### 7. Sub-Himalayan Pahari Painting Schools and Southern Gesso-and-Gold Traditions

As **Nadir Shah**’s invasion (**1739**) and subsequent **Afghan-Sikh-Maratha** wars disrupted the **Lahore-Delhi** imperial highway, trans-regional caravan trade (carrying Kashmir shawls, Tibetan musk, and Central Asian goods) diverted northward through the sheltered **Shivalik** foothill principalities (**Jammu**, **Basohli**, **Nurpur**, **Guler**, **Chamba**, and **Kangra**). Enriched by this sudden customs windfall and insulated by sub-Himalayan river valleys (**Ravi** and **Beas**), the **Pahari Rajput Rajas** absorbed migrant families of painters fleeing the plains, fusing local पहाड़ी folk vitality with the delicate linear naturalism of the **Muhammad Shah Rangila** era.

*   **The Basohli School:** Emerging in the late 17th century under **Raja Kirpal Pal (r. 1678–1695)** along the **Ravi River** in Jammu, **Basohli** represents the earliest and most fiercely expressive **Pahari** idiom. In the **1694–1695 Rasamanjari** series (based on **Bhanudatta**’s Sanskrit treatise on **Nayaka-Nayika** typology, painted by the master **Devida** of **Tarkhan** caste) and the **1730 Gita Govinda** (commissioned by the patroness **Manaku**), **Basohli** artists deployed scorching, flat backgrounds of mustard yellow and burnt orange, stylized geometric trees, high narrow horizons, and figures with receding foreheads and large, hypnotic lotus eyes. A diagnostic material marker of **Basohli** painting is the physical glueing of **iridescent green wing-cases of jewel beetles (Buprestidae)** directly onto the paper to simulate dazzling emerald jewelry alongside raised white paint dots simulating pearls.
*   **The Guler School and the Seu-Nainsukh Lineage:** Around **1740**, under **Raja Govardhan Chand (r. 1741–1773)** of **Guler**, the fierce expressionism of **Basohli** yielded to a quiet, naturalistic lyricism spearheaded by the legendary carpenter-painter family of **Pandit Seu** of **Guler** and his two brilliant sons, **Manaku** and **Nainsukh**. Having studied late **Mughal** court painting closely, **Nainsukh** (working extensively for **Raja Balwant Singh of Jasrota** before returning to the hills) revolutionized **Pahari** aesthetics by introducing unidealized psychological portraiture, soft pastel color harmonies, cool moonlight blues, and accurate receding spatial depth.
*   **The Kangra School under Maharaja Sansar Chand:** The **Pahari** tradition reached its supreme classical culmination at **Kangra** (**Tira Sujanpur** and **Alampur** along the **Beas River**) during the prosperous reign of the **Katoch** ruler **Maharaja Sansar Chand (r. 1775–1823)**. After capturing the ancient **Kangra Fort** in **1786**, **Sansar Chand** assembled a premier royal atelier led by the second generation of **Pandit Seu** and **Nainsukh**’s family—**Kushala**, **Kama**, **Gaudhu**, **Nikka**, **Ranjha**, and **Fattu** (alongside **Purkhu**). Translating intense **Vaishnava Bhakti** devotion into visual poetry across vast illustrated series of the **Bhagavata Purana**, **Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda**, **Bihari’s Sat Sai**, **Nala-Damayanti**, and **Keshavdas’s Baramasa**, the **Kangra** style is defined by the **"Kangra Kalam"**—a fluid, rhythmic, squirrel-hair brush line of unsurpassed delicacy. **Kangra** female figures (**Nayikas**) possess straight, continuous nose-to-forehead profiles (**Chashm-i-mishkin**),gazelle-like eyes, and translucent draperies silhouetted against blossoming **Basant** hillside landscapes, cascading mountain streams, and moonlit nights imbued with **Shringara Rasa**.
*   **Southern Gesso-and-Gold Traditions (Tanjore and Mysore):** In Peninsular India, the collapse of the **Vijayanagara Empire (1565)** drove Telugu-speaking **Raju** and **Naidu** painting guilds southward toward the **Nayaka** and **Maratha** courts of **Thanjavur** (ruled by **Venkoji**’s descendants, peaking under **Raja Serfoji II, r. 1798–1832**) and the **Wodeyar** court of **Mysore** (revived and systematized under **Krishnaraja Wodeyar III, r. 1799–1868**). Designed for dimly lit domestic **Puja** rooms and temple **Mandapas** where oil lamps flickered against metallic surfaces, both schools developed three-dimensional relief painting using **Gesso** and **Gold Leaf**:
    *   **Tanjore Painting (Palagai Padam):** Executed on seasoned **jackfruit** (*Artocarpus heterophyllus*) or teak wood planks pasted over with unbleached cotton cloth using tamarind seed glue (**Puli Kottai**), the artist builds high-relief sculptural contours (pillars, arches, crowns, and jewelry) using a dense **Gesso** paste compounded from unboiled limestone powder (**Sukkankallu**) and gum arabic. Over this raised **Gesso** base, heavy **22-karat gold foil** and cut glass, semi-precious **Kemp** stones, pearls, and diamonds are embedded. Iconographically, **Tanjore** paintings favor monumental, single iconic deities framed by architectural prabhavalis—most famously **Navaneetham Krishna** (chubby, butter-holding infant **Bala Krishna** painted in radiant white or blue) and **Rama Pattabhishekam**—against saturated vermilion, dark green, and indigo backgrounds.
    *   **Mysore Painting:** Executed primarily on paper or cloth pasted onto wood boards by traditional **Chitrakara** artists adhering to the **Sritattvanidhi** (an encyclopedic iconographic treatise compiled under **Krishnaraja Wodeyar III**), **Mysore** painting utilizes a much lower, more delicate **Gesso** relief (**Gada** or **Makki**, prepared from **white lead powder**, gamboge, and gum arabic) and thinner, matte gold leaf. Unlike the heavy gemstone encrustation and rotund figures of **Tanjore**, **Mysore** paintings emphasize intricate hair-line brushwork, muted earth tones, complex narrative scenes from the **Ramayana** and **Bhagavata**, and soft, realistic shading around the eyes and garments.

## IV. Divergence, Codification, and Fiscal Patronage of Classical Music and Performing Arts Lineages

### 8. North-South Musical Bifurcation: Hindustani Gharanas vs. the Carnatic Melakarta and Trinity

Although subcontinental classical music shared a unified ancient matrix rooted in **Bharata’s Natyashastra** and **Sarangadeva’s 13th-century Sangita Ratnakara**, the geopolitical and fiscal trajectory of the 14th to 18th centuries drove a permanent structural bifurcation between the northern **Hindustani** and southern **Carnatic** systems. In North India, the courtly patronage of the **Delhi Sultanate**, the **Mughal Imperium**, and successor **Nawabi** states (**Awadh**, **Rampur**, **Gwalior**) integrated Persian-Central Asian modal aesthetics (**Maqam**), courtly secular poetry (**Braj Bhasha** and **Urdu**), and elite salon performance (**Mehfil**), prioritizing prolonged, improvisational melodic exploration (**Alap** / **Badhat**) and vocal-timbral ornamentation. Conversely, in Peninsular India—insulated behind the **Vijayanagara**, **Nayaka**, and **Maratha** courts of the **Kaveri Delta**—musical patronage remained structurally anchored to the massive **Dravida** temple economy and **Bhakti** devotionalism, driving strict mathematical codification of scales and fixed, philosophical lyric compositions (**Kritis**).

#### The Hindustani Vocal Forms and the Gharana Lineage Economy

During the 15th to 17th centuries, the solemn, austere **Dhrupad** (structured in four formal sections: **Sthayi**, **Antara**, **Sanchari**, and **Abhog**, accompanied by the barrel-shaped **Pakhawaj** and classified into four **Vanis**—**Gauri**, **Khandar**, **Nauhar**, and **Dagar**) reigned supreme from **Raja Man Singh Tomar**’s court at **Gwalior** to **Akbar**’s court under **Miyan Tansen**. However, in the 18th century at the Delhi court of **Mughal Emperor Muhammad Shah Rangila (r. 1719–1748)**, the structural rigidity of **Dhrupad** was superseded by **Khayal** ("imagination"). Spearheaded by two court musicians—**Niamat Khan** (a descendant of **Tansen**’s daughter and master **Beenkar** who adopted the pen-name **Sadarang**) and his nephew **Firoz Khan (Adarang)**—**Khayal** fused a two-part composition (**Sthayi** and **Antara**) across a slow, meditative **Vilambit (Bada Khayal)** and a brisk **Drut (Chhota Khayal)** with virtuosic vocal runs (**Taans**), glides (**Meend**), and **Tabla** accompaniment.

As the Delhi court collapsed after **1739**, master **Kalawants** and **Qawwal Bachche** musicians migrated to regional princely capitals (**Gwalior**, **Rampur**, **Awadh**, **Jaipur**, **Baroda**, **Patiala**). To protect their proprietary compositions (**Bandishes**) and vocal techniques as scarce economic capital in a competitive patronage market, musical families organized into endogamous, hereditary guilds tied to specific urban centers known as **Gharanas** ("households"):

*   **Gwalior Gharana:** The oldest and foundational **Khayal Gharana**, patronized by the **Scindia** rulers (**Jankoji** and **Jayajirao Scindia**) and codified by **Nathan Pir Baksh** and his grandsons **Haddu Khan**, **Hassu Khan**, and **Natthu Khan**. It emphasizes open-throated voice projection (**Khula Gala**), strict adherence to the composition (**Asthai-Antara**) before improvisation, gravitational **Behlawa** (medium-tempo note expansion), and straight, wide-span **Sapat Taans**.
*   **Agra Gharana:** Tracing its lineage to **Haji Sujan Khan** (contemporary of **Tansen**) and consolidated in the 19th–20th centuries by **Ghagghe Khuda Baksh** and **Ustad Faiyaz Khan (Prem Piya)**, the **Agra Gharana** bridges **Dhrupad** and **Khayal**. Performers open with an extensive, rhythmic **Nom-Tom Alap** derived from **Dhrupad**, deploying a muscular, resonant voice and intricate **Bol-Bant** (rhythmic syncopation of lyric syllables against the **Tala**).
*   **Kirana Gharana:** Named after **Kairana** in Uttar Pradesh and founded by **Ustad Abdul Karim Khan** and **Ustad Abdul Wahid Khan** (originally **Sarangi** and **Been** instrumentalists), the **Kirana Gharana** prioritizes absolute microtonal pitch precision (**Swara-Sadhana** and **Shruti** intonation), a sweet, inward-focused vocal timbre, and an extremely slow, step-by-step architectural unfolding of the **Raga** (**Badhat**), de-emphasizing aggressive rhythmic gymnastics.
*   **Jaipur-Atrauli Gharana:** Founded in the late 19th century by **Ustad Alladiya Khan** (migrating from **Atrauli** to **Jaipur** and **Kolhapur**), who adapted his family’s **Dagar-vani Dhrupad** heritage after losing his voice's upper register. It is celebrated for executing rare, structurally complex compound and oblique **Ragas** (**Jod** and **Sankirna Ragas** like **Nat-Kamod** or **Basant-Bahar**), deploying intricate, spiraling **Vakra (zig-zag) Taans** glued strictly to the breath cycle and rhythmic **Laya**.
*   **Patiala Gharana:** Founded in the mid-19th century under the patronage of the **Maharajas of Patiala** by **Mian Kallu** and his sons **Ustad Ali Baksh Khan** and **Ustad Fateh Ali Khan** (**"Alia-Fattu"**), and later popularized by **Ustad Bade Ghulam Ali Khan**. Influenced by **Punjab** folk idioms and **Tappa**, **Patiala** style blends emotive lyricism with dazzling, lightning-fast **Ek-hara** and **Gamaka Taans** spanning three octaves alongside rich **Thumri** proficiency.
*   **Banaras Gharana and Semi-Classical Forms (Thumri and Tappa):** Centered in the sacred pilgrimage economy of **Varanasi** and the **Nawabi** court of **Lucknow** (under **Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, r. 1847–1856**, who composed under the pen-name **Akhtar Piya**), **Thumri** prioritized **Bhava** (emotional expression and **Shringara Rasa** erotic-devotional yearning) over strict **Raga** grammar, allowing the vocalist to weave across adjacent scales (**Mishra Ragas** like **Khamaj**, **Kafi**, **Bhairavi**) in the lighter **Bol-Banav** style of **Banaras** or the faster **Bandish-ki-Thumri** of **Lucknow**. Simultaneously, **Mian Ghulam Nabi Shori (Shori Mian)** at the **Awadh** court of **Nawab Asaf-ud-Daulah** transformed the guttural, undulating folk songs of **Punjabi** camel-drivers into the classical **Tappa**—a hyper-condensed vocal form characterized by continuous, rapid-fire looping **Zamzama** and **Gatkari** ornamentation.

#### The Carnatic System: Purandara Dasa, Venkatamakhin’s 72 Melakartas, and the Thanjavur Trinity

In South India, the pedagogical and mathematical architecture of **Carnatic Music** was forged through three sequential institutional breakthroughs culminating in the **Kaveri Delta**:

*   **Purandara Dasa (1484–1564):** Originally a wealthy diamond merchant (**Srinivasa Nayaka**) during the reign of **Krishnadevaraya** of **Vijayanagara** who renounced his wealth to join the **Haridasa** **Bhakti** movement (under **Vyasatirtha**’s **Dvaita** lineage), **Purandara Dasa** is revered as the **Sangeeta Pitamaha** ("Grandfather of Carnatic Music"). He standardized the foundational pedagogical curriculum still used today: selecting the symmetrically spaced double-harmonic scale **Mayamalavagowla** as the introductory teaching **Raga**, and designing graded vocal-finger exercises—**Sarali Varisai** (basic sequential scales), **Janta Varisai** (double-note exercises), **Alankaras** (exercises across the **Sapta Talas**), and simple melodic **Pillari Geethams**—alongside composing thousands of Kannada devotional songs (**Devaranamas** / **Padas** signed with the mudra **Purandara Vittala**) that established the tripartite structure of **Pallavi**, **Anupallavi**, and **Charanam**.
*   **Venkatamakhin and the 72 Melakarta Scheme (c. 1660):** Patronized by **Vijayaraghava Nayaka** of **Thanjavur** (where his father **Govinda Dikshitar** had served as chief minister), the musicologist **Venkatamakhin** composed the Sanskrit treatise **Chaturdandi Prakashika**. Applying rigorous combinatorics to the **12 semitones (Swarasthanas)** of the octave while keeping the tonic (**Shadja, Sa**) and fifth (**Panchama, Pa**) invariant, **Venkatamakhin** engineered the **72 Melakarta (Janaka / Parent) Raga Scheme**. By dividing the fourth note (**Madhyama**) into two halves—**36 Suddha Madhyama Ragas** (Natural 4th, **M1**) and **36 Prati Madhyama Ragas** (Sharp 4th, **M2**)—and permuting the six valid combinations of the 2nd and 3rd notes (**Rishabha-Gandhara**) against the six valid combinations of the 6th and 7th notes (**Dhaivata-Nishada**), he generated a complete, closed matrix of **12 Chakras** (6 Ragas each = 72). Later supplemented by **Govindacharya** in the **Sangraha Chudamani** using the **Katapayadi Sankhya** alphanumeric prefix formula (which allows a musician to calculate a Raga's exact serial number and scale intervals from the first two syllables of its name), this scheme enabled composers to invent entirely new **Janya (derivative) Ragas** purely through mathematical deduction.

*   **The 18th-Century Carnatic Trinity at Thanjavur:** Why did the three supreme composers of **Carnatic Music**—**Syama Sastri**, **Tyagaraja**, and **Muthuswami Dikshitar**—all emerge simultaneously within a single small town (**Tiruvarur**) inside the **Thanjavur** district during the late 18th century? The explanation is strictly fiscal and ecological: irrigated by the **Kallanai** and **Kaveri** distributary canals, the **Thanjavur** delta had been saturated for centuries by **Chola**, **Nayaka**, and **Maratha** kings (**Tulaja II** and **Serfoji II**) with hundreds of tax-exempt **Brahmadeya** and **Agrahara** villages, alongside rich **Devadana** temple endowments (such as the **Tyagarajaswami Temple** at **Tiruvarur**). This dense concentration of rent-free agrarian surplus liberated thousands of **Smarta** and **Vaishnava** Brahmin scholar-musician families from physical labor or commercial anxiety, creating an unparalleled intellectual ecosystem where the **Carnatic Trinity** elevated the **Kriti** (consisting of **Pallavi**, **Anupallavi**, and **Charanam**) to its classical pinnacle:
    1.  **Syama Sastri (1762–1827):** The eldest of the Trinity and hereditary priest of Goddess **Bangaru Kamakshi** at **Thanjavur**. Composing primarily in **Telugu** and **Sanskrit** using the mudra **Syama Krishna**, he produced fewer (~300) but rhythmically intricate compositions, specializing in the **Anandabhairavi** Raga, three monumental **Swarajatis** (in **Bhairavi**, **Yadukulakambhoji**, and **Todi**), and complex asymmetric time cycles (**Misra Chapu** [3+4] and **Viloma Chapu** [4+3] **Talas**).
    2.  **Tyagaraja (1767–1847):** A **Telugu Vaidiki Brahmin** residing at **Tiruvaiyaru** who famously rejected royal gifts and court appointments from **Raja Serfoji II** (*"Nidhi Chala Sukhama"*—"Does wealth bring happiness, or service at Rama's feet?"), living solely through **Unchavritti** (ritual grain alms) and complete devotion to **Lord Rama**. Composing nearly 700 extant **Kritis** in colloquial, melodic **Telugu** (plus two Sanskrit-Telugu operas: **Prahlada Bhakti Vijayam** and **Nowka Charitram**), **Tyagaraja** revolutionized the **Kriti** by introducing **Sangatis**—a series of progressive, built-in melodic-rhythmic variations on a single lyric phrase that systematically unpack the emotional (**Bhava**) and structural geometry of the **Raga**—culminating in his five monumental **Ghana Raga Pancharatna Kritis** (in **Nata**, **Gaula**, **Arabhi**, **Varali**, and **Sri**).
    3.  **Muthuswami Dikshitar (1775–1835):** Trained for five years in **Varanasi** under the ascetic **Chidambaranatha Yogi** (where he absorbed North Indian **Dhrupad** grandeur) and an accomplished master of the **24-fretted Thanjavur Veena**, **Dikshitar** composed exclusively in resonant, philosophical **Sanskrit** (using the mudra **Guruguha**, along with polyglot **Manipravalam** pieces). His ~450 **Kritis** are deliberate, majestic (**Vilambakala**) architectural monuments filled with continuous **Vainika (Veena-style) Gamakas** (glides), astronomical/Tantric **Sri Vidya** symbolism (such as the **Kamalamba Navavaranam** and **Navagraha Kritis**), and systematic compositions across all **72 Melakarta Ragas** (following **Venkatamakhin**’s older **Asampurna Mela** nomenclature), plus playful **Nottuswara Sahityas** setting Sanskrit lyrics to European military band melodies introduced at **Fort St. George** and **Thanjavur**.

### 9. Codification of the Eight Canonical Classical Dance Traditions and Martial Lineages

*   **1. Bharatanatyam (Tamil Nadu) and the Tanjore Quartet:** Evolving directly from the **Chola** and **Nayaka** temple **Devadasi** tradition (**Sadir** / **Dasi Attam**), the modern concert architecture of **Bharatanatyam** was codified in the early 19th century at the **Maratha court of Thanjavur** (under **Serfoji II** and **Shivaji II**) by four brothers known as the **Tanjore Quartet**—**Chinnayya**, **Ponnayya**, **Sivanandam**, and **Vadivelu** (disciples of **Muthuswami Dikshitar** belonging to the hereditary **Isai Vellalar / Nattuvanar** community). The **Tanjore Quartet** organized the performance into a progressive, diamond-shaped **Margam** ("path") balancing pure geometry and emotional **Bhakti**:
    1.  **Alarippu:** An invocatory rhythmic blossoming of the body’s joints (**Anga-Suddhi**) set to spoken syllables (**Sollukattu**).
    2.  **Jatiswaram:** Abstract **Nritta** weaving intricate footwork (**Adavus**) with musical notes (**Swaras**).
    3.  **Shabdam:** The introduction of narrative mime (**Abhinaya**) praising a deity or king.
    4.  **Varnam:** The monumental centerpiece combining complex rhythmic **Tirmanams** with profound emotional **Sanchari Bhava**.
    5.  **Padams and Javalis:** Slow, lyrical **Lasya** pieces exploring the nuances of **Nayika** love and longing.
    6.  **Tillana:** A dazzling, high-velocity rhythmic finale of sculptural poses (**Karanas**) culminating in a Mangalam.
    Biochemically, **Bharatanatyam** is anchored in the **Araimandi** (half-sitting plié with knees turned sharply outward), creating a series of interlocking equilateral triangles in space.
*   **2. Kathak (North India) and its Three Gharanas:** Originating from hereditary temple storytellers (**Kathakars** — *"Katha kahe so Kathak kahave"*) who narrated episodes from the **Ramayana** and **Bhagavata Purana** in the **Braj** belt, **Kathak** underwent a radical structural transformation during the 16th to 19th centuries when it migrated into the **Mughal** and **Nawabi** courts (**Darbars**). Performing on flat marble palace floors rather than temple sanctums, **Kathak** replaced the deep knee-bend (**Araimandi**) of southern dances with a **vertical, straight-legged stance**, shifting kinetic energy into rapid ankle-bell (**Ghungroo**) footwork (**Tatkar**), dazzling pirouettes (**Chakkars**) ending in razor-sharp sculptural freezes (**Thaat** / **Sam**), and rhythmic dialogues (**Jugalbandi**) with the **Tabla** or **Pakhawaj**. Three distinct **Gharanas** emerged based on regional patronage:
    *   **Lucknow Gharana:** Crystallized under the patronage of **Nawab Wajid Ali Shah of Awadh** by **Ishwari Prasad**, **Thakur Prasad**, and the ancestral lineage of **Bindadin Maharaj** and **Kalka Prasad**. It prioritizes refined, understated **Nazakat** (elegance), delicate eyebrow/wrist **Abhinaya** (**Gat-Bhava** and **Thumri** interpretation), and fluid torso torso movements (**Ang**).
    *   **Jaipur Gharana:** Founded by **Bhanuji** in the warrior courts of the **Kachhwaha Rajputs**, the **Jaipur Gharana** emphasizes fierce **Tandava** energy, complex mathematical cross-rhythms (**Layakari** and **Parans** drawn from **Pakhawaj** syllables), and long, athletic sequences of multiple spins (**Chakkars**).
    *   **Banaras Gharana:** Developed by **Janaki Prasad** in **Varanasi**, distinguished by the exclusive use of **Natwari Bols** (dance syllables distinct from **Tabla**/**Pakhawaj** syllables), spinning systematically on both the right and left heels, and frequent floor-kneeling postures invoking **Bhakti** narratives.
*   **3. Kathakali (Kerala), Ramanattam, and Kalaripayattu Biomechanics:** Emerging in the 17th-century feudal principalities of **Malabar** and **Travancore**, **Kathakali** synthesized ancient Sanskrit temple theatre (**Koodiyattam** and **Krishnanattam**, commissioned by the **Zamorin of Calicut**) with indigenous folk rituals (**Mudiyettu** and **Theyyam**) and the martial art **Kalaripayattu**. When the **Zamorin** refused to lend his **Krishnanattam** troupe to the **Raja of Kottarakkara**, the Raja created **Ramanattam** (performed in **Malayalam** rather than Sanskrit, eliminating masks in favor of facial paint), which evolved into **Kathakali** under the patronage of the **Raja of Vettathunadu** and **Kottayam Thampuran**. Because **Kathakali** is an all-night open-air dance-drama enacted exclusively by male dancer-actors without speaking a single word (accompanied by vocalists singing **Manipravalam** **Attakatha** verses and thunderous **Chenda**, **Maddalam**, **Chengila**, and **Ilathalam** percussion), its strenuous lower-body stance (standing on the outer edges of the feet in a wide rectangular **Mandala** squat) and high-flying leaps are trained through rigorous **Kalaripayattu** oil massages (**Chavitti Uzhichil**). Characters are classified by **Satvika, Rajasika, and Tamasika** Gunas via elaborate rice-paste and mineral facial makeup (**Chutti**):
    *   **Pacha (Green face with white Chutti ridge):** Noble, divine heroes (**Rama**, **Krishna**, **Arjuna**, **Nala**).
    *   **Kathi (Green with red upturned mustache "knife" and white pith knobs on nose/forehead):** Arrogant, royal anti-heroes possessing valor alongside evil (**Ravana**, **Duryodhana**, **Keechaka**).
    *   **Thadi (Beards):** **Chuvanna Thadi** (Red Beard) for vicious demons (**Dushasana**, **Baka**); **Vella Thadi** (White Beard) for divine/superhuman beings (**Hanuman**); **Karutha Thadi** (Black Beard) for forest hunters (**Kirata**).
    *   **Kari (Jet-black face):** Demonesses (**Surpanakha**, **Simhika**).
    *   **Minukku (Radiant yellow-orange tint):** Virtuous women, sages, and Brahmins.
*   **4. Mohiniyattam (Kerala / Travancore):** Literally the "Dance of the Enchantress (**Mohini**)," **Mohiniyattam** evolved as the solo female, exclusively **Lasya** counterpart to the hyper-masculine **Tandava** of **Kathakali** along the **Malabar** coast. Mentioned in the **1709** text **Vyavaharamala** by **Mazhamangalam Narayanan Namboodiri**, it was systematically codified in the 19th century at the **Travancore** court under the musician-king **Maharaja Swathi Thirunal (r. 1829–1846)** alongside **Vadivelu** (of the **Tanjore Quartet**), who integrated the **Bharatanatyam** **Margam** structure (**Cholkettu**, **Jatiswaram**, **Varnam**, **Padam**, **Tillana**) into the indigenous Kerala idiom. Dressed in the off-white and gold-bordered **Kasavu** sari with hair tied in a characteristic side-bun (**Kuduma**) adorned with jasmine, the dancer executes continuous, gentle figure-of-eight swaying movements of the torso (**Atibhanga** and **Andolika**) that visually mirror the swaying of coconut palms and gentle backwater waves of Kerala, accompanied by the lyrical **Sopana Sangeetham** vocal style and the hourglass-shaped **Idakka** drum.
*   **5. Kuchipudi (Andhra Pradesh):** Originating in the Brahmin **Agrahara** village of **Kuchipudi** (**Kuchelapuram**) in the **Krishna River** delta during the 17th century, **Kuchipudi** was systematized as a **Vaishnava Bhagavata Mela** dance-drama tradition by the ascetic **Siddhendra Yogi**, who composed the foundational dance-opera **Bhama Kalapam** (exploring the proud, possessive devotion of **Satyabhama** for **Lord Krishna**) and trained young Brahmin boys (**Bhagavathalu**) to perform it as an act of salvation. In **1678**, the last **Qutb Shahi** Sultan of **Golconda**, **Abul Hasan Tana Shah**, witnessed a performance and issued a copper-plate **Sanad** granting the **Kuchipudi** village permanently as a tax-free **Inam** to the dancing Brahmin families. Bridging dramatic **Natya** (where dancers historically spoke dialogues, such as **Pravesa Daru** self-introductions) and scintillating **Nritta**, **Kuchipudi** combines earthy, mercurial footwork with two iconic virtuosic feats: the **Tarangam** (drawn from **Narayana Tirtha’s Krishna Lila Tarangini**), in which the dancer executes complex rhythmic patterns while balancing her feet on the sharp rim of a **brass plate** with a pot of water or oil lamps balanced on the head; and **Jala Chitra Nrityam**, where the dancer draws an intricate ritual picture on the floor with her toes while dancing.
*   **6. Odissi (Odisha):** Rooted in the ancient **Odra-Magadhi** style described in the **Natyashastra** and visually documented across the 2nd-century BCE **Udayagiri-Khandagiri** Jain cave friezes (**Rani Gumpha**) and the 13th-century **Konark Sun Temple** **Nata Mandira**, **Odissi** evolved within the fiscal-ritual economy of the **Jagannath Temple at Puri**. It synthesized two parallel performance streams: the **Maharis** (temple **Devadasis** consecrated to **Lord Jagannath**, divided into **Bhitara Gauni** who danced in the inner sanctum during the nocturnal **Bada Singahara** ritual and **Bahara Gauni** who performed in the outer hall) and the **Gotipuas** (pre-pubescent boys dressed as females who emerged in the 16th century under **Vaishnava** influence to perform acrobatic **Bandha Nritya** outside the temple precincts). Accompanied by **Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda** and the barrel-shaped **Mardala** drum across a progressive repertoire (**Mangalacharan**, **Batu Nritya**, **Pallavi**, **Abhinaya**, and **Moksha**), **Odissi** is structurally defined by two foundational postures modeled directly on Kalinga temple sculpture: the **Chowk** (a symmetrical, masculine square stance with feet and arms forming right angles, mimicking the iconic wooden torso of **Lord Jagannath**) and the **Tribhanga** (a sinuous, feminine three-bend deflection of the head, torso, and hips).
*   **7. Sattriya (Assam):** Instituted in the late 15th and 16th centuries by the **Neo-Vaishnavite** reformer **Srimanta Sankaradeva (1449–1568)** and his chief disciple **Madhavadeva** to propagate **Ekasarana Dharma** across the multi-ethnic **Brahmaputra Valley**, **Sattriya** was preserved for five centuries as a living monastic ritual inside the celibate island and mainland monasteries (**Sattras**, especially on **Majuli Island**) by male monks known as **Bhokots**. Originally created as the choreographic medium for **Sankaradeva**’s one-act **Brajawali** vernacular plays (**Ankiya Nat**, narrated by the **Sutradhara**), **Sattriya** expanded into independent dance numbers such as **Chali** (graceful, peacock-inspired movements), **Jhumura**, **Nadu Bhangi**, and **Behar Nach**. Accompanied by devotional **Borgeets** (set to classical **Ragas**), the large terracotta/wood drum (**Khol**), and large brass cymbals (**Bhortal**), **Sattriya** adheres to strict canonical hand gestures (**Hasta Mudras** codified in **Shubhankara’s Hastamuktavali**), athletic leaps (**Chuta**), and distinct masculine (**Paurashik Bhangi**) and feminine (**Stri Bhangi**) kinetic vocabularies.
*   **8. Manipuri (Manipur), Bhagyachandra’s Ras Lila, Pung Cholom, and Thang-Ta:** Situated in the oval alluvial valley of **Imphal**, the performing arts of **Manipur** represent a profound synthesis of pre-Hindu **Meitei** animistic creation rituals (**Lai Haraoba**—the "Festivity of the Gods," enacted by **Maibas** and **Maibis** tracing the serpentine **Lairen Mathek** python curve matching the coils of the cosmic deity **Pakhangba**) and 18th-century **Gaudiya Vaishnavism**. Under King **Rajarshi Bhagyachandra (Ching-Thang Khomba, r. 1759–1798)**, who conceived the canonical **Manipuri Ras Lila** in **1779** following a visionary dream while in exile in Assam, the tradition was codified into five classical **Ras Lilas**: **Maharas** (performed on the full moon of Kartika), **Vasanta Ras** (Chaitra), **Kunja Ras** (Ashwin), **Nitya Ras**, and **Diba Ras** (daytime).
    *   *Biomechanical and Costume Signature:* Unlike every other Indian classical dance, **Manipuri Ras Lila** dancers wear only a pair of minimal ankle bells or none at all, never stamping the ground aggressively; instead, the feet touch the earth softly from toe to heel in continuous, floating figure-of-eight loops (**Chali**), while the **Gopis** wear the stiff, cylindrical barrel-skirt (**Kumil** / **Potloi**) decorated with mirrors and mica and a translucent white veil covering the face (**Koktumbi**), keeping the eyes downcast in humble **Bhakti** absorption (**Bhava**).
    *   *Pung Cholom and Thang-Ta Martial Lineage:* Balancing this ethereal feminine **Lasya** is the explosive **Tandava** energy of **Sankirtana** (**Nata Sankirtana**): **Pung Cholom** (where male dancers dressed in white dhotis and turbans execute acrobatic aerial leaps and spins while simultaneously playing the elongated **Pung** drum) and **Kartal Cholom** (dancing with large cymbals). Crucially, the entire physical grammar, footwork, and breath control of **Manipuri** dance share a single foundational biomechanical root with **Huyen Langlon**—the ancient **Meitei** martial art comprising **Thang-Ta** (armed combat with sword and spear) and **Sarit Sarak** (unarmed combat)—which trained every adult male under the **Meitei Lallup** corvée military system (structurally parallel to the **Ahom Paik** system) to defend the valley against Burmese invasions.

### 10. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Polycentric Regional Polities to the Mercantilist Fiscal-Military State

By **1761**, the structural trajectory of pre-colonial Indian history had reached a decisive institutional watershed. The decentralization of the **Mughal Imperium** had not plunged the subcontinent into an economic dark age; rather, it had redistributed agrarian revenue, commercial banking (**Jagat Seths**, **Chitpavan Sahukars**, **Chettiars**), and cultural patronage into vibrant regional capitals—from **Pune**, **Sivasagar**, **Jaipur**, and **Kangra** to **Lucknow**, **Thanjavur**, **Mysore**, and **Imphal**—generating the definitive codification of India’s regional miniature schools, **Hindustani** and **Carnatic** musical systems, and the eight classical dance traditions. However, this polycentric political order suffered from an insurmountable fiscal-military vulnerability. Relying on fragmented **Saranjam** levies (as exposed at the **Third Battle of Panipat in January 1761**), non-monetized **Paik** corvée labor, or unstandardized **Jagirdari** cavalry, indigenous states could not match the joint-stock corporate permanence, oceanic naval mastery, standardized flintlock-and-artillery firepower, and land-revenue annexation machine forged by the **English East India Company** after **Plassey (1757)** and **Buxar (1764)**. As the **Company Bahadur** seized the **Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha (1765)**—reversing the centuries-old global bullion sink by financing European industrial imports and sepoy regiments directly out of Indian peasant taxes—the subcontinent crossed the threshold into **Subject 2 (Modern Indian History)**, where indigenous agrarian, mercantile, and cultural institutions would confront the coercive architecture of British colonial capitalism.
