# Part 2: The Caste System (Varna and Jati): Structural Hierarchy, Untouchability, and the 2027 Caste Census

## I. Ontological & Theoretical Foundations of Caste: Scriptural Hierarchy vs. Material Power

### 1. The Epistemological Dualism: Scriptural Varna vs. Empirical Jati

The sociological deconstruction of social stratification in the Indian subcontinent begins with resolving a fundamental epistemological tension between the **Indological book-view**—anchored in classical Sanskritic normative texts—and the **empirical field-view** grounded in village-level ethnography. At the heart of this divergence lies the analytical distinction between **Varna** (literally "color" or ritual order) and **Jati** (derived from the Sanskrit root *jan*, meaning "to be born into," denoting an endogamous birth-status group). Early colonial Orientalists and classical Indologists frequently conflated the two, treating the thousands of empirically observable *Jatis* as mere degenerative fissures produced by inter-Varna unions (*anuloma* hypergamous and *pratiloma* hypogamous marriages) as codified in the *Dharmashastras* and *Manusmriti*. However, post-Independence empirical sociology led by **M.N. Srinivas**, **S.C. Dube**, and **André Béteille** demonstrated that while *Varna* operates as an ideological, pan-Indian cognitive meta-frame, *Jati* constitutes the concrete, operational unit of endogamy, commensality, occupational specialization, and local political mobilization.

The structural tension between these two categories reflects the interplay of competing forces in Indian society: ideological unification versus regional structural differentiation. While the four-fold *Varna* schema (**Brahmin**, **Kshatriya**, **Vaishya**, and **Shudra**, with the **Ati-Shudras** or **Panchamas** excluded outside the ritual four-fold chaturvarna) projects an immutable, subcontinental hierarchy, the empirical reality of over 3,000 major *Jatis* and tens of thousands of localized sub-castes exhibits profound regional variability and positional fluidity. As **M.N. Srinivas** articulated in *Varna and Caste* (1954), the *Varna* model distorts sociological understanding by presenting hierarchy as universally settled, whereas on the ground, particularly within the expansive middle strata of peasant, artisanal, and pastoral *Jatis*, rank is perpetually contested through material accumulation, political capture, and ritual emulation.

* **Scale and Spatial Architecture:** **Varna** is a macro-structural, pan-Indian ideological template comprising strictly four textual categories plus an exteriorized quinto-stratum (*Antyaja* / *Panchama*). Conversely, **Jati** is a micro-structural, regionally bounded social reality where an endogamous group’s ritual and secular rank is intelligible only within its specific linguistic-cultural region (e.g., **Vokkaligas** and **Lingayats** in Karnataka, **Kammas** and **Reddys** in Andhra Pradesh, or **Bhumihars** and **Kurmis** in Bihar).
* **Rigidity vs. Positional Fluidity:** The textual **Varna** hierarchy is normatively immutable; a *Kshatriya* category can never ascend above the *Brahmin* category in scriptural rank. In contrast, the **Jati** system permits historical and generational **positional mobility**, wherein an erstwhile lower or intermediate *Jati* acquires agrarian land, state patronage, or demographic dominance and successfully claims a higher *Varna* status through genealogical fabrication and ritual reform (**Sanskritization**).
* **Functional vs. Ideological Role:** **Jati** functions as the immediate boundary of social reproduction—governing marriage circles (*endogamy*), rules of food and water sharing (*commensality*), hereditary Jajmani service obligations, and local dispute adjudication (*Jati Panchayats*). **Varna**, despite its empirical imprecision, serves a crucial integrative cognitive function: it enables a *Jati* from one linguistic region to translate its approximate social standing when interacting with communities across regional boundaries, and provides an aspirational symbolic vocabulary for upward mobility.
* **The Asymmetrical Middle Zone:** In empirical regional hierarchies—particularly across South and East India—the classical middle *Varnas* (*Kshatriya* and *Vaishya*) are historically either absent or tenuous, leaving vast agrarian and commercial *Jatis* categorized scripturally as *Shudras* despite their overwhelming command over land, capital, and local state apparatus.

The dialectical relationship between *Varna* and *Jati* reveals that Indian social stratification is neither a frozen scriptural fossil nor a fluid class society. Rather, it operates as a dual-tiered system in which the ideological clarity of *Varna* legitimizes the overarching principle of graded hierarchy, while the cellular segmentation of *Jati* absorbs material and political shifts without rupturing the structural edifice of caste itself.

### 2. Classical Sociological Paradigms: Purity-Pollution, Repulsion, Dispersed Inequality, and Graded Subjugation

To explain how the caste system reproduces itself across millennia despite economic, political, and legal upheavals, sociological theory has coalesced around four competing analytical paradigms: the **structuralist-ideological model** (**Célestin Bouglé** and **Louis Dumont**), the **weberian triadic stratification model** (**André Béteille**), the **colonial-indological synthesis** (**G.S. Ghurye**), and the **subaltern materialist-emancipatory critique** (**B.R. Ambedkar**).

#### Célestin Bouglé and Louis Dumont: The Structuralist Paradigm of Purity and Pollution

In his seminal 1908 treatise *Essais sur le régime des castes*, French sociologist **Célestin Bouglé** isolated three interlocking structural principles that define the spirit of the caste regime: **hereditary specialization** (transmission of occupation by birth), **hierarchy** (differential ranking of groups), and **mutual repulsion** (segmentation through endogamy, commensal taboos, and avoidance of physical contact). Building directly upon Bouglé’s triad and Claude Lévi-Strauss’s structural anthropology, **Louis Dumont** published *Homo Hierarchicus: The Caste System and Its Implications* (1966), formulating the most influential ideological theory of caste. Dumont argued that traditional Indian civilization is governed by an overarching cognitive ideology of **holism and hierarchy** (*Homo Hierarchicus*), which stands in binary opposition to the modern Western ideology of **individualism and egalitarianism** (*Homo Aequalis*).

* **The Single Binary Opposition of Pure and Impure:** For **Dumont**, the entire architecture of caste rests upon a single fundamental ideological opposition: the **ritually pure** versus the **ritually impure**. Organic life processes—birth, menstruation, death, bodily emissions, and contact with carcasses or waste—generate ritual pollution (*ashaucha*). Because upper castes cannot remain ritually pure without someone absorbing their organic impurity, the system structurally requires specialized lower and "untouchable" castes (washermen, barbers, scavengers, leatherworkers) to permanently absorb ritual pollution on behalf of the superior strata.
* **Encompassment of Power (*Artha*) by Status (*Dharma*):** A cornerstone of Dumont’s thesis is the radical separation between **ritual status** (embodied by the **Brahmin**) and **secular politico-economic power** (embodied by the **Kshatriya** king or dominant landholder). In the Dumontian hierarchy, ritual status encompasses and subordinates secular power: the king exercises material dominion over the Brahmin, yet in the ideological totality, power remains inferior to priesthood (*status encompasses power*).
* **Empirical Critiques of Dumont:** Field-view sociologists (**M.N. Srinivas**, **André Béteille**, **Gerald Berreman**, and **Joan Mencher**) severely critiqued Dumont for constructing a **Brahmanical book-view** that mistakes elite priestly ideology for lived social reality. As Berreman and Mencher demonstrated from the vantage point of those at the bottom, caste is experienced not as a consensual, harmonious ritual holism, but as an institutionalized apparatus of material exploitation, agrarian servitude, and coercive violence where secular power routinely dictates ritual accommodation.

#### André Béteille: Caste, Class, and Power (From Cumulative to Dispersed Inequality)

Departing from both Dumont’s mono-causal ritual determinism and orthodox Marxist reductions of caste to mere economic class, **André Béteille** applied a **Weberian multidimensional stratification framework** (Class, Status, and Party/Power) in his classic ethnographic monograph *Caste, Class, and Power: Changing Patterns of Stratification in a Tanjore Village* (1965), based on intensive fieldwork in the multi-caste village of **Sripuram** in Tamil Nadu’s Cauvery delta. Béteille divided Sripuram’s spatial and social anatomy into three broad segments: the **Brahmins** (concentrated in the *Agraharam*), the **Non-Brahmins** (predominantly **Vellalar** and **Kallar** peasant castes residing in the *Kudiana* streets), and the **Adi-Dravidas** (**Pallar** and **Paraiyar** Dalit agricultural laborers segregated in the *Cheri*).

* **Cumulative Inequality (Traditional Baseline):** Historically in Sripuram, social stratification exhibited **cumulative inequality**, where ritual caste status, agrarian class ownership, and political authority converged within the same group. The **Mirasdar Brahmins** stood at the apex of ritual purity, owned the vast majority of fertile wet-paddy lands, monopolized western literacy, and commanded undisputed political hegemony over the village panchayat and local administration.
* **Dispersed Inequality (Modern Transformation):** Under the combined forces of post-Independence land reforms (abolition of intermediary *Inam* and *Mirasdari* tenures), the monetization of land markets, adult franchise, Panchayati Raj democratization, and anti-Brahmin Dravidian political mobilization, Sripuram transitioned toward **dispersed inequality**. While Brahmins retained top ritual status in the *Agraharam*, productive agricultural land increasingly passed to cultivating **Non-Brahmin** peasant castes, and formal political power in the village panchayat was captured by numerically preponderant Non-Brahmins in coalition with **Adi-Dravidas**. Thus, class and political power detached from ritual caste status, creating autonomous, albeit still overlapping, axes of stratification.

#### B.R. Ambedkar: Materialist Critique, Unnatural Division of Laborers, and Graded Inequality

Representing the **subaltern-emancipatory perspective**, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** dismantled both colonial racial theories and Gandhian/Indological defenses of *Varna* in two foundational texts: his 1916 Columbia University anthropological seminar paper *Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development* and his monumental 1936 undelivered address to the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal, *Annihilation of Caste*. Ambedkar identified **endogamy** (the superimposition of endogamy upon exogamous *Gotra* units) as the primary structural mechanism that generates and encloses caste, maintained through patriarchal technologies such as child marriage, enforced widow celibacy, and *Sati* to prevent surplus women and surplus men from marrying outside the caste boundary.

* **Division of Laborers vs. Division of Labor:** Against apologists who defended caste as a benign, functional division of labor, **Ambedkar** delivered a devastating materialist refutation: the caste system is **not merely a division of labor, it is an unnatural division of laborers** into watertight compartments. Unlike a rational economic division of labor founded on individual aptitude, choice, and market mobility, caste pre-assigns economic destiny by birth via the religious dogma of **predestination**, starving economic efficiency by binding workers to hereditary occupations toward which they feel social aversion rather than natural inclination.
* **The Architecture of Graded Inequality:** Ambedkar diagnosed why no general proletarian revolution had historically erupted in India through his concept of **graded inequality** (*Chaturvarna* hierarchy). In a simple binary system of inequality (capitalist vs. proletariat, or master vs. slave), the oppressed majority shares an identical structural position and readily unites to overthrow the ruling class. In contrast, caste arranges *Jatis* on an ascending scale of reverence and a descending scale of contempt. Every caste—except the Dalit at the absolute bottom—Simultaneously suffers subordination from castes above it while enjoying ritual privilege and material superiority over castes below it. Because each intermediate caste fears losing its relative privileges over those beneath it, graded inequality fractures subaltern solidarity and co-opts the oppressed into policing the very hierarchy that subjugates them.
* **Untouchability as Structural Exteriorization:** In *The Untouchables: Who Were They and Why They Became Untouchables?* (1948), Ambedkar conceptualized untouchability not as a mild extension of ritual hygiene, but as an institutionalized regime of socio-spatial apartheid (*Broken Men* historically defeated and forced outside the village settlement for refusing to abandon Buddhism and beef consumption) designed to extract coerced, unpaid labor while denying Dalits access to weapons, education, property, and civic dignity.

### 3. Colonial Governmentality and Census Codification: From Fluid Identities to Enumerated Fixity (1901–1931)

While pre-colonial Indian society indisputably possessed deeply entrenched hierarchies of *Varna*, *Jati*, and untouchability, historical sociologists and anthropologists—most notably **Bernard Cohn** (*An Anthropologist among the Historians*, 1987) and **Nicholas Dirks** (*Castes of Mind: Colonialism and the Making of Modern India*, 2001)—have demonstrated that the modern, bureaucratized, pan-Indian rigidity of caste was substantially forged in the crucible of British **colonial governmentality**. In pre-colonial regional polities, caste boundaries were mediated by royal sovereignty: kings conferred land grants (*Inams*), adjudicated *Jati* disputes, and elevated warrior or peasant lineages into *Kshatriya* status in exchange for military and fiscal loyalty. When the British East India Company and subsequently the Crown dismantled indigenous kingship, they severed caste from its political dynamic and re-anchored it within Orientalist textual codification (Anglo-Hindu law) and decennial **enumerative technologies**.

The decisive instrument of this transformation was the **Decennial Colonial Census**, initiated systematically in **1871–72** and reaching its apogee between **1901** and **1931**. Colonial administrators did not merely count pre-existing communities; by demanding that every colonial subject fit into a standardized, mutually exclusive, pan-Indian tabular grid ranked by scriptural precedence, the Census transformed fuzzy, localized, context-dependent identities into enumerated, bounded, and legally actionable political categories.

* **Herbert Risley and the 1901 Census "Warrant of Precedence":** Serving as Census Commissioner for the **1901 Census of India**, colonial ethnographer **Sir Herbert Hope Risley** combined pseudo-scientific **anthropometry** (nasal index measurements claiming high castes were of "Aryan" stock and lower castes of "Dravidian" or indigenous origin) with Brahmanical textual orthodoxy. Risley directed provincial superintendents to classify and list every *Jati* according to its exact rank in a formal **social precedence table** (*Varna* hierarchy) based on questions such as whether Brahmins accepted water from them or whether high-caste barbers and washermen served them.
* **Proliferation of Caste Sabhas and Petition Politics:** Risley’s 1901 warrant of precedence triggered an unprecedented subcontinental mobilization. Thousands of intermediate and lower *Jatis* immediately recognized that the colonial state’s Census report operated as an official record of collective honor and administrative entitlement. Across India, regional *Jatis* formed horizontally federated **Caste Sabhas** (e.g., Kurmi Kshatriya Mahasabha, Yadav Mahasabha, Vanniyakula Kshatriya Sangam, Kayastha Pathshala), hired Sanskrit pundits and colonial lawyers, and flooded the Census Commissioner’s office with thousands of petitions demanding reclassification as *Kshatriyas*, *Vaishyas*, or *Brahmins*.
* **J.H. Hutton and the 1931 Census Culmination:** By the time **J.H. Hutton** directed the **1931 Census of India**—the last comprehensive colonial enumeration of all castes—caste mobilization had merged with competitive electoral politics under the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (1919) and the impending Government of India Act (1935). The 1931 Census systematically enumerated the "Exterior Castes" (subsequently scheduled as **Scheduled Castes** via the Government of India [Scheduled Castes] Order, 1936), while generating the baseline demographic data for Other Backward Classes (**OBCs**, estimated at **52%** by the **Mandal Commission** in 1980 using extrapolated 1931 figures).
* **Administrative Institutionalization of Stereotypes:** Alongside Census enumeration, the colonial state enacted the **Criminal Tribes Act (1871)**, branding nomadic pastoralists, forest communities, and rebellious subaltern *Jatis* as "hereditary criminals by birth," and codified the doctrine of **Martial Races** post-1857, restricting military recruitment to select north-western agrarian castes (Jats, Rajputs, Sikhs, Gurkhas, Dogras) while excluding Dalit and eastern peasant soldiers who had previously formed the backbone of the Bengal Army.

Colonial governmentality thus executed a profound structural paradox: under the banner of modern rational enumeration, the British state froze the fluid, localized negotiations of pre-colonial *Jatis* into standardized bureaucratic categories, laying the institutional tracks for post-Independence identity politics and competitive affirmative action.

## II. Post-Independence Structural Transformations: Mobility, Politicization, and Market Dynamics

### 4. M.N. Srinivas and the Dynamics of Positional Mobility: Sanskritization, Westernization, Secularization, and Dominant Caste

Following Independence, **M.N. Srinivas** revolutionized Indian sociology by shifting inquiry from textual Indology to intensive participant-observation fieldwork in **Coorg** (*Religion and Society among the Coorgs of South India*, 1952) and **Rampura** village in Mysore (*The Remembered Village*, 1976). Rejecting the colonial cliché of the timeless, immobile Indian village, Srinivas formulated four foundational theoretical paradigms—**Sanskritization**, **Westernization**, **Secularization**, and **Dominant Caste**—to explain how *Jatis* negotiate ritual status, secular modernization, and local rural hegemony.

#### Sanskritization: Positional Mobility Within the Idiom of Caste

First conceptualized in his Coorg study (originally termed "Brahminization" before being broadened to **Sanskritization** in 1956), Srinivas defined **Sanskritization** as *"the process by which a 'low' Hindu caste, or tribal or other group, changes its customs, ritual, ideology, and way of life in the direction of a high, and frequently, 'twice-born' (dwija) caste."* Typically, an ambitious intermediate or lower *Jati* abandons meat-eating, liquor consumption, blood sacrifices, and widow remarriage, adopts vegetarianism, teetotalism, the sacred thread (*upanayana*), Sanskritic deity worship, and dowry hypergamy, and claims historical descent from *Kshatriya*, *Brahmin*, or *Vaishya* lineages.

* **Positional Change vs. Structural Change:** Crucially, Srinivas emphasized that Sanskritization results **only in positional changes within the system and does not lead to any structural change**. A particular *Jati* moves up a rung or two in the local hierarchy over a generation or two, but the overarching architecture of caste hierarchy, purity-pollution, and inequality remains completely intact—and is even ideologically reinforced by the aspirant caste’s validation of upper-caste norms.
* **Prerequisite of Secular Power and the Dalit Barrier:** Sanskritization is rarely effective in isolation; ritual claims succeed primarily when backed by prior acquisition of **economic wealth or political power** (e.g., the **Noniyas** of eastern UP acquiring wealth through saltpeter/brick-making, or the **Nadars** of Tamil Nadu ascending through palmyra trade and education). Furthermore, for **Dalits** positioned below the **pollution line**, Sanskritization historically failed to secure upper-caste recognition; when Dalits donned the sacred thread or adopted upper-caste wedding rituals, dominant castes frequently responded with violent social boycotts and retaliatory atrocities rather than ritual acceptance.
* **The Gender Cost of Sanskritization:** Feminist sociologists (**Leela Dube** and **Uma Chakravarti**) demonstrated that Sanskritization is deeply regressive for women. As lower and intermediate *Jatis* Sanskritize, women lose their traditional autonomy—including freedom of mobility in agricultural/artisanal labor, the right to divorce and widow remarriage (*Karewa* / *Nata*), and bride-price (*Shulka*)—and are subjected to *purdah* seclusion, rigid patrilocal subordination, and coercive dowry demands.

#### Westernization and Secularization

In *Social Change in Modern India* (1966), **Srinivas** paired Sanskritization with **Westernization**—defined as the changes brought about in Indian society and culture as a result of over 150 years of British rule, encompassing technology, modern bureaucracy, English education, constitutional law, and rational-humanitarian values—and **Secularization**, whereby spheres of social life previously regulated by ritual purity-pollution (public transit, urban restaurants, state employment, higher education) become progressively detached from religious and caste prescriptions.

* **The Two-Stage Mobility Sequence:** Srinivas observed an intriguing historical dialectic: while lower and intermediate castes aggressively pursued **Sanskritization** to elevate their local village standing, elite **twice-born castes** (especially **Brahmins**, **Kayasthas**, and **Baidyas**) utilized their traditional monopoly over literacy to pioneer **Westernization**—mastering English, entering the Indian Civil Service, law, medicine, and engineering, and migrating to metropolitan centers. Over time, therefore, Sanskritization became a preparatory stepping-stone toward Westernization for upward-moving peasant castes.
* **Compartmentalization ("Ritual Neutralization"):** As **Milton Singer** (*When a Great Tradition Modernizes*, 1972) and Srinivas noted, urban upper-caste professionals frequently practice **compartmentalization**: observing secular, egalitarian, Westernized norms in the public workplace (the office or factory), while reverting to orthodox commensal rules, domestic purity rituals, and strict caste endogamy within the private household sphere.

#### The Dominant Caste Paradigm

Introduced in his classic 1959 essay *The Dominant Caste in Rampura*, **Srinivas** demonstrated that day-to-day authority in rural India is wielded not necessarily by the ritually highest *Brahmin*, but by a locally **Dominant Caste**. For a caste to exercise dominance in a village or region, it must combine four empirical attributes (subsequently expanded with two modern attributes):

1. **Sizable Control over Cultivable Land:** Ownership of the bulk of local arable land, turning landless lower castes and artisans into economic dependents.
2. **Numerical Strength:** A substantial demographic share in the village or local electoral constituency, providing both voting clout under universal adult franchise and physical "muscle power" (*lathi*) during factional disputes.
3. **Relatively High Place in the Local Ritual Hierarchy:** Positioned above the line of abominable ritual pollution (typically middle peasant/Kshatriya-aspirant or clean *Sat-Shudra* status).
4. **Access to Western Education, Urban Administration, and Modern Occupations:** Presence of educated kinsmen in the state bureaucracy, police, judiciary, and cooperative banks, linking village dominance to state patronage networks.

Empirical examples of **Dominant Castes** identified by Srinivas and subsequent village ethnographers include the **Okkaligas (Vokkaligas)** and **Lingayats** in Karnataka, **Reddys** and **Kammas** in Andhra Pradesh/Telangana, **Marathas** in Maharashtra, **Patidars (Patels)** in Gujarat, **Jats** in Punjab, Haryana, and Western UP, **Rajputs**, **Bhumihars**, **Yadavs**, and **Kurmis** across the Gangetic plain, and **Vellalars** and **Vanniyars** in Tamil Nadu. Acting as local "kingpins," Dominant Castes adjudicate inter-caste disputes, enforce local norms upon subordinate Dalit agricultural laborers, and serve as vote-bank brokers connecting rural society to state-level political parties.

### 5. From Vertical Hierarchy to Horizontal Competition: Politicization of Caste and Discrete Identities

As post-Independence India institutionalized universal adult franchise, competitive multi-party democracy, land reforms, and monetized markets, the structural morphology of caste underwent a profound transformation: the erosion of vertical, ritualized interdependence and the rise of horizontal, political competition among autonomous identity blocs. Two landmark sociological frameworks capture this structural metamorphosis: **Rajni Kothari**’s theory of the **politicization of caste** and **Dipankar Gupta**’s paradigm of **discrete castes**.

#### Rajni Kothari: The Politicization of Caste (*Caste in Indian Politics*, 1970)

Challenging alarmist modernization theorists who lamented that caste was "corrupting" Indian democracy, political sociologist **Rajni Kothari** argued in his classic edited volume *Caste in Indian Politics* (1970) that the relationship between caste and democratic politics is neither unilateral nor pathological, but dialectical. Asking *"Is caste disappearing, or is it becoming stronger?"*, Kothari rejected the binary and demonstrated that **both caste and politics transform each other**: politicians needing organizational networks to mobilize mass electorates turned to ready-made primordial *Jati* networks, while *Jatis* seeking access to state resources, education, and employment reorganized themselves as modern political pressure groups.

* **Secularization of Caste:** For **Kothari**, politicization actually drives the **secularization of caste**—detaching *Jatis* from their traditional ritual purity-pollution moorings and reorienting them toward secular competition for power, public goods, and economic representation.
* **Kothari’s Three-Stage Evolutionary Trajectory:**
  1. **Stage I (Entrenched vs. Ascendant Castes):** Initially, electoral competition is restricted to the privileged strata. **Entrenched castes** (typically small, educated upper castes like Brahmins or Kayasthas holding traditional ritual and bureaucratic monopolies) are challenged by **ascendant castes** (numerically large, landowning intermediate peasant castes like Marathas, Reddys, Kammas, Jats, or Lingayats).
  2. **Stage II (Internal Factionalization and Multi-Caste Coalitions):** Once an ascendant Dominant Caste captures regional power, its internal unity fractures into competing **factions** led by rival political entrepreneurs. To defeat rival leaders within their own caste, faction leaders are forced to build **vertical and horizontal multi-caste coalitions**, recruiting lower backward castes, Dalits, and religious minorities into political alliances (e.g., the **KHAM** coalition of Kshatriyas, Harijans, Adivasis, and Muslims in Gujarat, or **AJGAR**—Ahir, Jat, Gurjar, Rajput—in North India).
  3. **Stage III (Differentiation and Weakening of Old Identities):** In the mature phase, cross-cutting socio-economic differentiation, education, and occupational diversification weaken monochromatic caste solidarity, producing complex, shifting political alignments where caste operates as one associational resource among many.

#### Dipankar Gupta: From Vertical Hierarchy to Horizontal Competition ("Discrete Castes")

In *Interrogating Caste: Understanding Hierarchy and Difference in Indian Society* (2000), **Dipankar Gupta** mounted a fundamental theoretical challenge to Louis Dumont’s notion of a single, consensual, continuous vertical hierarchy. Drawing on the breakdown of the rural *Jajmani* economy and empirical studies of caste origin myths, Gupta argued that caste has transitioned from a system of **vertical ritual hierarchy** into a system of **horizontal competition** among mutually autonomous **"discrete castes"** characterized by **hypersymbolism** and **difference** rather than consensual hierarchy.

* **Refutation of Consensual Single Hierarchy ("Muddled Hierarchies"):** Gupta demonstrated that Dumont’s "single true hierarchy" exists only in the imagination of the Brahmin at the top. In reality, India contains **multiple, competing hierarchies**—practically one for every *Jati*. Examination of lower-caste and Dalit origin myths (*Jati Puranas*) reveals that no lower caste accepts that it is intrinsically polluted by nature; instead, every low or untouchable caste attributes its degraded position to historic royal deceit, divine misunderstanding, or military defeat, while claiming an originally noble or *Kshatriya/Brahmin* origin.
* **Shift from Interdependent System to Discrete Castes:** Under the traditional rural *Jajmani* regime, castes were bound together in an asymmetric, village-level web of economic and ritual interdependence, forced by agrarian coercion to enact the dominant caste’s hierarchy. With the collapse of *Jajmani* patronage, agrarian fragmentation, and urban migration, *Jatis* have decoupled from mutual economic dependence. They now confront one another in the public sphere as **discrete, self-contained identity blocs**—freely forming horizontal alliances or engaging in fierce political and symbolic competition for state reservations, urban jobs, and cultural hegemony.

### 6. Subaltern Economic Assertion: Dalit Capitalism and the Market Question

While twentieth-century anti-caste movements focused primarily on constitutional reservations in public-sector employment and legislative capture, post-1991 economic liberalization and the shrinking share of state-sector jobs catalyzed a major ideological and sociological debate around **Dalit Capitalism**—the proposition that market entrepreneurship and capital accumulation can serve as an engine of Dalit emancipation. Institutionalized in **2005** through the founding of the **Dalit Indian Chamber of Commerce and Industry (DICCI)** in Pune by Dalit entrepreneur **Milind Kamble**, and theorized by public intellectual **Chandra Bhan Prasad** and Devesh Kapur (*Defying the Odds: The Rise of Dalit Entrepreneurs*, 2014), the Dalit Capitalism paradigm adopts the slogan *"Be Job Givers, Not Job Seekers"* and celebrates the emergence of a new cohort of Dalit multi-millionaire industrialists in manufacturing, construction, agro-processing, healthcare, and logistics.

* **The Market-Emancipation Thesis (Chandra Bhan Prasad & Milind Kamble):** Proponents argue that unlike the feudal village economy where transactions are mediated by birth status, capitalist commodity markets and modern financial instruments are structurally anonymous and "caste-neutral" (a consumer purchasing steel pipes or pharmaceutical formulations does not inquire into the ritual purity of the factory owner). By accumulating capital, employing workers across caste lines, and paying corporate taxes, Dalit entrepreneurs disrupt the deeply ingrained psychological hierarchy that equates Dalits exclusively with manual servitude. State initiatives such as the **Stand-Up India Scheme (2016)**, the **Venture Capital Fund for Scheduled Castes**, and the **4% public procurement mandate** from SC/ST-owned Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs) were instituted in response to DICCI advocacy.
* **The Critical Political-Economy Critique (Gopal Guru, Aseem Prakash, & Sukhadeo Thorat):** Conversely, critical political economists and anti-caste theorists (**Gopal Guru**, **Aseem Prakash** in *Dalit Capital: State, Markets and Civil Society in Urban India* [2015], and **Sukhadeo Thorat**) contend that Indian markets are **socially embedded in caste networks**, not neutral arenas of pure competition. Empirical field studies of Dalit businesses reveal systemic structural barriers at every node of capital accumulation:
  1. **Social Capital & Mercantile Guild Exclusion:** Traditional mercantile castes (**Banias**, **Marwaris**, **Chettiars**, **Patidars**, **Khatris**) control informal trade associations, rotating credit networks, and supply-chain distributorships through endogamous kinship trust, systematically excluding Dalit newcomers.
  2. **Institutional Credit Discrimination:** Formal banking institutions frequently deny or under-finance Dalit loan applications due to lack of historical land collateral and implicit caste bias among credit officers, forcing reliance on predatory informal interest rates.
  3. **Ghettoization in Low-Margin Sectors:** According to **Economic Census** and **MSME data**, although Scheduled Castes constitute ~16.6% of the population, SC-owned enterprises remain overwhelmingly concentrated in precarious, own-account micro-enterprises (petty retail, repairs, leather, sanitation contracting) with minimal hired labor, struggling to scale into corporate industry without state affirmative procurement.

## III. Constitutional Affirmative Action, Sub-Categorization Jurisprudence, and the 2027 Caste Census

### 7. The Architecture of Affirmative Action: Creamy Layer and the Rohini Commission on OBC Sub-Categorization

The Indian Constitution’s architecture of compensatory discrimination—anchored in **Articles 14, 15(4), 15(5), 16(4), 46, 330, 332, 340, 341, and 342**—represents the world’s largest institutional experiment in dismantling historical graded inequality through formal affirmative action. Yet, over seven decades of implementation, a profound sociological contradiction has emerged within the broad constitutional macro-categories of **Scheduled Castes (SCs, 15%)**, **Scheduled Tribes (STs, 7.5%)**, and **Other Backward Classes (OBCs, 27%)**: because these statutory schedules aggregate hundreds of distinct *Jatis* possessing vastly unequal baseline endowments of land, literacy, and political organization, the benefits of reservation have been disproportionately captured by a narrow tier of relatively advanced *Jatis*, leaving the most marginalized sub-castes structurally excluded.

To address intra-group asymmetry among the **Other Backward Classes** following the implementation of the **Mandal Commission Report** (1990), the nine-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court in ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992)** established two foundational constitutional doctrines alongside the **50% ceiling rule**: first, the mandatory exclusion of the socially and economically advanced **"Creamy Layer"** from OBC reservations (currently benchmarked at an annual parental non-agricultural/non-salaried income threshold of ₹8 lakh alongside Group A/B constitutional/administrative service criteria); and second, explicit judicial endorsement permitting the state to sub-categorize OBCs into "backward" and "more backward" strata.

#### The Justice G. Rohini Commission on OBC Sub-Categorization (2017–2023)

While 11 states and Union Territories (including Bihar, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, and West Bengal) had long operationalized state-level OBC sub-quotas (such as Bihar’s Karpoori Thakur formula dividing Backward Classes into **Annexure-I Extremely Backward Classes [EBCs]** and **Annexure-II Backward Classes [BCs]**), the Central List of OBCs remained a monolithic 27% bloc. In **October 2017**, the President of India constituted a four-member Commission under **Article 340** chaired by retired Delhi High Court Chief Justice **G. Rohini** to examine the empirical distribution of reservations across the ~2,633 caste entries in the Central OBC List and recommend a scientific mechanism for sub-categorization. Submitting its final report to the President in **July 2023** after nearly six years of quantitative analysis of central government recruitment and higher educational admissions, the Rohini Commission uncovered stark **asymmetric elite capture** within the OBC quota:

* **Empirical Findings of Extreme Concentration:** Analyzing over 1.3 lakh central government jobs granted under the OBC quota and nearly 1 lakh OBC admissions to central higher educational institutions (IITs, IIMs, NITs, AIIMS, Central Universities), the **Rohini Commission** found that:
  1. Just **10 dominant OBC caste communities** (approximately **1%** of all central OBC castes, predominantly landed agrarian or numerically powerful communities) cornered **24.95%**—nearly a full quarter—of all central OBC jobs and educational seats.
  2. A mere **37 caste communities** captured nearly **50%** of all OBC quota benefits, and **100 communities** accounted for over **75%** of total representation.
  3. At the bottom of the pyramid, **983 OBC communities (37% of the total list)** had **zero representation** across both central jobs and higher educational admissions, while another **994 communities** had a combined representation of only **2.68%**.
* **Proposed Four-Tier Sub-Categorization Formula:** To break this intra-OBC monopoly without breaching the overall **27% OBC quota**, the Commission proposed splitting the 2,633 central OBC castes into **four sub-categories** with earmarked sub-quotas of **2%, 6%, 9%, and 10%** respectively—allocating the 2% and 6% bands to the ~1,977 historically excluded artisanal, service, and nomadic EBC communities (who had zero or negligible representation), while restricting the 10% band to the ~100 dominant OBC communities that had previously captured three-fourths of the entire 27% quota.

### 8. Substantive Equality in the Supreme Court: The August 2024 *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* Verdict

While OBC sub-categorization enjoyed unambiguous constitutional backing since *Indra Sawhney* (1992), attempts by state governments to sub-classify **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** to protect the most marginalized Dalit sub-castes—such as **Mazhabi Sikhs** and **Balmikis (Valmikis)** in Punjab, **Madigas** in Andhra Pradesh/Telangana, and **Arundhatiyars** in Tamil Nadu—were struck down for two decades by the five-judge Supreme Court judgment in ***E.V. Chinnaiah v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (2004)**. In *Chinnaiah*, the Court had held that all castes notified in the Presidential List under **Article 341** constitute a single, homogeneous, indivisible "class of classes," and that any state legislation subdividing the SC quota amounted to unconstitutional tinkering with the Presidential List, a power reserved exclusively to Parliament under Article 341(2).

On **August 1, 2024**, in a historic **6:1 majority judgment** by a **7-Judge Constitution Bench** led by Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud (with Justices B.R. Gavai, Vikram Nath, Pankaj Mithal, Manoj Misra, and Satish Chandra Sharma concurring, and Justice Bela M. Trivedi dissenting), the Supreme Court in ***State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (2024)** overruled *E.V. Chinnaiah* (2004) and upheld the constitutional validity of **sub-classifying Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** for the purpose of providing earmarked sub-quotas to more backward communities within the SC/ST categories.

* **Sociological Rejection of the "Homogeneous Class" Fiction:** The 7-Judge Bench dismantled *Chinnaiah*’s legal fiction of SC homogeneity by anchoring constitutional interpretation in **empirical sociology** (explicitly engaging with Ambedkar’s concept of **graded inequality**). Writing for the majority, CJI Chandrachud held that **Article 341** merely creates a legal classification for the purpose of identifying castes that suffer from untouchability, not an empirical reality of internal uniformity. Systemic social and economic disparities exist *among* the Scheduled Castes themselves: sanitation workers, scavengers, and leatherworkers (**Balmikis**, **Mazhabi Sikhs**, **Madigas**, **Musahars**, **Arundhatiyars**) face far deeper structural destitution and social stigma—including intra-Dalit endogamy and commensal distance—than relatively better-represented SC sub-castes (such as **Chamar/Jatav/Ad-Dharmi** in Punjab/UP or **Malas** in Andhra Pradesh) that gained earlier access to colonial missionary/military education and post-Independence bureaucratic entry.
* **Substantive Equality over Formal Equality (Articles 14, 15, and 16):** The Court ruled that treating unequally situated Dalit sub-castes as identical under a single unreserved 15% SC pool reduces equality to a formalistic exercise where the most disadvantaged sub-castes cannot compete on a level playing field. Under **Articles 14, 15(4), and 16(4)**, **substantive equality** demands that the state recognize **degrees of backwardness** and design targeted sub-classifications so affirmative action reaches the "weakest of the weak." Furthermore, sub-classification by a state neither includes nor excludes any caste from the Presidential List under Article 341(2); every SC caste remains a Scheduled Caste, with the state merely rationalizing the internal distribution of quota benefits.
* **Rigorous Constitutional Guardrails Against Political Arbitrariness:** To prevent state governments from weaponizing sub-classification for electoral appeasement of numerically dominant Dalit sub-castes, the *Davinder Singh* judgment imposed strict constitutional parameters:
  1. **Empirical Quantifiable Data Mandate:** States cannot sub-classify based on political whim; they must collect rigorous, **quantifiable empirical data** proving both the **inadequacy of representation** and the **inter-se backwardness** of the sub-classified group across government services and cadre tiers.
  2. **Prohibition of 100% Exclusive Allocation:** A state cannot earmark 100% of the SC quota for a single sub-classified group, as doing so would de facto exclude all other Presidential List SCs and violate Article 341(2).
  3. **Judicial Review:** Every state sub-classification decision remains fully subject to constitutional judicial review under Articles 14 and 16.
* **The SC/ST "Creamy Layer" Concurrence Controversy:** In separate concurring opinions, **four of the seven judges** (led by **Justice B.R. Gavai**, alongside Justices Vikram Nath, Pankaj Mithal, and Satish Chandra Sharma) observed that the state must also evolve a policy to identify and exclude the **"Creamy Layer"** from Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, arguing that the children of an IAS/IPS officer or high constitutional functionary belonging to the SC/ST category do not suffer the same structural barriers as the child of a rural Dalit agricultural laborer studying in a panchayat school. While this observation remained *obiter dicta* (as the Creamy Layer issue was not directly referred to the 7-Judge Bench) and the Union Cabinet in August 2024 clarified that the Constitution contains no provision for a Creamy Layer in SC/ST reservations, the judicial debate highlighted emerging **intra-Dalit class stratification**.

### 9. Empirical Enumeration of the Social Matrix: The 2023 Bihar Caste Survey and the Digital Population Census 2027

For ninety-four years after the **1931 Colonial Census**, independent India maintained a deliberate policy of enumerating only **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** in its decennial Census, refusing to count the vast mosaic of intermediate and upper *Jatis* on the premise that official enumeration would perpetuate caste consciousness (a policy initiated by Home Minister Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and Census Commissioner R.A. Gopalaswami in the 1951 Census). Consequently, the entire architecture of post-1990 OBC affirmative action, welfare targeting, and judicial review under *Indra Sawhney* and *Davinder Singh* rested upon century-old extrapolations from 1931 data. The **Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC) of 2011**, conducted outside the statutory framework of the **Census Act, 1948** as an open-ended administrative survey without a standardized master list of castes, collapsed into methodological chaos—generating **46.7 lakh (4.6 million)** raw caste, sub-caste, surname, and *Gotra* entries due to spelling variations and unverified self-reporting, rendering its caste data unusable for constitutional policy.

#### The 2023 Bihar Caste-Based Survey (*Bihar Jaati Adharit Ganana*)

Breaking this nine-decade empirical vacuum, the Government of Bihar conducted a comprehensive, two-phase door-to-door **Caste-Based Survey** across all 38 districts and 13.07 crore residents, releasing its demographic findings on **October 2, 2023** and its detailed socio-economic report in **November 2023**. Using a pre-codified master schedule of **215 distinct caste codes** (plus a 216th "Others" code) mapped onto digital mobile applications, the Bihar survey provided the first rigorous modern empirical portrait of a major Indian state’s social stratification:

* **Demographic Redistribution:** The 2023 Bihar Survey revealed that **Other Backward Classes (OBCs)** collectively constitute **63.13%** of Bihar’s population—subdivided into **Extremely Backward Classes (EBCs / Annexure-I) at 36.01%** (comprising 112 predominantly artisanal, service, and Pasmanda Muslim communities) and **Backward Classes (BCs / Annexure-II) at 27.12%** (led by **Yadavs at 14.26%**, **Kushwahas/Koeris at 4.21%**, and **Kurmis at 2.87%**). **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** accounted for **19.65%** (up from ~15.9% in the 2011 Census), **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** stood at **1.68%**, and the **Unreserved / General Category** (including Hindu upper castes—Brahmins 3.65%, Rajputs 3.45%, Bhumihars 2.86%, Kayasthas 0.60%—and Ashraf Muslim groups) comprised just **15.52%**. Together, historically marginalized groups (EBC + BC + SC + ST) constituted **84.46%** of the state’s population.
* **Socio-Economic Correlation with Graded Hierarchy:** The November 2023 socio-economic tabulations confirmed a strong empirical persistence of **cumulative deprivation** at the bottom of the hierarchy: **34.13%** of all families in Bihar (approximately 94.4 lakh households) subsisted on a monthly family income of **₹6,000 or less**. Disaggregated by social group, poverty rates closely mirrored ritual rank: **42.93% of Scheduled Caste families** and **42.70% of Scheduled Tribe families** lived below the ₹6,000/month threshold, followed by **33.58% of EBCs** and **33.16% of BCs**, compared to **25.09% among General Category families** (though revealing significant intra-upper-caste poverty among Bhumihar [27.58%] and Brahmin [25.32%] smallholders). Similarly, graduate educational attainment stood at less than **1% among Musahars**, compared to **14.5%–15.5%** among upper castes, while government employment remained only **1.57%** of the state population overall.
* **Judicial Friction over the 50% Cap:** Based on these findings, the Bihar Legislature enacted two statutes in November 2023 raising caste-based reservations from 50% to **65%** (totaling **75%** including the 10% EWS quota). However, in **June 2024**, the **Patna High Court** struck down the 65% hike as violative of the *Indra Sawhney* 50% ceiling, holding that numerical population preponderance alone does not equate to inadequate representation in state services without a specific empirical study of service cadres—a constitutional battle currently pending before the Supreme Court.

#### The April 2025 Union Cabinet Approval of Statutory Caste Enumeration in the Digital Population Census 2027

Transforming the national landscape of social demography and affirmative action jurisprudence, the **Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs (CCPA)**, chaired by the Prime Minister on **April 30, 2025**, formally approved the inclusion of **comprehensive Caste Enumeration** in the forthcoming **Population Census 2027**—marking the first nationwide statutory enumeration of all castes by the Union Government since **1931**. Subsequently, on **December 12, 2025**, the Union Cabinet sanctioned a financial outlay of **₹11,718.24 crore** to execute **Census 2027** as India’s **first fully digital census**.

* **Statutory Backing and Institutional Architecture:** Unlike the flawed 2011 SECC (which was executed by the Ministries of Rural Development and Housing & Urban Poverty Alleviation without statutory Census Act protections), the **2027 Caste Enumeration** is conducted directly by the **Office of the Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India (ORGI)** under the statutory mandate of the **Census Act, 1948** and the amended **Census Rules, 1990** (updated in October 2026 to deploy ~30 lakh field functionaries, master trainers, and school teachers).
* **Two-Phase Digital Execution Timeline (2026–2027):**
  1. **Phase I (Houselisting and Housing Census - HLO):** Executed across a 30-day window in each State/UT between **April and September 2026**, establishing the digital Houselisting Block (HLB) web-mapping infrastructure and household asset baselines.
  2. **Phase II (Population Enumeration - PE & Caste Enumeration):** Scheduled across the country in **February 2027** (with snow-bound areas of Ladakh, Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand enumerated earlier in **September 2026**). During this phase, enumerators using secure Android/iOS mobile applications—alongside a citizen **online self-enumeration portal** monitored via the real-time **Census Management & Monitoring System (CMMS)** and stored in Critical Information Infrastructure (CII)-designated data centers—will capture electronic caste data using **closed-ended, pre-standardized master drop-down directories** of Central and State OBC, SC, and ST lists to prevent the 46-lakh phonetic fragmentation of 2011.
* **Structural & Constitutional Implications of Census 2027:**
  1. **Operationalizing *Davinder Singh* and the Rohini Formula:** Census 2027 provides the exact **quantifiable empirical denominator** required by the Supreme Court in *M. Nagaraj* (2006), *Jarnail Singh* (2018), and *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (2024) to legally defend SC/ST sub-classification and implement the **Rohini Commission**’s four-tier OBC sub-categorization.
  2. **Pressure on the *Indra Sawhney* 50% Ceiling:** If nationwide enumeration confirms that OBCs, SCs, and STs collectively exceed 80% of the national population while remaining under-represented in Group-A bureaucracy, judiciary, corporate boards, and higher academia, political and constitutional pressure to breach or constitutionally amend the **50% reservation ceiling** will intensify.
  3. **The Sociological Tension of Re-Reification:** From a critical sociological standpoint, just as Risley’s 1901 Census catalyzed *Jati Sabha* mobilization, the 2027 digital Caste Census carries the structural duality of **evidence-based social justice** versus **competitive identity solidification**, as micro-*Jatis* mobilize across states to prevent merger into broader umbrella categories and maximize their enumerated headcount prior to the post-2026 **Delimitation** and **Women’s Reservation (106th Amendment)** exercises.

## IV. Endogamous Reproduction, Patriarchal Honor, and the Judicial Dismantling of Institutional Casteism

### 10. Endogamy, Khap Panchayats, and Recent Supreme Court Interventions Against Structural Casteism (2024–2025)

While democratic politics, affirmative action, and urban labor markets have substantially altered the public, civic, and occupational dimensions of caste, the private reproductive core of the system—**endogamy**—exhibits extraordinary structural resilience. As **B.R. Ambedkar** diagnosed in 1916, endogamy is not merely a cultural preference; it is the biological and social reproduction engine of caste itself. Longitudinal empirical data from the **India Human Development Survey (IHDS)** and National Family Health Surveys reveal that **over 94% to 95% of all marriages in India remain strictly caste-endogamous**, with inter-caste marriages barely hovering around **5%**—a figure that has remained virtually stagnant over decades despite rising literacy and urbanization.

#### Digital Matrimonial-App Endogamy, Khap Panchayats, and *Shakti Vahini* (2018)

Rather than dissolving endogamy, modern digital technology and urban middle-class consumerism have frequently streamlined and reinforced it through **digital matrimonial-app endogamy**. Commercial matrimonial platforms (such as Shaadi.com, BharatMatrimony, Jeevansathi, and hundreds of dedicated *Jati*-specific portals like IyerMatrimony, AgarwalMatrimony, ReddyMatrimony, and JatMatrimony) deploy **algorithmic filtering architectures** that sort prospective spouses by exact *Varna*, *Jati*, sub-caste (*Pot-Jati*), *Gotra*, astrological *Kundali/Dosha*, vegetarianism, and parental net worth. While expanding the geographic search radius across global diasporic networks (enabling a Kanyakubja Brahmin or Kamma software engineer in Bengaluru to match with a sub-caste peer in Silicon Valley), algorithmic matchmaking preserves strict endogamous enclosure under the veneer of individual digital choice (**Glocalization of Endogamy**).

When young citizens transgress these endogamous or customary exogamous boundaries through self-choice (*Pratiloma* hypogamous or inter-caste/inter-Gotra unions), they frequently confront violent patriarchal retribution enforced by extra-constitutional caste councils—most prominently **Khap Panchayats** across Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, and the Delhi-NCR agrarian belt. Operating as traditional clan confederations (organized around single-clan *Gotra* units or multi-clan *Sarv-Khap* assemblies dominated by landed peasant castes, particularly **Jats**), Khap Panchayats enforce dual customary taboos: **strict caste endogamy** (forbidding marriage outside the *Jati*, especially with Dalits) and **village/Bhaichara exogamy** (treating all residents of the same or contiguous villages and all bearers of the same *Gotra* as agnatic siblings, even where the **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955** expressly permits such marriages beyond prescribed *Sapinda* degrees).

* **Sociology of "Honor Killings" (*Izzat* and Agrarian Crisis):** Sociological field studies (**Prem Chowdhry**, *Contentious Marriages, Eloping Couples*, 2007) demonstrate that Khap violence and **honor killings** are driven by three converging structural forces:
  1. **Control over Female Sexuality and Property:** Under the **Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005**, daughters possess equal coparcenary rights in ancestral agricultural land; a self-choice marriage outside the approved hypergamous/territorial network threatens to alienate fragmented family land to an outsider or lower caste.
  2. **Masculine Crisis of Dominant Peasant Castes:** Facing shrinking landholdings, agrarian distress, and a high deficit of marriageable women caused by skewed child sex ratios, unmarried dominant-caste youth and elderly lineage patriarchs use Khap decrees—including social boycotts (*huqqa-pani band*), public humiliation, forced divorces, and execution of eloping couples—to reassert waning patriarchal authority.
* ***Shakti Vahini v. Union of India* (2018) Jurisprudence:** In this landmark judgment, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court led by CJI Dipak Misra declared any assembly of a **Khap Panchayat** intended to scuttle the marriage of two consenting adults as **perse illegal and unconstitutional**. Holding that the right to choose a life partner is an intrinsic facet of fundamental personal liberty, dignity, and autonomy under **Articles 19 and 21**, the Court affirmed that collective caste or clan "honor" can never trump individual constitutional rights, and promulgated binding **Preventive, Remedial, and Punitive Guidelines** (mandating designated police nodal cells, safe houses for inter-caste couples, immediate FIR registration, and fast-track prosecution of Khap office-bearers).

#### The December 2025 Supreme Court Ruling on Matrilineal Caste Certificates

For decades, Indian administrative practice and presidential notifications (dating back to 1964 and 2002 Ministry of Home Affairs circulars) operated on an unstated **patrilineal default rule**: a child born to an inter-caste marriage automatically inherited the caste and domicile of the **father**, erasing the mother’s social identity even when the mother belonged to a Scheduled Caste and raised the child within her maternal Dalit community. While the Supreme Court in ***Rameshbhai Dabhai Naika v. State of Gujarat* (2012)** had clarified that paternal lineage is only a rebuttable presumption and that a child raised by an SC/ST mother amidst the social disabilities of her community could claim SC/ST status, revenue bureaucracy routinely rejected such applications on patriarchal grounds.

On **December 8, 2025**, a Supreme Court Bench comprising **Chief Justice Surya Kant** and **Justice Joymalya Bagchi** delivered a transformative intervention upholding a Madras High Court directive that ordered Puducherry revenue authorities to issue a **Scheduled Caste (SC) certificate** to a minor girl based on her **mother’s "Adi Dravida" Scheduled Caste lineage**, overriding her father’s non-SC status.

* **Factual & Sociological Matrix:** In the case, an **Adi Dravida (SC)** mother sought SC caste certificates for her three children so their education would not suffer. Crucially, following her inter-caste marriage, her non-SC husband had moved into and resided permanently at the mother’s parental home within the Adi Dravida community, meaning the children were socialized entirely within the maternal Dalit milieu.
* **Dismantling Patriarchal Lineage Defaults:** Questioning the automatic patrilineal transmission of caste identity from the Bench—*"With changing times, why can't a caste certificate be issued based on the mother's caste?"*—the Supreme Court recognized that caste in Indian society is not merely a biological Y-chromosome label transmitted by fathers, but a **lived social location** of upbringing, neighborhood environment, and structural disadvantage. While keeping the broader macro-constitutional question open for comprehensive adjudication, the December 2025 ruling struck at the intersection of **Brahmanical patriarchy** and **bureaucratic patrilineality**, affirming that state administrative records cannot erase maternal lineage where the child experiences the lived social reality of the mother’s Scheduled Caste community.

#### The October 2024 Supreme Court Judgment (*Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India*) Abolishing Casteism in State Prison Manuals

Demonstrating how colonial and Brahmanical purity-pollution hierarchies remained covertly embedded inside the coercive apparatus of the modern postcolonial state, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud (with Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra) delivered a landmark judgment on **October 3, 2024** in ***Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India* (2024)**, striking down **caste-based division of labor, residential segregation of barracks, and discriminatory treatment of Denotified Tribes (DNTs)** across the **State Prison Manuals** of over a dozen states (including Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh).

* **Empirical Anatomy of Institutionalized Prison Casteism:** Triggered by investigative journalism by **Sukanya Shantha**, the Supreme Court uncovered that state prison rules drafted on the template of the colonial **Prisons Act, 1894** explicitly replicated the *Varna/Jati* purity-pollution hierarchy inside state jails:
  1. **Purity-Pollution Division of Carceral Labor:** State manuals explicitly mandated that **cooking food** in prison kitchens must be performed exclusively by prisoners belonging to **"high castes" (Brahmins or high-caste Hindus)**, while **sweeping, cleaning latrines, sewage removal, cobbling, and barbering** must be assigned strictly to prisoners from **menial, "untouchable," or scavenger castes** (*Mehtar*, *Hari*, *Chandal*, *Chamar*).
  2. **Segregation of Barracks and "Thevaram" Yards:** Prison manuals in states like Tamil Nadu and West Bengal mandated physical segregation of prisoners by caste and religion to prevent ritual "pollution" of upper-caste convicts.
  3. **Colonial Stigmatization of Denotified Tribes (DNTs):** Several state manuals continued to classify members of **Denotified and Wandering Tribes** (formerly branded under the repealed colonial Criminal Tribes Act, 1871) as **"habitual offenders"** by default, subjecting them to harsher carceral surveillance and forced labor.
* **Constitutional Ratio Across Articles 14, 15, 17, 21, and 23:** The Supreme Court declared these provisions wholly unconstitutional across four interlocking fundamental rights pillars:
  1. **Article 14 & 15(1) (Anti-Stereotyping & Substantive Equality):** Assigning prison work based on caste stereotypes fails the intelligible differentia test; a prisoner’s caste has zero rational nexus with culinary skill or reformative rehabilitation.
  2. **Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability):** Prohibiting lower-caste prisoners from cooking and forcing Dalit prisoners to clean latrines is a textbook manifestation of **untouchability** and ritual purity-pollution practiced directly by the State.
  3. **Article 21 (Right to Dignity):** Carceral incarceration deprives a convict of physical movement, not human dignity; forcing marginalized castes into degrading sanitary labor assaults their core constitutional personhood.
  4. **Article 23 (Prohibition of Forced Labor / *Begar*):** Coercing prisoners from marginalized castes to perform unpaid or degraded menial labor strictly by virtue of their birth identity constitutes unconstitutional **forced labor**.
* **Binding Systemic Directives:** The Court ordered all States and Union Territories to purge every reference to caste in work allocation and barracks segregation from their Prison Manuals within three months, abolished the "caste column" and any caste markers in convict/undertrial registers, directed the Union Government to similarly scrub the **Model Prison Manual, 2016** and the **Model Prisons and Correctional Services Act, 2023**, and took *suo motu* cognizance (*In Re: Discrimination Inside Prisons in India*) to monitor nationwide compliance.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 3

The sociological trajectory examined across Part 2—from the scriptural-empirical dualism of *Varna* and *Jati*, Dumont’s purity-pollution binary, Béteille’s shift from cumulative to dispersed inequality, and Ambedkar’s graded inequality, through Srinivas’s Dominant Caste mobility, Kothari’s politicization, and Gupta’s discrete castes, down to the *Davinder Singh* sub-classification verdict, the *Sukanya Shantha* prison manual judgment, and the statutory **2027 Caste Census**—demonstrates that caste in India is not a vanishing archaic survival, but an adaptive structural force. Crucially, however, the gravitational pull of subcontinental hierarchy does not halt at the theological boundaries of Hinduism. Despite the scriptural egalitarianism of Islam, Christianity, Sikhism, and Buddhism, historical conversions and shared agrarian-occupational ecologies reproduced entrenched endogamous hierarchies within India’s non-Hindu communities.
