# Part 7: Tribal Societies (Adivasis): Continuum Debates, Development-Induced Displacement, and Indigenous Assertions

## I. Demographic Cartography, Internal Kinship Architecture, and Subaltern Vulnerability (PVTGs & DNTs)

### 1. Epistemological Framing and Spatial-Demographic Cartography of India’s 705+ Scheduled Tribes

In Indian sociology and social anthropology, the category of "Tribe" represents an epistemological battleground between colonial administrative ethnography, Indological civilizational models, and subaltern indigenous assertions. In classical Sanskritic texts, forest-dwelling and frontier communities were referred to as ***Jana*** (autonomous kinship-based folk) in contrast to ***Jati*** (endogamous occupational castes embedded within the hierarchical Varna order). British colonial administrators and census commissioners (from **W.W. Hunter** and **Herbert Risley** to **J.H. Hutton**) codified "Tribe" as an administrative-evolutionary category denoting isolated, animistic, pre-literate communities operating outside the ritual purity-pollution matrix of Brahmanical Hinduism. In the post-colonial constitutional order, **"Scheduled Tribes" (STs)** emerged strictly as a politico-administrative category notified by the President of India under **Article 342 of the Constitution**, guided by the informal **Lokur Committee (1965)** criteria: primitive traits, distinctive culture, geographical isolation, shyness of contact with the community at large, and general socio-economic backwardness. Sociologically, however, tribal movements and scholars (such as **Virginius Xaxa** and **Jaipal Singh Munda**) assert the vernacular identity of ***Adivasi*** ("Original Inhabitants" or *Moolnivasi*), which transforms a passive welfare category into a historic-political claim to autochthonous sovereignty over territorial commons (***Jal-Jungle-Jameen***). By contrast, right-wing assimilationist discourses deploy the term ***Vanvasi*** ("Forest Dwellers") to deny pre-Aryan autochthony and incorporate tribes into the broader Sanskritic fold.

Empirically, according to the **2011 Census** (and projected demographic baselines into **2026–2027**), India’s Scheduled Tribe population exceeds **104.5 million (8.6% of the national population)**, distributed across **more than 705 notified Scheduled Tribes** with immense linguistic diversity spanning the **Austroasiatic (Munda and Khasi-Aslian)**, **Dravidian**, **Tibeto-Burman (Sino-Tibetan)**, **Indo-Aryan**, and **Andamanese** language families. Unlike Scheduled Castes—who are spatially dispersed across villages throughout the subcontinent because their labor was structurally integrated into the agrarian **Jajmani** system—Scheduled Tribes exhibit extreme **spatial concentration** in ecologically fragile, resource-rich hilly, forested, and frontier terrains. Sociologist **B.K. Roy Burman** and geographer **Aijazuddin Ahmad** classify this demographic cartography into three macro-ecological zones: the **Central Indian Heartland**, the **North-Eastern Frontier Polities**, and the **Insular and Southern Enclaves**.

#### The Bifurcated Spatial Geographies: Central Indian Heartland vs. North-Eastern Frontier vs. Insular Archipelagos

* **The Central Indian Heartland (The Gondwana–Chotanagpur–Vindhyan Belt):** Stretching from Gujarat and southern Rajasthan across Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, and West Bengal, this belt houses **nearly 85% of India’s total ST population**, yet tribes constitute a **demographic minority (10% to 31%)** within each state, rendering them politically subordinate to dominant regional non-tribal castes (*Dikus*).
  * **Bhils:** Accounting for the single largest tribal population in India (~17 million, 37.7% of Rajasthan’s and 21%+ of Madhya Pradesh’s ST population), the **Bhils** speak Indo-Aryan Bhili dialects across the Aravalli–Vindhya–Satpura tri-junction. Historically skilled archers and forest rulers who negotiated tribute compacts with Rajput principalities, they transitioned into settled marginal cultivators (*Pal* settlements) facing severe seasonal distress migration into Gujarat’s urban construction and cotton markets.
  * **Gonds:** The second-largest tribal group (~13 million), speaking Dravidian **Gondi** dialects across the historical **Gondwana** region (Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, eastern Maharashtra, northern Telangana, and western Odisha). Historically builders of medieval kingdoms (Garha-Mandla, Deogarh, Chanda), the Gonds display profound internal stratification—ranging from landed **Raj Gonds** (who underwent **Surajit Sinha**’s Rajputization process) to forest-dwelling **Muria** and **Maria Gonds** of Bastar.
  * **Santhals:** The third-largest tribal group (~7.4 million), concentrated in the **Santhal Parganas** of Jharkhand, West Bengal (Jangalmahal), Bihar, and northern Odisha, speaking **Santhali** (an Austroasiatic Munda language included in the **Eighth Schedule** via the 92nd Constitutional Amendment, 2003). Governed by the traditional ***Manjhi-Paragana*** council system and famous for the anti-colonial ***Santal Hul* (1855–56)** led by **Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu** against colonial revenue permanent settlement and usurious *Mahajans*, Santhals pioneered the **Ol Chiki** script created by **Pandit Raghunath Murmu (1925)**.
  * **Oraons (Kurukh):** A major Dravidian-speaking agricultural tribe of the Chotanagpur Plateau (Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, and tea-garden diaspora in Assam/Dooars). Organized around exogamous totemic clans (*Gotar*) and a federated inter-village judicial confederacy known as the ***Parha* system** headed by a *Parha Raja*, the Oraons were at the vanguard of the socio-religious **Tana Bhagat Movement (1914)** led by **Jatra Bhagat** and remain central to contemporary **Sarna religious code** assertions.
  * **Mundas:** An Austroasiatic-speaking community of Chotanagpur whose social organization is anchored in the ***Khuntkatti* system**—customary communal land tenure wherein descendants of the original clan clearers of the primeval forest (*Khuntkattidars*) jointly own village land and ancestral burial stones (***Sasandiri***). The violent erosion of *Khuntkatti* tenure by British courts and *Jagirdars* catalyzed the messianic ***Ulgulan* ("Great Tumult", 1899–1900)** led by **Birsa Munda**, forcing the colonial state to enact the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908)**.
* **The North-Eastern Frontier Polities (The Indo-Burman & Himalayan Hills):** Comprising around **12% of India’s ST population**, the North-East exhibits an inverse demographic and political structure compared to Central India: tribes constitute an **overwhelming demographic majority** in **Mizoram (94.4%)**, **Nagaland (86.5%)**, **Meghalaya (86.1%)**, and **Arunachal Pradesh (68.8%)**, and hold protected autonomous enclaves in Assam, Manipur, and Tripura. Because British colonial administration isolated these hills via the **Inner Line Regulation (Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873)** and "Excluded/Partially Excluded Areas" under the Government of India Act 1935, North-Eastern tribes escaped large-scale peasant land alienation by plains castes, experienced rapid missionary-led English literacy, and negotiated asymmetric constitutional autonomy (**Sixth Schedule** and **Articles 371A, 371B, 371C, 371G**).
  * **Nagas:** A confederation of over 30 Tibeto-Burman-speaking tribes (including **Angami**, **Ao**, **Konyak**, **Sema/Sumi**, **Lotha**, **Chakhesang**, and **Tangkhul**) spanning Nagaland, Manipur’s hill districts, Arunachal Pradesh, and Assam. Sociological organization ranges from fiercely egalitarian, democratic village republics (such as the **Angamis** practicing terraced wet-rice cultivation and the **Aos** governed by *Putu Menden* councils of elders) to autocratic hereditary chieftaincies (**Ahngs**) among the **Konyaks** and **Semas**.
  * **Mizos:** Concentrated in the Lushai Hills of Mizoram, speaking Tibeto-Burman *Duhlian/Mizo* with near-universal literacy (~91.3%). Following the abolition of hereditary Lushai chieftainships in 1954 and the culmination of the Mizo National Front insurgency in the historic **Mizo Peace Accord (1986)**, Mizo social solidarity is anchored in the ethical code of ***Tlawmngaihna***—an indigenous moral doctrine obligating every citizen to practice unselfishness, hospitality, and civic altruism above personal gain, institutionally enforced by the **Young Mizo Association (YMA)**.
  * **Khasis and Garos:** Inhabiting the Meghalaya plateau, the **Khasis** (alongside Jaintias/Pnars, speaking an Austroasiatic Mon-Khmer language) and **Garos** (speaking a Tibeto-Burman Bodo-Garo language) represent one of the world’s largest surviving **matrilineal kinship systems**. Among the Khasis, ancestral lineage (*Kur*) and property descend through the youngest daughter (***Ka Khadduh***), who acts as the custodian of the family’s ancestral home and ritual hearth, while maternal uncles (*Mama*) historically exercised executive authority. Among the Garos, the selected heiress daughter (***Nokna***) marries a cross-cousin (***Nokrom***) from her father’s lineage to maintain inter-clan equilibrium across village territories (***A’king***).
  * **Apatanis:** Inhabiting the high-altitude **Ziro Valley** in Lower Subansiri district of Arunachal Pradesh, the **Apatanis** are renowned in ecological anthropology (extensively documented by **Christoph von Fürer-Haimendorf**) for their highly intensive, sedentary **wet-rice and fish-cum-paddy cultivation (*Ajing*)** coupled with sustainable bamboo-and-pine social forestry, practiced entirely with human labor without ploughs or draught animals. Internally, traditional Apatani society was stratified into two endogamous classes—the aristocratic landed ***Mite* (Patricians)** and the subordinate ***Mura* (Plebeians)**—governed by a non-hereditary village council called the ***Buliang***.
* **Insular and Southern Peninsular Enclaves:** Accounting for roughly **3% of the ST population**, this zone includes the Western and Eastern Ghats (such as the pastoral buffalo-herding **Todas** of the Nilgiris studied by **W.H.R. Rivers**, alongside honey-collecting **Jenu Kurubas**, **Irulas**, **Chenchus**, and **Konda Reddis**) and the island territories. In **Lakshadweep**, over 94% of the population consists of matrilineal Muslim Scheduled Tribes practicing *Marumakkathayam*-style taravad inheritance. In the **Andaman and Nicobar Archipelago**, a stark anthropological divide separates four **Negrito hunter-gatherer PVTGs**—the **Great Andamanese**, **Onge** (Little Andaman), **Jarawa** (South/Middle Andaman), and uncontacted **Sentinelese** (North Sentinel Island)—whose populations suffered catastrophic demographic collapse under colonial penal settlement and epidemic exposure, from two **Mongoloid tribal groups** in the southern islands: the horticultural, largely Christianized **Nicobarese** and the semi-nomadic inland forest-dwelling **Shompen** of Great Nicobar.

### 2. Structural Properties of Tribal Social Organization: Reciprocity, Commons, Youth Dormitories, and Cosmology

In contrast to peasant caste society—which is organized around an asymmetric ritual hierarchy of purity and pollution, hereditary occupational division of labor, and individualized private property—classical tribal social structure is governed by **segmentary kinship organization**, **generalized reciprocity**, **customary communal ownership of productive ecological resources**, and **ecocentric sacred cosmologies**. Drawing upon **Marshall Sahlins** and **Karl Polanyi**’s substantive economic anthropology, exchange within traditional Adivasi villages operates not through market profit maximization or hierarchical **Jajmani** servitude, but through **kinship-based reciprocity** and **communal labour-pooling**—exemplified by ***Madait* / *Pachha*** among Oraons and Mundas, ***Gata-Hal*** among Bhils, and ***Lom*** labor gangs among Kuki-Chin groups, where entire village lineages collectively clear fields, transplant paddy, or build houses in exchange for festive rice-beer (*Handia* / *Apong*) and mutual obligation.

Property relations in pre-colonial tribal formations were rooted in **customary communal stewardship** rather than alienable individual title. Whether in the Munda ***Khuntkatti*** and Oraon ***Bhuinhari*** tenures of Chotanagpur, the Garo ***A’king*** clan lands, or the shifting cultivation (***Jhum*** in the North-East, ***Podu*** in Odisha/Andhra, ***Bewar*** among Baigas) cycles across hill slopes, individual households possessed **usufructuary rights** (the right to cultivate and enjoy produce so long as the land was worked) while ultimate sovereignty resided in the clan, lineage, or village council. Because land was simultaneously an economic resource, a repository of ancestral bones, and the abode of village boundary spirits, it could not be mortgaged or sold as a commodity to outsiders—a structural incompatibility that triggered acute rebellion whenever colonial British revenue laws imposed individual *Zamindari* or *Ryotwari* alienable titles.

#### The Sociology of Youth Dormitories: Ghotul, Dhumkuria, and Morung

One of the most distinctive institutional pillars of tribal socialization, transitioning adolescents from the natal family into full civic and ritual citizenship, is the **Youth Dormitory**. Rather than segregating youth within patriarchal domestic enclosures, tribal societies constructed communal dormitories—either **mono-sexual** (separate for unmarried boys and girls) or **bi-sexual** (co-educational)—serving as schools of oral pedagogy, civic labor forces, defense barracks, and spaces of egalitarian gender socialization:

* **The *Ghotul* of the Muria Gonds (Bastar, Chhattisgarh):** Immortalized in **Verrier Elwin**’s classic monograph *The Muria and Their Ghotul* (**1947**), the **Ghotul** is a bi-sexual youth dormitory founded mythologically by the cultural deity **Lingo Pen**. Unmarried boys are known as ***Chelik*** (headed by a *Sirdar* and *Kotwar*) and unmarried girls as ***Motiari*** (headed by a *Belosa*). Every evening after dinner, village youth gather inside the *Ghotul* enclosure for folk music, dance, storytelling, and peer-led civic discipline. Elwin distinguished two structural variants: the older ***Jodidar* Ghotul** (where a *Chelik* and *Motiari* pair off in a stable, quasi-monogamous romantic partnership throughout their dormitory years) and the modern ***Mundaval* Ghotul** (where partners rotate regularly to prevent emotional possessiveness and jealousy, prioritizing communal solidarity). Remarkably, despite pre-marital intimacy, post-marital divorce and domestic infidelity among the Muria were recorded as exceptionally low.
* **The *Dhumkuria* of the Oraons (Chotanagpur, Jharkhand):** Extensively analyzed by anthropologist **Sarat Chandra Roy** (*The Oraons of Chota Nagpur*, **1915**), the **Dhumkuria** is a strictly segregated mono-sexual dormitory system: the boys’ dormitory is called ***Jonkh-Erpa*** (headed by a youth leader called *Dhangar-Mahto* under the supervision of the village priest *Pahan*) and the girls’ dormitory (kept in a confidential location guarded by an elderly widow) is called ***Pelo-Erpa*** (headed by the *Barkidhangrin*). Admission occurs every three years during the *Magh* festival across three age-grades (*Puna Jokhar* novices, *Majh-Turia* intermediates, and *Koha Jokhar* seniors). The *Dhumkuria* acts as a village cooperative: wealthy or elderly villagers hire the *Jonkh-Erpa* youth for collective paddy harvesting or roof-thatching, with payments going into the dormitory’s communal fund to purchase musical instruments and feast supplies.
* **The *Morung* of the Nagas (Nagaland):** Among the **Konyak** (who call it *Ban*) and **Ao Nagas** (who call it *Arichu*), the **Morung** was the monumental architectural and socio-political heart of each village *Khel* (ward). Built on strategic cliff edges with carved wooden pillars depicting hornbills, tigers, and human heads, the *Morung* functioned as a martial guardhouse, a bachelor’s sanctuary from age six/puberty until marriage, and a meritocratic republic where boys learned warfare, wood-carving, bamboo basketry, and clan history from senior age-sets, while girls gathered in a parallel women’s dormitory (**Yo** or **Tsuki**).
* **Comparative Regional Dormitories:** Parallel institutions permeate India’s tribal belt, including the ***Gitiora*** among the Mundas, Hos, and Birhors; the ***Nokpante*** (bachelors’ house) among the matrilineal Garos; the ***Moshup*** (boys) and ***Rasheng*** (girls) among the Adis of Arunachal Pradesh; the ***Dhangarbassa*** among the Bhuiyas of Odisha; and the ***Rangbang*** social club among the transhumant Bhotias of Uttarakhand. Across the late 20th and early 21st centuries, however, these dormitories suffered rapid institutional decay due to two converging ideological forces: **Brahmanical Sanskritization** (where middle-class caste Hindus stigmatized dormitory freedom as "licentiousness," prompting reformist movements like the **Rajmohini Devi** and **Gahira Guru** sects in Chhattisgarh to demolish *Ghotuls*) and **Christian Missionary Puritanism** in the North-East and Chotanagpur, alongside the spread of formal state boarding schools (*Ashram Shalas* and *Eklavya Model Residential Schools*).

#### Animistic, Totemic, and Ecocentric Cosmologies

Sociologically, tribal religion is neither a "primitive superstition" waiting to evolve into text-based monotheism nor a degraded fragment of Puranic Hinduism, but a coherent **ecocentric cosmology** embedding social solidarity within natural ecosystems. Drawing on **Émile Durkheim**’s *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (**1912**) and **Roy Rappaport**’s ritual ecology, Adivasi religious life integrates three structural elements:
1. **Totemism and Clan Exogamy:** Every major Central Indian tribe is divided into exogamous clans (***Kili*** among Mundas/Hos, ***Gotar*** among Oraons, ***Paris*** among Santhals, ***Saga*** among Gonds) named after a specific animal, plant, bird, or natural phenomenon (e.g., *Tirkey* [young mice], *Lakra* [tiger], *Minz* [eel], *Toppo* [woodpecker], *Kachhap* [tortoise], *Kujur* [medicinal creeper]). Clan members regard their totem as a mythical ancestor or benefactor; consequently, killing, eating, or harming the totem species is strictly taboo, while marrying within the same totem clan is treated as incest. Totemism thus simultaneously enforces biological exogamy and institutionalizes micro-ecological biodiversity conservation across overlapping clan territories.
2. **Animism, Sacred Groves (*Sarna* / *Law Kyntang*), and Bongaism:** Conceptualized by **D.N. Majumdar** in his study of the Hos of Kolhan as **Bongaism**, Adivasis perceive the universe as pervaded by an impersonal spiritual energy (*Bonga* or *Aren*) manifesting in mountain peaks, rivers, ancestral spirits (*Hapramko*), and village boundary deities (*Gaon Deoti*). Central to this landscape is the **Sacred Grove**—known as ***Sarna*** among Oraons and Mundas, ***Jaherthan*** among Santhals, and ***Law Kyntang*** or ***Law Lyngdoh*** among the Khasis—a pristine patch of virgin primeval forest (predominantly *Sal* [*Shorea robusta*] trees in Chotanagpur) left untouched since the founding of the village, where no axe may be lifted and where the village priest (*Pahan* or *Naike*) propitiates the Earth Mother (*Chala Pachcho* / *Marang Buru*) during seasonal agrarian festivals like **Sarhul** and **Karam**.
3. **Symbiotic Landscape Sacralization:** Unlike extractive industrial capitalism, which views mountains as inert deposits of bauxite or iron ore, Adivasi cosmology personifies entire topographical ecosystems as living juridical and sacred kin—exemplified by the **Dongria Kondh** of Odisha, who revere the **Niyamgiri** hills as ***Niyam Raja*** (the Lord of Law who provides perennial mountain streams, wild tubers, and forest sustenance), making open-cast bauxite mining an act of deicide and ecocide.

At the absolute bottom of the developmental and civil-rights hierarchy stand two acute sociological categories: the **75 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs)**—who suffer extreme ecological, nutritional, and demographic fragility—and the **Denotified, Nomadic, and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (DNTs)**—who bear the lingering carceral stigma of colonial biopolitical criminalization.

#### The 75 PVTGs: Pre-Agricultural Technology, Stagnant Demography, and PM-JANMAN

Recognizing that developmental benefits and constitutional reservations within the Scheduled Tribe category were disproportionately absorbed by larger, settled peasant tribes, the **U.N. Dhebar Commission (Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes Commission, 1960–61)** identified an internal bottom tier of "Primitive Tribal Groups" characterized by acute survival precarity. During the Fifth Five-Year Plan (1975), the Union Government initially notified 52 such groups, adding 23 more by 1993 to reach a total of **75 groups**, and officially renamed them **Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs)** in **2006** to remove pejorative evolutionary connotations.

* **The Four Diagnostic Sociological Criteria:** A Scheduled Tribe is classified as a PVTG based on four cumulative parameters: (1) **pre-agricultural level of technology** (reliance on hunting-gathering, food foraging, artisanry, or rudimentary *Bewar*/*Podu* shifting cultivation with digging sticks and hoes), (2) **stagnant or declining population growth**, (3) **extremely low levels of literacy** (often in single digits among women), and (4) **a subsistence level of economy** devoid of surplus accumulation or market monetized buffers.
* **Geographical and Demographic Profile:** The **75 PVTGs** (~4.5 million population across 22,500+ habitations) reside across **18 States and the Union Territory of Andaman & Nicobar Islands**. **Odisha houses the highest number of PVTGs (13)**—including the **Bonda (Bondo Poraja)**, **Dongria Kondh**, **Kutia Kondh**, **Juang**, **Paudi Bhuyan**, **Lanjia Saora**, **Mankidia**, and **Birhor**—followed by unified Andhra Pradesh/Telangana (12, such as **Chenchus** and **Konda Reddis**), Bihar/Jharkhand (9, including **Asur**, **Birhor**, **Korwa**, **Mal Paharia**, and **Sauria Paharia**), Madhya Pradesh/Chhattisgarh (7, including **Abujh Maria**, **Baiga**, **Bharia**, **Kamar**, and **Sahariya**), and Tamil Nadu/Kerala (including **Todas**, **Kotas**, **Irulas**, **Cholanaikkan**, and **Kadar**). While some PVTGs like the **Sahariyas** (MP/Rajasthan) or **Maria Gonds** number in the hundreds of thousands, insular PVTGs face demographic extinction: the **Sentinelese** (~50–100), **Great Andamanese** (~60), **Onge** (~120), **Jarawa** (~500), and **Shompen** (~230).
* **Biomedical Vulnerability, Habitat Rights, and PM-JANMAN (2023–2026):** Sociologically, PVTGs suffer from a dual ecological-biomedical trap. When evicted from forest reserves or denied access to tubers, wild honey, and medicinal flora, their transition to state Public Distribution System (PDS) polished rice induces severe micronutrient malnutrition, tuberculosis (exceptionally high among the **Sahariyas**), and high infant mortality, compounded by genetic hemoglobinopathies such as **Sickle Cell Anemia** (targeted by the **National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission, 2023–2047**). Under **Section 3(1)(e) of the Forest Rights Act (2006)**, PVTGs are uniquely entitled to **"Habitat Rights"** over their broader customary socio-cultural and ecological territories (*Deh* or *Baiga Chak*), though by 2026 only a handful of states (Madhya Pradesh for the **Baigas** and **Bharias**, Chhattisgarh for the **Kamars** and **Baigas**, and Odisha for the **Paudi Bhuyans**, **Juangs**, and **Mankidias**) have operationalized formal habitat titles. Furthermore, while the **Pradhan Mantri Janjati Adivasi Nyaya Maha Abhiyan (PM-JANMAN, launched November 2023 with a ₹24,104 crore outlay)** seeks to saturate PVTG habitations with *pucca* housing, piped water, mobile medical units, and all-weather roads, anthropologists caution that standardized concrete housing and aggressive road connectivity—if divorced from customary livelihood protection—risk accelerating cultural assimilation and land encroachment.

#### Denotified, Nomadic, and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (DNTs): Colonial Biopolitics, Idate Commission, and the SEED Scheme

If PVTGs represent ecological marginalization, **Denotified, Nomadic, and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (DNTs)** represent the sociological scars of **colonial state biopolitics and legal stigmatization**. For centuries prior to British rule, nomadic pastoralists (**Raikas/Rabaris**, **Gujjars**, **Bakarwals**, **Dhangars**, **Kurubas**, **Gaddis**), itinerant inland salt-and-grain caravan traders (**Banjaras / Lambadas**), peripatetic artisans (**Gadia Lohars**), and forest-foraging/acrobat communities (**Pardhis**, **Sansis**, **Kanjars**, **Baurias**, **Nats**, **Kalbelias**, **Koravas**, **Ramoshis**, **Yerukulas**, **Lodhas**) formed a vital circulatory network linking agrarian villages across the subcontinent.

* **The Colonial Criminal Tribes Act (1871–1952):** Following the Revolt of 1857—in which mobile groups like the Banjaras and Ramoshis disrupted colonial supply lines—and driven by the colonial state’s imperative to sedentarize populations for land-revenue extraction, monopolize forests (*Indian Forest Act, 1865/1878*), and replace caravan trade with British railways, the colonial government enacted the **Criminal Tribes Act (CTA) of 1871** (initially in North India, extended to Madras in **1911** and consolidated across British India in **1924**). Applying pseudo-scientific Victorian eugenic anthropology combined with a distorted reading of caste heredity, British officials declared nearly **200 mobile communities** to be *"addicted to the systematic commission of non-bailable offences"*—constructing the legal monstrosity of the **"born criminal."** Under the CTA, entire communities (including children) were subjected to mandatory finger-printing, night roll-calls by village headmen, pass-laws restricting movement, and forced internment in fenced "reformatory settlements" managed by the Salvation Army or police as bonded industrial/quarry labor.
* **Post-Colonial "Denotification" and the Habitual Offenders Act (1952):** Following the recommendations of the **Ananthasayanam Ayyangar Committee (1949–50)**, the Government of India formally repealed the Criminal Tribes Act on **August 31, 1952** (celebrated by DNT movements as *Vimukti Diwas* or Liberation Day), "denotifying" these communities. However, legal repeal was simultaneously subverted by the enactment of state-level **Habitual Offenders Acts (1952 onwards)**. While replacing the collective category of "Criminal Tribe" with the individualized legal category of "Habitual Offender," police station crime registers (*History Sheets* and *K.D. Registers*) and colonial police training manuals remained unchanged, perpetuating systemic custodial torture, preventative detentions before festivals, and mob lynchings against **Pardhis** (Maharashtra/MP), **Lodhas** (West Bengal, extensively documented by anthropologist **P.K. Bhowmick** and writer **Mahasweta Devi**), and **Kuravars** (Tamil Nadu).
* **Institutional Diagnostics (Renke and Idate Commissions) and Constitutional Limbo:** Because the 1952 denotification was not accompanied by a unified constitutional schedule (unlike SCs and STs), DNTs suffered acute administrative fragmentation: the exact same community (e.g., **Banjara** or **Ramoshi**) is classified as ST in one state, SC in another, OBC in a third, and completely unreserved in a fourth.
  * The **First National Commission for Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (chaired by Balkrishna Sidram Renke, 2008)** estimated that DNTs/NTs constitute roughly **10% of India’s population (~11 to 15 crore people)** living in extreme landlessness without voter IDs, ration cards, or permanent domiciles due to their peripatetic lifestyle.
  * Subsequently, the **Bhikhu Ramji Idate Commission (2015–2017)** cataloged **1,262 distinct DNT, Nomadic, and Semi-Nomadic communities** across India and discovered that **269 communities** were not included in *any* constitutional reserve category (neither SC, nor ST, nor OBC), recommending their immediate inclusion under a sub-categorized OBC list (**Schedule G**) or a dedicated **Third Constitutional Schedule** alongside a permanent statutory commission.
* **Contemporary Policy and the October 2024 Supreme Court *Sukanya Shantha* Verdict:** In response to the Idate Commission, the Union Government constituted the **Development and Welfare Board for Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Communities (DWBDNC, 2019)** and launched the **SEED Scheme (Scheme for Economic Empowerment of DNTs, February 2022)** providing four targeted interventions: free coaching for competitive examinations, health insurance under *Ayushman Bharat*, community-level livelihood clusters, and housing assistance under *PMAY*. Crucially, on the jurisprudential front, the **Supreme Court’s landmark judgment in *Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India* (October 2024)** struck down colonial-era provisions in State Prison Manuals across India that assigned sanitation/sweeping labor by caste and segregated prisoners from Denotified Tribes as "habitual criminals." Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud held that treating members of Denotified Tribes as inherently criminal inside prisons violates **Articles 14, 15, 17, and 21 of the Constitution**, directing all states and police academies to purge references to "habitual offenders" linked to DNT lineage from police registers and jail manuals.

## II. Classical Sociological Debates: Isolation, Assimilation, Integration, and the Tribe-Caste Continuum

### 4. The Triadic Policy Debate: Elwin’s Isolationism, Ghurye’s Assimilationism, and Nehru’s Tribal Panchsheel

On the eve of Indian Independence during the **1930s and 1940s**, the question of how the emerging nation-state should relate to its tribal populations ignited one of the most consequential intellectual controversies in Indian sociology: the polemical clash between British-born missionary-turned-anthropologist **Verrier Elwin** and the doyen of Indological sociology at Bombay University, **Govind Sadashiv Ghurye**. This confrontation over **Isolation vs. Assimilation** directly shaped the Constituent Assembly debates (where **Jaipal Singh Munda** famously challenged both colonial paternalism and majoritarian assimilation) and culminated in Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru**’s middle-path synthesis of **Integration** enshrined in the **Tribal Panchsheel (1958)**.

#### Verrier Elwin’s "National Park" Isolationism and Later Strategic Shift

Rooted in immersive participant-observation fieldwork among the **Baigas** of Mandla (*The Baiga*, **1939**), the **Agarias** (*The Agaria*, **1942**), and the **Muria Gonds** of Bastar, **Verrier Elwin** documented the catastrophic socio-psychological trauma inflicted upon forest tribes by sudden, unregulated incorporation into colonial-capitalist and caste-Hindu society. In his tract *Loss of Nerve: A Comparative Study of the Result of the Contact of Peoples in the Aboriginal Areas of Bastar State* (**1941**), Elwin identified six destructive vectors of external contact:
1. The criminalization of shifting cultivation (*Bewar*) and hunting by the colonial Forest Department, which severed the Baigas from their cosmological refusal to "tear the breast of Mother Earth with an iron plough."
2. The penetration of predatory plains moneylenders (*Mahajans* and *Kalar* liquor distillers), who used illiterate debt-bonds to expropriate tribal harvests and reduce free forest cultivators to bonded *Kamiyas*.
3. The imposition of alien colonial courts and police stations that criminalized customary marital and dispute-resolution norms.
4. The puritanical moral crusades of both Christian missionaries and Hindu reformists, who shamed tribal youth into abandoning their vibrant dances, songs, and *Ghotul* dormitories, inducing deep cultural inferiority and psychological depression—what Elwin termed a **"loss of nerve."**

To arrest this cultural and economic annihilation in the most vulnerable interior pockets, Elwin in *The Baiga* (**1939**) proposed the creation of a **"National Park"** in the tribal heartland of Mandla/Bilaspur—an autonomous sanctuary administered by a Special Commissioner through traditional tribal chiefs, where non-tribal moneylenders, land-grabbers, and proselytizing missionaries would be barred from entering without a license. Although nationalist critics fiercely attacked Elwin for treating human beings as "anthropological museum specimens" or "zoological exhibits," Elwin himself substantially modified his stance after Independence. Serving as Anthropological Adviser to the Governor of Assam for the **North-East Frontier Agency (NEFA, modern Arunachal Pradesh)**, Elwin wrote *A Philosophy for NEFA* (**1957**), explicitly clarifying that temporary protection in 1939 was a desperate defensive tactic under colonial neglect, whereas in a democratic welfare state the goal must be **respectful integration without cultural destruction**.

#### G.S. Ghurye’s Indological Assimilationism: The "Backward Hindus" Thesis

Directly confronting Elwin and colonial census commissioners (such as **W.V. Grigson** and **J.H. Hutton**), **G.S. Ghurye** published his combative monograph *The Aborigines — "So-Called" — and Their Future* (**1943**, republished in 1959 as *The Scheduled Tribes*). Relying on an Indological and nationalist macro-historical framework rather than localized village ethnography, Ghurye advanced four core propositions:
* **Deconstructing the "Aborigine" Category:** Ghurye argued that the historical record of ancient and medieval India does not prove that today’s hill tribes were the exclusive original inhabitants (*Adivasis*) prior to Indo-Aryan or Dravidian populations; rather, many tribes migrated across internal frontiers over millennia.
* **The "Backward Hindus" Thesis:** Examining tribal religious pantheons (*Mahadeo* worship among Gonds, *Singbonga* solar worship, marriage rituals, and agricultural festivals), Ghurye contended that tribal beliefs are structurally indistinguishable from the folk religion of lower- and middle-rung rural Hindu castes. Therefore, tribes are neither culturally alien "animists" nor distinct nationalities, but **"imperfectly integrated classes of Hindu society"**—or succinctly, **"Backward Hindus"**—who fell behind due to their residence in inaccessible hills and forests.
* **Critique of Colonial "Excluded Areas" as Imperialist Balkanization:** Ghurye charged British anthropologist-administrators and Verrier Elwin with being "isolationists" and "revivalists" who sought to cordon off tribal territories into "Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas" under the 1935 Act solely to prevent tribes from joining the Indian National Congress’s anti-colonial mass movement and to preserve British sovereignty over mineral-rich frontiers.
* **Assimilation as Emancipation:** For Ghurye, the socio-economic misery of tribes (debt bondage, drunkenness, and land loss) was not unique to tribes, but was shared by the entire rural Hindu peasantry (*Kisan*) under British colonial rule. Consequently, the solution lay not in protective territorial walls, but in **accelerated cultural and economic assimilation** into mainstream Hindu agricultural society, arguing that historic contact with Hindu peasants had progressively elevated tribal material techniques (introducing the iron plough and settled cultivation) and tempered destructive practices like head-hunting.

Sociologists such as **André Béteille** and **Virginius Xaxa** have since critiqued Ghurye’s assimilationist model for two fatal blind spots: first, it romanticized the "assimilation" of tribes into Hindu society while glossing over the reality that tribes absorbed into the caste fold were almost universally consigned to the stigmatized bottom of the hierarchy as **Untouchables or impoverished Shudra laborers**; second, its Indological lens failed to account for the Tibeto-Burman and Austroasiatic tribes of the North-East (Nagas, Mizos, Khasis), who possessed zero historical or cultural affinity with Sanskritic Hinduism.

#### Jawaharlal Nehru’s Integrationist Synthesis: The Tribal Panchsheel (1958)

Seeking to transcend the polarization between Elwin’s early protective isolationism and Ghurye’s cultural assimilationism, Prime Minister **Jawaharlal Nehru**—working closely with Verrier Elwin during the 1950s—articulated the official state doctrine of **Integration** in his famous **Foreword to the second edition of Elwin’s *A Philosophy for NEFA* (1958)**. Nehru rejected two developmental fallacies: first, treating tribal people as exotic museum pieces to be kept frozen in primitive poverty for anthropological curiosity; and second, allowing the "avaricious" forces of the chaotic market economy and arrogant cultural superiority of plains administrators to engulf and uproot tribal life. To institutionalize democratic integration within a pluralist republic, Nehru laid down the **Five Fundamental Principles of Tribal Development**, universally known as the **Tribal Panchsheel**:
1. **Autonomous Cultural Flourishing:** *"People should develop along the lines of their own genius and we should avoid imposing anything on them. We should try to encourage in every way their own traditional arts and culture."*
2. **Sovereignty over Ecological Commons:** *"Tribal rights in land and forests should be respected."*
3. **Indigenous Administrative Cadres:** *"We should try to train and build up a team of their own people to do the work of administration and development. Some technical personnel from outside will, no doubt, be needed, especially in the beginning. But we should avoid introducing too many outsiders into tribal territory."*
4. **Institutional Minimalism and Customary Synergy:** *"We should not over-administer these areas or overwhelm them with a multiplicity of schemes. We should rather work through, and not in rivalry to, their own social and cultural institutions."*
5. **Humanistic Qualitative Evaluation:** *"We should judge results, not by statistics or the amount of money spent, but by the quality of human character that is evolved."*

While the **Tribal Panchsheel** remains the gold standard of constitutional humanism—inspissated into the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules**—the empirical trajectory of post-colonial Indian political economy in Central India systematically violated its second and fourth principles: state-led mega-industrialization, coal mining, and forest bureaucracy monopolies overrode tribal rights in land and forests, reducing the Panchsheel in Schedule V areas to a rhetorical ideal subverted by extractive accumulation.

Moving beyond normative policy debates, Indian field-view anthropologists in the mid-20th century investigated the actual empirical mechanisms through which autonomous tribal communities interacted with, transitioned into, or resisted incorporation into caste-peasant society. Rather than treating "Tribe" and "Caste" as hermetically sealed binary opposites, **Nirmal Kumar Bose**, **F.G. Bailey**, and **Surajit Sinha** demonstrated that Indian civilization operated through dynamic processes of **structural absorption** and **structural-cultural continua**.

#### Nirmal Kumar Bose’s "Hindu Method of Tribal Absorption" (1941)

In his seminal paper *"The Hindu Method of Tribal Absorption"* presented at the Indian Science Congress (**1941**) and grounded in ethnographic fieldwork among the **Juangs** of Pal Lahara in Odisha and the **Oraons/Mundas** of Chotanagpur, Marxist-Gandhian anthropologist **Nirmal Kumar Bose** explained how Brahmanical civilization absorbed hundreds of forest tribes over two millennia without a centralized church or standing missionary army. Bose argued that the primary driver of absorption was **material and technological asymmetry**, not religious proselytization:
* **Ecological Siege of Shifting Cultivation:** Shifting cultivation (*Podu* / *Jhum*) and foraging can sustain only roughly 20 to 30 persons per square mile, whereas settled wet-rice plough cultivation practised by caste-Hindu peasants sustains hundreds per square mile. As Hindu peasant populations expanded into fertile valleys and colonial forest laws restricted access to woodlands, tribes like the **Juangs** were pushed onto barren rocky slopes where their traditional technology could no longer feed them.
* **The Non-Competitive Guild Guarantee of the Caste System:** Forced to seek economic survival on the fringes of Hindu villages, the beleaguered tribe was incorporated into the **Brahmanical Jajmani division of labor** by being assigned a specialized, hereditary occupational niche—for instance, the Juangs in Pal Lahara took up making bamboo baskets and collecting fuelwood for Hindu villagers, while other tribes became rope-makers, drummers, or landless agricultural serfs. Because the Hindu caste system operated as a federation of non-competitive hereditary guilds (guaranteed by royal/customary law), the absorbed tribe gained basic subsistence security, shielded from economic competition by other castes.
* **Cultural Pluralism Masked by Structural Exploitation:** Culturally, Hindu society exercised what Bose called **"cultural laissez-faire"**: unlike Semitic monotheisms that demand total erasure of prior gods, Brahmanism allowed the absorbed tribe to continue worshipping its indigenous village goddesses (*Gramadevati* / *Thakurani*), who were gradually syncretized as local manifestations of *Durga*, *Kali*, or *Shiva*, while the tribe slowly emulated upper-caste food taboos (abandoning beef and pork) to seek ritual respectability. However, Bose underscored the brutal **political-economic contradiction** at the core of this process: just as ancient defeated rulers were turned into *Dasa* slaves, economically weak forest tribes were systematically slotted into the lowest, most polluted rungs of the *Shudra* and *Ati-Shudra* (Untouchable) order so that the upper castes could extract their cheap labor while denying them upward occupational mobility.

* **F.G. Bailey’s Political-Economic Continuum:** In Bisipara, Bailey defined "Tribe" and "Caste" strictly by their **politico-economic structure of access to land**: in a **Tribal society** (the Kondhs), access to land is direct, egalitarian, and mediated through segmentary kinship clans; in a **Caste society** (the Oriyas), access to land and resources is hierarchical, unequal, and mediated through organic, non-egalitarian patron-client dependencies upon a dominant landowning caste.
* **Surajit Sinha’s Dual Structural-Cultural Continuum and "Tribal Rajputization":** Based on intensive ethnography among the **Bhumij** of Barabhum (Manbhum/Purulia on the Bengal-Jharkhand border) and comparative study of the **Hill Maria Gonds**, **Santals**, and **Mundas**, **Surajit Sinha** argued that movement along the continuum occurs simultaneously across two dimensions: **social structure** (from isolated, egalitarian clan-based subsistence to stratified, multi-ethnic peasant market integration) and **culture** (from local animistic *Little Tradition* to Sanskritic *Great Tradition*). Crucially, in his landmark essay *"State Formation and Rajput Myth in Tribal Central India"* (**1962**), Sinha demonstrated that tribes did not merely enter the Hindu order at the bottom as impoverished Shudras (as in Bose’s model); rather, numerically strong, land-controlling tribes such as the **Bhumij**, **Mundas (Nagvanshi Raj of Chotanagpur)**, **Gonds (Raj Gonds of Garha-Mandla and Chanda)**, **Cheros (Palamu)**, and **Ahoms/Kochs** underwent **indigenous state formation and "Tribal Rajputization"**. As tribal lineage chiefs (*Manki* or *Sardar*) accumulated agricultural surplus through settled plough cultivation, they elevated themselves into territorial monarchs (*Rajas*), imported Brahmin ritualists from the plains through tax-free land grants (*Brahmottar*), fabricated mythical genealogies (*Vanshavalis*) tracing their descent from Solar or Lunar Kshatriya dynasties, adopted purdah and child marriage for women, and created an internal class hierarchy separating royal/aristocratic lineages (*Raj Gond*, *Atgaria Bhumij*, *Thakur*) from ordinary tribal cultivators (*Dhur Gond*, *Nagadi Bhumij*).

### 6. Internal Class Stratification, Elite Capture, and the Virginius Xaxa Committee Report (2014)

While classical anthropology analyzed tribal stratification through medieval state formation and Sanskritization, contemporary sociology confronts how **post-colonial capitalist penetration**, **commercial agriculture**, **formal education**, and **75+ years of constitutional affirmative action** have generated modern **internal class differentiation** within Scheduled Tribes, alongside severe macro-structural marginalization relative to non-tribal society.

#### The Virginius Xaxa High-Level Committee Report (2014): Empirical Diagnostics

In August **2013**, the Prime Minister’s Office constituted a **High-Level Committee chaired by eminent sociologist Prof. Virginius Xaxa** to comprehensively evaluate the socio-economic, health, educational, and legal status of Scheduled Tribes across India. Submitting its landmark report in **May 2014**, the **Virginius Xaxa Committee** provided the definitive 21st-century sociological indictment of India’s tribal development paradigm:
* **The Thesis of "Double Colonialism" and Internal Colonialism:** Prof. Xaxa conceptualized the predicament of Central Indian Adivasis as a condition of **double subjugation**: first, subjugation by colonial British laws (*Land Acquisition Act 1894* and *Indian Forest Act 1927*) that turned ancestral commons into state monopoly property; and second, subjugation by the post-colonial Indian state and dominant regional non-tribal linguistic elites, who treated tribal territories as internal resource colonies to extract coal, iron, electricity, and timber for urban-industrial metropolises while transferring the ecological and human costs of displacement onto Adivasis.
* **Livelihood Collapse and Proletarianization:** The Committee documented a sharp structural decline in the proportion of STs who are self-employed *Cultivators* (dropping from **68% in 1961 to under 35% by 2011**) and a corresponding surge in STs reduced to **landless Agricultural Wage Laborers (~45%)** and footloose migrant construction workers—proving systematic land dispossession despite protective tenancy laws.
* **Educational Alienation and the "Ashram Shala" Critique:** While ST literacy rose from 8.5% (1961) to 59% (2011), a massive gap persisted vis-à-vis the national average (73%), accompanied by high secondary-school dropout rates (~55%). The Xaxa Committee critiqued the pedagogy of state residential schools (*Ashram Shalas*), noting that teaching Adivasi children in dominant regional state languages (Hindi, Odia, Telugu, Marathi) while denigrating their mother tongues (**Gondi, Kui, Mundari, Ho, Bhili**) and indigenous knowledge systems breeds psychological alienation and "cultural erasure" rather than empowerment.
* **Health Deprivation and Carceral Over-Incarceration:** Over **40% of STs** lived below the poverty line, with ST child mortality (under-5 mortality rate at 35.8 to 50+ per 1,000 in heartland pockets per NFHS-4/5) and maternal anemia significantly worse than national averages due to the absence of functional Primary Health Centres in hilly terrains. Furthermore, the Committee flagged the **weaponization of criminal and security laws** against Adivasis caught between Left-Wing Extremism (Maoists) and state security forces in Bastar, Jharkhand, and Odisha, recommending a **Judicial Commission** to review thousands of false or prolonged undertrial detentions of Adivasis under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA) and Forest Acts, alongside strict opposition to the forcible eviction of PVTGs from wildlife sanctuaries.

#### Emerging Internal Class Differentiation and the Sub-Classification Jurisprudence (2024–2026)

Parallel to the macro-marginalization of the Adivasi peasantry documented by the Xaxa Committee, empirical sociological studies by **Ghanshyam Shah** (among the **Chodhras** and **Gamits** of South Gujarat), **Sengupta**, and **Xaxa** himself demonstrate that Scheduled Tribes are no longer internally egalitarian or classless. Three structural vectors have produced internal class polarization within tribes:
1. **Agrarian Commercialization:** In irrigated plains-adjacent pockets, a small stratum of affluent tribal peasants (*Kulaks*) accumulated land alienated from poorer clan members through informal usufructuary mortgages, adopting Green Revolution inputs and hiring impoverished landless tribals as wage laborers.
2. **Inter-Tribal Asymmetry in Reservation Capture:** Because the **705+ Scheduled Tribes** vary wildly in historical literacy and geographical proximity to urban centers, constitutional reservations in central civil services (**Article 16(4)**) and higher education (**Article 15(4)**) have been disproportionately cornered by a narrow band of advanced/literate ST communities—most notably the **Meenas** of Rajasthan (a settled agricultural community historically distinct from forest-dwelling Bhils and Sahariyas), Christianized literate tribes of the North-East (**Mizos**, **Khasis**, **Nagas**), and specific Christianized **Oraon/Munda** urban elites in Ranchi—leaving forest-dwelling **PVTGs**, **Santhals**, **Korkus**, **Baigas**, and **Hos** severely under-represented.
3. **Intra-Tribal "Creamy Layer" Formation:** Within advanced tribes, a third- and fourth-generation urban burocratic, professional, and political elite (*Adivasi Bourgeoisie*) has emerged whose children attend elite metropolitan English-medium schools and repeatedly capture reserved IAS/IPS/medical seats, while their rural co-ethnics remain trapped in illiteracy. Moreover, tribal political elites elected on reserved parliamentary and assembly seats (**Articles 330 and 332**) are frequently co-opted by mainstream political parties and mining lobbies, failing to articulate radical *Jal-Jungle-Jameen* agendas in legislatures (a phenomenon **Galanter** and **McMillan** analyzed as the structural containment of reserved representation).

This sociological reality of inter-tribal and intra-tribal inequality directly informed the **landmark 7-Judge Constitution Bench judgment of the Supreme Court in *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (August 2024)**. Overruling its earlier 5-Judge ruling in *E.V. Chinnaiah v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2004)*, the Supreme Court held by a **6:1 majority** that Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes notified under **Articles 341 and 342** do not constitute a sociologically homogeneous class; consequently, State Governments are constitutionally empowered under **Articles 15(4) and 16(4)** to **sub-classify Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** (creating internal sub-quotas for the most backward tribes/castes, such as PVTGs or marginalized forest tribes) provided the state furnishes **quantifiable empirical data** proving both inadequate representation in state services and inter-se backwardness, and ensures no single sub-group monopolizes 100% of the quota. In a far-reaching concurring opinion joined by three other judges, **Justice B.R. Gavai** urged the state to evolve a policy to identify and exclude the **"Creamy Layer"** from SC and ST reservations, observing that the children of an IAS/IPS officer from an ST/SC community cannot be equated with the child of a disadvantaged villager studying in a gram panchayat school. While subaltern PVTGs welcomed sub-classification to break the monopoly of dominant ST groups, Adivasi sociologists and the Union Cabinet (which in **August 2024** clarified that the Constitution does not provide for a Creamy Layer in SC/ST quotas) cautioned that imposing an income-based Creamy Layer on STs ignores the fact that ST reservations are grounded not merely in economic poverty, but in **structural geographical isolation, cultural exclusion, and historical dispossession**, which frequently leave high-cutoff ST civil service posts vacant if middle-class tribal youth are excluded.

## III. The Development-Displacement Nexus: Extractive Accumulation and Customary Dispossession

### 7. Political Economy of Resource Curses: Mega-Dams, Mining Corridors, and Conservation Evictions

The central structural tragedy of modern Adivasi life in the Central Indian heartland is the **Resource Curse Paradox**: the exact geological formations (the Archean-Proterozoic Gondwana craton of Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, and northern Andhra/Telangana) that contain **over 80% of India’s coal reserves, 85% of iron ore, 90% of bauxite and chromite, and 100% of Jaduguda’s uranium**, alongside the dense forested catchments of peninsular India’s major river basins (Narmada, Godavari, Mahanadi, Subarnarekha, Koel-Karo, Indravati), are coterminous with the ancestral homelands of India’s **Fifth Schedule** tribes.

#### David Harvey’s "Accumulation by Dispossession" in the Tribal Heartland

Applying Marxist geographer **David Harvey**’s theory of **"Accumulation by Dispossession"** (an ongoing historical reactivation of Karl Marx’s "primitive accumulation") and **Michael Levien**’s sociology of **"Regimes of Dispossession"**, post-colonial Indian modernization operated through two distinct phases of tribal expropriation. In the **Nehruvian state-capitalist phase (1950s–1980s)**, dispossession was legitimized under the developmentalist ideology of national self-reliance—constructing public-sector steel plants (Rourkela on Munda/Oraon land, Bhilai on Gond land, Bokaro on Santhal land), coal mines, and multi-purpose river valley dams ("Temples of Modern India"). In the **post-1991 neoliberal phase**, dispossession shifted toward acquiring tribal lands and forests for **private corporate mining conglomerates**, thermal power plants, Special Economic Zones (SEZs), and industrial corridors to fuel private capital accumulation.

Both phases relied on the colonial juridical weapon of **Eminent Domain**—whereby the state claims supreme sovereign ownership over all land not backed by individual private *patta* titles (*Terra Nullius* / "government wasteland") and exercises coercive power to acquire private land for "public purpose" under the **Land Acquisition Act, 1894** (belatedly replaced by the **Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement [RFCTLARR] Act, 2013**, which introduced mandatory Social Impact Assessments and 70%–80% consent clauses, though subsequent state-level amendments and central infrastructure exemptions diluted these safeguards).

* **Empirical Metrics of the Displacement Crisis (Walter Fernandes & Xaxa Committee):** According to foundational empirical databases compiled by sociologist **Walter Fernandes** (Indian Social Institute / NESRC) and cited by the **Virginius Xaxa Committee (2014)**, between **1947 and 2014**, an estimated **60 to 65 million people** were displaced or deprived of livelihoods by development projects in India without their consent. Strikingly, **over 40% of all Development-Induced Displaced Persons (DIDPs) in India are Scheduled Tribes**, despite STs constituting only **8.6%** of the national population (rising to **55%–60% tribal share** in mining and dam projects across Jharkhand, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh).
* **The Rehabilitation Deficit and Multiple Displacement:** Fewer than **25% to 28%** of displaced Adivasis ever received formal rehabilitation, and because compensation under the 1894 Act was paid exclusively in **cash for individually registered patta land**—ignoring customary communal forest lands, grazing commons, and shifting cultivation slopes—illiterate Adivasi families with low monetized financial literacy quickly lost their meager cash compensation to fraud, debt, or inflation. Worse still, numerous communities suffered **multiple consecutive displacements**: for instance, **Rihand Dam** oustees in Singrauli (MP/UP) were displaced first by the reservoir in the 1960s, a second time by open-cast NCL coal mines in the 1970s, and a third time by super-thermal power plants and ash dykes in the 1980s–90s. Sociological pioneer **B.D. Sharma** (former Commissioner for SCs and STs in his classic **28th and 29th Constitutional Reports, 1986–1989**) diagnosed how this process transforms proud, self-sufficient Adivasi community-owners into **Jan Breman**’s destitute, footloose informal laborers, domestic maids, and bonded brick-kiln workers in metropolitan slums.

#### The Four Engines of Adivasi Dispossession: Dams, Open-Cast Mines, Industrial Enclaves, and "Fortress Conservation"

1. **Mega-Dams and Hydrological Submergence:** Large dams account for the single largest share of tribal displacement (~60% of tribal DIDPs). From early post-colonial projects like **Hirakud** (Odisha), **Damodar Valley (Maithon/Panchet)**, and **Ukai** (Gujarat) to the **Sardar Sarovar Project** on the Narmada river (where **Tadvi**, **Vasava**, and **Bhilala** Adivasis constituted nearly 58% of the 250,000+ affected people, catalyzing the **Narmada Bachao Andolan** led by **Medha Patkar** and **Baba Amte**) and the ongoing **Polavaram Multipurpose Project** on the Godavari river in Andhra Pradesh, mega-dams submerge the most fertile alluvial valley floors along with surrounding hillsides. At **Polavaram**, over **370 habitations and ~100,000+ people—predominantly Konda Reddi (a PVTG) and Koya Adivasis** across Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh—face submergence; when resettled in cramped, tin-roofed plains colonies devoid of forest access, their communal kinship reciprocity, seed diversity, and *Podu* food security collapse entirely.
2. **Open-Cast Mining and Ecological Devastation:** The technological shift in Indian coal, iron, and bauxite extraction from underground shaft mining (which was labor-intensive and left surface forests intact) to **mechanized open-cast strip mining** has multiplied surface land destruction while offering virtually zero skilled employment to displaced locals.
   * **Coal and Iron Corridors:** In **Hasdeo Arand** (Chhattisgarh—one of Central India’s largest contiguous *Sal* forests and elephant corridors inhabited by **Gonds** and **Oraons**), the allocation of coal blocks (such as Parsa East-Kanta Basan and Parsa) despite Gram Sabha opposition under PESA sparked sustained indigenous resistance (**Hasdeo Bachao Andolan**). Similarly, iron ore mining in **Bailadila** (Bastar—where red iron-oxide slurry polluted the Shankini and Dankini rivers, depriving **Muria/Maria Gonds** of drinking water while fueling an exploitative mining-town sex economy targeting tribal women) and **Saranda** forest (Jharkhand **Ho** heartland), alongside radioactive uranium tailings contamination in **Jaduguda** (Jharkhand, causing congenital deformities and cancers among **Santhals** and **Hos**), exemplify extractive sacrifice zones.
   * **The Niyamgiri Counter-Paradigm (2013):** The most celebrated democratic triumph against corporate mining occurred in the **Niyamgiri Hills** of Kalahandi and Rayagada districts (Odisha), where Vedanta Resources sought to mine bauxite from the summit of *Niyam Dongar*, the sacred mountain abode of the **Dongria Kondh (PVTG)**. In its landmark judgment in ***Orissa Mining Corporation v. Ministry of Environment and Forest* (April 2013)**, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court held that under **Articles 25 and 26 of the Constitution** read with **PESA (1996)** and the **Forest Rights Act (2006)**, the right of the Dongria Kondhs to worship their sacred mountain is an integral fundamental religious and cultural right, empowering the local **Gram Sabhas** to decide whether bauxite mining would infringe their customary and religious rights. In **July–August 2013**, across India’s first-ever environmental referendums, all **12 Gram Sabhas unanimously rejected** the mining project, forcing the MoEF to cancel environmental clearance.
3. **Industrial Enclaves and Insular Mega-Projects:** From the anti-displacement massacre of **13 Adivasis at Kalinga Nagar (Odisha, January 2006)** protesting Tata Steel land acquisition and the **Koel-Karo** / **Netarhat Field Firing Range** movements in Jharkhand to the contemporary **₹72,000-crore Great Nicobar Island Mega-Infrastructure Project (2022–2026)**—which involves diverting ~130 sq. km of pristine tropical rainforest and denotifying tribal reserve territories inhabited by the **Shompen (a PVTG numbering ~230)** and **Nicobarese** for a transshipment port, airport, and township—industrial enclaves continuously collide with constitutional protections for vulnerable indigenous habitats.
4. **"Fortress Conservation" and Wildlife Sanctuaries:** Sociologists **Amita Baviskar**, **Mahesh Rangarajan**, and **Madhav Gadgil** have critiqued the Western colonial model of **"Fortress Conservation"** (patterned on the Yellowstone National Park myth of "human-free wilderness") embedded in the **Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972** and **Project Tiger**. Ignoring millennia of co-evolution between indigenous forest-dwellers and wildlife—where communities like the **Soligas** (Biligiri Rangaswamy Temple [BRT] Tiger Reserve, Karnataka), **Baigas** (Kanha National Park, MP), **Chenchus** (Nallamala/Nagarjunasagar-Srisailam, AP/TG), and **Jenu Kurubas** (Nagarhole, Karnataka) treat the tiger as an elder kinsman (*Huli Veerappa* / *Baghdeo*) and use controlled low-intensity ground fires that prevent invasive *Lantana camara* weeds—the conservation bureaucracy frames Adivasis as "encroachers" and "poachers." Over **600,000+ tribals** have been displaced or face eviction notices from **55+ Tiger Reserves** and over 600 Protected Areas to create inviolate "Critical Tiger Habitats," even as luxury eco-tourism resorts, highways, and mining leases proliferate along sanctuary boundaries (a tension highlighted again following the **June 2024 National Tiger Conservation Authority [NTCA] directive** ordering expedited relocation of 590+ villages containing ~64,000 families from core tiger zones, sparking nationwide protests by forest rights movements citing mandatory consent provisions under **Section 4(2) of the FRA 2006** and **Section 38V of the Wildlife Protection Act**).

### 8. Customary Land Alienation and Institutional Bottlenecks of the Forest Rights Act (2006)

#### Mechanisms of Tribal Land Alienation in Fifth Schedule Areas

Beyond state-led displacement for mega-projects, silent, continuous **individual and communal land alienation** to non-tribal settlers (*Dikus*) has eroded the agrarian base of Fifth Schedule areas. Following 19th-century tribal uprisings (**Kol Mutiny 1831–32**, **Santal Hul 1855**, **Birsa Munda’s Ulgulan 1899**, **Rampa Rebellion 1922**), the colonial and post-colonial state enacted stringent protective land-transfer prohibitions: the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (CNTA), 1908** (Section 46 prohibiting transfer of tribal land to non-tribals without the Deputy Commissioner’s permission within the same police station), the **Santhal Parganas Tenancy Act (SPTA), 1949** (Section 20 imposing an absolute bar on sale or transfer of *raiyati* land), the **Odisha Scheduled Areas Transfer of Immovable Property (By Scheduled Tribes) Regulation 2 of 1956**, and the **Andhra Pradesh Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation (LTR) 1 of 1959 (as amended by Regulation 1 of 1970)**.

Yet, empirical field studies by the **B.D. Sharma Reports** and the **Xaxa Committee** reveal that nearly **50% of tribal land** in several Fifth Schedule districts was alienated through four structural loopholes:
* **Usurious Debt-Bondage and Informal *Bhugat-Bandha* Mortgages:** Facing medical emergencies, wedding expenses, or crop failure in rainfed uplands without institutional bank credit, tribal cultivators borrow cash from non-tribal moneylenders (*Sahukars*), surrendering physical possession of their fertile *Don* (lowland paddy) fields under informal oral mortgages that become perpetual de-facto expropriations.
* **Fraudulent *Benami* Transactions and Instrumental Marriages:** Non-tribal real-estate speculators, merchants, and politicians register tribal land in the names of tribal domestic servants/employees (*Benami*), enter into collusive civil-court declaratory suits where a tribal landowner is paid to admit a pre-existing title in favor of a non-tribal, or contract second/polygamous marriages with tribal women solely to acquire real estate in scheduled areas (such as around Ranchi, Visakhapatnam/Araku, and Koraput).
* **Cadastral Manipulation and Survey Erasure of Uplands:** During state revenue settlement surveys (*Bhu-Bandobast*), revenue officials (*Patwaris* / *Karamcharis*) systematically failed to record tribal customary titles over hill slopes (*10-degree slope rules* in Odisha, *Gair Mazrua Khas/Aam* commons in Jharkhand, and *Orange Areas* in MP/Chhattisgarh), transferring millions of hectares of ancestral *Khuntkatti* and shifting-cultivation fallows into government revenue "Wasteland" (*Anabad*) or Forest Department books.
* **Circumventing the 1997 *Samatha* Judgment via Public-Private MDOs:** In the historic judgment ***Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (July 1997)**, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court ruled that under Paragraph 5(2) of the Fifth Schedule and the AP Land Transfer Regulation, the word "person" includes the State Government; therefore, **government land and forest land in Fifth Schedule areas cannot be leased out to non-tribal private mining companies** (directing that mining in Scheduled Areas be undertaken either by the State itself or by cooperatives of Scheduled Tribes). To bypass *Samatha*, subsequent courts limited its ratio to states with identical regulations (*BALCO Employees Union v. Union of India*, 2001), while state governments created a legal fiction: leasing coal/iron blocks in Fifth Schedule areas to state-owned Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs), which then immediately subcontract 100% of actual mining operations and profits to private corporate conglomerates tagged as **Mine Developer and Operators (MDOs)**.

#### The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA)

Enacted in December **2006** (and operationalized via Rules on **January 1, 2008**) following massive mobilization by the **Campaign for Survival and Dignity** after the Forest Department launched nationwide evictions in 2002, the **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA)** represents a watershed paradigm shift in Indian environmental jurisprudence. Its Preamble explicitly acknowledges that during colonial and post-colonial consolidation of state forests, the ancestral rights of forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes (**FDSTs**) and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (**OTFDs**) were not recorded, resulting in a **"historical injustice"** to those who are integral to the very survival and sustainability of the forest ecosystem.

Unlike colonial forest laws that treated forest villagers as licencees at the mercy of the Beat Guard, the **FRA (2006)** democratizes forest governance through four pillars:
* **Individual and Collective Rights Matrix (Section 3):** Recognizes **Individual Forest Rights (IFR)** over self-cultivated and habitation land up to **4 hectares** occupied before the cutoff date of **December 13, 2005**; **Community Forest Rights (CFR)** over *Nistar*, grazing grounds, water bodies, and complete ownership, access to collect, use, and dispose of **Minor Forest Produce (MFP / NTFP)** including bamboo and *tendu* leaves; **Community Forest Resource (CFRr) management rights under Section 3(1)(i)** empowering villages to protect, conserve, and manage their customary forests; and **Habitat Rights for PVTGs** alongside seasonal access for nomadic pastoralists (**Van Gujjars**, **Gaddis**, **Maldharis**).
* **Bottom-Up Three-Tier Institutional Architecture (Section 6):** The **Gram Sabha** (at the hamlet/*Tola*/*Pada* level in Scheduled Areas, not the large revenue Panchayat) is the statutory authority to initiate the verification of claims through an elected **Forest Rights Committee (FRC)** (comprising one-third women and two-thirds STs), which then forwards approved claims to the **Sub-Divisional Level Committee (SDLC)** and the **District Level Committee (DLC)** for final record-of-rights titling.
* **Gram Sabha Veto over Forest Diversion and Wildlife Relocation:** Under **Section 4(5)**, no forest dweller can be evicted until the recognition and verification of rights is complete. Under **Section 4(2)**, no relocation from Critical Wildlife Habitats in National Parks/Sanctuaries can occur unless it is scientifically proven that human co-existence is impossible, resettlement is completed, and the **free informed consent of the GramSabhas** in writing is obtained. Furthermore, under **Section 5** and the **2009 MoEF Circular (codified in the 2013 FCA Rules)**, prior to diverting any forest land for mining or industrial projects under the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, the District Collector must certify that all FRA rights have been settled and that the **Gram Sabhas have given their informed consent** with a 50% quorum.

Despite transformative success models—such as **Mendha-Lekha** and **Pachgaon** villages in **Gadchiroli district (Maharashtra)**, where **Gond** Gram Sabhas secured CFR titles over thousands of hectares of bamboo forests, auctioned bamboo worth crores of rupees directly without middlemen, and invested the revenue in community development and sustainable ecological patrolling (**"In Mumbai and Delhi we have our government, but in our village, we ourselves are the government" — *Mawa Nate Mawa Raj***)—the nationwide implementation of the FRA over two decades (**2006–2026**) has encountered severe structural bottlenecks:
1. **High Rejection Rates and Bureaucratic Subversion of CFRs:** According to Ministry of Tribal Affairs (MoTA) data through **2025–2026**, out of roughly **5.1 million total claims filed**, nearly **35% to 38% (~1.8 million claims)** were rejected—often arbitrarily at the SDLC/DLC level by Forest and Revenue officers without communicating written reasons to claimants to enable appeals. Moreover, the bureaucracy skewed implementation overwhelmingly toward **Individual Forest Rights (~2.3 million titles distributed)** while stalling transformative **Community Forest Resource (CFR) titles (~115,000+ titles)**, except in select states like **Chhattisgarh**, **Maharashtra**, and **Odisha**, because CFRs directly strip the Forest Department of its lucrative revenue control over timber and bamboo coupes.
2. **The Impossible OTFD Evidentiary Burden and the *Wildlife First* Litigation (2019–2026):** For **Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (OTFDs)**—which include Dalits, OBC artisans, and unscheduled forest communities—Section 2(o) demands proof of residence and dependence on forest land for **three generations (75 years) prior to December 13, 2005**. Because colonial and post-colonial forest departments never issued receipts to impoverished villagers, millions of OTFD claims were rejected. In **February 2019**, in the ***Wildlife First v. Ministry of Environment and Forests*** case (filed by conservation NGOs challenging the constitutional validity of the FRA), the Supreme Court ordered the immediate eviction of over **1.1 million tribal and forest-dwelling households** whose claims had been rejected—an order the Court **stayed on February 28, 2019** after the Union Government and MoTA admitted that state rejection procedures had violated natural justice, failure to provide legal aid, and flawed reliance on satellite imagery that cannot detect cultivation beneath tree canopies.
3. **Institutional Bypass via CAMPA (2016) and the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act (2023):** The **Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act (CAMPA), 2016** channeled over ₹50,000+ crore of corporate forest-diversion funds directly into the hands of state Forest Departments, resulting in monoculture plantations (eucalyptus, teak) frequently planted directly upon Adivasi shifting-cultivation commons and CFR lands without Gram Sabha consent. More critically, the **Forest Conservation Rules (2022)** and the **Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023** fundamentally weakened the FRA’s protective shield: the 2022 Rules shifted the requirement of obtaining Gram Sabha consent until *after* the Union Government has already granted Stage-I/Stage-II forest clearance to a corporate developer, while the **2023 Amendment Act** narrowed the statutory scope of the historic ***T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad* (1996)** Supreme Court definition of "deemed forests" (excluding unrecorded forest lands converted before 1996) and exempted strategic, national-security, and infrastructure projects within **100 km of international borders**—affecting the entire biodiversity-rich **North-East tribal frontier** and Himalayan belt—from mandatory forest clearance procedures.

## IV. Indigenous Identity Assertions: Constitutional Autonomy, Pathalgadi, and the Sarna Code

### 9. Constitutional Asymmetric Federalism: Fifth Schedule (PESA) vs. Sixth Schedule Autonomy and the Pathalgadi Movement

#### Structural Asymmetry Between the Fifth Schedule (Article 244(1)) and Sixth Schedule (Article 244(2))

Sociologically, the gulf between the **Fifth Schedule** and the **Sixth Schedule** explains why Central Indian Adivasis suffered catastrophic land alienation and corporate displacement while North-Eastern hill tribes largely retained territorial control:
* **The Paternalistic Deficit of the Fifth Schedule (10 Heartland States):** Covering Scheduled Areas across **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Rajasthan**, the Fifth Schedule vests extraordinary protective powers in the **Governor of the State** (Paragraph 5)—including the power to direct that any Parliamentary or State Legislature Act shall not apply to a Scheduled Area, or shall apply with modifications, and to make regulations prohibiting the transfer of tribal land and regulating moneylending. The Governor is assisted by a **Tribes Advisory Council (TAC)** consisting of up to 20 members, three-fourths of whom must be Scheduled Tribe MLAs of the state legislative assembly. However, as **B.D. Sharma** and the **Xaxa Committee** demonstrated, the Fifth Schedule failed in practice for three structural reasons: (1) Governors constitutionally act on the aid and advice of the **State Council of Ministers**, which is dominated by non-tribal political and mining interests; hence, no Governor in 75 years ever exercised Paragraph 5 powers to strike down an anti-tribal land acquisition or forest law; (2) the **TAC** is a toothless body that meets only when convened by the Chief Minister and advises solely on matters referred to it by the Governor; and (3) thousands of tribal majority villages in West Bengal, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Kerala have never even been notified as Fifth Schedule Areas despite fulfilling demographic criteria.
* **The Self-Governance Model of the Sixth Schedule (4 North-Eastern States) and the Ladakh Mobilization:** Operating across **10 Autonomous Districts in Assam (Bodoland Territorial Region, Karbi Anglong, Dima Hasao), Meghalaya (Khasi, Jaintia, and Garo Hills), Tripura (TTADC), and Mizoram (Chakma, Mara, and Lai)**, the Sixth Schedule establishes "states within a state": democratically elected **Autonomous District Councils (ADCs)** and **Regional Councils** of up to 30 members (26 elected by adult suffrage, 4 nominated by the Governor). Unlike the advisory TACs of the Fifth Schedule, Sixth Schedule ADCs possess **autonomous legislative powers** over land allocation, management of non-reserved forests, regulation of *Jhum* cultivation, appointment/succession of traditional chiefs (*Syiem*, *Doloi*, *Nokma*), inheritance of property, and social customs; **autonomous judicial powers** to constitute traditional village courts for trials between Scheduled Tribes; and **executive/fiscal powers** to collect land revenue, levy taxes on professions/trades/entry of goods, and regulate money-lending and trading by non-tribals. Furthermore, **Articles 371A (Nagaland)** and **371G (Mizoram)** go even further, stipulating that no Act of Parliament regarding religious/social practices, customary law, and **ownership and transfer of land and its resources** shall apply to Nagaland or Mizoram unless their State Legislative Assemblies so decide by resolution. It is precisely this robust constitutional shield over land, minerals, and fragile ecology that has propelled the **post-2019 indigenous mobilization in the Union Territory of Ladakh** (where **over 90% of the population belongs to Scheduled Tribes**—**Balti, Beda, Bot/Boto, Brokpa/Drokpa, Changpa, Garra, Mon, and Purigpa**—led by the **Leh Apex Body [LAB]** and **Kargil Democratic Alliance [KDA]** through **2024–2026**) to demand **Sixth Schedule inclusion** to prevent external real-estate/industrial colonization of fragile high-altitude Himalayan pastures.
* **The Bhuria Committee (1995) and the PESA Act (1996):** To bridge this institutional chasm and extend the **73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992)** (which had excluded Fifth Schedule areas under Article 243M) to the tribal heartland, Parliament enacted the **Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)** based on the recommendations of the **Dileep Singh Bhuria Committee (1995)**. PESA sought to implant Sixth Schedule-style participatory self-rule into Fifth Schedule villages by establishing the **Hamlet/Village-level Gram Sabha** (recognizing organic Adivasi *Tolas/Padas* rather than artificial multi-village administrative Panchayats) as the supreme sovereign authority competent to: (1) safeguard cultural identity, community resources, and customary dispute resolution (**Section 4(d)**); (2) enjoy mandatory consultation before land acquisition and rehabilitation (**Section 4(i)**); and (3) exercise **ownership of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)**, prevent and restore alienated tribal land, regulate village markets (*Haats*), enforce prohibition of intoxicants, and control minor minerals and minor water bodies (**Section 4(m)**). Yet, three decades after its enactment, PESA remains structurally crippled: states delayed framing State PESA Rules for up to 25 years (Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand notified rules only in **2022–2026** amidst intense controversy over whether state rules diluted customary *Manki-Munda* and *Manjhi-Paragana* authority), failed to amend conflicting colonial State Forest, Excise, and Mining Acts to align with PESA, and reduced mandatory Gram Sabha consent into paper-forged "consultations" orchestrated by local block bureaucrats.

#### The Sociology of the Pathalgadi Movement (2017–2026) and Constitutional Subalternity

The acute contradiction between the emancipatory promise of the **Fifth Schedule / PESA** and the reality of corporate land expropriation erupted between **2017 and 2020** (with continuing organizational resonance through **2026**) in the **Pathalgadi Movement** across the **Munda**, **Ho**, and **Oraon** heartlands of **Khunti, West Singhbhum, Gumla, and Saraikela-Kharsawan districts in Jharkhand**, spilling into neighboring **Jashpur/Surguja (Chhattisgarh)** and **Sundargarh/Mayurbhanj (Odisha)**.

* **From Funerary Megalith to Vernacular Constitutionalism:** Etymologically meaning "erecting a stone," ***Pathalgadi*** is an ancient megalithic custom among the Austroasiatic **Mundas** and **Hos** whereby clan members erect vertical stone slabs (***Buru-diri*** or ***Sasandiri***) to commemorate deceased ancestors, record genealogies, and serve as inviolable title deeds proving that the family belongs to the original *Khuntkatti* forest-clearers of the village. In **2016–2017**, when the Jharkhand state government passed ordinances to amend the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908)** and **Santhal Parganas Tenancy Act (1949)** to permit agricultural tribal land to be diverted for non-agricultural infrastructure/commercial purposes and quietlytransferred over 2.1 million acres of village commons (*Gair Mazrua* land) into a state **"Land Bank"** for corporate investors (Momentum Jharkhand summit), Adivasi communities repurposed *Pathalgadi* into a potent repertoire of **subaltern constitutional resistance** (what legal sociologists **Rohit De** and **Nandini Sundar** term **vernacular or insurgent constitutionalism**).
* **The Epigraphy of Gram Sabha Sovereignty:** Across hundreds of villages in Khunti (the birthplace of **Birsa Munda**), villagers erected 10-to-15-foot-high green-painted stone slabs at village entry points inscribed verbatim with provisions of the **Constitution of India** and **Supreme Court judgments**:
  1. **Article 13(3)(a):** Defining "Law" as including *"custom or usage having in the territory of India the force of law"*—asserting that Adivasi customary *Parha* and *Manki-Munda* governance supersedes statutory bureaucracy.
  2. **Article 244(1) (Fifth Schedule, Paragraph 5) and PESA (1996):** Declaring that general parliamentary and state laws do not automatically apply to Fifth Schedule Areas unless specifically notified by the Governor, and vesting supreme authority over village land and resources in the **Gram Sabha**.
  3. **Article 19(1)(d)/(e) read with Article 19(5):** Citing the state’s power to restrict the free movement and settlement of outsiders "for the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe," the plaques prohibited non-residents, police, and government officials from entering the village without the prior permission of the traditional Gram Sabha (*Munda* / *Pahan*).
  4. **Supreme Court Precedents:** Citing ***Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1997)** and ***Orissa Mining Corporation v. MoEF* (Niyamgiri, 2013)** to assert that the Gram Sabha is the permanent constitutional legislature of the village.
* **Internal Ideological Cleavages and State Securitization:** Sociologically, the Pathalgadi movement split into two distinct streams: a **mainstream constitutionalist stream** (led by traditional *Manki-Munda* associations and land-rights movements demanding the strict implementation of PESA, cancellation of corporate Land Banks, and notification of state PESA rules) and an **ultra-radical secessionist fringe** (influenced by the Gujarat-based *Kunwar Kesari* / *Sati Pati* sect and led by Vijay Kujur and Joseph Purty), which misread the Government of India Act 1935 to claim that Adivasis are the exclusive "owners of India" immune to the Indian Republic, urging villagers in opium-cultivating pockets of Khunti to burn Aadhaar and Voter ID cards, boycott government schools and elections, and create a parallel "Bank of Gram Sabha." The state responded in **2018** with massive paramilitary repression, registering **19+ omnibus FIRs for sedition (Section 124A IPC) against over 10,000 named and unnamed Adivasis**. Following the 2019 Jharkhand Assembly elections—in which Adivasi voters decisively defeated the incumbent government over the CNTA/SPTA and Pathalgadi crackdown—the incoming Hemant Soren Cabinet in its very first meeting on **December 29, 2019** withdrew the mass sedition charges against Pathalgadi protestors, re-channeling the movement’s energy into the struggle for **customary PESA rules** and the **Sarna Religious Code**.

* **The Politics of Census Erasure (1871 vs. 1951):** During British colonial rule, every decennial census from **1871 to 1941** provided a distinct enumeration category for tribal religions (variously termed *"Aboriginals"*, *"Animists"*, or *"Tribal Religions"*). However, in the first post-Independence **Census of 1951**, the Registrar General of India abolished the separate column for Tribal Religions, restricting the primary coded religious schedule to **six major religions**: Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, Sikhism, Buddhism, and Jainism (coded 1 through 6 on modern census schedules), followed by a residual seventh blank for **"Other Religions and Persuasions."** As **Virginius Xaxa** and **Sitakant Mahapatra** have shown, this bureaucratic deletion institutionalized **G.S. Ghurye**’s assimilationist thesis by statistical fiat: whenever census enumerators visited non-Christian Adivasi villages in Central and Western India, they routinely recorded nature-worshipping Bhils, Gonds, Santhals, Oraons, and Mundas as **"Hindus"** by default.
* **The Sociological Distinctiveness of *Sarna* Cosmology:** Led by the **Oraons**, **Mundas**, **Hos**, **Santhals**, and **Bhumij** across **Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Chhattisgarh, Bihar, and the Assam tea belt** (spearheaded by organizations such as the *Adivasi Sengel Abhiyan* and *Kendriya Sarna Samiti*), the **Sarna movement** asserts that Adivasi religion is structurally antithetical to Sanskritic Hinduism across four fundamental axes:
  1. **Sacred Ecology vs. Temple Idol Worship:** Followers of *Sarna* worship the Supreme Creator (***Dharmesh*** among Oraons, ***Singbonga*** among Mundas/Hos, ***Marang Buru / Thakur Jiu*** among Santhals) and Mother Earth (*Chala Pachcho* / *Jaher Era*) directly within living **Sacred Groves (*Sarna* / *Jaherthan* of Sal trees)**, explicitly rejecting anthropogenic idol worship (*Murti Puja*) and brick-and-mortar temples.
  2. **Absence of Varna Hierarchy and Purity-Pollution:** *Sarna* society possesses no four-fold Varna hierarchy, no hereditary Untouchability, and no Brahmanical priesthood; village rituals are officiated by an indigenous tribal priest (***Pahan***, ***Naike***, or ***Deuri***) selected from within the community who farms his own land alongside fellow villagers.
  3. **Egalitarian Life-Cycle Rituals and Dietary Autonomy:** Unlike upper-caste Hindu injunctions against widow remarriage, divorce, and meat/alcohol consumption, *Sarna* customs treat fermented rice-beer (***Handia***) and sacrificial fowl as sacred ritual offerings (*Tapan*) to ancestors, practice bride-price (*Gonong*) rather than patriarchal dowry, and honor buried ancestral megaliths (*Sasandiri*) rather than Vedic cremation on the Ganges.
  4. **Demographic Resistance to Dual Assimilation:** In the **2011 Census**, despite the absence of a dedicated checkbox, **4,957,270 (~49.57 lakh) citizens voluntarily wrote "Sarna"** in the residual "Others" column—a figure larger than the entire national population of **Jains (44.51 lakh)**, who enjoy a dedicated census code and national minority status. Alongside **Sarna**, other indigenous movements recorded **Gondi Punem (~10.26 lakh)** in Central India, **Sari Dharma (~5.06 lakh)** among Santhals in West Bengal, **Donyi-Polo (~3.31 lakh)** among the Tani tribes of Arunachal Pradesh, **Sanamahi (~2.22 lakh)** in Manipur, and **Niamtre / Seng Khasi** in Meghalaya. In **November 2020**, the **Jharkhand Legislative Assembly** convened a special session and unanimously passed a resolution urging the Union Government to include a separate **Sarna Code** in the forthcoming **Population Census 2027**—a demand caught in deep ideological friction as Hindutva organizations (*Vanvasi Kalyan Ashram*) oppose a separate code on the ground that it would detach Adivasis from the Hindu demographic fold and expose them to Christian proselytization, while Sarna leaders argue that without a separate code, Adivasi cultural identity faces silent statistical extinction.

#### The Intersection of Customary Autonomy and Constitutional Gender Equality (2022–2026 Jurisprudence)

While Adivasi movements rightly defend **customary law** against corporate land-grabbing and cultural erasure, feminist sociologists (**Leela Dube**, **Nitya Rao**, **Nandini Sundar**, and **Virginius Xaxa**) highlight a profound internal contradiction within tribal society: **the invocation of "customary autonomy" by male tribal elites to deny tribal women equal property inheritance and political representation**.

Although tribal women enjoy substantially greater personal autonomy, freedom of movement, economic participation (high Female Labor Force Participation Rates in agriculture and NTFP collection), absence of purdah/sex-selective abortion (reflected in a robust **ST sex ratio of 990 females per 1,000 males** in Census 2011 compared to the national average of 943), and freedom of divorce/remarriage compared to upper-caste Hindu women, **customary law in patrilineal tribes is deeply patriarchal regarding land ownership and village governance**:
* **Exclusion from Ancestral Land Inheritance:** Under the customary laws of patrilineal tribes (such as the **Mundas**, **Oraons**, **Santhals**, **Hos**, **Gonds**, and **Bhils**), agricultural land belongs exclusively to the male agnatic lineage (*Khunt*). Unmarried daughters and widows are granted only **usufructuary maintenance rights** (the right to be maintained from the land’s produce until marriage or death), never absolute inheritable ownership or the right to demand partition. Even among the matrilineal **Khasis** of Meghalaya—where ancestral property passes to the youngest daughter (*Khadduh*)—executive managerial control over land disposal and traditional village councils (***Dorbar Shnong***) has historically been monopolized by men, while among the **Nagas**, customary bodies like the *Naga Hoho* fiercely resisted the **33% women’s reservation in Urban Local Bodies (ULBs)** for over two decades by invoking **Article 371A** until the **Supreme Court’s intervention and the historic June 2024 Nagaland ULB elections** finally elected the state’s first women municipal councillors.
* **The Statutory Exclusion under Section 2(2) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956:** Crucially, **Section 2(2) of the Hindu Succession Act (HSA), 1956** explicitly states that nothing contained in the Act shall apply to members of any **Scheduled Tribe** within the meaning of Article 366(25) unless the Central Government by official gazette notification otherwise directs. Consequently, when the **Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005** and the landmark ***Vineeta Sharma* (2020) Supreme Court judgment** granted Hindu daughters equal coparcenary property rights by birth alongside sons, **Scheduled Tribe daughters were legally excluded from this gender-justice revolution**.
* **From *Madhu Kishwar* (1996) to Breakthrough Supreme Court Jurisprudence (2022–2026):**
  1. In ***Madhu Kishwar v. State of Bihar* (1996)**—a challenge to Sections 7 and 8 of the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908) which restricted succession of tenancies strictly to male descendants in the male line among the Ho and Munda tribes—a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court stopped short of striking down male-only inheritance (fearing sudden judicial abolition might fragment communal tribal land if daughters married non-tribals), reading down the statute only to protect a female descendant’s right to **hold and cultivate the land for livelihood during her lifetime** as long as she remained dependent upon it, while Justice K. Ramaswamy in dissent held that denying ST women equal succession violates Articles 14, 15, and 21.
  2. Furthermore, to punish tribal women who exercise personal autonomy, rural patriarchal backlashes frequently take the violent form of **Witch-Hunting (*Dayan Pratha* / *Tonhi*)** across Jharkhand, Odisha, Assam, and Chhattisgarh—where widowed or single Adivasi women who possess lifetime usufructuary rights over prime agricultural plots are accused by male agnatic relatives and village exorcists (*Ojhas* / *Bhagats*) of practicing witchcraft so they can be lynched or ostracized and their land seized by male collaterals.
  3. **The *Kamla Neti* Paradigm Shift (December 2022–2026):** In the landmark judgment ***Kamla Neti (D) through LRs v. The Special Land Acquisition Officer* (December 2022)**—where a tribal daughter from Odisha was denied an equal 1/5th share in Rs 54 lakh of land acquisition compensation by her brothers on the ground that Section 2(2) of the HSA barred tribal female inheritance—the Supreme Court declared that *"when the daughter belonging to a non-tribal is entitled to the equal share in the property of the father, there is no reason to deny such right to the daughter of the Tribal community."* Holding that denying a tribal daughter survivorship survivorship/inheritance after 70+ years of the Constitution violates the **Right to Equality under Article 14**, the Court directed the **Union Government to re-examine Section 2(2) of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956** and amend it to grant Scheduled Tribe women equal parity in inheritance. Building on this trajectory in **2024–2025 succession rulings**, the Supreme Court and High Courts (such as in Himachal Pradesh and Chhattisgarh) have increasingly applied fundamental principles of **justice, equity, and good conscience under Article 14** to grant tribal daughters equal inheritance where families have undergone cultural modernization or where no proven, unbroken customary bar to female inheritance exists.
  4. **The December 2025 Supreme Court Ruling on Matrilineal Caste/Tribe Certificates:** Parallel to property parity, in a major **December 2025 ruling** expanding the jurisprudence of ***Rameshbhai Dabhai Naika v. State of Gujarat* (2012)**, the Supreme Court adjudicated the transmission of Scheduled Tribe/Caste status in inter-community marriages involving an ST/SC mother and a non-ST/SC father. Rejecting rigid patriarchal automatism (which presumed a child mechanically inherits only the father’s privileged caste/non-tribal status), the Court held that the paternal presumption is wholly **rebuttable**: where empirical evidence demonstrates that the child was brought up within the mother’s Scheduled Tribe or Scheduled Caste environment, was accepted by the mother’s community, and suffered the same structural social, educational, and economic deprivations without enjoying the privileges of the non-tribal father’s lineage, the child is constitutionally entitled to a Scheduled Tribe/Caste certificate—bringing constitutional jurisprudence closer to the sociological reality of matrilineal and single-mother tribal households.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 8: From Dispossessed Forest Commons to the Urban Informal Proletariat

The structural deconstruction of India’s tribal societies reveals that the contemporary Adivasi predicament cannot be contained within the static frame of isolated forest ethnography. As **mega-dams**, **open-cast mining corridors**, **fortress conservation evictions**, and **customary land alienation** dismantle the subsistence foundations of *Jal-Jungle-Jameen* across the Fifth Schedule heartland, and as agrarian fragmentation and rural distress push millions of dispossessed **Bhils**, **Gonds**, **Santhals**, **Oraons**, **Mundas**, and **Denotified Tribes** out of their ancestral villages, they are propelled directly into the circulatory circuits of **urban-industrial capitalism**. Arriving at the fringes of India’s metropolises and peri-urban boomtowns without formal credentials or tenurial security, these displaced indigenous and agrarian cohorts constitute the bottom tier of the **urban informal proletariat**—building gated residential enclaves as seasonal migrant construction workers, staffing middle-class households as feminized paid domestic workers, and navigating spatial exclusion in precarious squatter settlements.
