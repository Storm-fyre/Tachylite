# Part 6: Agrarian Social Structure, Rural Class Stratification, and Peasant Mobilizations

## I. Disintegration of Traditional Patronage and the Political Economy of Agrarian Class Stratification

### 1. From Hereditary Reciprocity to Market Monetization: The Breakdown of the Jajmani System

The structural transformation of the Indian village from a relatively self-contained ritual-economic moral community into a monetized, fragmented node of global and national commodity chains pivots upon the disintegration of the traditional **Jajmani system**. Historically, the agrarian social structure of pre-colonial and colonial India operated through an institutionalized nexus between ritual hierarchy (**Varna/Jati**) and control over land. The village economy did not allocate labor, artisanal goods, or ritual services primarily through impersonal price mechanisms or free wage contracts. Instead, it functioned through hereditary, dyadic, and multi-generational patron-client relationships linking landed dominant castes with landless servicing, artisanal, and untouchable castes. This intersection of ritual purity-pollution rules with material production created an orthogenetic social equilibrium where economic survival was contingent upon acquiescence to caste subordination.

#### William Wiser’s Reciprocal Functionalism vs. Beidelman’s Exploitative Asymmetry

First conceptualized in empirical village sociology by **William H. Wiser** in his foundational monograph ***The Hindu Jajmani System*** (**1936**), based on ethnographic fieldwork in **Karimpur village** (Mainpuri district, Uttar Pradesh), the system organized inter-caste exchanges around two structural poles: the **Jajman** (the patron, originally the Vedic sacrificial patron, empirically the landowning peasant or landlord) and the **Kamin** (also regionally termed **Praja**, **Purjan**, **Balutedar** in Maharashtra, **Ayagar** in South India, or **Mirasi** in Tamil Nadu—the hereditary provider of specialized craft, menial, and ritual services). Within sociological theory, the interpretation of the **Jajmani system** has been fiercely contested between functionalist models of organic solidarity and Marxist/Dalit-subaltern models of coercive feudal extraction:

*   **The Consensus and Reciprocity Model (William Wiser, Louis Dumont, and M.N. Srinivas):** **William Wiser** framed the **Jajmani system** as a benevolent, functionalist institution of symmetrical interdependence in which each caste group within the village performed hereditary functions that secured the collective welfare and social security of the whole. Customary grain payments—distributed as fixed shares at the threshing floor (**Khalihan**) during harvests (**Fasalana**), supplemented by perquisites during life-cycle rituals (**Biyah-Shadi**, **Jajmani Haq**), free homestead plots (**Abadi**), fodder rights, and credit during famines—insulated the **Kamin** from market fluctuations. **Louis Dumont**, in ***Homo Hierarchicus*** (**1966**), integrated the **Jajmani** framework into his structuralist binary of purity and pollution, arguing that the economic distribution of grain was subordinated to an overarching religious ideology: the division of labor was not oriented toward individual material maximization, but toward the holistic preservation of cosmic and ritual order centered on the **Jajman**.
*   **The Coercive Exploitation and Power-Asymmetry Model (Thomas O. Beidelman, Oscar Lewis, and Kathleen Gough):** Challenging Wiser’s idyllic functionalism, **Thomas O. Beidelman** in ***A Comparative Analysis of the Jajmani System*** (**1959**) and **Oscar Lewis** in his study of **Rampur village** (***Village Life in Northern India***, **1958**) demonstrated that the **Jajman-Kamin** nexus was fundamentally rooted in asymmetric land monopoly and extra-economic coercion. Because land ownership was concentrated almost exclusively in upper and dominant intermediate castes while **Dalits** (**Chamars**, **Bhangis**, **Pasis**, **Paraiyars**, **Pallars**) were structurally barred from land tenure by customary law and colonial revenue settlements (such as the Punjab Land Alienation Act of 1900), the **Kamin** lacked bargaining power. **Kathleen Gough**’s ethnographic work in **Kumbapettai** (Tanjore district, Tamil Nadu) and **Joan Mencher**’s perspective of viewing caste "from the bottom up" revealed that the **Jajmani system** operated as an apparatus of institutionalized servitude (**Begar** or unpaid forced labor), kept intact through the threat of social boycott (**Hukka-Pani Band**), physical violence, and eviction from village homestead sites.

#### Monetization, Green Revolution Mechanization, and the Collapse of Kamin-Jajman Ties

Over the late colonial and post-Independence eras, the interplay between **orthogenetic cultural continuity** and **heterogenetic capitalist modernization** (**Yogendra Singh**, ***Modernization of Indian Tradition***, **1973**) systematically dismantled the **Jajmani** edifice. The transition from subsistence-oriented multi-cropping to market-integrated commercial agriculture eroded the material and normative foundations of hereditary patronage through five interlocking structural shifts:

1.  **Monetization and Industrial Mass Production:** The penetration of factory-manufactured consumer goods into rural weekly markets (**Haats**) rendered the traditional artisanal **Kamin** obsolete. Aluminum, steel, and plastic utensils displaced the **Kumhar** (potter); mill-spun textiles displaced the **Julaha/Kori** (weaver); industrial footwear and synthetic leather displaced the **Chamar** (leatherworker); and factory-forged agricultural implements reduced the **Lohar** (blacksmith) and **Barhai** (carpenter) from hereditary co-sharers of the harvest to fee-for-service repair technicians.
2.  **Green Revolution Biochemical and Mechanical Technological Displacement:** The introduction of High-Yielding Variety (**HYV**) seeds, chemical fertilizers, diesel/electric pump-sets, tractors, and combine harvesters from the late **1960s** onward fundamentally altered the temporal demand for rural labor. Landowners observed that rising foodgrain prices made traditional fixed-percentage harvest shares (**Fasalana**) far more expensive than paying daily cash wages during peak sowing and harvesting windows. Consequently, **Jajmans** unilaterally abrogated customary grain obligations, converting attached servants into casual daily-wage workers (**Dihadi Mazdoor**).
3.  **Abolition of Intermediaries and Land Tenure Reforms:** Post-1950 **Zamindari**, **Jagirdari**, and **Inamdari** abolition legislations, alongside tenancy reforms, eliminated the ultra-large feudal estates capable of sustaining sprawling retinues of ritual and artisanal dependents. As land holdings fragmented across generations, smallholder peasants lacked the economic surplus required to maintain hereditary **Kamins**.
4.  **Constitutional Anti-Caste Emancipation and Dalit Refusal of Degrading Labor:** Driven by **B.R. Ambedkar**’s emancipatory ideology, the expansion of the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act (1989)**, welfare schemes (**MGNREGA**, **Public Distribution System [PDS]** subsidized foodgrains under the **National Food Security Act, 2013**), and political mobilization, **Dalit** castes increasingly refused to perform stigmatized, polluting **Jajmani** duties—such as flaying dead cattle, disposing of night-soil, beating drums at upper-caste funerals, or performing midwifery (**Dai** work)—viewing withdrawal from **Jajmani** servitude as a foundational assertion of civic dignity (**Swabhiman**).
5.  **Out-Migration and Delinking of Caste from Village Residence:** The expansion of peri-urban construction, brick kilns, transport, and rural non-farm employment allowed lower-caste youths to exit the spatial jurisdiction of the village dominant caste, replacing lifetime hereditary subordination with contractual, albeit precarious, wage relationships.

### 2. Agrarian Class Taxonomies: Mapping Land, Labor, and Ritual Status

To move beyond the orientalist myth of the egalitarian "village republic" (**Charles Metcalfe**) and the purely ritualistic lens of Indological caste studies, Indian agrarian sociology developed rigorous materialist taxonomies linking property rights in land, labor deployment, and ritual rank. In Indian villages, class is neither an abstract economic income bracket nor a pure replica of Western industrial bourgeoisie-proletariat binaries; rather, agrarian class structure is deeply embedded within—yet increasingly diverging from—the traditional caste hierarchy.

#### Daniel Thorner’s Tripartite Schema: Malik, Kisan, and Mazdoor

In his landmark lectures collected in ***The Agrarian Prospect in India*** (**1956**), political economist **Daniel Thorner** rejected colonial census categories (which obscured real relations of production behind legalistic revenue labels) and synthesized an indigenous tripartite agrarian class taxonomy based on three operational criteria: **(i) Type of income obtained from the soil** (rent, fruits of own cultivation, or wages); **(ii) Nature of legal/customary rights in land** (proprietary, tenancy, sharecropping, or landless); and **(iii) Extent of fieldwork actually performed** (absentee, supervisory, family labor, or hired labor):

*   **Maliks (Proprietors / Landlords):** Derive their primary agricultural income from property rights in the soil rather than personal physical toil. Their income takes the form of rent (in cash or kind via sharecropping/**Batai**) or surplus extracted through hired wage labor. **Thorner** subdivided **Maliks** into two tiers: **(a) Big Landlords**, holding vast estates across multiple villages, functioning as absentee rentiers with zero personal participation in cultivation; and **(b) Rich Landowners**, residing within the village, avoiding direct manual work (particularly touching the plough, which carries ritual stigma for high castes like **Brahmins** and **Rajputs** under traditional scriptural norms), and relying on full-time attached laborers (**Halis/Padiyals**) or seasonal wage laborers under personal supervision.
*   **Kisans (Working Peasants):** Cultivate the land primarily through the physical labor of their own household members, possessing a recognized legal or customary title to the land that remains smaller and less secure than that of the **Maliks**. They are bifurcated into: **(a) Small Landowners**, who hold proprietary rights over modest holdings sufficient for subsistence and reproduce their household without regularly hiring out their own labor; and **(b) Substantial Tenants**, who hold secure occupancy rights (**Maurusi Kashtkar**) or lease in land, producing enough only by combining family labor with occasional seasonal hiring.
*   **Mazdoors (Agricultural Laborers):** Earn their livelihood primarily from selling their physical labor power on the fields of others in exchange for wages in cash or kind. This stratum comprises: **(a) Poor Tenants and Sharecroppers (Bataidars/Bargadars)**, whose miniature, insecure plots yield so little that they must work as hired laborers to survive; and **(b) Landless Laborers**, who possess neither proprietary nor tenancy rights in agricultural land, subdivided further into **free casual laborers** and **unfree/attached debt-bonded laborers**. Empirically, the **Mazdoor** class exhibits a stark structural overlap with **Scheduled Castes (Dalits)** and **Scheduled Tribes (Adivasis)**.

#### André Béteille’s Sripuram and D.N. Dhanagare’s Structural Class Differentiations

Building upon **Thorner**’s framework, **André Béteille** executed a watershed ethnographic study in **Sripuram** (Tanjore district, Tamil Nadu), published as ***Caste, Class, and Power: Changing Patterns of Stratification in a Tanjore Village*** (**1965**). Applying a **Weberian** multidimensional framework, **Béteille** demonstrated that traditional agrarian society was characterized by **cumulative inequality**: ritual status (**Brahmin Mirasdars**), economic class (landlordship), and political power (control over the village panchayat) converged within the same upper-caste stratum, while **Adi Dravidas (Dalits)** stood at the bottom of all three hierarchies. However, under the impact of land ceiling laws, the monetization of land markets (transforming land from a hereditary lineage estate into a marketable commodity), adult franchise, and anti-Brahmin **Dravidian** political mobilization, **Sripuram** transitioned toward **dispersed inequality**. Non-Brahmin intermediate castes (**Kallars**, **Vellalars**) acquired land from **Brahmins** who migrated to urban white-collar professions, while numerical dominance and **Panchayati Raj** institutions allowed non-Brahmins to capture local political power, breaking the monolithic isomorphism of caste, class, and power.

Concurrently, **D.N. Dhanagare**, in ***Peasant Movements in India, 1920–1950*** (**1983**), adapted the classical Marxist-Leninist and Maoist five-fold agrarian class schema to analyze the socio-structural bases of rural insurrections (**Tebhaga**, **Telangana**, **Moplah**, **Bardoli**):

*   **Landlords:** Feudal and semi-feudal rentiers holding large estates via colonial **Zamindari/Jagirdari** settlements.
*   **Rich Peasants:** Prosperous proprietors or substantial occupancy tenants holding surplus land, employing wage labor, and driving capitalist accumulation (the principal beneficiaries of reformist Gandhian no-tax campaigns like **Bardoli**).
*   **Middle Peasants:** Self-sufficient family farmers whose household labor matches their landholding size, possessing the structural autonomy that **Eric Wolf** and **Hamza Alavi** identified as the initial tactical vanguard of peasant rebellions, though **Dhanagare** showed their vacillating character in India.
*   **Poor Peasants:** Marginal holders and insecure tenants-at-will (**Bargadars** in Bengal, **Verumpattamdars** in Malabar) crippled by rack-renting and usury.
*   **Landless Laborers:** The rural proletariat (**Halpatis**, **Pannaiyals**, **Bhagelas**) who formed the radical militant base of the **Telangana armed struggle (1946–1951)** and the **Naxalbari movement (1967)**.

### 3. The 1970s Mode of Production Debate in Indian Agriculture

During the late **1960s** and **1970s**, the introduction of the **Green Revolution** triggered one of the most sophisticated theoretical controversies in global Marxist political economy, conducted primarily in the pages of the *Economic and Political Weekly* (**EPW**): the **Mode of Production in Indian Agriculture Debate**. The core sociological question was whether state-sponsored technological modernization and land reforms had successfully transformed Indian agriculture from a pre-capitalist feudal structure into an authentic **Capitalist Mode of Production**, or whether the persistence of sharecropping, usurious debt, and caste-based extra-economic coercion trapped the countryside in a stagnant **Semi-Feudal Mode of Production**.

#### Semi-Feudalism and Usury-Tenancy Traps (Amit Bhaduri, Pradhan H. Prasad, Pranab Bardhan)

Economists and sociologists investigating eastern and central India (Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, eastern Uttar Pradesh)—most notably **Amit Bhaduri** (*A Study in Agricultural Backwardness Under Semi-Feudalism*, **1973**), **Pradhan H. Prasad**, **Nirmal Kumar Chandra**, and **Pranab Bardhan**—argued that Indian agriculture remained structurally **semi-feudal**. According to **Bhaduri**’s formal model, semi-feudalism rested upon four mutually reinforcing pillars:

1.  **Extensive Non-Legalized Sharecropping (Batai/Bargadari):** Landlords leased tiny parcels of land to unregistered tenants-at-will on a 50:50 crop-sharing basis without security of tenure.
2.  **Perpetual Indebtedness of the Small Tenant:** Because the tenant’s harvest share could not cover annual household subsistence, the **Kisan/Bataidar** was forced to take consumption loans (**Kharaki**) from the very same landlord at exorbitant usurious interest rates.
3.  **Conflation of Landlord and Moneylender in a Single Person:** The dominant landowner exercised a dual monopoly over land and credit (**Interlocked Factor Markets**).
4.  **Distress Commercialization and Lack of Market Access:** Tenants had to hand over their entire harvest at the farm-gate during post-harvest price troughs to service debt, only to borrow grain again when prices peaked during the lean season. Crucially, **Bhaduri** demonstrated that under semi-feudalism, landlords *deliberately resisted productivity-enhancing technological innovations*: if yields rose substantially, the sharecropper might clear his consumption debt, shattering the debt-bondage mechanism through which the landlord extracted both usurious interest and political submission.

#### Emerging Agrarian Capitalism and Free Wage Labor (Ashok Rudra, Utsa Patnaik, Jairus Banaji)

Contrasting the eastern semi-feudal thesis with empirical surveys from the Green Revolution heartlands of Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, coastal Andhra, and South Gujarat, **Ashok Rudra** (in collaboration with **A. Majid** and **B.D. Talib**, **1969–1970**), **Utsa Patnaik**, **Daniel Thorner**, and **Gail Omvedt** contended that a distinct class of **Capitalist Farmers** had emerged. However, intense debate erupted over the exact criteria defining agrarian capitalism:

*   **Ashok Rudra’s Statistical Skepticism and Later Shift:** Initially, based on a 1969 random sample survey of 261 large farms (>20 acres) in Punjab, **Rudra** argued that true capitalist farmers had not yet crystallized because a majority of rich farmers did not simultaneously exhibit all five classical indicators (high cash expenditure per acre on wage labor, high ratio of market-sold output, high investment in modern machinery, profit maximization, and self-cultivation). Yet, following rigorous methodological critiques, **Rudra** later acknowledged the polarization of rural society into two antagonistic classes: a class of big landowners (**Kulaks/Capitalist Farmers**) and a class of agricultural laborers.
*   **Utsa Patnaik’s Labor-Exploitation Criterion and Reinvestment:** **Utsa Patnaik** (**1971**, **1976**) argued that the mere presence of wage labor—which had existed in India since colonial de-industrialization created a destitute landless caste stratum—was *necessary* but *not sufficient* to prove capitalism. In a labor-surplus economy lacking urban industrial absorption, destitute peasants paid high pre-capitalist land rents or worked for starvation wages without any capitalist transformation. For **Patnaik**, the decisive criterion of **Agrarian Capitalism** was the **accumulation and productive reinvestment of surplus value** generated by hired wage labor into expanding the technical forces of production (tractors, tubewells, high-yielding inputs) to intensify productivity per acre. She later formulated the **Labor-Exploitation Ratio ($E$)**, measuring the ratio of net labor days hired-in to family labor days expended on the operational holding, to empirically demarcate capitalist landlords, rich peasants, middle peasants, small peasants, and full-time laborers.
*   **The Colonial Mode of Production Synthesis (Hamza Alavi, Jairus Banaji, Paresh Chattopadhyay):** **Paresh Chattopadhyay** challenged Patnaik from an orthodox Marxist standpoint, insisting that generalized commodity production and the purchase of free labor power were sufficient conditions for capitalism. Conversely, **Hamza Alavi** (**1975**) and **Jairus Banaji** (**1972**, **1977**) rejected the false dichotomy between "feudalism" and "capitalism." **Alavi** formulated the concept of a specific **Colonial Mode of Production** (succeeded by a **Post-Colonial Mode**), arguing that British imperialism restructured pre-capitalist agrarian relations—preserving apparent feudal forms like sharecropping and caste subordination while subordinating them entirely to global capitalist reproduction and metropolitan extraction. **Banaji** similarly showed how formal subsumption of labor under capital operated through debt-advances to small peasants who functioned as disguised wage laborers for merchant-usurer capital.

## II. Green Revolution, Bullock Capitalists, and the Dominant Caste Reservation Paradox

### 4. The Rise of Bullock Capitalists and Intermediate Peasant Castes

The twin institutional upheavals of post-Independence rural India—the **First Wave of Land Reforms** (abolition of **Zamindari/Jagirdari** intermediaries during the **1950s**) and the **Green Revolution** (state-subsidized biochemical and mechanical intensification from **1966–1967** onward)—did not distribute land to landless **Dalit Mazdoors**. Instead, they transferred proprietary land titles and agricultural state subsidies directly to the erstwhile occupancy tenants belonging to **intermediate Shudra and dominant peasant castes**. This structural shift generated a powerful rural-political class that fundamentally reconfigured Indian democracy, shifting the locus of power from urban, English-educated upper-caste elites to vernacular agrarian proprietors.

#### Rudolph and Rudolph’s "Bullock Capitalists" and State-Subsidized Agrarian Accumulation

In their seminal political-economic treatise ***In Pursuit of Lakshmi: The Political Economy of the Indian State*** (**1987**), **Lloyd I. Rudolph and Susanne Hoeber Rudolph** coined the concept of **"Bullock Capitalists"** to capture the unique structural position of India’s self-employed, intermediate-sized agricultural proprietors (typically holding between **2.5 and 14.99 acres** of irrigated land). **Rudolph and Rudolph** argued that **Bullock Capitalists** defied both classical Marxist predictions (which anticipated the rapid polarization of the countryside into vast capitalist corporate farms and landless proletarians) and neo-classical modernization theories:

*   **Hybridity of Capital and Family Labor:** Unlike **Thorner**’s absentee **Maliks** or pure capitalist entrepreneurs who rely exclusively on hired wage labor, **Bullock Capitalists** combine ownership of productive capital (land, pair of bullocks or small tractors, electric/diesel pump-sets, **HYV** seeds) with intensive, self-exploitative household family labor, hiring in wage labor only during peak seasonal operations. Because they belonged to traditional cultivating castes (**Jats**, **Kurmis**, **Yadavs**, **Koiris**, **Marathas**, **Vokkaligas**), they bore no ritual taboo against touching the plough, enabling far higher labor efficiency per acre than upper-caste **Brahmin** or **Rajput** supervisory landlords.
*   **Capture of the State Subsidy Regime ("Demand Polity"):** By combining their substantial control over operational landholdings (controlling the single largest share of cultivated acreage in India—over **51%** of total operational area by the **1970s–1980s**) with sheer numerical preponderance under universal adult suffrage, **Bullock Capitalists** organized powerful cross-caste, non-party agrarian pressure groups during the **1970s and 1980s** (**New Farmers’ Movements**). Led by **Chaudhary Charan Singh** (Bharatiya Kranti Dal / Lok Dal in UP), **Mahendra Singh Tikait** (**Bharatiya Kisan Union [BKU]** in Western UP/Punjab), **Sharad Joshi** (**Shetkari Sanghatana** in Maharashtra, mobilizing around the binary of **"Bharat vs. India"**), and **Prof. M.D. Nanjundaswamy** (**Karnataka Rajya Raitha Sangha [KRRS]**), these movements shifted rural politics from intra-village **class struggle** (landless vs. landlord) to **sectoral populism** (rural agriculturalists demanding higher **Minimum Support Prices [MSP]**, free electricity, fertilizer subsidies, and institutional loan waivers from the urban-industrial state).

#### Regional Cartography of Dominant Peasant Castes (Jats, Marathas, Patidars, Kapus, Kammas, Reddys, Vokkaligas, Lingayats, Yadavs)

Connecting **Rudolph and Rudolph**’s political economy with **M.N. Srinivas**’s anthropological paradigm of the **Dominant Caste** (formulated in ***The Dominant Caste in Rampura***, **1959**, where dominance is defined by **(1) sizable ownership of arable land**, **(2) numerical strength**, **(3) a non-low position in the local ritual hierarchy**, and **(4) modern education and urban administrative contacts**), Indian agrarian sociology maps two distinct historical waves of intermediate peasant caste ascendancy across regional political economies:

*   **First-Tier Landed Dominant Castes (Early Capitalist Diversifiers):** Comprising **Jats** (Punjab, Haryana, Western UP, Rajasthan), **Marathas** (Maharashtra), **Patidars / Kanbis** (Gujarat), **Kammas** and **Reddys** (Andhra Pradesh and Telangana), **Kapus** (Coastal Andhra), **Vokkaligas** (Old Mysuru / Southern Karnataka), and **Lingayats / Veerashaivas** (Northern Karnataka). Historically occupying the status of self-cultivating warrior-peasants or **Sat-Shudras** right below the twice-born (**Dwija**) castes, these groups already controlled village headmanships (**Patel**, **Deshmukh**, **Chaudhary**, **Reddy**, **Gowda**) under colonial **Ryotwari** and **Mahalwari** settlements. During the **1960s–1990s**, **Paul Brass**, **Francine Frankel**, and **K. Balagopal** documented how these castes converted agrarian surplus from sugarcane, cotton, paddy, tobacco, and dairy cooperatives into regional agro-industrial capitalism:
    *   **Kammas and Reddys** in Andhra Pradesh channeled deltaic paddy and tobacco surpluses from the Krishna-Godavari basin into cinema studios, vernacular print/television media, private engineering and medical colleges, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and infrastructure contracting (**Carol Upadhya**’s study of the **Kamma** entrepreneurial elite).
    *   **Patidars** in Central and South Gujarat utilized tobacco and cash-crop wealth, white-revolution dairy cooperatives (**AMUL**), and transnational kinship networks in East Africa, the UK, and the US to build diamond-polishing, textile, and chemical SMEs.
    *   **Marathas** in Western Maharashtra constructed an entrenched rural politico-economic hegemony through interconnected sugar cooperatives (**Sakhar Karkhanas**), District Central Cooperative Banks, and agricultural credit societies (**Donald Attwood**, **Suhas Palshikar**).
*   **Second-Tier OBC Cultivating Castes (The Mandal & Silent Revolution Ascendancy):** Comprising **Yadavs / Ahirs** (pastoral-agriculturalists), **Kurmis**, **Koiris / Kushwahas**, and **Lodhs** in the Gangetic plains (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh), alongside **Vanniyars** and **Thevars** in Tamil Nadu. Operating primarily under the oppressive colonial **Zamindari** system in eastern India, their rise occurred later via the abolition of landlordism, dairy and vegetable market Gardening, and the post-**1990 Mandal Commission** political mobilization analyzed by **Christophe Jaffrelot** in ***India’s Silent Revolution: The Rise of the Lower Castes in North India*** (**2003**).

### 5. Contemporary Agrarian Stagnation and the Dominant Caste Quota Paradox

By the second and third decades of the twenty-first century, the structural trajectory of India’s **Dominant Peasant Castes** underwent a dramatic sociological inversion. The very communities that once boasted of their lordly agrarian autonomy (**Sardar**, **Chaudhary**, **Patil**, **Patel**) and fiercely opposed the **1990 Mandal Commission** reservations began launching massive, often violent street agitations demanding downward reclassification as **"Socially and Educationally Backward Classes" (OBCs)**—attempting to trade their historical claim to high ritual and secular status for constitutional affirmative action quotas in public employment and higher education.

#### Land Fragmentation, Ecological Exhaustion, and the Crisis of Capital Reproduction

This inversion is driven by a terminal structural crisis in the reproduction of agrarian capital. Under **Mitakshara** coparcenary inheritance rules—where agricultural land is subdivided equally among heirs in every succeeding generation (further expanded to daughters under the **Hindu Succession [Amendment] Act, 2005** and affirmed retrospectively by the Supreme Court in ***Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma***, **2020**)—the robust **Bullock Capitalist** holdings of the **1970s** have shattered into economically unviable micro-plots:

*   **Empirical Fragmentation Metrics (Agriculture Census & NSO Situational Assessment Survey):** According to successive **Agriculture Censuses (2015–16 and 2021–22 parameters)** and the **NSO 77th Round Situational Assessment Survey of Agricultural Households**, the average size of an operational landholding in India has plummeted from **2.28 hectares in 1970–71** to **1.08 hectares in 2015–16**, and further down toward **~0.9–1.0 hectare** in the mid-2020s. **Marginal (<1 hectare) and Small (1–2 hectares) farmers now constitute 86.2% of all operational holdings in India**, cultivating nearly **47.3%** of the total operated area. A dominant-caste patriarch who owned 16 acres in 1970 left 4 acres to each of his four sons in 1995, whose sons in **2026** inherit barely **1 to 1.5 acres** each.
*   **Ecological Exhaustion and the Input-Output Price Squeeze:** In the Green Revolution heartlands of Punjab, Haryana, Western Maharashtra, and Gujarat, intensive monocropping of water-guzzling paddy, wheat, and sugarcane has triggered catastrophic **groundwater table depletion** (requiring deep, capital-intensive submersible borewells), soil salinization, and declining marginal returns to chemical fertilizers (**NPK** imbalance). Simultaneously, deregulation of input markets has caused the cost of diesel, hybrid/GM seeds, pesticides, and private rural healthcare/education to escalate far faster than farm-gate crop realizations, eroding net cultivation income to less than **37% of the average agricultural household’s total monthly income** (with wage income taking the lead in **NSO** surveys).

#### Dominant Landed Castes Seeking Backwardness: Sociological Anatomy of OBC/EWS Agitations and Judicial Boundaries

As examined by **Christophe Jaffrelot**, **Surinder S. Jodhka**, **Satish Deshpande**, and **Ashwini Deshpande**, the contemporary agitations by **Jats** (in Haryana, Rajasthan, and Western UP), **Marathas** (**Maratha Kranti Morcha** in Maharashtra), **Patidars** (**Patidar Anamat Andolan Samiti [PAAS]** in Gujarat), and **Kapus** (in Andhra Pradesh) represent a profound **crisis of blocked non-farm transition** coupled with **relative deprivation**:

*   **Internal Class Stratification within Dominant Castes:** While a thin billionaire and upper-middle-class stratum of **Marathas**, **Patidars**, and **Jats** controls cooperative banks, sugar mills, diamond firms, and state cabinets, the bottom **60–70%** of their caste brethren are marginal **Kunbi-Maratha** cultivators in drought-prone **Marathwada** and **Vidarbha**, small **Kadva/Leuva Patel** farmers in Saurashtra, or land-fragmented **Jat** youths in Rohtak and Sonipat. Equipped with vernacular rural schooling or low-tier private degrees, these youths lack the cosmopolitan **cultural and linguistic capital** (**Pierre Bourdieu**) monopolized by urban upper castes (**Brahmins**, **Baniyas**, **Kayasthas**) to enter high-paying corporate services, yet consider manual informal labor beneath their historical **Kshatriya/Dominant Caste** dignity.
*   **Status Anxiety and the Politicization of Relative Deprivation (Robert K. Merton):** Simultaneously, these youths witness historically subordinate **Scheduled Castes** and recognized **OBCs** (**Yadavs**, **Kurmis**, **Malas**) entering state civil services, police forces, and public universities through reservations. Government jobs in rural India carry disproportionate social prestige, matrimonial premium, and stable rent-seeking security. Consequently, dominant castes weaponize their rural numerical hegemony to coerce state governments into granting them **OBC** status—inverting **M.N. Srinivas**’s **Sanskritization** into **"De-Sanskritization"** or **"Downward Competitive Backwardness."**
*   **Constitutional Jurisprudence and the Supreme Court’s Empirical Gatekeeping:** The Supreme Court of India has repeatedly struck down state attempts to appease dominant peasant castes with **OBC** reservations when objective social backwardness is absent:
    *   In ***Ram Singh v. Union of India*** (**2015**), the Supreme Court struck down the inclusion of **Jats** in the Central **OBC** list across nine states, holding that while backwardness need not be solely caste-based, **historical social and educational backwardness**—distinct from mere contemporary economic distress—remains the constitutional *sine qua non* under **Articles 15(4) and 16(4)**, and that the **National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)**’s empirical rejection could not be overridden for political expediency.
    *   In the landmark 5-Judge Constitution Bench verdict in ***Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. The Chief Minister (Maratha Reservation Case)*** (**May 2021**), the Supreme Court struck down the **Maharashtra Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBC) Act, 2018**, which had granted separate reservations to **Marathas**. The Court ruled that **Marathas** are a politically and socially dominant mainstream community occupying over **30–40%** of state legislative and bureaucratic posts; that no "extraordinary circumstances" existed to breach the **50% reservation ceiling** established in ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India*** (**1992**); and that under the **102nd Constitutional Amendment Act (2018)** (**Articles 338B and 342A**), states had lost the power to unilaterally notify **SEBCs** (a power subsequently restored to states by Parliament via the **105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021**).
    *   In **February 2024**, the Maharashtra legislature enacted a fresh **10% SEBC reservation for Marathas** based on the **Justice Sunil Shukre Backward Class Commission Report**, alongside issuing **Kunbi** (**OBC**) certificates based on historical **Nizam-era** revenue records, keeping the constitutional friction between dominant-caste agitations, existing **OBC** groups fearing quota encroachment, and the **50% ceiling** (also tested in the **Patna High Court’s June 2024 strike-down** of Bihar’s **65% quota expansion** post-**2023 Bihar Caste Survey**) at the center of judicial scrutiny.
    *   **The EWS Constitutional Valve:** Crucially, the Union Government enacted the **103rd Constitutional Amendment Act (2019)**, inserting **Articles 15(6) and 16(6)** to provide a **10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)** among non-SC/ST/OBC (general category) citizens with annual family incomes below **₹8 lakh** and agricultural land below **5 acres**. Upheld by a **3:2 majority** of a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India*** (**November 2022**), the **EWS** quota structurally functioned as a constitutional safety valve—absorbing the downward-mobility anxiety of unreserved dominant peasant castes (**Marathas**, **Patidars**, **Kapus**, upper-caste smallholders) into the affirmative action architecture without forcing them to pass the stringent test of historical social stigma required for **OBC** status.

## III. Agrarian Labor Regimes: Footloose Proletarianization, Neo-Bondage, and Gendered Dispossession

### 6. Jan Breman’s "Footloose Labour," Neo-Bondage, and Circular Distress Migration

While **Bullock Capitalists** and dominant peasant castes navigated the contradictions of land fragmentation and political hegemony, the bottom tier of the agrarian hierarchy—the landless **Mazdoors**, overwhelmingly drawn from **Scheduled Castes (Dalits)** and **Scheduled Tribes (Adivasis)**—experienced a radically different structural trajectory. Rather than transitioning into a stable, unionized rural or urban industrial proletariat as envisioned by classical European modernization theory, India’s landless rural poor were cast out of hereditary village patronage into a hyper-precarious, seasonally circulating reserve army of labor.

#### From Halpati Patronage to Monetized Neo-Bondage in Capitalist Agriculture

Through over five decades of longitudinal ethnographic fieldwork in the **Bardoli** and **Surat** plains of South Gujarat—published in foundational monographs including ***Patronage and Exploitation: Changing Agrarian Relations in South Gujarat*** (**1974**), ***Of Peasants, Migrants and Paupers*** (**1985**), ***Footloose Labour: Working in India’s Informal Economy*** (**1996**), and ***Capitalism, Inequality and Labour in India*** (**2019**)—Dutch social anthropologist **Jan Breman** dismantled the orthodox liberal-Marxist assumption that the rise of agrarian capitalism inevitably replaces unfree feudal labor with "free" contractual wage labor:

*   **Dissolution of the Traditional Halpati System:** In pre-Green Revolution South Gujarat, agricultural labor was organized through the **Halpati system** (derived from *Hal* [plough] and *Pati* [lord/holder], locally known as **Hali-Dhani**). Landless tribal **Dublas** (later renamed **Halpatis** by Mahatma Gandhi) were bound for life to dominant **Anavil Brahmin** and **Kanbi/Patidar** landowners (**Dhanis**). Although the **Hali** was unfree—initially tied through an marriage loan (**Khavti**)—the **Dhani** bore a customary paternalistic obligation (**Patronage**) to provide year-round subsistence grain, medical aid, clothing, and housing even during slack agricultural months when no fieldwork was needed.
*   **Capitalist "Neo-Bondage" (Unfreedom without Patronage):** With the arrival of canal irrigation from the **Ukai dam**, commercial sugarcane cultivation, and mechanization, **Patidar** and **Anavil Brahmin** capitalist farmers stripped away every vestige of customary patronage—refusing to feed or house **Halpatis** during lean seasons. Yet, instead of relying on open, free labor markets, capitalist employers instituted **Neo-Bondage**: a purely monetized, stripped-down, contractual debt-attachment administered through labor contractors (**Mukadams**, **Jamadars**, or **Sardars**). Under **Neo-Bondage**, the **Mukadam** distributes a cash advance (**Peshgi** or **Kharchi**) to destitute **Adivasi** or **Dalit** households during the monsoon hunger months when local employment is zero. In exchange, the worker (and often the entire household unit, such as the **Koyta** husband-wife team in sugarcane harvesting or the **Jodi** pair in brick kilns) is legally and physically bound to migrate to distant sugarcane fields, brick kilns, salt pans, or stone quarries for 6 to 8 months.
*   **Structural Comparison of Old Bondage vs. Neo-Bondage:** Unlike traditional feudal bondage (**Halpati**, **Jeetha** in Karnataka, **Pannaiyal** in Tamil Nadu, **Kamiauti** in Bihar/Jharkhand), which was lifetime, multi-generational, embedded in village ritual hierarchy, and tempered by subsistence guarantees, **Breman** shows that **Neo-Bondage** is **(1) seasonal or time-bound**, **(2) strictly economic and impersonal** (void of paternalistic care; sick or aged workers are instantly discarded), **(3) mediated by sub-contractors** from the workers' own caste/tribe who enforce discipline, and **(4) designed specifically to depress wages below the market equilibrium and prevent worker unionization** during time-sensitive capitalist production cycles. Wages are withheld until the very end of the season, reducing the worker to survival handouts (**Kharchi**) while in the labor camp.

#### Circular Distress Migration and the Rural-Urban Informal Continuum

Expelled from a year-round agricultural livelihood yet denied permanent absorption into the urban formal sector, these workers become what **Jan Breman** terms **"Footloose Labour"**—a massive, floating proletariat numbering over **100 to 120 million seasonal and circular migrants** across India (**Economic Survey** and **UNESCO/UNICEF** internal migration estimates). Their mobility follows two distinct spatial vectors that expose the ruthlessness of contemporary rural class warfare:

1.  **Rural-to-Rural Capitalist Circulation (Importing Migrants to Discipline Local Dalits):** One of **Breman**’s most profound sociological discoveries in South Gujarat (and echoed by **Surinder Jodhka** and **Jens Lerche** in Punjab and Western UP) is that prosperous **Patidar** and **Jat** farmers actively *bypass local landless **Halpati** and **Chamar** laborers*—even when local workers are unemployed right outside the fields—and instead import hundreds of thousands of seasonal migrant **Koytas** from the impoverished tribal drylands of **Khandesh (Maharashtra)** and **Dang**, or from **Bihar and Eastern UP**. Capitalist farmers deploy migrant labor as a deliberate political-economic weapon: imported migrants live in makeshift plastic tents on the field edge, have no local kinship support or voting rights, can be worked 14 hours a day around the clock, and serve to break the bargaining power and nascent political assertiveness of local **Dalit** workers.
2.  **Distress-Driven Circularity ("Hunter-Gatherers of Wage Labor"):** Pushed out of the village by agrarian distress (**Push Factors**) and repelled from permanent settlement in cities by prohibitive housing costs, lack of civic entitlements, and hostile policing (**Exclusionary Urbanization**), footloose laborers circulate endlessly between the village and informal work sites. The village functions not as a romantic agrarian refuge, but as a **"waiting room"** and unpaid social-reproduction sink where pregnant women, children, and the broken elderly are left behind while prime-age labor power is extracted by urban and agrarian capital at below-subsistence cost.

### 7. Feminization of Agricultural Labor vs. Patriarchal Land Dispossession

As male members of marginal peasant and landless households circulate outward as **footloose migrants** into peri-urban construction, transport, brick kilns, and informal services, the structural composition of the workforce remaining inside Indian agriculture has undergone a profound gender shift known sociologically as the **"Feminization of Agriculture"**—or more accurately, the **"Agrarianization of the Female Workforce"** (**Bina Agarwal**, **Vina Mazumdar**, **Padmini Swaminathan**, **Sujata Patel**). However, far from signifying women’s economic empowerment or autonomous control over the agrarian means of production, this phenomenon represents a **feminization of agrarian drudgery and distress**: women bear the overwhelming physical burden of cultivation while remaining structurally dispossessed of legal land titles, institutional credit, irrigation water rights, and market agency.

#### Creeping Feminization of Agrarian Drudgery (PLFS, NSO Time-Use Survey 2024, and Agriculture Census Metrics)

Empirical data from the **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS 2023–24)**, the **NSO Time-Use Survey (2024)**, **NFHS-5 (2019–21)**, and the **Agriculture Census** expose a stark structural asymmetry between women’s labor absorption in agriculture and their ownership of productive assets:

*   **Concentration of Female Workforce in Agriculture (PLFS Metrics):** While the share of male workers engaged in agriculture declined steadily from **80.6% in 1977–78** to **~45–46% by 2023–24** due to male diversification into non-farm sectors, **agriculture continues to absorb 64.4% of all working women in India** (and nearly **76.2% of all rural female workers** in the **2023–24 PLFS** baseline). Crucially, the post-2018 bounce in India’s aggregate Female Labour Force Participation Rate (**FLFPR**, rising to **41.7% in 2023–24**) has been driven overwhelmingly by rural women entering **unpaid family helper roles** on fragmented household farms or self-employed subsistence animal husbandry to cushion household income shocks—rather than entry into salaried formal employment.
*   **Gendered Segregation of Tasks and Wage Discrimination:** Within the agricultural production cycle, tasks are rigidly segregated by Brahmanical and customary patriarchal norms. Ploughing (historically subject to a universal cultural taboo prohibiting women from touching the plough across India, studied by **Leela Dube** through the **"Seed and Earth"** metaphor where the male provides the seed/ploughshare and the female is the passive earth), tractor driving, irrigation management, and wholesale mandi marketing are monopolized by men. Conversely, the most back-breaking, labor-intensive, stoop-labor operations—**paddy transplanting in knee-deep stagnant water, manual weeding, cotton picking, tea-leaf plucking, threshing, and winnowing**—are designated as "unskilled female tasks" and paid **25% to 40% less** than male agricultural wages for equivalent hours. Furthermore, whenever an agricultural operation is mechanized (e.g., mechanical rice transplanters or combine harvesters), control of the machine immediately transfers to men, displacing female wage laborers.
*   **Double Burden and Time Poverty (NSO Time-Use Survey 2024):** The **NSO Time-Use Survey (2024)** (released in early 2025) empirically demonstrates that rural women are trapped in an acute **Care-Economy and Subsistence Double Burden**: rural females spend an average of **Over 300+ minutes (5+ hours) per day on unpaid domestic services and care work** (compared to under 50 minutes for rural men performing such tasks), alongside unpaid collection of firewood, fodder, and water, and unremunerated labor on family farms—yielding severe nutritional depletion (**57% anemia prevalence among women aged 15–49 in NFHS-5**).

#### Bina Agarwal’s "A Field of One’s Own" and the Structural Denial of Female Legal Land Titles

In her classic feminist political-economic work ***A Field of One’s Own: Gender and Land Rights in South Asia*** (**1994**), **Bina Agarwal** demonstrated that the single most decisive determinant of a rural woman’s economic security, intra-household bargaining power (within **Amartya Sen**’s **Cooperative Conflict** household model), and protection against domestic violence and abandonment is **independent legal ownership and effective control over arable land**. Yet, across India, a vast chasm separates female agricultural labor from female land ownership:

*   **The Empirical Land Ownership Chasm:** According to the **Agriculture Census**, women operate only **13.96% of all operational holdings** (covering just **11.72% of operated area**), and according to the **India Human Development Survey (IHDS)** and **NCAER Land Records and Services Index**, women hold legal title to barely **8.3% to 14% of private agricultural land** (mostly as widows rather than daughters).
*   **Why Constitutional Law Fails Against Kinship Structure (The 2005 HSA vs. "Haq Tyag"):** Prior to **2005**, Section 4(2) of the **Hindu Succession Act (HSA), 1956** explicitly exempted agricultural tenures governed by state land-reform laws from central gender-equal inheritance rules, allowing Northern states (such as UP under the **UP Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act, 1950**, Punjab, and Haryana) to vest devolution of agricultural land exclusively in male lineal descendants to prevent "fragmentation." Although the **Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005** deleted Section 4(2) and made daughters equal **Mitakshara** coparceners by birth in all property including agricultural land—a right made unconditionally retroactive by the Supreme Court in ***Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma*** (**2020**)—empirical studies by **Bina Agarwal**, **Prem Chowdhry**, and **Nitya Rao** show why de jure equality fails to translate into de facto land titles:
    1.  **Patrilocal Village Exogamy:** Especially in **Iravati Karve**’s **Northern Kinship Zone**, daughters marry outside their natal village into distant affinal villages, making physical cultivation of natal plots socially and logistically impossible without male mediation.
    2.  **Coercive "Haq Tyag" (Voluntary Renunciation) and Maternal Altruism:** Under intense patriarchal pressure, daughters are routinely made to sign formal relinquishment deeds (**Haq Tyag**) transferring their ancestral land shares to their brothers immediately after the father's death. Refusing to sign **Haq Tyag** is stigmatized as destroying fraternal bonds (**Bhai-Behen** reciprocity on **Raksha Bandhan/Bhai Dooj**) and forfeiting the natal home (**Maika/Pihar**) as a refuge in the event of marital crisis or dowry harassment.
    3.  **Ideology of Dowry as Pre-Mortem Inheritance:** Patriarchal custom treats marriage expenses and **Dowry** (which rarely consists of productive real estate and is controlled by the husband’s family) as having permanently extinguished the daughter’s claim to ancestral agricultural land.
    4.  **Bureaucratic Obstruction by Patwaris/Tehsildars:** Local village revenue officers (**Patwaris/Lekhpals**) routinely omit daughters' names during mutation of land records (**Virashat/Intkal**) following a landowner’s death, recording only male heirs.
*   **Exclusion from the Institutional "Kisan" Identity:** Because Indian agrarian state policy defines a **"Farmer" (Kisan)** not by who performs agricultural labor on the soil, but by whose name appears on the **7/12 extract or Khasra-Khatauni land revenue title**, women farmers are rendered legally invisible as "cultivators' wives" or "helpers." Without a land title (**Patta**), women farmers cannot obtain **Kisan Credit Cards (KCC)**, collateralized bank loans, **PM-KISAN** direct income transfers, subsidized seeds/irrigation equipment, crop insurance (**PMFBY**), or registration at **APMC** procurement mandis—forcing them into high-interest microfinance (**MFI**) debt traps when male migrants default on remittances.

## IV. Agrarian Anomie, De-Agrarianization, and the Rurban Metamorphosis

### 8. The Sociology of Farmer Suicides: Durkheimian Anomic and Fatalistic Currents

Since the mid-**1990s**, the structural contradictions of post-liberalization Indian agriculture have manifested in a tragic social epidemic: **over 400,000 recorded suicides by farmers and agricultural laborers** between **1995 and 2026** according to **National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB)** *Accidental Deaths & Suicides in India* (**ADSI**) reports (with recent annual figures hovering around **10,700 to 11,300 deaths per year**, split almost evenly between cultivating farmers and landless agricultural laborers). Crucially, sociological mapping by **A.R. Vasavi**, **Surinder S. Jodhka**, **K. Nagaraj**, **Srijit Mishra**, and **D.N. Reddy** reveals a striking spatial paradox: **farmer suicides are NOT concentrated in the poorest, subsistence-oriented, rain-fed feudal regions of eastern India (Bihar or Odisha), but overwhelmingly in the commercially integrated, cash-crop-intensive states** of **Maharashtra (Vidarbha and Marathwada)**, **Karnataka**, **Andhra Pradesh**, **Telangana**, **Madhya Pradesh**, and **Punjab**.

#### Individualization of Risk, Cash-Crop Volatility, and Status Anxiety in Commercialized Agriculture

In her landmark anthropological monograph ***Shadow Space: Suicides and the Predicament of Rural India*** (**2012**), **A.R. Vasavi** conceptualizes the agrarian suicide epidemic through the framework of **"Individualization"** (**Ulrich Beck**) and **"Dissonance"**:

1.  **Commercialization of Subsistence and Input-Centric Deskilling:** Post-1991 trade liberalization and structural adjustment dismantled state agricultural extension networks and exposed smallholders to volatile global commodity price swings (especially in **Bt Cotton**, **soybean**, **groundnut**, **chilli**, and **onions**). Traditional ecological polyculture and indigenous seed-saving (**Orthogenetic agrarian knowledge**) were replaced by commercial monocropping dependent on proprietary corporate seeds, chemical pesticides, and deep borewells. **Vasavi** terms this **"Deskilling"**: farmers lost autonomous control over their ecological inputs and became captive consumers of aggressive local agro-input dealers (**Krishi Seva Kendras**).
2.  **The "Triad of Distress" (Arhtiya / Input Dealer Nexus):** In commercialized regions, the **Arhtiya** (commission agent in Punjab) or the **Agro-Input Dealer-cum-Moneylender** in Vidarbha and Telangana operates an interlocked credit-input-marketing trap. Marginal and small farmers—and notably **upwardly mobile OBC, Dalit, and Adivasi cultivators** attempting to emulate dominant-caste commercial farming without deep capital buffers or canal irrigation—borrow heavily at **24% to 60% annual interest** to sink multiple failed borewells and buy expensive inputs. A single monsoon failure, pest attack (such as the **pink bollworm** in Bt cotton), or global price crash triggers catastrophic debt insolvency.
3.  **Loss of Honor (Izzat / Abhru) and Patriarchal Status Humiliation:** In rural Indian social psychology, land ownership and financial solvency are inseparably fused with masculine family honor (**Izzat** in North India, **Abhru** in Maharashtra, **Maan-Maryada**). When private moneylenders, microfinance recovery agents, or bank officials publicly humiliate a farmer at the village square, seize his tractor/cattle, or paste auction notices on his house, or when a father cannot assemble the **Dowry** required to marry off his daughter under hypergamous caste norms, the resulting collapse of social dignity becomes unbearable—frequently driving the farmer to consume the very organophosphate pesticide bought on credit to save his crop.

#### Durkheimian Anomie vs. Fatalism in the Indian Countryside (A.R. Vasavi, Surinder Jodhka, NCRB Data)

Applying **Émile Durkheim**’s classical four-fold typology of suicide from ***Le Suicide*** (**1897**)— structured along the axes of **Social Integration** (Egoistic vs. Altruistic) and **Social Regulation** (Anomic vs. Fatalistic)—sociologists demonstrate that Indian farmer suicides represent a lethal convergence of **Anomic**, **Egoistic**, and **Fatalistic** currents:

*   **Anomic Suicide (Deregulation and Aspirational Dissonance):** **Durkheim** defined **Anomie** as a state of moral deregulation and normlessness triggered by rapid, unregulated economic upheaval in which traditional social restraints collapse and newly inflamed desires outstrip the structural means to satisfy them (**Robert K. Merton**’s **Strain Theory**). In the post-1991 Indian village, satellite television, smartphones, and urban consumerism have infused rural households with middle-class aspirations (English-medium private schooling, motorcycles, elaborate weddings), while state withdrawal from agriculture and market volatility have shattered the stable regulatory framework of farming. The farmer is cast into a normative vacuum where the rules of traditional agriculture no longer yield survival.
*   **Egoistic Suicide (Atomization and Collapse of Village Collective Solidarity):** Concurrently, the competitive individualization of borewell drilling (where one farmer’s deeper tubewell sucks dry his neighbor’s well) and the disintegration of caste/kinship mutual-aid networks have eroded **social integration**. The indebted peasant suffers in isolated secrecy (**Egoism**), lacking a supportive community moral cushion.
*   **Fatalistic Suicide (Hyper-Regulation, Debt Entrapment, and Blocked Futures):** While **Durkheim** treated **Fatalistic Suicide** (resulting from excessive regulation, where "futures are pitilessly blocked and passions violently choked by oppressive discipline") as a minor historical footnote applicable to slaves, Indian sociologists (**B.B. Mohanty**, **A.R. Vasavi**) show that **Fatalism** is central to the suicides of marginal sharecroppers, **Dalit/Adivasi** agricultural laborers (who account for **~50% of all agricultural suicides in recent NCRB reports**), and heavily indebted smallholders. Trapped in an inescapable iron cage of usurious compound interest, caste humiliation, patriarchal dowry obligations, and ecological exhaustion with zero exit pathways, suicide emerges as a fatalistic response to absolute structural closure.

### 9. Dipankar Gupta’s "Whither the Indian Village?", Rural Non-Farm Employment, and Rurbanization

The cumulative weight of **Jajmani** collapse, land fragmentation, **Bullock Capitalist** stagnation, circular migration, and agrarian distress has forced contemporary sociology to confront a foundational ontological question: *Does the "Indian village" still exist as a coherent agrarian sociological category, or has it fundamentally mutated into a rurban residential appendix of the non-farm economy?*

#### The "Vanishing Village" Thesis and the Decoupling of Rural Residence from Agrarian Occupation

In his provocative and influential essay ***"Whither the Indian Village? Culture and Agriculture in 'Rural' India"*** (*Economic and Political Weekly*, **2005**) and his subsequent monograph ***The Caged Phoenix: Can India Fly?*** (**2009**), sociologist **Dipankar Gupta** declared that the traditional Indian village—conceived by **M.N. Srinivas**, **Louis Dumont**, and **McKim Marriott** as an organic socio-cultural cosmos anchored in agriculture—is **vanishing before our eyes**:

*   **"The Village as a Waiting Room":** **Dipankar Gupta** argues that while **~65% of India’s population** continues to be classified as **"Rural"** by residence, agriculture has ceased to be the cultural and economic anchor of village life. Because **over 86% of holdings are marginal or small (<2 hectares)** and contribute less than a sixth of national GDP, agriculture can no longer sustain either the **Malik**, the **Kisan**, or the **Mazdoor**. Consequently, across classes and castes, the cultural ethos of the villager is defined by an urgent desire to *exit agriculture* (**"Townward Ho"**). As **Surinder S. Jodhka** corroborated in his longitudinal revisiting of Haryana villages (***Village Society***, **2012**; ***The Indian Village: Rural Lives in the 21st Century***, **2023**), the village is increasingly reduced to a residential site where people sleep and maintain ancestral homes while commuting daily or seasonally to non-agricultural work.
*   **Discrete Castes and the Collapse of Rural Holistic Hierarchy:** **Gupta** links this economic de-agrarianization directly to the mutation of caste: without a functioning **Jajmani** system or a unified village agrarian economy to enforce daily ritual hierarchy, castes no longer accept a vertical **Dumontian** ladder of purity and pollution. Instead, they operate as **"Discrete Castes"** engaged in horizontal political competition, asserting their own mythical origin tales, building separate **Gurdwaras** (such as **Dalit Ravidassia/Ad-Dharmi** shrines in Punjab) or caste halls, and refusing subordination to the erstwhile **Dominant Caste**.

#### Rural Non-Farm Employment (RNFE), Census Towns, and the Architecture of Rurbanization

The structural vacuum left by receding agriculture has been filled by the rapid expansion of **Rural Non-Farm Employment (RNFE)** and the spatial phenomenon of **Rurbanization**—a concept originally introduced into Indian sociology by **G.S. Ghurye** in ***Anatomy of a Rurban Community*** (**1963**) to describe rural settlements acquiring urban infrastructural, occupational, and consumption traits without losing their village spatial morphology:

*   **Anatomy of the Rural Non-Farm Economy (RNFE):** According to **NABARD’s All India Rural Financial Inclusion Survey (NAFIS)**, the **Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES 2022–23 and 2023–24)**, and **PLFS** datasets, **nearly half to over 55% of total rural household income** in India now originates from non-agricultural sources: rural construction (stimulated by **PM Awas Yojana - Gramin** and highway corridors), transport (e-rickshaws, tempos, trucking), brick kilns, agro-processing, petty retail shops (**Kirana**), mobile repair, salon services, and public-sector employment (schoolteachers, **Anganwadi/ASHA** workers, **MGNREGA**). Crucially, access to **RNFE** is sharply bifurcated by caste and class:
    *   **Accumulative RNFE (Upper & Dominant Castes):** Landed **Maliks** and prosperous **Bullock Capitalists** who sell agricultural plots along expanding national highways or peri-urban corridors reinvest huge real-estate windfalls into capital-intensive non-farm enterprises: marriage palaces, cold-storage chains, rice mills, stone crushers, petrol pumps, private coaching/school franchises, and liquor vends (**Aseem Prakash**, **K. Balagopal**).
    *   **Distress / Survivalist RNFE (Dalits, Adivasis, and Marginal OBCs):** Landless **Mazdoors** and marginal peasants enter low-return, high-drudgery casual wage work in construction or self-employed petty vending. Yet, even precarious **RNFE** carries transformative sociological significance: it liberates **Dalit** workers from daily ritual dependence on village landlords, narrowing the **Rural-Urban Consumption Expenditure Gap** to **69.7% in HCES 2023–24** (down from 83.9% in 2011–12).
*   **Subaltern Urbanization, Census Towns, and the Rurban Policy Framework:** As documented by **Éric Denis** and **Marie-Hélène Zérah** in ***Subaltern Urbanization in India*** (**2017**), thousands of large villages have crossed the demographic thresholds of a **"Census Town"** (**population > 5,000**, **density > 400 persons per sq. km**, and **>75% of the male main workforce engaged in non-agricultural pursuits**)—surging from **1,362 Census Towns in 2001 to 3,892 in 2011**, and projected to exceed **6,000 to 8,000 in the forthcoming Digital Population Census 2027**. However, many such settlements deliberately resist conversion into statutory municipalities (**Nagar Panchayats**) so that residents can retain rural tax exemptions, lower electricity tariffs, **MGNREGA** entitlements, and **73rd Amendment Gram Panchayat** funds. To address the acute infrastructural deficits (sewage, solid waste, piped water, skill centers) of these hybrid spaces, the Indian state launched the **Shyama Prasad Mukherji Rurban Mission (SPMRM)** (**2016**), organizing **300 Rurban Clusters** around the principle of **" preserving the soul of the village with the facilities of the city."**

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 7: From Peasant De-Agrarianization to the Adivasi Resource Frontier

While the caste-stratified agrarian plains of India have undergone this turbulent trajectory—from **Jajmani** patronage and **Bullock Capitalist** ascendancy to land fragmentation, **footloose labor** circulation, and **Rurbanization**—a fundamentally distinct yet deeply entangled sociological confrontation unfolds across India’s forested plateaus, river valleys, and frontier hills. As the caste peasantry’s agrarian economy stagnates and urban-industrial capitalism hungers for coal, bauxite, iron ore, timber, and hydroelectric power, the extractive gaze of the state and corporate capital shifts aggressively toward the customary homelands of India’s **705+ Scheduled Tribes (Adivasis)**. The footloose tribal **Koyta** sugarcane harvesters documented by **Jan Breman** in the fields of South Gujarat are the direct human casualties of dispossession in the **Adivasi** heartland.
