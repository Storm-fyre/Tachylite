## I. Structural Trajectory & The Civil Nuclear Watershed: From Estranged Democracies to De-Hyphenated Balancing

### 1. Structural Realism vs. Ideational Friction: Cold War Estrangement to Post-1998 Convergence

The trajectory of Indo-US relations illustrates a fundamental axiom of **structural realism**: shared domestic political institutions (**democratic peace theory**) cannot override systemic polarity and divergent regional threat perceptions, whereas a shifting distribution of capabilities in an anarchical international system compels strategic convergence even between historically distrustful states. Throughout the Cold War, India and the United States operated as what diplomatic historian **Dennis Kux** famously termed **"estranged democracies."** While both states shared constitutional liberalism, rule of law, and electoral democracy, their grand strategies collided along the fault lines of **bipolarity** and **regional security dilemmas**.

#### The Bipolar Security Dilemma and the Non-Aligned Friction Matrix (1947–1991)
* **Containment Alliances vs. Non-Aligned Strategic Autonomy:** Following World War II, the United States prioritized global perimeter containment against the Soviet Union and Maoist China, viewing Jawaharlal Nehru’s doctrine of **Non-Alignment** as moral equivocation. Conversely, New Delhi viewed Washington’s incorporation of Pakistan into the **1954 Mutual Defense Assistance Agreement**, **SEATO (1954)**, and the **Baghdad Pact/CENTO (1955)** as an intrusion of Cold War military alliances into the subcontinental security architecture, artificially altering the regional balance of power and fueling an enduring **security dilemma** (as theorized by **Robert Jervis**).
* **The 1962 Interlude and the 1971 Nadir:** Although the Kennedy Administration provided emergency military supplies during the **October–November 1962 Sino-Indian Border War**, Washington’s linkage of long-term defense aid to a Kashmir settlement and its refusal to supply supersonic combat aircraft pushed India toward Soviet defense industrialization (beginning with the **1962 MiG-21 licensing agreement**). This divergence reached its strategic nadir during the **1971 Bangladesh Liberation War**, when the Nixon–Kissinger administration—seeking to utilize Yahya Khan’s military regime in Islamabad as a diplomatic conduit for the **1971–1972 Sino-US rapprochement**—executed a geopolitical "tilt" toward Pakistan. Washington deployed the nuclear-powered aircraft carrier **USS Enterprise (Task Force 74)** into the Bay of Bengal, prompting New Delhi to operationalize Article IX of the **August 1971 Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation**.
* **Technology Denial and the 1974 Pokhran-I Rupture:** India’s May 18, 1974 **"Smiling Buddha" (Pokhran-I)** peaceful nuclear explosion triggered a fundamental institutional counter-reaction from Washington. The United States spearheaded the creation of the **Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG)** in 1974 and enacted the domestic **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Act (NNPA) of 1978**, institutionalizing a three-decade regime of technology denial, export controls, and dual-use embargoes against Indian space (ISRO cryogenic engine sanctions in 1992) and defense establishments.

#### Pokhran-II, the Strobe Talbott–Jaswant Singh Dialogue, and De-Hyphenation (1998–2004)
* **The 1998 Nuclear Breakout and Glenn Amendment Sanctions:** The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 removed India’s primary diplomatic and military balancer just as post-1991 economic liberalization accelerated Indo-US commercial ties. However, when India conducted the **May 11–13, 1998 Pokhran-II (Operation Shakti)** thermonuclear and fission tests to establish an overt deterrent against the Sino-Pakistani nuclear-missile nexus, the Clinton Administration invoked the **1994 Glenn Amendment (Section 102 of the Arms Export Control Act)**, imposing sweeping economic, military, and dual-use technology sanctions on over 200 Indian laboratories and public-sector enterprises.
* **The Talbott–Singh Strategic Dialogue (1998–2000):** Paradoxically, Pokhran-II broke the diplomatic stalemate by forcing Washington to engage directly with India’s structural security calculations. Between June 1998 and September 2000, US Deputy Secretary of State **Strobe Talbott** and Indian External Affairs Minister **Jaswant Singh** conducted 14 rounds of intensive strategic dialogue. While India declined to sign the **Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT)** or roll back its nuclear deterrent, it committed to a unilateral moratorium on explosive testing, strict statutory export controls (culminating in the **WMD Act of 2005**), and a doctrine of **Credible Minimum Deterrence**.
* **The Kargil Inflection and Strategic De-Hyphenation:** During the **May–July 1999 Kargil War**, President Bill Clinton broke with Cold War precedent by refusing to equate the aggressor with the defender, compelling Pakistani Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif on July 4, 1999, to unconditionally withdraw Pakistani Northern Light Infantry regulars across the **Line of Control (LoC)**. Under the George W. Bush administration, strategist **Condoleezza Rice** and Ambassador **Robert Blackwill** codified the doctrine of **Strategic De-Hyphenation**: decoupling US relations with India from US crisis-management and counter-terrorism dependencies on Pakistan. Under **Stephen Walt’s Balance-of-Threat Theory**, the Bush administration recognized that a rising, democratic India was not a non-proliferation problem to be penalized, but an indispensable structural counterweight to the rapid military and economic rise of the People’s Republic of China in Asia. This conceptual breakthrough produced the **2004 Next Steps in Strategic Partnership (NSSP)**, setting the stage for the 2005 civil nuclear accord.

### 2. The 2005–2008 Civil Nuclear Deal: Dismantling Technology Denial and the Liability Impasse

The **July 18, 2005 Indo-US Joint Statement** signed by Prime Minister **Manmohan Singh** and President **George W. Bush** constituted the single most consequential diplomatic breakthrough in modern Indian foreign policy. Rather than demanding that India abandon its nuclear arsenal and accede to the **1968 Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT)** as a Non-Nuclear-Weapon State (NNWS), the agreement carved out a bespoke legal exception in the global non-proliferation architecture. It recognized India as a **"responsible state with advanced nuclear technology"** that would acquire the same benefits and advantages as formal NPT Nuclear-Weapon States (NWS) without signing the NPT.

#### Legal Mechanics of the Hyde Act (2006), 123 Agreement (2007), IAEA Separation Plan, and 2008 NSG Clean Waiver
* **The Henry J. Hyde United States-India Peaceful Atomic Energy Cooperation Act (December 2006):** Under **Section 123(a)(2) of the US Atomic Energy Act of 1954** (as amended by the 1978 NNPA), the US President was legally barred from engaging in civil nuclear commerce with any non-nuclear-weapon state that did not accept **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) "full-scope" (comprehensive) safeguards** on all nuclear activities within its territory. Passed with overwhelming bipartisan majorities in the US Congress, the **Hyde Act** granted a statutory waiver from Section 123(a)(2), enabling the US Executive to negotiate a bilateral cooperation pact while containing non-binding "sense of Congress" clauses regarding Indian cooperation on containing Iran and maintaining its testing moratorium.
* **The Indian Civil-Military Separation Plan and IAEA Safeguards (2006–2008):** To reconcile India’s strategic deterrent with international non-proliferation norms, New Delhi tabled a phased **Separation Plan** in March 2006. India designated **14 of its 22 thermal power reactors** (either operating or under construction) as civilian facilities to be placed permanently under **India-Specific IAEA Safeguards (INFCIRC/754, approved August 1, 2008)**, alongside an **IAEA Additional Protocol** signed in 2009 (ratified in 2014). Crucially, India retained its remaining 8 thermal reactors, its **Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR)** program, its dedicated military enrichment and reprocessing facilities (such as BARC Trombay and Kalpakkam), and its indigenous **three-stage thorium fuel-cycle R&D** completely outside international inspection, thereby preserving an unconstrained fissile material production pipeline for **Strategic Forces Command (SFC)**.
* **The Bilateral 123 Agreement (2007–2008):** Because the Hyde Act contained prescriptive domestic US reporting requirements, India insisted that the legally binding bilateral text—the **Agreement for Cooperation Between the Government of the United States of America and the Government of India Concerning Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy (123 Agreement)**, finalized in July 2007 and signed into force in October 2008—shield India’s strategic program from future fuel embargoes (such as the post-1974 US cutoff of low-enriched uranium to the **Tarapur Atomic Power Station**). Under **Article 5(6)**, the agreement guaranteed corrective measures and strategic fuel reserves for the lifetime of safeguarded reactors, while **Article 6(iii)** granted India upfront programmatic consent to reprocess spent US-origin nuclear fuel at a dedicated national reprocessing facility under IAEA safeguards.
* **The September 2008 NSG Clean Waiver:** Armed with the IAEA Safeguards Agreement, the United States and India overcame stiff resistance from non-proliferation hardliners (Austria, Ireland, New Zealand, Switzerland) and reluctant obstruction from China at the **45-nation Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG)** plenary in Vienna. On **September 6, 2008**, the NSG granted India a **"Clean and Unconditional Waiver"** from its 1992 guideline requiring full-scope IAEA safeguards as a condition of supply. This single multilateral exemption ended India’s 34-year nuclear apartheid, enabling India to immediately conclude bilateral civil nuclear uranium supply and reactor agreements with **France (2008)**, **Russia (2008)**, **Kazakhstan (2009)**, **Canada (2010)**, **Australia (2014)**, and **Japan (2016)**. Imported uranium freed up India’s scarce domestic uranium reserves (from Jaduguda and Tummalapalle) exclusively for its unsafeguarded military nuclear weapons program.

#### The CLNDA 2010 Impasse: Section 17(b) Right of Recourse, Section 46 Tort Liability, and the SMR Pivot
* **Channeling Strict Liability vs. The Bhopal Legacy:** Paradoxically, while Russia (Rosatom at **Kudankulam Units 1–6**) and France benefited commercially from the 2008 NSG waiver, private US reactor vendors (**Westinghouse Electric** and **GE-Hitachi**) failed to construct a single commercial nuclear reactor in India over the subsequent 18 years. The structural impediment lay in the **Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act (CLNDA), 2010**, enacted by the Indian Parliament under the dark historical shadow of the **1984 Bhopal Gas Tragedy** (in which Union Carbide escaped full financial accountability). Internationally, the **1997 Convention on Supplementary Compensation for Nuclear Damage (CSC)** follows the principle of **exclusive legal channeling**: the nuclear plant operator (in India, the state-owned monopoly **NPCIL**) bears strict, no-fault financial liability (capped in India at **₹1,500 crore** for the operator, backed by a central government cap of **300 million Special Drawing Rights / SDRs**), while private equipment suppliers are indemnified against third-party lawsuits.
* **Section 17(b) and Section 46 Legal Friction:** India’s CLNDA 2010 deviated from standard CSC channeling through two statutory clauses that deterred private Western suppliers and their reinsurers:
  1. **Section 17(b) (Right of Recourse for Latent/Patent Defects):** While the CSC permits an operator a right of recourse against a supplier only if explicitly written into a commercial contract or if damage results from an intentional act, **Section 17(b) of CLNDA** grants the operator (NPCIL) a statutory right of recourse if *"the nuclear incident has resulted as a consequence of an act of supplier or his employee, which includes supply of equipment or material with patent or latent defects or sub-standard services."*
  2. **Section 46 (Parallel Tort Liability Exposure):** Section 46 states that the provisions of the CLNDA are *"in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law for the time being in force,"* creating legal ambiguity that foreign suppliers could be sued directly by victims in Indian courts under ordinary tort or criminal law for unlimited damages outside the ₹1,500 crore cap.
* **The 2015 Insurance Pool and the 2025–2026 SMR / Statutory Reform Pivot:** Although New Delhi ratified the CSC in 2016 and established the **₹1,500 crore India Nuclear Insurance Pool (INRIP)** through General Insurance Corporation (GIC-Re) in 2015 to allow suppliers to purchase insurance covering Section 17(b) risk, Westinghouse’s 2017 Chapter 11 bankruptcy restructuring and persistent legal risk stalled the proposed six-reactor **AP1000 project at Kovvada (Andhra Pradesh)**. By 2025–2026, under the **U.S.-India COMPACT and TRUST frameworks** and India’s Union Budget 2025–26 **Nuclear Energy Mission for Viksit Bharat** (targeting 100 GW of nuclear capacity by 2047), New Delhi initiated legislative measures to amend both the **Atomic Energy Act, 1962** (to allow private sector minority equity participation in nuclear power operations) and the **CLNDA, 2010** (to align supplier liability strictly with CSC norms), specifically to unlock US-India co-production and deployment of **Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)** with firms such as Holtec International and NuScale.

### 3. Export Control Deregulation and Institutional Command: MDP, STA-1, and the 2+2 Dialogue

In the absence of a formal mutual defense treaty (which India’s doctrine of **Strategic Autonomy** precludes to avoid **Glenn Snyder’s alliance security dilemma** of entrapment in peripheral US wars), Washington and New Delhi constructed an unprecedented quasi-allied institutional framework between 2016 and 2026. Because the US export control system is heavily codified in congressional statute—specifically the **Arms Export Control Act (AECA)**, the **International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR)** administered by the State Department, and the **Export Administration Regulations (EAR)** administered by the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS)—integrating a non-ally into the US defense industrial base required creating sui generis legal designations.

#### Major Defense Partner (2016), STA-1 (2018), and ITAR/EAR Licensing Mechanics
* **Major Defense Partner (MDP, 2016):** Unlike Pakistan or non-NATO states designated as **Major Non-NATO Allies (MNNA)** under Section 517 of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961, India rejected the MNNA label as asymmetrically clientelist. Consequently, in June 2016 (subsequently codified into US law via **Section 1292 of the National Defense Authorization Act / NDAA for FY2017**), the United States created the bespoke designation of **Major Defense Partner (MDP)** exclusively for India. The MDP designation legally directs the US Secretaries of Defense, State, and Commerce to facilitate defense trade and technology sharing with India to a level commensurate with that of the United States' closest treaty allies.
* **Strategic Trade Authorization Tier-1 (STA-1, 2018):** Following India’s sequential accession to three of the four multilateral strategic export control cartels—the **Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR, June 2016)**, the **Wassenaar Arrangement (December 2017)**, and the **Australia Group (January 2018)**—the US Department of Commerce elevated India to **Strategic Trade Authorization Tier-1 (STA-1)** status in August 2018. Only the third Asian nation (after Japan and South Korea) and one of only 37 countries globally to hold STA-1 status, India gained license-exception access to roughly 90% of dual-use technologies on the **Commerce Control List (CCL)** under the EAR. This bypassed tedious case-by-case individual export licenses for high-end sensors, lasers, electronics, aerospace composites, and navigation equipment, directly facilitating private-sector supply chain integration.

#### The 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue and Combatant Command Liaison Integration (INDOPACOM, NAVCENT, SOCOM)
* **The 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue Architecture:** Initiated in **September 2018**, the annual **2+2 Ministerial Dialogue** brings together the Indian Ministers of External Affairs and Defence with the US Secretaries of State and Defense. Replacing the earlier commercial-track Strategic and Commercial Dialogue, the 2+2 mechanism institutionalized whole-of-government synchronization between diplomatic grand strategy and kinetic military operational planning across the Indo-Pacific and Western Indian Ocean theaters.
* **Combatant Command Geographies and Liaison Officers:** Historically, bureaucratic friction arose from the Pentagon’s geographic **Unified Combatant Command (UCC)** boundary dividing the Indian Ocean at the **68° East longitude** (the India-Pakistan maritime border): **US Indo-Pacific Command (INDOPACOM)** in Honolulu, Hawaii, held responsibility for India and the eastern IOR, whereas **US Central Command (CENTCOM)** and its naval component **NAVCENT (US Fifth Fleet)** in Bahrain held responsibility for Pakistan, the Persian Gulf, the Arabian Sea, and the Red Sea. To bridge this operational seam:
  1. In **2018**, the Pentagon renamed PACOM to **INDOPACOM** explicitly acknowledging the strategic unity of the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
  2. India deployed a naval **Liaison Officer to NAVCENT headquarters in Bahrain** (marking India’s first military posting inside a US combatant command in the Western IOR, vital for coordinating **Combined Maritime Forces / CMF** anti-piracy and Red Sea maritime security).
  3. Pursuant to the 2024–2025 2+2 roadmaps, India operationalized reciprocal **Liaison Officer deployments to INDOPACOM headquarters in Hawaii** and **US Special Operations Command (SOCOM) at MacDill Air Force Base in Tampa, Florida**, achieving real-time operational connectivity across both the eastern and western maritime flanks of the Indian Peninsula.

### 4. The Four Foundational Defense Agreements: C4ISR, Logistics, and Geospatial Fusion

For over fifteen years, the Pentagon required partner militaries to sign four standardized "foundational agreements" to enable seamless **Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (C4ISR)** and logistical interoperability. Under the UPA administrations (2004–2014), India’s Ministry of Defence resisted signing the final three agreements due to concerns that US inspection teams might compromise Indian sovereign communications or that Indian military bases might be automatically commandeered during US kinetic contingencies. Between 2016 and 2020—driven directly by escalating PLA coercion along the **Line of Actual Control (LAC)** from **Doklam (2017)** to the **Galwan Valley clash (June 2020)**—India negotiated India-specific texts with strict sovereign safeguards for all four pacts.

#### GSOMIA (2002) & ISA (2019), LEMOA (2016), COMCASA (2018), and BECA (2020)
* **1. General Security of Military Information Agreement (GSOMIA, 2002) & Industrial Security Annex (ISA, 2019):** Signed in January 2002, **GSOMIA** guarantees that the Indian government, armed forces, and public-sector defense entities (such as **DRDO** and **HAL**) will protect classified US military information at security standards equivalent to those of the Pentagon. However, as India opened defense manufacturing to the private sector under *Atmanirbhar Bharat*, GSOMIA proved insufficient because US law barred American defense primes (Lockheed Martin, Boeing, GE, Raytheon) from sharing classified technical blueprints directly with private Indian companies. At the **December 2019 2+2 Dialogue**, India and the US signed the **Industrial Security Annex (ISA)** to GSOMIA, establishing a joint physical and cyber facility-clearance protocol that allows private Indian defense conglomerates (such as Tata Advanced Systems, Larsen & Toubro, and Bharat Forge) to receive classified US technology and bid directly for global Pentagon supply chains.
* **2. Logistics Exchange Memorandum of Agreement (LEMOA, August 2016):** An India-specific adaptation of the standard US Acquisition and Cross-Servicing Agreement (ACSA), **LEMOA** establishes an automated accounting, reimbursement, and replacement-in-kind framework for reciprocal logistical support—covering food, water, billeting, transportation, petroleum, oils, lubricants (POL), spare parts, medical services, and port/airfield maintenance—during joint military exercises, training, Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief (**HADR**), and mutually agreed operations. Crucially, LEMOA **is not a basing agreement**: it creates zero legal obligation for either nation to provide logistical support during a unilateral war involving a third party without case-by-case sovereign clearance. Strategically, LEMOA acts as a massive force multiplier for the **Indian Navy**, allowing Indian surface combatants and **P-8I Poseidon** maritime patrol aircraft to refuel from US Navy oilers and access US naval logistics nodes at **Diego Garcia (Chagos Archipelago)**, **Camp Lemonnier (Djibouti)**, **Bahrain**, **Guam**, and **Yokosuka (Japan)**, dramatically extending India’s blue-water operational endurance across the Indo-Pacific.
* **3. Communications Compatibility and Security Agreement (COMCASA, September 2018):** An India-specific version of the Communications and Information Security Memorandum of Agreement (CISMOA), **COMCASA** resolved a severe operational handicap in India’s fleet of US-origin platforms (**C-17 Globemaster III**, **C-130J Super Hercules**, **P-8I Neptune**, **AH-64E Apache**, and **CH-47F Chinook**). Prior to 2018, because India had not signed COMCASA, US law required the Pentagon to strip NSA-certified encrypted communications equipment from aircraft sold to India, forcing the Indian military to rely on less secure commercial-grade radios and preventing direct data-sharing between Indian P-8I aircraft and US P-8A aircraft tracking Chinese **PLAN** submarines in the Indian Ocean. Under COMCASA, India acquired sovereign control over cryptographic keys and selective installation of **CENTRIXS (Combined Enterprise Regional Information Exchange System)** and **Link-16** tactical datalinks, enabling real-time, jam-resistant maritime domain awareness sharing between Indian and US naval task forces without exposing India’s Russian-origin platforms (**Su-30MKI**, **S-400**) to US electronic inspection.
* **4. Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement for Geospatial Cooperation (BECA, October 2020):** Signed during the height of the **2020 Eastern Ladakh LAC military standoff** with China, **BECA** established institutional linkage between India’s Defence Geo-Informatics Research Establishment (DGIRE) and the **US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA)**. BECA allows the United States to share unclassified and classified high-resolution satellite imagery, topographical, nautical, and aeronautical digital maps, geodetic data, and gravimetric/geomagnetic surveys (essential for correcting inertial navigation drift in long-range cruise missiles, ballistic missiles, and armed UAVs). During the 2020–2024 Ladakh and **December 2022 Tawang (Yangtse)** LAC standoffs, geospatial intelligence shared under BECA provided Indian mountain divisions with real-time early warning of PLA troop mobilization and artillery deployments across the Tibetan Plateau.

#### The 2024 Security of Supply Arrangement (SOSA) and Reciprocal Defense Priority Ratings
* **Mechanics of SOSA (August 2024):** During Defence Minister Rajnath Singh’s visit to Washington in **August 2024**, India and the United States signed a bilateral **Security of Supply Arrangement (SOSA)**, making India the **18th SOSA partner** of the United States (alongside allies such as Australia, Canada, Israel, Japan, South Korea, and the UK). Under SOSA, the US Department of Defense incorporates Indian defense procurement requests into the **US Defense Priorities and Allocations System (DPAS)** authorized under **Title I of the US Defense Production Act (DPA) of 1950**.
* **Eliminating Wartime Supply Disruptions:** Historically, Indian strategic planners feared that during a crisis on the LAC or LoC, American commercial sub-contractors might delay deliveries of critical spare parts (such as jet engine turbine blades or avionics chips) due to competing commercial orders or domestic US bottlenecks. Under SOSA, India can request priority contracting assurances through DPAS so that US industrial vendors are legally required to prioritize Indian defense orders ahead of commercial contracts, while India commits via an industry code of conduct to grant reciprocal priority assurance to the Pentagon from Indian defense manufacturers. Crucially, SOSA served as the administrative precursor to negotiating a legally binding **Reciprocal Defense Procurement (RDP) Agreement**, which waives **"Buy American Act"** restrictions for Indian defense firms bidding directly on Pentagon tenders.

#### Master Ship Repair Agreements (MSRA) and Indian Shipyard Forward Sustainment Hubs
* **Transforming India into an Indo-Pacific Naval MRO Hub:** In the maritime theater, a navy’s combat effectiveness is dictated by **tooth-to-tail logistics**: the distance a damaged or maintenance-due warship must steam to reach a certified drydock. Previously, US Navy **Seventh Fleet** and **Military Sealift Command (MSC)** vessels operating in the Indian Ocean had to transit thousands of nautical miles back to **Yokosuka (Japan)**, **Guam**, or **Bahrain** for mid-voyage repairs.
* **The Three Indian MSRA Shipyards:** Following the April 2022 2+2 Ministerial decision to operationalize India as a regional maintenance, repair, and overhaul (**MRO**) hub, the US Navy conducted rigorous technical and security audits and concluded **Master Ship Repair Agreements (MSRAs)** with three major Indian public and private shipyards by 2023–2024:
  1. **Larsen & Toubro (L&T) Shipyard at Kattupalli (near Chennai, Tamil Nadu)** on the Coromandel Coast facing the Bay of Bengal (which serviced the **USNS Charles Drew** in August 2022 and **USNS Matthew Perry** in 2023).
  2. **Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders Limited (MDL) in Mumbai (Maharashtra)** on the Arabian Sea coast.
  3. **Cochin Shipyard Limited (CSL) in Kochi (Kerala)** strategically positioned near the **Nine Degree Channel** and the main East-West international shipping lane.
* **Strategic Utility:** Under an MSRA, pre-certified Indian shipyards can bid directly on expedited, non-bureaucratic US Navy contracts for both scheduled voyage repairs and emergency battle-damage repairs during an Indo-Pacific contingency, anchoring US naval logistics to the Indian littoral without establishing permanent foreign military bases on Indian soil.

#### Institutional Evolution: Overcoming the Technology Transfer Paradox via COMPACT and TRUST
* **The iCET Paradigm Shift (January 2023):** Launched in January 2023 by the National Security Councils of both countries (led by Indian NSA **Ajit Doval** and his US counterpart), the **Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology (iCET)** bypassed lower-tier regulatory inertia by placing the White House and the Prime Minister’s Office directly in charge of dismantling export control barriers across six strategic domains: AI, quantum technologies, semiconductors, defense industrial co-production, advanced telecom, and space.
* **The February 2025 Transition to COMPACT and TRUST:** Following the inauguration of the second Trump administration (**Trump 2.0**) in January 2025 and Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s summit with President Donald Trump in Washington on **February 13, 2025**, both governments institutionalized and upgraded this architecture to insulate it against partisan transitions:
  1. **U.S.-India COMPACT (*Catalyzing Opportunities for Military Partnership, Accelerated Commerce & Technology* for the 21st Century):** Established as the overarching strategic meta-framework synchronizing defense co-production, bilateral trade rebalancing (**Mission 500**), and frontier technology integration.
  2. **The TRUST Initiative (*Transforming the Relationship Utilizing Strategic Technology*):** Directly superseding and expanding **iCET**, **TRUST** retained the NSA-led executive coordination model while sharpening the focus on industrial commercialization, private capital deployment, deregulated export controls (reviewing ITAR and EAR restrictions on dual-use AI weights and aerospace items), and secure supply chains stripped of Chinese dependencies.

#### Critical Minerals, Semiconductors, AI, and Quantum Supply Chain De-Risking under TRUST
* **Semiconductor Supply Chain Diversification:** Recognizing the acute geopolitical vulnerability of global advanced chip fabrication concentrated in Taiwan (**TSMC**) and China’s dominance in legacy node packaging, the US Department of Commerce (under the **CHIPS and Science Act**) and the **India Semiconductor Mission (ISM)** signed a Semiconductor Supply Chain and Innovation Partnership. Under iCET/TRUST, US memory giant **Micron Technology** broke ground on a **$2.75 billion Assembly, Testing, Marking, and Packaging (ATMP)** facility in **Sanand, Gujarat**, while **Applied Materials** ($400M engineering center in Bengaluru) and **Lam Research** (Semiverse training for 60,000 Indian engineers) integrated India into the US semiconductor design and equipment ecosystem. Furthermore, a landmark fabrication partnership was launched with the **US Space Force**, Bharat Semi, and 3rdiTech to establish India’s first national security semiconductor fab ("**Shakti**") producing infrared, **Gallium Nitride (GaN)**, and **Silicon Carbide (SiC)** chips for military night-vision, radar, and missile guidance.
* **Critical Mineral Recovery and Processing:** To break China’s 60–90% monopoly over the refining of **Lithium, Cobalt, Nickel, Graphite, Gallium, Germanium, and Rare Earth Elements (REEs)**, India joined the US-led **Minerals Security Partnership (MSP)** in June 2023. Under the **2025 TRUST framework**, Washington and New Delhi launched the **Strategic Mineral Recovery initiative**, a bilateral program to recover and process critical minerals (specifically lithium, cobalt, and rare earths) from heavy industries such as aluminum, coal mining, and oil and gas extraction, alongside joint overseas equity investments in South America’s **Lithium Triangle** and Africa.
* **Sovereign AI Infrastructure and Pharmaceuticals:** Under **TRUST**, US and Indian private industry committed to a **U.S.-India Roadmap on Accelerating AI Infrastructure**, establishing next-generation data centers in India powered by American GPU architectures alongside bilateral cooperation on **Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs)** to reduce both nations' structural reliance on Chinese chemical precursors for critical generic medicines.

#### Propulsion and Strike Capabilities: GE F414-INS6 Jet Engine Co-Production, 31 MQ-9B HALE Drones, and Stryker/Javelin Systems
* **GE Aerospace–HAL F414-INS6 Jet Engine Co-Production:** Aero-engine metallurgy represents the ultimate crown jewel of military aerospace technology; following the discontinuation of India’s indigenous **GTRE Kaveri** engine project for fighter applications, the Indian Air Force’s indigenous combat aircraft roadmap depended entirely on imported powerplants. In **June 2023** (with congressional notification completed in August 2023 and commercial pricing/industrial negotiations advancing through 2025–2026), **GE Aerospace** and **Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL)** signed a historic Memorandum of Understanding to co-produce **99 F414-INS6 afterburning turbofan engines** in India to power the **Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) Tejas Mk-2** (and initial prototypes of the fifth-generation **Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft / AMCA Mk-1**). Crucially, the US government authorized an unprecedented **80% Transfer of Technology (ToT)**—surpassing any previous US engine transfer to a non-ally—encompassing **11 critical propulsion technologies**, including machining of **single-crystal turbine blades**, **thermal barrier coatings (TBC)** for hot-section survival, **laser drilling for combustors**, powder metallurgy discs, and **Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC)** assembly.
* **Acquisition of 31 MQ-9B HALE Armed Drones (October 2024):** To eliminate reconnaissance blind spots along both the 3,488-km **LAC** and the vast **Indian Ocean Region (IOR)** chokepoints, India signed an inter-governmental **Foreign Military Sales (FMS)** contract worth roughly **$3.5–$3.9 billion** in **October 2024** for **31 General Atomics MQ-9B High-Altitude Long-Endurance (HALE)** Unmanned Aerial Vehicles. The tri-service distribution allocates **15 MQ-9B SeaGuardians to the Indian Navy**, **8 SkyGuardians to the Indian Army**, and **8 SkyGuardians to the Indian Air Force**. Capable of flying above 40,000 feet for over **40 hours** in all weather conditions, equipped with **Lynx Multi-Mode Radar**, anti-submarine sonobuoy dispensers, and **AGM-114 Hellfire** precision-guided missiles, the MQ-9B fleet integrates directly with India’s **P-8I Poseidon** aircraft (with six additional P-8Is cleared for procurement under the 2025 COMPACT) via **COMCASA/BECA** datalinks. The contract also mandates that General Atomics establish a comprehensive **Global MRO facility in India**.
* **Stryker ICV and Javelin ATGM Co-Production:** Expanding co-production from aerospace into land warfare, the United States and India initiated high-altitude trials in **Eastern Ladakh** for the **General Dynamics Land Systems (GDLS) Stryker** 8x8 armored **Infantry Combat Vehicle (ICV)**—including variants mounted with remote weapon stations and **Shorad** air-defense systems—aimed at co-producing a customized Indian version to replace aging Soviet-era BMP-2s. Simultaneously, under the **February 2025 COMPACT declaration**, both leaders announced plans to co-produce **Javelin Anti-Tank Guided Missiles (ATGMs)** in India (via the Raytheon–Lockheed Martin joint venture partnering with Bharat Dynamics Limited / BDL), providing Indian mountain infantry brigades on the Tibetan plateau with man-portable, fire-and-forget, top-attack lethality against PLA light tanks (Type-15). Furthermore, the two nations launched the **Autonomous Systems Industry Alliance (ASIA)** in February 2025 to scale co-production of autonomous Unmanned Surface Vessels (USVs) and Unmanned Underwater Vehicles (UUVs) such as Wave Gliders and acoustic towed arrays for Indo-Pacific undersea domain awareness.

* **DIU–iDEX Operational Synergy:** Managed jointly by the **US Department of Defense’s Defense Innovation Unit (DIU)** and the Indian Ministry of Defence’s **Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX)** (supported by the US-India Business Council and IIT Kanpur/Stanford GSB academic nodes), INDUS-X operationalizes:

### 8. Frontier Space Collaboration: NASA-ISRO NISAR and the Artemis Accords

Space has transitioned from a benign scientific global commons into a contested warfighting and geo-economic domain. As China accelerated its **Tiangong** space station and partnered with Russia on the **International Lunar Research Station (ILRS)**, Indo-US space cooperation shed its decades-long legacy of US export sanctions against **ISRO** to become a central pillar of the bilateral techno-strategic partnership.

#### Dual-Frequency Earth Observation: The NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) Mission
* **Technical Architecture of NISAR:** The **$1.5 billion NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR)** satellite mission represents the most expensive and technologically complex civilian Earth-observation satellite ever jointly built by the two space agencies. NISAR is the first satellite in history to carry a **dual-frequency Synthetic Aperture Radar** payload operating simultaneously on two microwave wavelengths:
  1. **NASA’s L-Band SAR (24 cm wavelength):** Together with the massive 12-meter deployable gold-plated wire-mesh radar reflector antenna, payload data subsystem, and GPS receivers, the L-band radar penetrates dense forest canopies and soil layers.
  2. **ISRO’s S-Band SAR (12 cm wavelength):** Together with the **I-3K spacecraft bus** and the **GSLV Mk-II launch vehicle** operating from the **Satish Dhawan Space Centre (Sriharikota)**, the S-band radar provides high-resolution surface sensitivity to light vegetation, snow moisture, and agricultural crops.
* **Geophysical and Strategic Utility:** Utilizing an advanced radar imaging technique known as **SweepSAR**, NISAR scans nearly the entire globe's land and ice-covered surfaces every **12 days** on ascending and descending passes, detecting crustal deformations as minuscule as **0.4 inches (1 centimeter)**. While strictly a civilian open-data mission tracking Himalayan glacial melt, groundwater depletion, tectonic fault strain along the Himalayan Frontal Thrust, and coastal sea-level rise, dual-frequency all-weather day-and-night SAR imagery also establishes unmatched institutional interoperability between ISRO and NASA in high-resolution radar telemetry processing.

#### Cislunar Norm Generation: The Artemis Accords (2023), Human Spaceflight, and Space Situational Awareness
* **India’s Accession to the Artemis Accords (June 2023):** During Prime Minister Modi’s State Visit to Washington in **June 2023**, India became the **27th signatory to the US-led Artemis Accords**. Grounded in the **1967 Outer Space Treaty (OST)**, the Artemis Accords establish a non-binding plurilateral normative framework governing civil space exploration and commercial resource utilization on the Moon, Mars, comets, and asteroids. By signing the Accords, India aligned with the Western normative architecture on transparency, emergency astronaut assistance, **interoperability of cislunar infrastructure**, registration of space objects, mitigation of orbital debris, and the controversial concept of **"Safety Zones"** around lunar resource extraction (such as water-ice mining at the **Lunar South Pole**, where ISRO’s **Chandrayaan-3** achieved its historic landing in August 2023), providing a counterweight to the Sino-Russian **ILRS** bloc.
* **Human Spaceflight (Axiom-4 / ISS) and Space Situational Awareness (SSA):** Under the 2023–2025 space roadmap, NASA provided advanced training to two Indian Air Force test pilots (**Gaganyatris** Group Captain **Shubhanshu Shukla** and Group Captain **Prashanth Balakrishnan Nair**) at the **Johnson Space Center in Houston** for the joint **ISRO-NASA Axiom Mission 4 (Ax-4)** to the **International Space Station (ISS)**, directly supporting ISRO’s indigenous **Gaganyaan** human spaceflight program. Simultaneously, on the military-strategic side, the two nations operationalized a bilateral **Space Situational Awareness (SSA) Arrangement**, enabling real-time data sharing between the **US Space Command (USSPACECOM)** and India’s **Defence Space Agency (DSA)** / ISRO’s **NETRA (Network for Space Objects Tracking and Analysis)** to protect Indian intelligence and communications satellites against kinetic and co-orbital **Anti-Satellite (ASAT)** threats and orbital debris collisions.

## IV. Bilateral Friction Matrices Under the 2025–2026 US Administration (Trump 2.0): Geo-Economics, Sanctions, and Sovereignty

### 9. Geo-Economic Contestation: Reciprocal Tariffs, GSP, Special 301 IPR, and H-1B Mobility

#### Reciprocal Tariff Pressures, the Trade Deficit, and the 2019 GSP Revocation Legacy
* **The Structural Trade Asymmetry and the 2019 GSP Revocation:** Bilateral goods and services trade between the United States and India surpassed **$200 billion** by 2024–2025, making the US India’s largest single-country export destination and generating a persistent Indian trade surplus of roughly **$45 billion in goods** (plus a substantial surplus in IT services). During the first Trump administration, trade friction culminated on **June 5, 2019**, when Washington formally revoked India’s beneficiary status under the **Generalized System of Preferences (GSP)**—a 1976 unilateral trade program that had allowed approximately **$5.6 billion to $6.3 billion** worth of Indian exports (engineering goods, auto components, chemicals, textiles) to enter the US market duty-free. The GSP revocation was triggered by petitions from the US dairy industry (protesting Indian religious/cultural requirements that imported dairy derive from cows never fed animal extracts) and US medical-device manufacturers (protesting Indian price caps on **coronary stents** and **orthopedic knee implants**), combined with **Section 232 national security tariffs** (25% on steel, 10% on aluminum) and retaliatory Indian tariffs on 28 US products (such as California almonds and Washington apples, which were later mutually resolved in June 2023).
* **Trump 2.0 Reciprocal Tariff Pressures and "Mission 500" (2025–2026):** Upon returning to office in **January 2025**, President Donald Trump repeatedly labeled India a **"tariff king"** due to the wide gap between India’s simple average WTO MFN applied tariff rate (**~17%**, with peaks of 50–150% on automobiles, alcoholic beverages, and agricultural items) and the US simple average applied tariff (**~3.3%**), alongside India’s proliferation of non-tariff **Quality Control Orders (QCOs)** mandated by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS). To preempt or cushion the blow of the Trump administration’s universal and **Reciprocal Tariff** regime (launched in early 2025), New Delhi executed a calibrated dual strategy:
  1. **Preemptive Tariff Rationalization:** In the **Union Budget 2025–26** and subsequent notifications, India unilaterally slashed peak customs duties on iconic US export items, including heavy high-capacity motorcycles (**Harley-Davidson** class reduced from 50% to 30–40%), **Bourbon whiskey** (slashed from 150% to 100%), luxury cars, synthetic flavorings, and ethernet switches, while abolishing the **6% "Google Tax" (Equalisation Levy)** on foreign digital advertising.
  2. **The "Mission 500" Bilateral Trade Agreement (BTA) Pivot:** At the **February 2025 Washington Summit**, both governments launched **"Mission 500"**—a commitment to more than double bilateral trade to **$500 billion by 2030**—and mandated trade ministers to negotiate a comprehensive, multi-sector **Bilateral Trade Agreement (BTA)**. Simultaneously, to narrow the bilateral trade deficit organically, India committed to scaling up imports of **US crude oil, Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG), and coking coal** from ~$15 billion toward **$25 billion annually**, restoring the US as a top-five energy supplier to India. Nevertheless, sharp negotiating red lines persisted throughout 2025–2026 over US demands for unrestricted market access into India’s politically sensitive **dairy, genetically modified (GM) corn/soybean, and poultry sectors**.

#### USTR Special 301 IPR Friction: Section 3(d) Evergreening, Compulsory Licensing, and Digital Trade
* **The USTR Special 301 "Priority Watch List":** Every year without interruption through 2025–2026, the Office of the United States Trade Representative (**USTR**) has placed India on the **"Priority Watch List"** in its statutory **Special 301 Report** on Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) protection. This friction pits the profit model of research-intensive US pharmaceutical and software multinationals against India’s role as the **"Pharmacy of the Global South."**
* **Core Statutory Battlegrounds:**
  1. **Section 3(d) of the Indian Patents Act, 1970 (as amended in 2005):** To prevent multinational pharmaceutical corporations from engages in **"evergreening"**—extending a 20-year patent monopoly by making minor chemical modifications (salts, esters, polymorphs) to an existing drug—**Section 3(d)** bars patents on new forms of known substances unless they demonstrate significantly enhanced **"therapeutic efficacy"** (upheld by the Supreme Court of India in the landmark **2013 *Novartis v. Union of India* (Glivec)** judgment). The USTR argues Section 3(d) violates WTO TRIPS spirit; India defends it as fully compliant with the **2001 Doha Declaration on the TRIPS Agreement and Public Health**.
  2. **Compulsory Licensing (Section 84) and Absence of Patent Linkage:** Washington opposes India’s statutory authority under **Section 84** to issue compulsory licenses to domestic generic manufacturers if a patented drug is unaffordable or unavailable (invoked in **2012 for Bayer’s cancer drug *Nexavar***), as well as the absence of regulatory "patent linkage" (where drug safety regulators refuse marketing approval to generics while a patent dispute is pending) and narrow protections against unfair commercial use of regulatory test data.
  3. **Digital Sovereignty and Data Localization:** US technology hyperscalers (Meta, Google, Amazon, Visa/Mastercard) have repeatedly lobbied the USTR against Indian regulatory mandates—ranging from the **Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) 2018 payment data localization directive** to fiduciary rules under the **Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023** and the promotion of India’s sovereign **UPI / RuPay** rails—viewing them as digital non-tariff barriers.

#### Skilled Migration Politics: H-1B Visa Quotas, Prevailing Wage Rules, and Totalization Agreement Deadlocks
* **The H-1B Structural Dependency:** Indian nationals account for over **70% of all US H-1B specialty occupation non-immigrant visas** issued annually (capped statutorily at **65,000 regular visas plus 20,000 for advanced US degree holders**). For decades, the H-1B pipeline served as the circulatory system connecting Indian IT service majors (**TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech**) and US Silicon Valley technology firms.
* **Mobility Friction vs. Tech-Nationalist Divides:** While the Biden-era 2023–2024 reforms introduced domestic **H-1B stateside visa stamping renewals** and streamlined lottery integrity rules, the **2025–2026 Trump 2.0 administration** exposed an internal ideological fissure within the US governing coalition: Silicon Valley technocrats demanded expanded high-skilled STEM talent pipelines, whereas economic nationalists pushed for higher **Department of Labor (DOL) prevailing wage floors**, stricter scrutiny of third-party IT outsourcing contractors, restrictions on **H-4 spousal Employment Authorization Documents (EADs)**, and intensified enforcement against irregular migration (prompting bilateral cooperation on verified deportations of undocumented Indian migrants in early 2025).
* **The Social Security Totalization Agreement Impasse:** Furthermore, a persistent financial grievance for New Delhi remains the United States’ refusal to sign a bilateral **Totalization Agreement** with India. Indian professionals working temporarily in the US on H-1B and L-1 visas contribute an estimated **$4 billion to $5 billion annually** in US **Social Security (FICA) and Medicare payroll taxes**, yet because most return to India before completing the mandatory **40 quarters (10 years)** of contribution credits required under US law, they forfeit these billions in retirement benefits without receiving a refund.

#### Extraterritorial Sanctions Friction: CAATSA (S-400 Waiver), Russian Crude Price Caps, and OFAC Pressures on Iran/Chabahar
* **The CAATSA Section 231 Crisis and the S-400 Waiver:** In August 2017, the US Congress enacted the **Countering America’s Adversaries Through Sanctions Act (CAATSA)**, of which **Section 231** mandates secondary sanctions against any third-country entity conducting a *"significant transaction"* with the Russian defense or intelligence sectors (**Rosoboronexport**). When India ignored US warnings and signed a **$5.43 billion contract in October 2018** to procure five squadrons of the Russian **S-400 Triumf** long-range surface-to-air missile system (while Turkey was expelled from the F-35 program and sanctioned under CAATSA for buying the same system), a major diplomatic rupture loomed. However, recognizing that sanctioning India would cripple the **QUAD** and leave Indian airspace vulnerable to PLA Air Force fifth-generation **J-20** fighters along the LAC, the US House of Representatives passed the **Ro Khanna amendment** to the **FY2023 NDAA** in July 2022, backing the use of modified presidential waiver authority under **Section 231(d)** to exempt India from CAATSA sanctions while encouraging India’s gradual diversification away from Russian platforms.
* **Post-2022 Russian Crude Imports and 2025–2026 OFAC Shipping Sanctions:** Following the February 2022 Ukraine war, India leveraged discounted Russian **Urals and ESPO crude oil**, expanding Russia’s share of India’s import basket from `<2%` pre-2022 to **~35–40% (~1.7–2.0 million barrels per day)** by 2024–2025. Initially, Washington tacitly endorsed Indian purchases—provided they settled below or near the **$60/barrel G7 Price Cap**—because Indian refiners (Reliance Jamnagar and Nayara Energy) refined Russian crude into diesel and jet fuel exported to Europe, preventing a global oil price shock above $150/barrel. However, by **January 2025–2026**, tightening **US Treasury Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)** sanctions targeting Russia’s **Sovcomflot**, Gazprom Neft, Surgutneftegas, and over 180 "shadow fleet" oil tankers—combined with Trump 2.0 tariff/secondary-sanction pressures aimed at forcing a Ukraine settlement—compelled Indian state and private refiners to refuse OFAC-blacklisted vessels and incrementally recalibrate their crude sourcing back toward West Asian and American grades.
* **Iran, the 2018 IFCA Waiver, and the May 2024 Chabahar 10-Year Contract:** A parallel sanctions fault line runs through the Islamic Republic of Iran. In 2018–2019, when the first Trump administration withdrew from the **JCPOA** nuclear deal and revoked Significant Reduction Exceptions (SREs), India was forced to **zero out its Iranian crude oil imports** (which previously accounted for ~10% of Indian oil supplies). However, under the **Iran Freedom and Counter-Proliferation Act (IFCA) of 2012**, Washington granted India a narrow statutory sanctions waiver to develop the **Shahid Beheshti terminal at Chabahar Port** in southeastern Iran, acknowledging its necessity for humanitarian reconstruction in Afghanistan. When **India Ports Global Limited (IPGL)** signed a historic **10-year operational contract in May 2024** (committing **$120 million in port equipment and a $250 million credit line**) to manage Chabahar, the US State Department publicly warned that entities doing business with Iran faced potential exposure to US sanctions. Under Trump 2.0’s **"Maximum Pressure 2.0" National Security Presidential Memorandum (February 2025)**—which directed the Secretary of State to modify or rescind existing Iran sanctions waivers, explicitly scrutinizing Chabahar—New Delhi engaged in intensive backchannel diplomacy to protect its Chabahar footprint, arguing that forcing an Indian exit would hand the strategic Gulf of Oman port directly to **China** alongside Pakistan’s **Gwadar Port**.

#### Transnational Khalistani Separatist Investigations: Five Eyes Intelligence Dynamics, Legal Accountability, and Sovereign Red Lines
* **The November 2023 SDNY Indictment and Five Eyes Friction:** Between late 2023 and 2026, a sensitive non-traditional security friction emerged at the intersection of diaspora politics, intelligence operations, and Western domestic legal norms. While India’s diplomatic relationship with **Canada** suffered a severe rupture in September 2023–October 2024 following Prime Minister Justin Trudeau’s public, politically charged allegations regarding the June 2023 killing of designated Khalistani terrorist **Hardeep Singh Nijjar** in British Columbia, a parallel legal process unfolded in the United States. In **November 2023** (with superseding unsealed filings in **October 2024**), federal prosecutors in the **US District Court for the Southern District of New York (SDNY)** unsealed a murder-for-hire indictment against an Indian national (**Nikhil Gupta**, extradited from the Czech Republic to the US in June 2024) and a named former Indian government employee (**Vikash Yadav**), alleging an aborted plot targeting **Gurpatwant Singh Pannun**—a dual US-Canadian citizen and leader of the banned secessionist outfit **Sikhs for Justice (SFJ)**, designated as an individual terrorist under India’s **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA)**.
* **Bilateral Institutional Compartmentalization vs. Canadian Escalation:** In stark contrast to the breakdown of Indo-Canadian ties, both Washington and New Delhi demonstrated high-level strategic maturity by strictly **compartmentalizing** the legal inquiry from the broader Indo-Pacific defense and technology partnership:
  1. **Indian Institutional Response:** Rather than dismissing the US Department of Justice’s evidentiary dossier (which differed sharply from Ottawa’s initial refusal to share actionable legal evidence), India’s Ministry of External Affairs declared that transnational extrajudicial operations were **"contrary to government policy"** and constituted a **High-Level Enquiry Committee** in November 2023 to investigate the security-criminal nexus, resulting in domestic administrative removals and legal action that satisfied US accountability benchmarks during bilateral security consultations in October 2024 and 2025.
  2. **Indian Counter-Demands on Sovereign Security:** Simultaneously, New Delhi pressed the US Department of Justice and the FBI to curb the weaponization of First Amendment protections by **SFJ** and Khalistani extremist networks operating from US soil—specifically demanding federal prosecution for the **March and July 2023 arson and vandalism attacks on the Indian Consulate in San Francisco**, threats against Indian diplomats and Air India civil aviation, and transnational narco-terror financing networks linking North American Khalistani elements with Pakistan’s **ISI**.
