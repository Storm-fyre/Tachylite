# Part 13: West Asia and the Extended Western Neighborhood: Energy, De-Hyphenation, Minilateral Corridors, and Regional Ruptures

## I. Grand Strategy in the Extended Western Neighborhood: From "Look West" to "Think West" and the Mechanics of Tripolar De-Hyphenation

### 1. Structural Drivers of the "Think West" Doctrine: Energy Security, the 9-Million Gulf Diaspora, and Remittance-Intelligence Networks

#### The Geo-Economic Transition from Transactional Buyer-Seller Ties to Complex Interdependence

For the first six decades of post-independence statecraft, India’s engagement with West Asia operated within a reactive, ideologically constrained, and narrowly transactional paradigm. During the Cold War, New Delhi’s non-aligned solidarity with Arab nationalist regimes (Nasserist Egypt, Ba’athist Iraq, and Syria) and its vocal normative championing of the Palestinian cause failed to yield reciprocal diplomatic backing during India’s conventional wars with Pakistan in 1965 and 1971, as Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) states routinely aligned with Rawalpindi. Even after the formal adoption of the **"Look West" Policy** in 2005, bilateral interaction remained dominated by a simplistic economic circuit: the import of Gulf hydrocarbons and the export of low- and semi-skilled Indian contractual labor. Beginning in 2015, Indian grand strategy executed a structural pivot from this transactional **"Look West"** approach to a proactive, multi-domain **"Think West" Doctrine**. Grounded in **Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye’s Complex Interdependence** framework, **"Think West"** reconceptualizes the **Extended Western Neighborhood**—stretching from the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf across the Arabian Peninsula to the Levant and the Red Sea littoral—as an indivisible component of India’s core security, capital accumulation, and connectivity perimeter.

This doctrinal transformation was driven by converging systemic shifts inside both India and the Gulf Cooperation Council (**GCC**). As the Gulf monarchies accelerated post-oil economic diversification programs (such as **Saudi Vision 2030** and the **UAE Centennial 2071** strategy) in anticipation of the global energy transition, they sought stable, fast-growing Asian demand centers capable of absorbing downstream petrochemicals while offering deep technology, digital public infrastructure, and manufacturing ecosystems for sovereign wealth deployment. Simultaneously, India’s rapid industrial expansion demanded long-term patient capital, supply-chain de-risking away from China, and reliable security cooperation to neutralize transnational terror networks operating across the Arabian Sea.

* **From Commodity Trade to Capital-Technology Symbiosis:** Under **"Think West"**, bilateral commerce shifted from spot-market crude purchases to joint equity ownership in upstream oil concessions (such as the Indian consortium’s stake in Abu Dhabi’s **Lower Zakum** offshore field), downstream refinery-petrochemical complexes in India, bidirectional free trade regimes (**2022 India-UAE CEPA**), and frontier collaboration in space, green hydrogen, and artificial intelligence.
* **Institutionalization of High-Level Political Signaling:** Overcoming decades of diplomatic neglect—exemplified by a 34-year gap in Indian Prime Ministerial visits to the UAE prior to August 2015—New Delhi established institutionalized summitry, including the **Saudi-India Strategic Partnership Council (SPC)** and the **India-UAE Comprehensive Strategic Partnership**, insulating bilateral ties from third-party ideological vetoes.
* **Neutralization of the Pakistan Factor in the OIC Core:** By elevating economic and counter-terrorism interdependence with Riyadh and Abu Dhabi, India systematically eroded Pakistan’s historical ability to weaponize OIC communiqués over Jammu and Kashmir, vividly demonstrated when the UAE invited India as the **"Guest of Honour"** to the **46th OIC Foreign Ministers' Meet in Abu Dhabi (March 2019)** immediately following the Balakot airstrike and declined to penalize New Delhi after the **August 2019 constitutional reorganization of Jammu and Kashmir**.

#### Hydrocarbon Vulnerability, Strategic Petroleum Reserves, and the Remittance-Consular Nexus

The material bedrock of India’s West Asia strategy rests on two non-negotiable structural imperatives: **energy security** and the welfare of the **9-million Indian diaspora** residing in the region. Despite the post-2022 surge in discounted Russian seaborne crude imports, West Asia remains the irreplaceable anchor of India’s energy architecture, consistently supplying **50–60% of India’s crude oil and Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) dependencies**. Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE constitute three of India’s top four crude suppliers, while **Qatar** alone accounts for over **45–50% of India’s total LNG imports**—a dependency locked in through the **February 2024 20-year LNG supply renewal agreement ($78 billion)** between **Petronet LNG** and **QatarEnergy** extending deliveries of 7.5 million metric tons per annum (MMTPA) through **2048**. Because Gulf crudes enjoy a massive geographical freight advantage (4 to 7 days of sailing time to Indian west-coast refineries at Jamnagar, Vadinar, and Kochi compared to 25–35 days from the Russian Baltic/Arctic or the US Gulf Coast), any kinetic disruption in the **Strait of Hormuz** immediately transmits inflationary current-account shocks into the Indian macroeconomy.

Concurrently, the **9-million-strong Indian expatriate community** across the six GCC states (led by roughly **3.5–3.8 million in the UAE** and **2.4–2.6 million in Saudi Arabia**) forms the largest geographic concentration of Indian citizens (NRIs) abroad. Unlike the permanent settler diaspora (OCIs) in North America or Western Europe, the Gulf diaspora consists predominantly of contractual workers and white-collar professionals who retain Indian citizenship and repatriate almost their entire disposable savings. This demographic generates **$50B+ in annual inward remittances** (accounting for roughly 38–40% of India’s world-leading **$125B+ total remittance inflows**), providing a structural structural cushion for India’s balance of payments and financing household consumption across Kerala, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Tamil Nadu, and Rajasthan.

* **Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR) Co-Investment:** To insulate its industrial base from West Asian supply shocks, India constructed 5.33 MMT of underground rock-cavern **Strategic Petroleum Reserves** at **Visakhapatnam** (1.33 MMT), **Mangaluru** (1.5 MMT), and **Padur** (2.5 MMT) under Phase I, followed by Phase II expansions at Chandikhol and Padur. Crucially, the **Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC)** became the first foreign entity to lease and store crude oil directly in India’s **Mangaluru** and **Padur** underground caverns, granting India sovereign first-right-of-refusal during wartime or supply emergencies while giving ADNOC a forward commercial distribution hub.
* **Labor Mobility Governance and Consular Statecraft:** Moving beyond reactive crisis evacuations, India institutionalized bilateral labor welfare mechanisms through the **e-Migrate portal** integrated with UAE and Saudi recruitment systems, skill-mapping harmonization agreements, and domestic worker protection pacts to mitigate *Kafala* (sponsorship) system vulnerabilities while steadily upgrading the skill profile of Indian migrants toward healthcare, engineering, and financial services.

#### Counter-Terrorism Intelligence Sharing and De-Radicalization Synchronization

The third pillar of **"Think West"** is an unprecedented convergence in internal security, counter-terrorism intelligence sharing, and maritime domain awareness between New Delhi and the Arab Gulf monarchies. For decades, porous financial networks in the Gulf—particularly informal *hawala* clearinghouses and organized crime syndicates in Dubai—served as safe havens and logistical conduits for Pakistan-backed terrorist outfits (such as **Lashkar-e-Taiba** and **Jaish-e-Mohammed**) and fugitive operatives like the D-Company syndicate. Following the rise of the Islamic State (ISIS) and the post-Arab Spring anxieties of Gulf rulers over transnational Islamist radicalization, the threat perceptions of Riyadh and Abu Dhabi converged sharply with New Delhi’s security calculus.

* **Extradition and Real-Time Intelligence Loops:** Elevated cooperation between India’s **Research and Analysis Wing (R&AW)**, **National Investigation Agency (NIA)**, and Gulf intelligence services resulted in the rapid deportation and extradition of dozens of high-value terror suspects, ISIS sympathizers, and financial fugitives from the UAE and Saudi Arabia without protracted judicial delays.
* **FATF Coercion and Terror-Financing Chokepoints:** Bilateral joint working groups on counter-terrorism coordinated closely on **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** compliance, starving anti-India proxy networks of illicit Gulf charity and *hawala* flows while institutionalizing defense cooperation through regular naval port calls, the **Zayed Talwar** (India-UAE) and **Al-Mohed Al-Hindi** (India-Saudi Arabia) naval exercises, and air force interoperability drills (**Desert Flag**).

Synthesizing these three structural drivers, the **"Think West"** doctrine transformed the Arabian Peninsula from a passive hydrocarbon station and terror-financing blind spot into India’s foremost economic, financial, and internal-security partner in the immediate maritime neighborhood.

### 2. The Tripolar De-Hyphenation Architecture: Balancing the Arab Gulf, Israel, Iran, and Palestine

#### Multi-Alignment under Systemic Polarity: Separating Bilateral Tracks in a Fragmented Theater

West Asia is structurally defined by an acute **tripolar regional rivalry** pitting the **Sunni Arab Gulf monarchies** (led by Saudi Arabia and the UAE), **Israel**, and the **Shia Islamic Republic of Iran** against one another across ideological, sectarian, nuclear, and territorial fault lines. Under classical alliance theory, an external power engaging deeply with one pole risks automatic alienation or retaliation from the competing poles. To navigate this regional anarchy without sacrificing strategic equities in any camp, Indian diplomacy operationalized the **De-Hyphenation Doctrine**—the deliberate compartmentalization of bilateral relationships so that India’s strategic partnership with Country A is conducted strictly on its own intrinsic merits, completely autonomous of and unconditioned by India’s relationship with Country A’s adversary, Country B.

Unlike passive neutrality, which avoids deep commitments to preserve equidistance, **strategic de-hyphenation** is an active manifestation of **Vishwamitra multi-alignment**: India simultaneously maximizes high-intensity strategic, defense, and geo-economic integration across all three mutually hostile West Asian poles:

1. **The Arab Gulf Pole (UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Oman):** Anchored in **50–60% energy supplies**, **$50B+ annual remittances**, **$100B+ sovereign wealth investment pledges**, the **9-million Indian diaspora**, and the **IMEC** connectivity corridor.
2. **The Israeli Pole:** Anchored in critical military-technological force multipliers (**Barak-8**, **Phalcon AWACS**, **Heron UAVs**), cyber-intelligence cooperation, **drip-irrigation agricultural technology**, and the **I2U2** minilateral framework.
3. **The Iranian Pole:** Anchored in overland Eurasian bypass geometry around Pakistan via **Chabahar Port (Shahid Beheshti terminal)** and the **7,200-km International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC)** to Afghanistan, Central Asia, and Russia, alongside shared opposition to Sunni jihadist militancy in the Af-Pak theater.

#### The Israel-Palestine Equilibrium: Decoupling Defense-Tech Realism from Normative Two-State Support

The clearest operational test of de-hyphenation occurred along the **Israel-Palestine dyad**. Although India recognized Israel in September 1950, it withheld full diplomatic normalization for over four decades due to domestic political sensitivities, Cold War Arab alignment, and unwavering commitment to the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO)—making India the first non-Arab state to recognize the PLO in 1974 and the State of Palestine in 1988. Even after Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao established full diplomatic relations with Israel in **January 1992**, New Delhi practiced "hyphenated stealth": importing billions of dollars in Israeli defense hardware out of public view while packaging every high-level diplomatic visit into a joint **Israel-Palestine itinerary** (linking Tel Aviv and Ramallah in a single hyphenated trip) and consistently voting against Israel at the United Nations.

Between **2017 and 2018**, India dismantled this diplomatic hyphenation through two landmark state visits:
* **July 2017 Standalone Visit to Israel:** Prime Minister Narendra Modi executed the first-ever visit by an Indian Prime Minister to Israel while deliberately omitting a stopover in Ramallah, elevating bilateral ties to a **Strategic Partnership** focused openly on defense, innovation, water management, and agriculture.
* **February 2018 Standalone Visit to Palestine (Ramallah):** Seven months later, the Indian Prime Minister made a historic standalone official visit to **Ramallah** (traveling via Amman, Jordan with logistical coordination from Israel), conferring India’s highest civilian honor for foreign leaders on Palestinian President Mahmoud Abbas and committing over **$40 million** in developmental infrastructure assistance (hospitals, technoparks, and schools).
* **Calibrated UN Voting Posture:** At the UN General Assembly and Human Rights Council, India shifted from automatic anti-Israel voting to calibrated abstentions on resolutions targeting Israel with ICC war-crimes probes, while continuing to vote in favor of resolutions affirming Palestinian self-determination and humanitarian ceasefire demands during the **2023–2026 Gaza conflict**. India’s official declaratory line remains anchored to a **negotiated Two-State Solution** establishing a sovereign, independent, and viable State of Palestine living side-by-side in peace and security with Israel, combined with zero tolerance for terrorism (explicitly condemning the **October 7, 2023 Hamas terror attacks** immediately upon occurrence).

## II. The Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) Transformation and the Israeli-Minilateral Technology Matrix

### 3. The India-UAE Strategic Vanguard: CEPA, Rupee-Dirham Settlement, Sovereign Capital, and Institutional Fusion

#### The 2022 India-UAE CEPA and Rules-of-Origin Geo-Economic Mechanics

Within the GCC, the **United Arab Emirates (UAE)** functions as the vanguard laboratory of India’s **"Think West"** economic and strategic integration. Negotiated in a record 88 days, signed on **February 18, 2022**, and brought into force on **May 1, 2022**, the **India-UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA)** marked India’s first full-fledged bilateral free trade agreement in over a decade and its first ever in West Asia. Structurally, the CEPA moved India away from defensive protectionism toward offensive export-led integration with its third-largest global trading partner and second-largest export destination (with bilateral trade surging past **$85 billion** toward a **$100 billion non-oil trade target by 2030**).

Under the tariff-elimination schedule of the **2022 CEPA**, the UAE granted immediate **zero-duty market access on 90% of Indian exports by value** (scaling up to **99% of tariff lines** over 5 to 10 years), benefiting labor-intensive Indian manufacturing sectors including gems and jewelry, textiles, apparel, leather, footwear, pharmaceuticals, engineering products, and agricultural goods. In return, India eliminated duties on roughly 80% of tariff lines (scaling to 90% over a decade), including a tariff-rate quota (TRQ) concession allowing the import of **up to 200 metric tons of gold per annum from the UAE at a 1% tariff rebate**, alongside duty reductions on dates, petrochemicals, and aluminum.

* **Strict Rules of Origin (RoO) and Anti-Circumvention Safeguards:** To prevent Chinese or third-country goods from being routed duty-free into India via Dubai’s Jebel Ali Free Zone through superficial repackaging, the **India-UAE CEPA** embeds stringent **Rules of Origin** requiring a minimum **40% domestic value addition (VA)** in the exporting country alongside a **Change in Tariff Sub-Heading (CTSH)**, supplemented by mandatory **Certificates of Origin** issued exclusively by designated government authorities and a permanent bilateral safeguard mechanism triggered by sudden import surges.
* **Regulatory Fast-Tracking for Indian Pharmaceuticals:** In a landmark non-tariff breakthrough, the CEPA incorporated an annex committing UAE health regulators to grant automatic marketing authorization and time-bound (90-day) registration to Indian generic pharmaceutical products already approved by stringent developed-world regulators (US FDA, UK MHRA, EMA, Japan PMDA, or Australia TGA).

#### Weaponized Interdependence Hedge: Rupee-Dirham Local Currency Settlement (LCS) and UPI-AANI Interoperability

To insulate bilateral commerce from dollar-clearing friction, exchange-rate volatility, and the structural vulnerabilities of **weaponized interdependence** (Farrell and Newman’s chokepoint effect over **CHIPS/SWIFT**), the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** and the **Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE)** signed two historic Memoranda of Understanding in **July 2023**:

1. **The Rupee-Dirham Local Currency Settlement (LCS) System:** The framework enables Indian importers and Emirati exporters to invoice and settle bilateral trade directly in **Indian Rupees (INR)** and **UAE Dirhams (AED)** through a real-time foreign exchange market pair without converting through the US Dollar as an intermediate vehicle currency. By **August 2023**, **Indian Oil Corporation (IOC)** executed a landmark settlement with **ADNOC** for the purchase of **1 million barrels of Emirati crude oil paid directly in Indian Rupees**, while UAE gold exporters began settling bullion shipments into India under the INR-AED mechanism. Furthermore, accumulated Rupee balances held by UAE banks in **Special Rupee Vostro Accounts (SRVAs)** can be recycled directly into Indian corporate bonds, government securities (G-Secs), and foreign direct investment equities.
2. **Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) Interlinking (UPI–AANI and RuPay–JAYWAN):** The second MoU linked India’s **Unified Payments Interface (UPI)** with the UAE’s instant payment platform (**AANI**), enabling seamless, low-cost cross-border remittances for the **3.5-million Indian diaspora** in the UAE, alongside the interlinking of domestic card switches (**RuPay** and the UAE’s **JAYWAN** card, built directly on India’s NPCI RuPay technological stack).

#### Sovereign Wealth Deployment (ADIA, Mubadala, ADQ), ADNOC Strategic Petroleum Reserves, and IIT Delhi-Abu Dhabi

Beyond trade and monetary plumbing, the India-UAE partnership is anchored by massive long-term sovereign capital flows and institutional knowledge fusion:
* **Abu Dhabi Sovereign Wealth Investments:** The UAE’s premier sovereign wealth funds—headed by the **Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA)**, **Mubadala Investment Company**, and **ADQ**—manage combined assets exceeding **$1.5 trillion** and have committed **$75 billion to $100 billion** into Indian ports, airports, renewable energy grids, logistics parks, digital telecom (Jio Platforms), and retail infrastructure. To catalyze this capital influx, India amended Section 10(23FE) of its **Income Tax Act** to grant a **100% tax exemption** on dividend, interest, and long-term capital gains income earned by wholly owned subsidiaries of **ADIA** on Indian infrastructure investments, prompting **ADIA** to become the first global sovereign wealth fund to establish a dedicated institutional presence inside **GIFT City (Gujarat International Finance Tec-City)** in 2024. Additionally, the **February 2024 India-UAE Bilateral Investment Treaty (BIT)** replaced the expired 2013 agreement, balancing investor protection with sovereign regulatory autonomy.
* **IIT Delhi-Abu Dhabi and Cultural Soft Power:** Demonstrating India’s transition from an importer of unskilled labor contracts to an exporter of elite scientific education, the **Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Delhi** inaugurated its first overseas international campus—**IIT Delhi-Abu Dhabi**—in **2024**, offering Master’s and undergraduate programs in Energy Transition, Sustainability, and Computer Science. Simultaneously, the inauguration of the monumental **BAPS Hindu Mandir in Abu Dhabi (February 2024)** on land gifted by UAE President Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan symbolized profound pluralistic trust and cultural statecraft in the heart of the Arabian Peninsula.

### 4. The Saudi-India Strategic Partnership Council (SPC) and Pan-GCC Realignment

#### Institutionalizing Bilateralism: Political-Security and Economy-Investment Pillars of the SPC

As the custodian of the Two Holy Mosques, the dominant geographic landmass of the Arabian Peninsula, and the swing producer of **OPEC+**, the **Kingdom of Saudi Arabia** holds immense structural weight in India’s West Asia calculus. Bilateral relations underwent a paradigm shift following the **2006 Delhi Declaration**, the **2010 Riyadh Declaration** (which elevated ties to a Strategic Partnership), and the **October 2019 establishment of the Saudi-India Strategic Partnership Council (SPC)**. Co-chaired directly by the Indian Prime Minister and Saudi Crown Prince and Prime Minister **Mohammed bin Salman (MBS)**, the **SPC** makes India only the fourth nation globally (after the UK, France, and China) with which Riyadh has formed such an apex bilateral governing mechanism.

The **Saudi-India SPC** operates through two permanent ministerial-level committees supported by four functional sub-committees:
1. **The Committee on Political, Security, Social, and Cultural Cooperation:** Led by the respective Foreign Ministers, overseeing counter-terrorism intelligence sharing, defense co-production (under the Joint Committee on Defense Cooperation), maritime security in the Red Sea and Persian Gulf, and consular welfare for the **2.6-million Indian diaspora** in the Kingdom.
2. **The Committee on Economy andInvestments:** Led by India’s Minister of Commerce & Industry and Saudi Arabia’s Minister of Energy, tasked with operationalizing Saudi Arabia’s **$100 billion investment commitment** across Indian energy, refining, petrochemicals, infrastructure, agriculture, and technology sectors, alongside fast-tracking negotiations for a comprehensive **India-GCC Free Trade Agreement (FTA)**.

#### Petrodollar Recycling into Green Hydrogen, Grid Interconnections, and Refinery Ecosystems

Under the **SPC** framework (significantly advanced during Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman’s State Visit to New Delhi in **September 2023** alongside the G20 Summit), bilateral economic integration expanded from crude oil trade ($43B–$52B total bilateral trade) into clean energy transition and physical infrastructure connectivity:
* **Downstream Refinery Mega-Ventures and Strategic Storage:** While the initial trilateral **$44 billion Ratnagiri Refinery and Petrochemicals Project** (involving **Saudi Aramco**, **ADNOC**, and Indian PSUs) encountered land-acquisition delays in Maharashtra, both sides re-engineered the commitment into modular refinery investments, Saudi participation in Phase II of India’s **Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR)**, and long-term crude supply assurances.
* **Cross-Border Electrical Grid Interconnection and Green Hydrogen:** Under the September 2023 SPC agreements, India and Saudi Arabia initiated technical feasibility frameworks for a **subsea high-voltage direct current (HVDC) electrical grid interconnection** across the Arabian Sea (aligned with India’s **One Sun, One World, One Grid - OSOWOG** initiative), joint development of **green hydrogen and green ammonia** supply chains, and cooperation through the Saudi **Public Investment Fund (PIF)**.

### 5. The Indo-Israeli Defense-Agricultural Ecosystem, the Abraham Accords, and the I2U2 Minilateral Grid

#### Asymmetric Military-Technological Integration: Barak-8 MR-SAM, Phalcon AWACS, and Heron UAVs

While the Arab Gulf anchors India’s energy and financial security, **Israel** serves as an irreplaceable supplier of force-multiplying military technologies, intelligence-surveillance-reconnaissance (ISR) sensors, and co-developed weapons systems. Because Israel faces stringent domestic operational environments without imposing the geopolitical political conditionalities characteristic of Western export-control regimes or suffering the supply-chain bottlenecks affecting post-2022 Russia, Tel Aviv has consistently functioned as India’s vital **"supplier of last resort"** during wartime crises—providing laser-guided munitions for IAF Mirage-2000 fighters during the **1999 Kargil War**, expedited **Spike anti-tank guided missiles (ATGMs)** and **SPICE-2000 precision-guided bombs** during the **2019 Balakot crisis**, and leased **Heron TP UAVs** during the **2020 Eastern Ladakh LAC standoff** with China.

Unlike purely transactional buyer-seller arms deals, the Indo-Israeli defense nexus has evolved into deep joint research, development, and co-production under India’s *Atmanirbhar Bharat* (self-reliance) framework:
* **Barak-8 Long-Range and Medium-Range Surface-to-Air Missile (LR-SAM / MR-SAM):** Jointly designed and developed by India’s **DRDO** and **Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI)** and manufactured in collaboration with **Bharat Dynamics Limited (BDL)**, the **Barak-8** system (with a 70–100 km interception envelope supported by the Israeli MF-STAR active electronically scanned array radar) forms the primary fleet air-defense shield aboard Indian Navy aircraft carriers and destroyers (*Vikrant*, *Visakhapatnam*, and *Kolkata* classes) as well as land-based air-defense regiments for the Indian Air Force and Army.
* **Phalcon AWACS and Green Pine Radar:** Through a trilateral arrangement integrating Israeli **EL/W-2090 Phalcon** active phased-array radar domes onto Russian **Ilyushin Il-76** airframes, Israel delivered India’s premier strategic **Airborne Warning and Control System (AWACS)** fleet, supplemented by **Green Pine** long-range ballistic missile early-warning radars and **Aerostat** tether radars along the Western border.
* **Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (Heron Mk-II, Searcher, and Hermes 900) and Loitering Munitions:** The Indian armed forces rely heavily on Israeli **Heron Mk-I/II** and **Searcher** medium-altitude long-endurance (MALE) UAVs for continuous surveillance across the **Line of Actual Control (LAC)** in Ladakh/Arunachal Pradesh and the **Line of Control (LoC)**, alongside **Harop** anti-radiation loitering munitions ("kamikaze drones") and **Adani-Elbit** joint manufacturing of **Hermes 900 (Drishti-10 Starliner)** drones in Hyderabad, supplemented by **Negev LMG** and **Tavor** assault rifles for special forces.

#### Hydro-Agricultural Innovation: Drip-Irrigation Centres of Excellence (CoEs) and Water Statecraft

To broaden the bilateral relationship beyond security hardware and create visible developmental dividends across rural India, both nations established the multi-phase **Indo-Israeli Agricultural Project (IIAP)** coordinated by **MASHAV** (Israel’s Agency for International Development Cooperation) and India’s Ministry of Agriculture. Addressing India’s acute groundwater depletion and erratic monsoon cycles, Israel transferred advanced arid-zone agronomy, fertigation, micro-irrigation, and post-harvest management technologies.

* **Network of Centres of Excellence (CoEs):** By 2025–2026, more than **30 fully operational Indo-Israeli Centres of Excellence (CoEs)** (expanding toward a target of 40+ CoEs paired with over **100 "Villages of Excellence"**) operate across 12+ Indian states—including Haryana, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, and Karnataka. Each CoE serves as an R&D and demonstration hub where Indian farmers receive high-yield disease-free saplings (mangoes, pomegranates, citrus, dates, and greenhouse vegetables) and training in **precision drip-irrigation** that reduces water consumption by **50–70%** while doubling crop yields.
* **Strategic Water Partnership:** Complementing the agricultural CoEs, the **India-Israel Strategic Partnership on Water** deploys Israeli desalination, wastewater recycling (Israel recycles nearly 90% of its wastewater for agriculture), and aquifer-management technologies into the **Namami Gange** river-rejuvenation program and water-stressed regions of Bundelkhand and Marathwada.

#### The 2020 Abraham Accords and the I2U2 Quad (India, Israel, UAE, United States) Geo-Economic Synergy

For decades, the greatest structural friction in India’s **"Think West"** strategy was the diplomatic hostility between its primary Gulf partners (UAE/Saudi Arabia) and its primary technology partner (Israel). The signing of the **Abraham Accords in August–September 2020**—which normalized full diplomatic, commercial, and aviation relations between **Israel** and the **United Arab Emirates** (alongside **Bahrain**, **Morocco**, and **Sudan**)—dissolved this diplomatic firewall and created a historic geopolitical opening for Indian minilateralism.

Capitalizing immediately on the **Abraham Accords**, the Foreign Ministers of **India, Israel, the UAE, and the United States** convened in October 2021 and held the inaugural **I2U2 Leaders' Summit in July 2022**. Unlike the Indo-Pacific QUAD, which carries a distinct maritime security and naval deterrence profile, **I2U2** is engineered as a **geo-economic and technological minilateral platform** structured around six focal pillars: **Water, Energy, Transportation, Space, Health, and Food Security**. The institutional logic of **I2U2** rests on a four-way factor-endowment complementarity:

* **UAE Sovereign Capital** + **Israeli High-Tech & Agronomic Innovation** + **US Private Sector & Diplomatic Convening Power** + **Indian Market Scale, Land, Manufacturing Base, and Human Talent**.
* **Flagship I2U2 Projects in India:** At the July 2022 Summit, the grouping launched two mega-projects on Indian soil:
  1. **The $2 Billion Integrated Food Corridor:** Financed by the UAE across **Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh**, deploying Israeli climate-smart drip-irrigation and cold-chain storage technologies to maximize Indian agricultural yields, reduce post-harvest wastage, and secure food-supply resilience for the Gulf while raising Indian farmer incomes.

Although public high-visibility political summits of **I2U2** slowed after the outbreak of the **October 2023 Gaza War** due to Arab domestic sensitivities, the underlying private-sector, space, and food-corridor working tracks remained intact via the ** UAE-preserved Abraham Accords channel**, laying the institutional blueprint for the broader **India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)**.

## III. Eurasian Versus Trans-Mediterranean Connectivity: Chabahar, INSTC, and the IMEC Geo-Economic Competition

### 6. The Iranian Geopolitical Pivot: The May 2024 Chabahar 10-Year Contract and the 7,200-km INSTC

#### Bypassing the Pakistani Land Blockade: The Shahid Beheshti Terminal and Trilateral Transit Geometry

In classical geopolitical terms (**Halford Mackinder’s Heartland** and **Nicholas Spykman’s Rimland**), Pakistan’s geographic position across the Indus basin functions as a hostile continental barrier severing peninsular India from overland access to **Afghanistan**, the resource-rich **Central Asian Republics (CARs)**, **Russia**, and **Eastern Europe**. Because Rawalpindi has consistently denied Indian commercial trucks transit rights across the Wagah-Attari border to Afghanistan under the Afghanistan-Pakistan Transit Trade Agreement (APTTA), India faced a severe geopolitical enclosure on its northwestern continental flank—compounded after 2015 by the launch of the **China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC)** terminating at **Gwadar Port** in Balochistan.

To break this continental encirclement, India partnered with the **Islamic Republic of Iran** to develop **Chabahar Port**, located in Iran’s southeastern **Sistan-Balochistan province** along the **Makran Coast** of the **Gulf of Oman**. Crucially, **Chabahar** is Iran’s only oceanic deep-water port situated *outside* the narrow **Strait of Hormuz** chokepoint, lying just **72 nautical miles (approx. 170 km) west of Pakistan’s Chinese-operated Gwadar Port** and roughly 550 nautical miles from Gujarat’s **Kandla** and **Mundra** ports. Following the **2003 New Delhi Declaration** and the landmark **May 2016 Trilateral Transit and Transport Corridor Agreement** signed by **India, Iran, and Afghanistan**, New Delhi established **India Ports Global Limited (IPGL)**—a special-purpose vehicle under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways—to equip and operate the **Shahid Beheshti Terminal** at Chabahar.

* **Operational Validation in Afghan & Central Asian Trade:** Between 2018 and 2026, India utilized the **Shahid Beheshti terminal** at Chabahar to handle over **9 million tons of bulk cargo** and more than **90,000 TEUs of containerized traffic**, including the shipment of **over 2.5 million tons of humanitarian wheat, pulses, medical supplies, and malathion pesticide** directly to Afghanistan (bypassing Pakistan entirely via the Indian-built **218-km Zaranj-Delaram Highway** linking the Iranian border at Milak to the Afghan Ring Road).

#### The May 2024 10-Year Operational Contract and Extraterritorial US Sanctions Navigation (OFAC/CAATSA)

For years, full-scale infrastructure modernization at Chabahar was hampered by short-term, annually renewed lease arrangements and the reluctance of global maritime crane manufacturers and banks to supply equipment due to fear of US extraterritorial secondary sanctions (**OFAC** and **IFCA**) after the United States withdrew from the **Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA)** in May 2018. Although India secured a specific statutory exemption for Chabahar from the US State Department in November 2018 on the grounds that the port supported Afghan economic reconstruction, the absence of a long-term legal contract deterred capital mobilization.

On **May 13, 2024**, India and Iran resolved this bottleneck by signing a historic **10-year long-term bilateral contract** between **India Ports Global Limited (IPGL)** and the **Ports and Maritime Organization (PMO) of Iran** for the operation of the **Shahid Beheshti Terminal at Chabahar**:
* **Financial & Infrastructure Commitments:** Under the **May 2024 10-year agreement** (automatically renewable thereafter), **IPGL** committed an direct capital investment of **$120 million** to procure advanced mobile harbor cranes, rail-mounted quay cranes, and terminal equipment, supplemented by an Indian credit window (Line of Credit) equivalent to **$250 million** for mutually identified Chabahar-linked multimodal hinterland infrastructure (including integration with the **628-km Chabahar–Zahedan railway line** connecting to the Iranian national rail grid).
* **Navigating 2024–2026 US Sanctions Pressure:** Despite immediate warnings from the US State Department in May 2024 (and tightened "Maximum Pressure 2.0" enforcement under the **2025–2026 Trump 2.0 administration**) that entities engaging in business with Iran risked potential sanctions exposure, New Delhi defended the Chabahar contract as an indispensable pillar of regional connectivity enabling landlocked Central Asian states (**Uzbekistan** and **Kazakhstan**) to reduce dependence on Russian and Chinese transit monopolies.

#### The 7,200-km International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC): Multimodal Mechanics and Bottlenecks

While Chabahar provides the western oceanic gateway, the overarching continental framework is the **7,200-km International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC)**—a multimodal network of ship, rail, and road routes originally established by an intergovernmental agreement signed by **India, Iran, and Russia** in St. Petersburg in **September 2000** (ratified in 2002) and subsequently expanded to **13 member states** (including Azerbaijan, Armenia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyz Republic, Tajikistan, Turkey, Ukraine, Belarus, Oman, and Syria).

The **INSTC** operates across three parallel geographic axes linking **Mumbai (Jawaharlal Nehru Port / Mundra)** to **St. Petersburg**:
1. **The Central (Trans-Caspian) Route:** Sea transit from Mumbai to Iran’s **Bandar Abbas** (inside the Strait of Hormuz) or **Chabahar**, overland road/rail across Iran to the Caspian Sea ports of **Bandar Anzali** or **Amirabad**, maritime ferry across the Caspian Sea to Russia’s **Astrakhan** port, and onward via the Russian rail/Volga river network.
2. **The Western Route:** Overland transit from Iran through **Azerbaijan** (via the **Astara** border crossing) directly into the Russian Federation along the western shore of the Caspian Sea.
3. **The Eastern Route:** Overland rail connectivity from Iran through **Turkmenistan** and **Kazakhstan** into Russia (utilized for the first-ever commercial container train runs from Russia to India via the **Kazakhstan–Turkmenistan–Iran railway** in **2022–2024**).

Despite reducing freight transit time by **40%** (from 45–60 days via the **Suez Canal–Red Sea route** to **25–30 days**) and cutting transport costs by **30%**, full capacity utilization of the **INSTC** remains constrained by three technical and geopolitical bottlenecks:
* **The 164-km Rasht–Astara Missing Rail Link:** On the Western route inside northern Iran, a 164-km rail gap between **Rasht** and **Astara** forces cargo to be offloaded from trains onto trucks before re-entering Azerbaijan’s rail network (though Russia and Iran signed a €1.6 billion agreement in May 2023 to finance and complete this segment).
* **Track Gauge Breaks and Customs Friction:** Cargo moving from the Iranian/European **Standard Gauge (1,435 mm)** into the post-Soviet Azerbaijani/Central Asian/Russian **Broad Gauge (1,520 mm)** requires bogie-exchange stations at border crossings, compounded by fragmented customs documentation that India is addressing through integration with the **TIR (Transports Internationaux Routiers) Convention (acceded 2017)** and the **Ashgabat Agreement (acceded 2018)**.
* **Sanctions Compliance and Insurance:** Because both terminal poles of the INSTC (**Iran** and **Russia**) are under heavy Western financial sanctions, mainstream Western P&I (Protection and Indemnity) marine insurers and dollar-clearing banks refuse to underwrite INSTC bills of lading, necessitating sovereign reinsurance and **Rupee-Ruble / Rupee-Rial** payment structures.

Signed by **India, the United States, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, France, Germany, Italy, and the European Union**, **IMEC** represents the flagship physical manifestation of the **G7 Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment (PGII)** and the structural extension of the **I2U2** minilateral concept.

Structurally, **IMEC** is designed not merely as a container transit route, but as a four-layered **multimodal geo-economic spine** comprising two distinct geographic legs:
1. **The Eastern Corridor (Maritime Leg):** Connecting Indian west-coast ports (**Mundra**, **Kandla**, and **Jawaharlal Nehru Port Trust - JNPT Mumbai**) across the Arabian Sea directly to **Jebel Ali** and **Fujairah** in the **UAE** and **Dammam** in **Saudi Arabia**.
2. **The Northern Corridor (Overland Rail + Energy + Digital Leg):** A trans-Arabian railway network running from UAE ports through **Saudi Arabia** (via Ghuwaifat, Haradh, Riyadh, and Al-Haditha) and **Jordan** to the Mediterranean port of **Haifa in Israel**—where an **Indian-Israeli consortium led by the Adani Group (70% stake) and Gadot Group (30% stake)** acquired the privatization lease for **Haifa Port for $1.18 billion in January 2023**—followed by a short Mediterranean maritime crossing to **Piraeus (Greece)**, **Marseille (France)**, and **Genoa/Trieste (Italy)**.
3. **Parallel Clean Energy, Grid, and Digital Conduits:** Running directly alongside the rail tracks, the **IMEC MoU** mandates the laying of:
   * A **clean hydrogen pipeline** to export low-cost solar-generated green hydrogen from India and the Arabian Peninsula to industrial Europe;
   * A **high-speed fiber-optic telecommunications and data cable** (linking the **Blue-Raman cable** infrastructure) to bypass vulnerable subsea cables in the Red Sea/Egypt; and
   * A **cross-border high-voltage electricity grid interconnection**.

#### Geo-Economic Counter-Balancing Against China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and the Turkish Development Road

From a grand strategic vantage point, **IMEC** executes three simultaneous geopolitical operations:
* **Bypassing the Suez Canal and Bab-el-Mandeb Maritime Chokepoints:** By offloading containers at Fujairah/Jebel Ali and moving them overland by rail across the Arabian Peninsula to Haifa, **IMEC** eliminates the need to transit the **Bab-el-Mandeb Strait**, the **Red Sea**, and the **Suez Canal**, cutting India-to-Europe transit times by **40% (roughly 10 to 12 days faster than the Suez maritime route)** and reducing maritime freight costs by **30%**.
* **Countering China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI):** Where China’s **BRI** relies on opaque bilateral sovereign debt contracts and dual-use port leases (such as Hambantota, Gwadar, and Djibouti), **IMEC** offers a transparent, multi-stakeholder infrastructure corridor uniting US diplomatic backing, European green standards, Gulf sovereign capital, and Indian manufacturing scale—while reinforcing Saudi Arabia and the UAE’s independence from Beijing’s economic gravity.
* **Competing Regional Corridors:** **IMEC** also triggered competitive corridor diplomacy from states excluded from its route—most notably **Turkey** and **Iraq**, which accelerated the **$17 billion Iraq–Turkey "Development Road" project** linking Iraq’s Grand Faw Port at the head of the Persian Gulf through Baghdad and Mosul into the Turkish rail network to Europe, as well as **Egypt**, which viewed IMEC as a potential revenue threat to its **$9–$10 billion annual Suez Canal toll collections**.

#### Technical, Financial, and Geopolitical Bottlenecks: Missing Rail Links, Gauge/Customs Harmonization, and Normalization Freezes

Despite its transformative geo-economic vision, **IMEC** confronts severe physical and political barriers that must be resolved before the Northern Corridor can carry commercial freight:
* **Missing Physical Rail Links:** While the UAE’s **Etihad Rail** network (264 km Stage 1 and 605 km Stage 2) and Saudi Arabia’s **North-South Railway (SAR)** are largely built, critical cross-border rail segments remain unconstructed—specifically the **missing rail links across the Jordan-Israel border (connecting Beit She'an to Irbid/Amman)** and segments upgrading the Jordan-Saudi border at Al-Haditha.
* **Regulatory and Technical Fragmentation:** The **September 2023 IMEC MoU** did not contain binding financial commitments from the G7 or EU, leaving billions of dollars in rail-construction financing, customs-clearance harmonization, security screening protocols, and container double-stacking interoperability to subsequent working-group negotiations.
* **India’s Pragmatic Bilateral Hedging on the Eastern Leg:** Recognizing that the **post-October 2023 Gaza War** had frozen Saudi-Israeli normalization and halted progress on the **Northern Corridor** (Saudi-Jordan-Israel rail), New Delhi refused to allow the entire corridor to stall. During Prime Minister Modi’s visit to Abu Dhabi on **February 13, 2024**, **India and the UAE signed a bilateral Intergovernmental Framework Agreement on IMEC**, operationalizing the **Eastern Maritime Corridor** between Indian ports and UAE ports through a **Virtual Trade Corridor (VTC)** and digital customs integration (**MAITRI - Master Application for International Trade and Regulatory Interface**), ensuring that the India-Gulf segment of IMEC delivers immediate commercial efficiencies regardless of Levantine hostilities.

## IV. The 2023–2026 West Asian Geopolitical Ruptures and Maritime Deterrence in the Red Sea

### 8. Systemic Shocks: The 2023 Beijing-Brokered Saudi-Iran Détente and the Post-October 2023 Gaza-Israel-Lebanon War

#### Extra-Regional Multipolarity: The March 2023 Beijing Accord and China’s Diplomatic Entry into the Persian Gulf

For over half a century, the security architecture of the Persian Gulf operated under an unchallenged **Pax Americana**: the United States guaranteed the external security of the Arab Gulf monarchies (anchored by the US Navy’s Fifth Fleet in Bahrain and Al-Udeid Air Base in Qatar) while acting as the sole indispensable diplomatic mediator in West Asia, leaving China to act as a free-riding economic consumer of Gulf oil. However, following the perceived retrenchment of US security guarantees—highlighted by the **September 2019 drone and cruise-missile strikes on Saudi Aramco’s Abqaiq and Khurais facilities** that met with zero kinetic US military retaliation, and the chaotic August 2021 US withdrawal from Afghanistan—Riyadh and Abu Dhabi initiated a strategic hedging pivot toward multipolarity.

On **March 10, 2023**, this realignment culminated in a seismic geopolitical breakthrough: **Saudi Arabia and the Islamic Republic of Iran signed a trilateral Joint Statement in Beijing, brokered by Chinese President Xi Jinping and Wang Yi**, restoring full diplomatic relations after a seven-year rupture (triggered in January 2016 when Iranian protesters stormed the Saudi Embassy in Tehran following the execution of Shia cleric Nimr al-Nimr).

* **Strategic Mechanics of the Beijing Détente:** Building on earlier Iraqi- and Omani-hosted backchannel talks, the **March 2023 Beijing Accord** reactivated the **1998 Economic Cooperation Agreement** and the **2001 Saudi-Iran Security Cooperation Agreement**, committing both states to mutual non-interference in domestic affairs, reopening embassies in Riyadh and Tehran within 60 days, and de-escalating proxy friction across Yemen and Lebanon.
* **Implications for Indian Grand Strategy:** The Beijing-brokered détente presented a dual-edged reality for New Delhi. Positively, reduced Saudi-Iranian military tension lowered the risk of a direct Persian Gulf war closing the **Strait of Hormuz** (through which ~20 million barrels per day of global oil and over half of India’s crude imports pass) and eased Saudi anxieties over India’s involvement in **Chabahar Port**. Negatively, the accord signaled that **China** had transitioned from a purely commercial trade actor into a premier diplomatic power-broker in India’s **Extended Western Neighborhood**—shortly followed by the simultaneous induction of **Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iran, and Egypt** into the expanded **BRICS+ grouping** at the August 2023 Johannesburg Summit (formally taking effect in January 2024 for UAE, Iran, and Egypt).

#### The Post-October 2023 Gaza-Israel-Lebanon War, Direct Iran-Israel Missile Exchanges (2024–2026), and the Stalling of IMEC

Just four weeks after the celebratory launch of **IMEC** at the New Delhi G20 Summit, the entire geopolitical equilibrium of West Asia was shattered on **October 7, 2023**, when **Hamas** launched a massive cross-border terror assault from the Gaza Strip into southern Israel, killing roughly 1,200 people and abducting over 250 hostages. Israel responded with a high-intensity, multi-year military campaign in Gaza aimed at dismantling Hamas’s military and governance infrastructure, resulting in over 45,000 Palestinian casualties and severe humanitarian devastation across the enclave.

What began as a localized Gaza-Israel war rapidly metastasized into a **seven-front regional conflagration** activating Iran’s **"Axis of Resistance"** network across Lebanon, Yemen, Syria, Iraq, and the West Bank, fundamentally altering the regional balance of power between 2023 and 2026:
* **The Lebanon Escalation and Decapitation of Hezbollah (2023–2024):** Beginning on October 8, 2023, the Iranian-backed Lebanese militia **Hezbollah** initiated daily cross-border rocket and anti-tank missile barrages across the UN-demarcated **Blue Line** into northern Israel, displacing over 60,000 Israeli civilians. In **September–November 2024**, Israel shifted its strategic center of gravity northward—executing unprecedented intelligence operations (the detonation of thousands of Hezbollah pagers and walkie-talkies), eliminating Hezbollah Secretary-General **Hassan Nasrallah** and virtually the entire senior **Radwan Force** command in Beirut airstrikes, and launching a ground incursion into southern Lebanon prior to the **November 2024 ceasefire agreement**. The severe degradation of Hezbollah dismantled the jewel in the crown of Iran’s forward deterrence architecture.
* **Direct Iran-Israel Ballistic Missile and Air Exchanges (2024–2026):** Breaking decades of "shadow war" rules of engagement (historically restricted to cyberattacks, covert assassinations, and proxy skirmishes), **Iran and Israel engaged in direct, state-on-state homeland military exchanges**. Following an April 1, 2024 strike on the Iranian consular annex in Damascus, Iran launched **Operation True Promise I (April 13–14, 2024)**—firing over **300 drones, cruise missiles, and ballistic missiles** directly from Iranian soil at Israel (intercepted by an Israel-US-UK-France-Jordan air-defense coalition). After the July 2024 assassination of Hamas leader Ismail Haniyeh in Tehran and the September 2024 killing of Nasrallah, Iran launched **Operation True Promise II (October 1, 2024)** featuring nearly **200 ballistic missiles** (*Fattah* and *Emad* variants), to which Israel responded on **October 26, 2024 (Operation Days of Repentance)** with long-range precision air strikes neutralizing Iran’s Russian-supplied **S-300 air-defense batteries** and solid-fuel ballistic missile planetary mixers—establishing dangerous escalation dominance and recurring missile-deterrence instability into 2025–2026.
* **Geopolitical Paralysis of IMEC’s Northern Corridor:** The Gaza-Lebanon war and direct Iran-Israel exchanges immediately derailed US-brokered **Saudi-Israeli diplomatic normalization**, as Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman stated unequivocally that Riyadh would not establish diplomatic ties with Israel without an irreversible pathway to an independent Palestinian state. Consequently, while India and the UAE pushed ahead with the **Eastern Maritime Leg**, the **Northern Rail Leg of IMEC** (requiring physical transit from Saudi Arabia through Jordan into **Haifa Port, Israel**) entered an indefinite geopolitical freeze, alongside supply-chain disruptions to Israeli defense exports (such as artillery shells and radar components) diverted to the IDF’s wartime consumption.

### 9. The December 2024 Collapse of the Assad Regime in Syria and the Reconfiguration of the Levant

#### Fracture of the Iranian "Axis of Resistance" Land Bridge and Russian Mediterranean Retrenchment

In **late November and early December 2024**, the geopolitical fallout of the Gaza-Lebanon war and the Ukraine conflict converged in the Levant to produce a historic regime collapse: the rapid overthrow of Syrian President **Bashar al-Assad** on **December 8, 2024**, ending 54 years of Ba’athist Assad family rule and a 13-year Syrian civil war. For nearly a decade following the 2015 Russian aerospace intervention, the Assad regime had survived through two external pillars: **Russian air power** (anchored at **Khmeimim Air Base** and the **Tartus naval facility**) and **Iranian/Hezbollah ground forces** (commanded by the IRGC Quds Force).

By late 2024, both external pillars had been hollowed out: **Russia** had redeployed critical air assets, Wagner/Africa Corps mercenaries, and S-300 systems to the high-attrition **Ukraine theater**, while **Israel’s September–November 2024 offensive in Lebanon** had decimated **Hezbollah’s** elite ground units and interdicted IRGC supply convoys across Syria. Exploiting this strategic vacuum, a lightning 11-day offensive spearheaded by **Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS)**—led by **Ahmed al-Sharaa (formerly Abu Mohammad al-Jolani)**—and Turkish-backed Syrian opposition coalitions swept from Idlib through **Aleppo, Hama, and Homs** into **Damascus**, forcing Bashar al-Assad to flee into political asylum in Moscow on **December 8, 2024**.

#### Geopolitical Implications for Levantine State Stability, Turkish-Israeli Friction, and Indian Counter-Terror Calculus

The **December 2024 collapse of the Assad regime** triggered four profound structural realignments across West Asia and the Mediterranean:
* **Severance of the Tehran–Beirut Overland Supply Bridge:** Syria was the geographic linchpin ("the golden ring") of Iran’s **Shia Crescent / Axis of Resistance**, allowing the IRGC to transport precision-guided munitions overland from Iran through Iraq and Syria (via the Al-Bukamal crossing) to **Hezbollah in Lebanon**. The rise of a Sunni Islamist-led administration under **Ahmed al-Sharaa** in Damascus—deeply hostile to Tehran and Hezbollah for their wartime destruction of Syrian Sunni cities—permanently severed Iran’s overland logistics corridor to the Mediterranean, exacerbating Tehran’s strategic vulnerability and increasing internal Iranian pressure to reconsider its nuclear-threshold posture.
* **Israeli Buffer Expansion and Turkish-Israeli Rivalry:** Immediately following Assad’s fall in December 2024, the **Israel Defense Forces (IDF)** launched **Operation Bashan Arrow**, executing hundreds of airstrikes that destroyed over 80% of the former Syrian Arab Army’s chemical weapons sites, naval vessels at Latakia, air-defense radars, and MiG/Sukhoi squadrons to prevent them from falling into jihadist hands. Simultaneously, Israeli ground troops moved past the **1974 Agreement on Disengagement (UNDOF) Purple Line** to occupy the Syrian side of **Mount Hermon** and adjacent buffer zones in Quneitra, bringing Israeli forces into direct geopolitical friction with **Turkey**, which emerged as the dominant external patron of the new Damascus authorities while pressuring the US-backed Kurdish **Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF)** in northeastern Syria.
* **Indian Diplomatic, Evacuation, and Counter-Terror Equities:** During the December 2024 regime collapse, India’s Ministry of External Affairs executed a swift consular evacuation of **75+ Indian nationals** from Syria across the Lebanese border via Beirut. Strategically, while India had maintained cordial ties with the secular Ba’athist state in Damascus (which historically supported India’s stance on Kashmir at the OIC), New Delhi adopted a cautious, pragmatic wait-and-see approach toward the post-Assad transitional government—prioritizing Syrian territorial integrity, the protection of religious minorities, the prevention of an **Islamic State (ISIS)** resurgence in the Badiya desert, and the monitoring of foreign terrorist fighters alongside Turkish influence in the region.

### 10. Chokepoint Warfare at Bab-el-Mandeb: The Houthi Maritime Crisis and the Indian Navy’s Operation Sankalp

#### Asymmetric Sea-Denial in the Red Sea: Drone/Missile Harassment of Commercial Shipping and Cape of Good Hope Rerouting

The most direct material threat to the Indian macroeconomy arising from the **2023–2026 West Asian geopolitical ruptures** erupted along the **Bab-el-Mandeb Strait**—the 18-mile-wide (29 km) maritime chokepoint separating Yemen on the Arabian Peninsula from Djibouti and Eritrea in the Horn of Africa, linking the **Red Sea** and the **Suez Canal** to the **Gulf of Aden** and the **Arabian Sea**. Normally, roughly **12–15% of global seaborne trade**, **30% of global container traffic**, and **$200 billion+ of India’s annual merchandise trade** with Europe, the US East Coast, and North Africa transit the **Suez Canal–Red Sea–Bab-el-Mandeb** artery.

Beginning in **November 2023**, Yemen’s Iranian-backed **Houthi movement (Ansar Allah)** initiated a sustained campaign of asymmetric **sea-denial warfare** purportedly in solidarity with Gaza—deploying one-way attack UAVs (Shahed variants), unmanned surface vessels (USVs), anti-ship cruise missiles, and **anti-ship ballistic missiles (ASBMs)** against commercial container ships, oil tankers, and bulk carriers transiting the **Bab-el-Mandeb** and **Gulf of Aden**. Concurrently, Somali pirate syndicates exploited the distraction of Western naval forces in the Red Sea to revive high-seas hijackings across the western and central **Arabian Sea** (as close as 500–700 nautical miles off the Indian coast of Gujarat).

* **Geo-Economic Shockwaves and Cape of Good Hope Rerouting:** Even though Western states launched the US-led **Operation Prosperity Guardian (December 2023)** and the EU’s **EUNAVFOR Operation Aspides (February 2024)** alongside US-UK airstrikes on Houthi launch sites in Yemen, Houthi mobile missile batteries continued to strike commercial vessels—including Indian-crewed or India-bound tankers such as the **MV Chem Pluto** (struck by a drone 217 nautical miles off Porbandar in December 2023), the **MV Sai Baba**, and the **MV Marlin Luanda**. Consequently, major global shipping lines (Maersk, Hapag-Lloyd, MSC) abandoned the Red Sea–Suez route and rerouted vessels around Africa’s **Cape of Good Hope**, adding **3,500+ nautical miles (10 to 14 additional sailing days)** to India-Europe voyages, surging container freight rates by **150–300%**, and inflating war-risk marine insurance premiums for Indian agricultural, engineering, and textile exporters, as well as Russian crude shipments bound for Indian refineries.

#### The Indian Navy’s Operation Sankalp: Forward Deployment, Anti-Piracy Interdictions, and Net Security Provider Statecraft

Rather than joining the US-led **Operation Prosperity Guardian**—which would have compromised India’s **strategic autonomy** and exposed Indian-flagged vessels to direct Houthi targeting due to India’s diplomatic ties with Tehran—New Delhi dramatically expanded its independent national maritime operation: **Operation Sankalp**. Originally launched in **June 2019** in the **Strait of Hormuz / Gulf of Oman** following tanker sabotage incidents to escort Indian-flagged merchant vessels, **Operation Sankalp** was scaled up between **December 2023 and 2026** into the largest sustained forward naval deployment in post-independence Indian history.

At the peak of the Red Sea and Arabian Sea crisis, the Indian Navy maintained a continuous forward presence of **10 to 12 frontline guided-missile destroyers and stealth frigates** (*INS Visakhapatnam*, *INS Mormugao*, *INS Kolkata*, *INS Kochi*, *INS Chennai*, *INS Tarkash*, *INS Talwar*, and *INS Sumitra*), supported by **P-8I Neptune** long-range maritime patrol aircraft, **MQ-9B SeaGuardian** HALE UAVs, and **Marine Commandos (MARCOS)** across three overlapping operational boxes: (1) the **Gulf of Aden / Bab-el-Mandeb approaches**, (2) the **Central/Western Arabian Sea and Somali Basin**, and (3) the **Persian Gulf / Gulf of Oman**.

* **High-Impact Kinetic Interdictions and Rescue Operations:** Under **Operation Sankalp**, the Indian Navy responded to over **30 maritime incidents**, escorted more than **15 million metric tons of critical cargo**, rescued over **110 mariners (including 45+ Indian seafarers)** regardless of nationality, and neutralized multiple piracy hijacking attempts:
  * **Rescue of MV Lila Norfolk (January 2024):** *INS Chennai* intercepted the Liberian-flagged bulk carrier in the North Arabian Sea and deployed **MARCOS** via helicopter and rigid-hull inflatable boats (RHIBs) to safely rescue all **21 crew members (including 15 Indians)**.
  * **Firefighting Aboard MV Marlin Luanda (January 2024):** After a Houthi anti-ship ballistic missile struck the Marshall Islands-flagged oil tanker carrying Russian naphtha in the Gulf of Aden, the Indian guided-missile destroyer ***INS Visakhapatnam*** deployed its nuclear-biological-chemical defense and firefighting teams for hours to prevent a catastrophic hull explosion and save its 22 Indian crew members.
  * **The MV Ruen Expeditionary Takedown (March 2024):** In a textbook display of expeditionary blue-water reach **1,400 nautical miles (2,600 km) from the Indian coastline**, the destroyer ***INS Kolkata*** (supported by *INS Subhadra*, P-8I aircraft, SeaGuardian drones, and an IAF **C-17 Globemaster** that precision-airdropped **MARCOS** commandos and combat boats into the Arabian Sea) cornered the hijacked Maltese-flagged vessel **MV Ruen**, forced the unconditional surrender of **35 armed Somali pirates** (who were brought to Mumbai for prosecution under India’s **Maritime Anti-Piracy Act, 2022**), and rescued all **17 crew hostages** without a single friendly casualty.
* **Operationalizing the MAHASAGAR Doctrine:** Combining kinetic deterrence against pirates in the Arabian Sea, quiet diplomatic backchanneling with **Iran** (where External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar visited Tehran in **January 2024** to directly convey India’s red lines regarding attacks on merchant shipping near Indian waters), and de-confliction with the Bahrain-based **Combined Maritime Forces (CMF)** and the Gurugram-based **IFC-IOR**, **Operation Sankalp** cemented the Indian Navy’s credibility as the premier **First Responder and Preferred Security Partner** across the Western Indian Ocean under the **MAHASAGAR Doctrine**.
