# Part 4: Statutory Wildlife Regimes, Forest Jurisprudence, Biodiversity Laws, and Global Biodiversity Treaties (CBD COP16 and CITES COP20)

## I. Statutory Wildlife Regimes and the Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2022

### 1. Institutional Machinery and Statutory Architecture of the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972

The **Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 (WPA 1972)** represents the foundational statutory intervention by the Indian state to arrest the thermodynamic degradation of trophic webs caused by unregulated commercial hunting, colonial game-licensing, and habitat fragmentation. Prior to **1972**, wildlife governance was fragmented across state-level game laws and the **Indian Forest Act, 1927**, which treated fauna merely as an incidental "forest-produce" adjunct to commercial timber extraction. Enacted by Parliament on **September 9, 1972**, by invoking **Article 252** of the Constitution (resolutions passed by 11 State Legislatures empowering Parliament to legislate on a State List subject), and subsequently anchored into the **Concurrent List (Entry 17B: Protection of wild animals and birds)** alongside **Article 48A** and **Article 51A(g)** through the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, the **WPA 1972** establishes a centralized-cum-federal command-and-control architecture. It operates at the structural faultline between sovereign police powers restricting private property/trade in biological specimens and the ecological imperative of maintaining Minimum Viable Populations (MVPs) across fragmented landscapes.

The operational efficacy of the **WPA 1972** rests upon a multi-tiered institutional matrix that integrates apex policy-making, specialized scientific oversight, transboundary trade intelligence, and field-level magisterial enforcement:

*   **National Board for Wildlife (NBWL) and its Standing Committee (Sections 5A–5C):** Inserted by the **Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2002** (replacing the non-statutory Indian Board for Wildlife constituted in 1952), the **NBWL** is a **47-member** apex statutory body chaired *ex-officio* by the **Prime Minister of India**, with the **Union Minister in charge of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC)** serving as Vice-Chairperson. Because the full Board rarely convenes, Section 5B empowers the NBWL to constitute a **Standing Committee**, chaired by the **Union Minister of Environment, Forest and Climate Change**, which exercises delegated statutory authority. Under **Sections 29, 35(6), and 36A**, no alteration of the boundaries of a National Park or Wildlife Sanctuary, and no destruction, exploitation, or diversion of wildlife or forest land within them (or within notified Eco-Sensitive Zones as mandated by the Supreme Court in *Goa Foundation*), can occur without the prior recommendation of the **NBWL Standing Committee**.
*   **State Boards for Wildlife (SBWL) and Chief Wildlife Wardens (Sections 4 and 6):** Mirroring the federal structure, **Section 6** mandates every State and Union Territory to constitute a **State Board for Wildlife (SBWL)** chaired by the **Chief Minister** (or Administrator in UTs), responsible for advising the state government on the selection and management of protected areas and the harmonization of forest-dweller rights with conservation. At the executive apex within each state sits the **Chief Wildlife Warden (CWLW)** appointed under **Section 4**, who exercises statutory control over all Wildlife Sanctuaries and National Parks, issues permits for scientific research or translocation under **Sections 11 and 12**, and regulates the possession of captive Schedule I animals.
*   **Central Zoo Authority (CZA) (Chapter IVA, Sections 38A–38J):** Inserted via the **1991 Amendment Act**, the **CZA** (established in **1992**, headquartered in **New Delhi**) is a **12-member** statutory body (Chairperson: **Union Environment Minister**; plus 10 members and a full-time Member-Secretary) tasked with enforcing minimum ecological, veterinary, and behavioral husbandry standards across Indian zoos. No zoo can operate without **CZA recognition (Section 38H)**, and the CZA coordinates planned *ex-situ* conservation breeding programs (studbooks for **73+ critically endangered species**), regulates the acquisition, exchange, or loaning of Schedule I and II animals between zoos (**Section 38I**), and prevents the proliferation of unregulated menageries.
*   **National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) (Chapter IVB, Sections 38K–38X):** Constituted in **December 2006** following the **2006 Amendment Act** (triggered by the local extinction of tigers in **Sariska** and **Panna**), the **NTCA** is chaired by the **Union Environment Minister** and brings statutory enforceability to **Project Tiger**. It approves State **Tiger Conservation Plans (Section 38V)**, enforces inviolate **Core/Critical Tiger Habitat (CTH)** norms, prescribes normative guidelines for tourism and buffer-zone coexistence, and prohibits the alteration of Tiger Reserve boundaries or de-notification without both **NTCA** and **NBWL** concurrence (**Section 38W**).
*   **Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB) (Chapter IVC, Sections 38Y–38Z):** Simultaneously established under the **2006 Amendment Act** (operationalized in **2007**, headquartered in **New Delhi** with five regional offices in Delhi, Kolkata, Mumbai, Chennai, and Jabalpur), the **WCCB** is a multidisciplinary statutory intelligence agency mandated to combat organized wildlife poaching and illicit transnational trafficking. Under **Section 38Z**, it collects and collates intelligence, maintains a centralized crime data bank, assists state enforcement agencies in prosecutions, coordinates with **INTERPOL**, the **UN Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC)**, and the **CITES Secretariat**, and trains customs officials at international exit ports to detect concealed wildlife derivatives (such as pangolin scales, shahtoosh wool, red sanders, and star tortoises).

#### Protected Area Declaration Mechanics (Sections 18 to 38V)

The **WPA 1972** establishes a graded spatial hierarchy of *in-situ* Protected Areas (PAs), calibrated by the degree of human-use exclusion, tenurial settlement procedures, and ecological sensitivity:

*   **Wildlife Sanctuaries (Sections 18–26A):** The State Government may declare its *intention* to constitute any area (outside already existing Reserved Forests or territorial waters) of adequate ecological, faunal, floral, geomorphological, or zoological significance as a **Sanctuary** under **Section 18**. Immediately thereafter, the District Collector acts as the Settlement Officer (**Sections 19–25**) to inquire into and determine pre-existing private and community land or *nistar* rights. Under **Section 24(2)(c)**, the Collector, in consultation with the **Chief Wildlife Warden**, may allow the continuation of any right of any person in or over any land within the limits of the Sanctuary (such as regulated livestock grazing or non-commercial minor forest produce collection by local communities), before issuing the final notification under **Section 26A**. Crucially, if the proposed area comprises a **Reserved Forest** or any part of the **territorial waters**, the State Government can directly issue a final notification under **Section 26A(1)(b)** without repeating the Section 19–25 settlement process, provided occupational and marine-fishing livelihoods of local Scheduled Tribes and traditional fishers are protected.
*   **National Parks (Sections 35 and 38):** Declared by the State Government under **Section 35** (or by the Central Government under **Section 38** over areas leased/transferred to it), a **National Park** represents an ecological climax zone of the highest statutory insulation. Unlike a Sanctuary, **Section 35(6) and (7)** impose an absolute prohibition: **no grazing of any livestock** shall be permitted inside a National Park, and no private tenurial or forest-produce rights can be allowed to continue under Section 24(2)(c) once the final notification is issued. All rights must be completely extinguished or acquired prior to final notification, and entry is restricted strictly to persons granted explicit permission by the **Chief Wildlife Warden** under **Section 28** for research, photography, tourism, or official duty.
*   **Conservation Reserves and Community Reserves (Sections 36A–36D):** Inserted by the **2002 Amendment Act** to establish landscape-level migratory corridors without triggering displacement, **Conservation Reserves (Section 36A)** are declared by the State Government on **uninhabited government-owned lands** adjacent to National Parks and Sanctuaries (after consulting local communities) and managed by a **Conservation Reserve Management Committee (Section 36B)**. Conversely, **Community Reserves (Section 36C)** are declared on **private or community-owned lands** (not comprised within a NP, WLS, or Conservation Reserve) where an individual or community has volunteered to conserve wildlife and its habitat, managed by a **Community Reserve Management Committee (Section 36D)** consisting of five representatives nominated by the local **Gram Sabha** or Panchayat alongside one Forest Department officer. No change in land-use pattern can be made inside a Community Reserve except by a resolution passed by the Management Committee and approved by the State Government.

### 2. The Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2022: Schedule Rationalization, CITES Integration, and Invasive Ecology

Enacted in **December 2022** and brought into force on **April 1, 2023**, the **Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2022** represents the most comprehensive structural overhaul of Indian wildlife law in five decades. For fifty years, the **WPA 1972** suffered from two critical legal defects: first, its **six-schedule architecture** was scientifically opaque, splitting animal protection across four arbitrary schedules (Schedules I, II, III, and IV) ordered by common English names rather than taxonomic phylogeny, while maintaining a problematic **Schedule V (Vermin)** and an unaligned **Schedule VI (Specified Plants)**; second, although India ratified **CITES** in **1976**, it lacked dedicated domestic implementing legislation, forcing enforcement agencies to rely on fractured provisions of the **Customs Act, 1962** and the **Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992**, resulting in India being placed in **Category 2** of the CITES National Legislation Project.

The **2022 Amendment Act** resolves these structural bottlenecks through five decisive statutory interventions:

*   **Rationalization of 6 Schedules down to 4 Taxonomic Schedules:** The Amendment abolishes the archaic six-schedule structure and replaces it with **four scientifically ordered schedules** utilizing binomial nomenclature:
    *   **Schedule I:** Contains wild animal species (mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fishes, corals, molluscs, and arthropods) requiring the **highest degree of statutory protection** against hunting, poaching, and trade, carrying the severest mandatory minimum imprisonment (**3 to 7 years**) and financial penalties (**minimum ₹25,000** under amended **Section 51**).
    *   **Schedule II:** Consolidates animal species requiring a **lesser degree of protection** (merging species previously scattered across old Schedules II, III, and IV), where offenses carry general penalties enhanced by the 2022 Amendment from ₹25,000 up to **₹1,00,000**.
    *   **Schedule III:** Replaces the old Schedule VI to cover **Protected Plant Species** (such as *Beddomes' cycad*, *Blue Vanda*, *Kuth*, *Ladies slipper orchids*, *Pitcher plant*, *Red Vanda*, and newly reorganized flora), prohibiting their willful picking, uprooting, cultivation without license, or commercial sale from wild habitats (**Chapter IIIA**).
    *   **Schedule IV:** A wholly new schedule listing **Scheduled Specimens** corresponding directly to Appendices I, II, and III of **CITES**, legally binding international exotic wildlife trade into the domestic penal framework of the WPA.
    *   **Abolition of Schedule V (Vermin) while Retaining Section 62:** The standalone **Schedule V** (which permanently branded Common Crows, Fruit Bats, Rats, and Mice as unprotected "vermin" nationwide) has been **omitted**. Ecologically, fruit bats are vital nocturnal pollinators and seed dispersers, and permanent nationwide vermin listing was scientifically indefensible. Instead, under **Section 62**, the **Central Government** retains the targeted power to declare by notification any wild animal **other than those specified in Schedule I** to be **vermin** for any specified area and for a specified period (for instance, localized crop-raiding populations of Nilgai or Rhesus Macaque upon verified state requests), during which period such animals are deemed not to be included in Schedule II.
*   **Insertion of Chapter VB (Sections 49D–49R) for CITES Enforcement:** To elevate India to **Category 1** compliance under the CITES National Legislation Project, **Chapter VB** creates dedicated domestic CITES machinery:
    *   **Management Authority (Section 49E):** The Central Government designates an officer not below the rank of **Additional Director General of Forests (Wildlife)** as the **CITES Management Authority**, empowered to grant export/import permits and re-export certificates for any **Schedule IV** specimen only after verifying lawful acquisition, humane transport conditions, and absence of survival detriment.
    *   **Scientific Authority (Section 49F):** The Central Government designates premier premier research institutes—including the **Zoological Survey of India (ZSI)**, **Botanical Survey of India (BSI)**, **Wildlife Institute of India (WII)**, **Central Marine Fisheries Research Institute (CMFRI)**, and **Indian Council of Forestry Research and Education (ICFRE)**—as **Scientific Authorities** to conduct **Non-Detriment Findings (NDFs)** advising whether international trade in a specimen is detrimental to its wild survival and monitoring export quotas.
    *   **Mandatory Registration of Exotic Live Species (Section 49M):** Every person possessing a living specimen of any species listed in **Schedule IV** (such as exotic macaws, lemurs, kangaroos, or ball pythons imported via the pet trade) must register such possession, report births and deaths of progeny, and obtain prior sanction before transferring ownership with the Authorized Officer through the digital **PARIVESH portal**.
*   **Regulation of Invasive Alien Species (Section 62A):** Addressing one of the primary drivers of the **HIPPO** extinction framework (detailed in Parts 2 and 3), the newly inserted **Section 62A** empowers the **Central Government** to regulate or prohibit the import, trade, possession, or proliferation of **Invasive Alien Species**—defined strictly as any non-native species of animal or plant whose introduction or spread threatens or adversely impacts native wildlife or its habitat—and authorizes officers to seize, confiscate, and destroy such invasive specimens.
*   **Captive Elephant Transfer Exemption (Section 43(2) Proviso and 2024 Rules):** Under the original **Section 43(1)**, no person having a certificate of ownership for a captive animal could transfer it by way of sale or any commercial transaction. However, the **2022 Amendment** inserted a contentious proviso to **Section 43(2)** permitting the transfer or transport of a **captive elephant** for a **"religious or any other purpose"** by a person holding a valid certificate of ownership, subject to terms and conditions prescribed by the Central Government. Operationalizing this, the MoEFCC notified the **Captive Elephant (Transfer or Transport) Rules, 2024**, which mandate that before any transfer within or between states occurs, the **genetic profile** of the elephant must be registered on the MoEFCC electronic database, a veterinary health certificate must be issued, and the **Chief Wildlife Wardens** of both the originating and recipient states must certify that the current owner can no longer maintain the elephant properly and the new custodian possesses adequate housing and upkeep capacity. Conservation biologists critique this carve-out as creating a potential legal laundering channel for wild-caught elephants captured from Northeast and East Indian forest corridors.
*   **Voluntary Surrender (Section 42A) and Gram Sabha Harmonization (Section 33B):** Under **Section 42A**, any person may voluntarily surrender any captive animal or animal article to the Chief Wildlife Warden without compensation and without facing prosecution for simple possession, whereupon the surrendered item becomes State property. Furthermore, amending **Section 33**, the Act mandates that Wildlife Sanctuary management plans must be prepared in accordance with guidelines issued by the Central Government and, where the Sanctuary falls within a **Scheduled Area** or an area where rights have been recognized under the **Forest Rights Act, 2006**, only after **consultation with the concerned Gram Sabha**.

## II. Forest Jurisprudence: The Indian Forest Act 1927, the Forest Rights Act 2006, and Animal Welfare Law

### 3. The Indian Forest Act, 1927 and the 2017 Bamboo Amendment

The **Indian Forest Act, 1927 (IFA 1927)** was enacted by the British colonial administration to consolidate the earlier Forest Acts of **1865** and **1878**, establishing state **eminent domain** over subcontinental woodlands to guarantee an uninterrupted supply of railway sleepers (Sal, Teak, Deodar) and revenue extraction. Structurally, the **IFA 1927** does not define a "forest" ecologically; instead, it operates as a territorial land-classification and transit-policing statute that partitions forest landscapes into three legal categories based on the extinguishment or regulation of customary community rights, alongside regulating the movement of **timber and other forest-produce (Sections 41–42)**.

The three statutory forest categories and the **2017 Amendment** operate through distinct legal mechanisms:

*   **Reserved Forests (Chapter II, Sections 3–27):** Constituted by the State Government over any forest-land or waste-land which is the property of the Government, **Reserved Forests** represent the most stringently policed forest category, governed by the legal maxim that ***everything is prohibited unless specifically permitted***. Under **Section 4**, the State issues a preliminary notification and appoints a **Forest Settlement Officer (FSO)**, who inquiries into all claims of pasture, forest-produce, or shifting cultivation (**Sections 6–16**). Claims are either extinguished via compensation, commuted, or transferred before the final **Section 20 notification** is published. Once notified, no new rights can be acquired except by succession or written government contract (**Section 23**), and **Section 26** criminalizes any fresh clearing, setting of fire, cattle trespass, quarrying, or tree-felling without permission.
*   **Protected Forests (Chapter IV, Sections 29–34):** Declared over government forest-land or waste-land that has not been included in a Reserved Forest, **Protected Forests** are governed by the inverse legal maxim that ***everything is permitted unless specifically prohibited***. Under **Section 29**, the State Government records existing government and private rights without undergoing the prolonged extinguishment settlement of Chapter II. However, under **Section 30**, the State Government may by notification declare specific classes of trees in a Protected Forest to be reserved, prohibit stone-quarrying or lime/charcoal burning, and close any portion of the forest for up to **30 years** (suspending private rights during closure, provided sufficient remainder areas are left open for community exercise of rights).
*   **Village Forests (Chapter III, Section 28):** Under **Section 28**, the State Government may assign to any **village community** the rights of the Government to or over any land which has been constituted a **Reserved Forest**, and may make rules prescribing the conditions under which the community is to be provided with timber, other forest-produce, or pasture, and their corresponding duties for protecting and improving such forest (historically exemplified by the **Kumaon Van Panchayats** of Uttarakhand).
*   **The Indian Forest (Amendment) Act, 2017 (Non-Forest Bamboo Exemption):** Botanically, **bamboo** is a perennial monocotyledonous grass belonging to the family **Poaceae** (subfamily **Bambusoideae**). However, **Section 2(7)** of the colonial **IFA 1927** legally classified bamboo as a **"tree"** (*"‘tree’ includes palms, bamboos, stumps, brush-wood and canes"*). Consequently, bamboo fell within the definition of "timber" under **Section 2(6)**, meaning that even a farmer or tribal household cultivating bamboo on private agricultural land required onerous Forest Department felling and transit permits (**Sections 41–42**) to harvest and transport it, severely depressing domestic supply and forcing India to import bamboo products despite holding ~19% of global bamboo area. The **Indian Forest (Amendment) Act, 2017** deleted the word *"bamboos"* from **Section 2(7)**. This created a clean statutory bifurcation:
    *   **Bamboo grown in Non-Forest Areas:** Ceased to be a "tree" or "timber," completely freeing farmers and artisans from felling and transit permit requirements across state borders and catalyzing the restructured **National Bamboo Mission**.
    *   **Bamboo grown inside Forest Areas:** Continues to be governed by the **IFA 1927** because **Section 2(4)(b)(i)** explicitly includes *"trees and leaves, flowers and fruits, and all other parts or produce not hereinbefore mentioned, of trees"* and *"plants not being trees (including grass, creepers, reeds and moss)"* found in or brought from a forest as **"forest-produce"**. Inside forest areas, the ownership and transit of bamboo are concurrently governed by the **Gram Sabha** under **Section 3(1)(c)** of the **Forest Rights Act, 2006**.

### 4. The Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006: Tenurial Justice, Community Forest Resources, and Critical Wildlife Habitats

Enacted in **December 2006** and brought into force on **January 1, 2008**, the **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA 2006)**—administered by the **Ministry of Tribal Affairs (MoTA)** as the nodal ministry rather than the MoEFCC—marks a paradigm shift from colonial "fortress conservation" to rights-based democratic conservation. Its Preamble explicitly acknowledges that during the colonial and post-colonial consolidation of state forests under the **IFA 1927**, ancestral rights and tenures of forest-dwelling communities were unrecorded, turning traditional stewards into "encroachers" on their own homelands despite their integral role in sustaining forest ecosystem resilience.

The **FRA 2006** resolves the competing tension between human livelihood security and ecological carrying capacity through four statutory pillars:

*   **Bifurcated Eligibility Thresholds (Section 2(c) and Section 2(o)):** The Act recognizes rights for two distinct socio-legal categories:
    *   **Forest Dwelling Scheduled Tribes (FDSTs) (Section 2(c)):** Members or communities of Scheduled Tribes who primarily reside in and who depend on the forests or forest lands for *bona fide* livelihood needs, having occupied forest land prior to the statutory cut-off date of **December 13, 2005** (**Section 4(3)**).
    *   **Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (OTFDs) (Section 2(o)):** Any member or community who has for at least **three generations (defined as 75 years)** prior to **December 13, 2005**, primarily resided in and depended on the forests or forest lands for *bona fide* livelihood needs. This strict 75-year documentary/evidentiary threshold for OTFDs often acts as an administrative bottleneck compared to FDST claims.
*   **Four Clusters of Statutory Forest Rights (Section 3):**
    *   **Individual Forest Rights (IFR) / Title Rights (Section 3(1)(a)):** Right to hold and live in the forest land under the individual or common occupation for habitation or for self-cultivation for livelihood, capped at the actual area under occupation as of **December 13, 2005**, and subject to a **maximum ceiling of 4 hectares (Section 4(6))**. IFR titles are registered jointly in the names of both spouses, and are **inheritable, but not alienable or transferable (Section 4(4))**.
    *   **Use and Livelihood Community Rights (CR) (Sections 3(1)(b), (c), (d), (f), (g)):** Includes traditional *nistar* rights, conversion of **forest villages**, old habitations, or unrecorded settlements into **revenue villages**, grazing and seasonal pastoralist access (for nomadic/transhumant communities like **Gujjars, Bakarwals, Gaddis, and Rabaris**), and the **right of ownership, access to collect, use, and dispose of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)**—defined under **Section 2(i)** to include all non-timber forest produce of plant origin, explicitly including **bamboo, brushwood, stumps, cane, tussar, cocoons, honey, wax, lac, tendu/kendu leaves, medicinal plants, and herbs**. By vesting MFP ownership and transit pass issuance in the **Gram Sabha**, the FRA legally dismantles state forest corporation monopolies over tendu leaves and bamboo.
    *   **Community Forest Resource (CFR) Governance Rights (Section 3(1)(i) and Section 5):** The most ecologically transformative provision of the Act, conferring the right to **protect, regenerate, conserve, or manage any Community Forest Resource** (customary common forest land within the traditional boundaries of the village, including Reserved Forests, Protected Forests, and Protected Areas) which they have been traditionally protecting and conserving for sustainable use, reinforced by **Section 5** which empowers Gram Sabhas to prevent any activity that adversely affects wild animals, forest, and biodiversity.
    *   **Developmental Facilities Diversion (Section 3(2)) and PVTG Habitat Rights (Section 3(1)(e)):** **Section 3(1)(e)** recognizes community tenures of **habitat and habitation** for **Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs)** and pre-agricultural communities (covering large landscape territories, sacred groves, and foraging grounds, as granted to the **Baiga, Kamar, Bharia, Mankidia, and Paudi Bhuyan** tribes). Separately, **Section 3(2)** overrides the **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980** to allow the diversion of **less than 1 hectare** of forest land (involving the felling of not more than **75 trees per hectare**) for **13 specified public utility facilities** managed by the Government (such as schools, dispensaries, fair price shops, electric/telecom lines, drinking water pipelines, and non-conventional energy sources), provided it is recommended by the **Gram Sabha**.
*   **Critical Wildlife Habitats (CWH) vs. Critical Tiger Habitats (CTH) (Section 2(b) and Section 4(2)):** While the **FRA 2006** applies across all forest lands including **National Parks and Wildlife Sanctuaries**, it creates a scientifically rigorous mechanism for **Critical Wildlife Habitats (CWH)**—defined under **Section 2(b)** as areas of National Parks and Sanctuaries that are required to be kept as **inviolate** for the purposes of wildlife conservation, as determined and notified by the **Central Government in the MoEFCC** after open consultation by an Expert Committee (including a representative of MoTA). Under **Section 4(2)**, no forest rights holder can be resettled or have their rights modified in a CWH unless:
    1. The process of recognition and vesting of rights under the FRA is **complete** in all areas under consideration;
    2. It has been scientifically established that the activities of rights holders cause **irreversible damage** and threaten the existence of species, and that **no other reasonable option (coexistence)** exists;
    3. The State Government has prepared a resettlement and livelihood package that secures the **Free and Prior Informed Consent (FPIC)** of the concerned **Gram Sabhas** in writing, and the resettlement facilities and land allocation at the new site are **completed prior to actual relocation**.
    4. **The Statutory Non-Diversion Lock (Proviso to Section 4(2)):** Once a **Critical Wildlife Habitat** is notified and communities are resettled, such critical wildlife habitats **shall not subsequently be diverted** by the State Government or the Central Government or any other entity for **any other uses** (preventing the state from evicting tribals in the name of conservation and later leasing the cleared land for mining, dams, or luxury ecotourism). Unlike **Critical Tiger Habitats (CTH)** under **Section 38V(4)** of the **WPA 1972** (which apply only to Tiger Reserves and are notified by the State Government), **CWHs** under the FRA apply across all National Parks and Sanctuaries, are notified by the **MoEFCC**, and carry the absolute non-diversion lock.

### 5. The Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 and the 2023 Constitutional Verdict on Jallikattu and Kambala

While the **WPA 1972** governs wild fauna *in-situ* as components of ecological food webs, the **Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (PCA) Act, 1960** (enacted under **Entry 17 of the Concurrent List**) governs the ethical treatment and welfare of **all animals** (defined under **Section 2(a)** as any living creature other than a human being, primarily covering domestic, draught, companion, performing, and laboratory animals). Grounded in **Section 3**, which casts a statutory duty on every person having charge of an animal to take all reasonable measures to ensure its well-being and prevent the infliction of **"unnecessary pain or suffering,"** the Act operationalizes institutional oversight and penalizes specific acts of cruelty under **Section 11** (including beating, overriding, overloading, mutilation, confinement in cages failing to provide reasonable opportunity for movement, and organizing animal fights or shooting competitions).

*   **Animal Welfare Board of India (AWBI) (Sections 4–10):** Established in **1962** under **Section 4** of the PCA Act under the pioneering leadership of **Rukmini Devi Arundale** (headquartered originally in Chennai and relocated in **2018** to **Ballabhgarh, Faridabad, Haryana**, functioning under the **Department of Animal Husbandry and Dairying**), the **AWBI** is a **28-member** statutory advisory body with a **3-year term** (including 6 Members of Parliament: 4 from Lok Sabha and 2 from Rajya Sabha). Under **Section 9**, the AWBI keeps the law on animal cruelty under constant review, advises the Central and State Governments on structural corrections to transport vehicles and slaughterhouses, grants recognition and financial aid to **Societies for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (SPCAs)** and *Pinjrapoles* (animal shelters), regulates the registration of **Performing Animals (Chapter V, Sections 21–27)** in films and circuses, and frames norms for **Animal Birth Control (ABC) Rules, 2023** for street dogs.Separately, **Chapter IV (Sections 14–20)** constitutes the statutory **Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA, formerly CPCSEA)** to enforce ethical bio-medical experimentation protocols.
*   **The Constitutional Trajectory from *A. Nagaraja* (2014) to the 5-Judge Constitution Bench Verdict (May 2023):** The sharpest jurisprudential collision in Indian animal law centered on whether traditional agrarian bovine sports—specifically **Jallikattu** (bull-taming in Tamil Nadu), **Kambala** (slush-track buffalo racing in coastal Karnataka), and **Bailgada Shariat** (bullock-cart racing in Maharashtra)—violate the PCA Act and the Constitution.
    *   In ***Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja* (2014)**, a 2-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court banned **Jallikattu** and bullock-cart races nationwide, holding that bulls are physiologically evolved as "flight-or-fight" herbivorous draught animals rather than racing animals (unlike horses), that forcing them into the *Vaadivasal* (narrow arena entrance) through tail-twisting, nose-ropes, and irritants inflicts inherent, structural **"unnecessary pain and suffering"** prohibited by **Sections 3 and 11(1)(a) & (m)** of the PCA Act, and that the **"Five Freedoms" of Animals** (recognized by the World Organisation for Animal Health - WOAH) must be read into the right to life with dignity under **Article 21** alongside **Article 51A(g)** (compassion for living creatures).
    *   To overcome the *A. Nagaraja* judicial prohibition following massive public protests in **2017**, the State Legislatures of **Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Maharashtra** enacted state-specific Amendments to the **PCA Act, 1960** under **Article 254(2)** of the Constitution (Concurrent List legislation receiving **Presidential Assent**), explicitly exempting **Jallikattu, Kambala, and bullock-cart racing** from the ambit of the PCA Act on the grounds of preserving **cultural heritage** (**Article 29(1)**) and ensuring the survival and genetic conservation of **native indigenous cattle breeds** (such as *Pulikulam*, *Kangayam*, *Umblachery*, *Bargur*, and *Malai Maadu*, which would otherwise be abandoned to slaughter due to farm mechanization), accompanied by strict statutory arena rules prohibiting physical abuse.
    *   In ***Animal Welfare Board of India v. Union of India* (May 18, 2023)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** of the Supreme Court headed by Justice K.M. Joseph **unanimously upheld the constitutional validity** of the Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, and Karnataka Amendment Acts. The Constitution Bench laid down three foundational legal ratios:
        1. **Curing of the Statutory Defect:** The 2017 State Amendments were not a mere legislative override ("colorable legislation") of *A. Nagaraja*, because the states coupled the exemptions with binding statutory rules and Standard Operating Procedures (veterinary checks, arena barricades, banning sticks/irritants, District Collector monitoring) that substantially **minimized physical cruelty**, fundamentally altering the factual and legal substrate on which the 2014 judgment rested.
        2. **Refusal to Extend Article 21 Fundamental Rights to Animals in Abstract:** While reaffirming the statutory duty of human care under the PCA Act and **Article 51A(g)**, the 5-Judge Bench clarified that animals cannot be accorded the full panoply of fundamental rights guaranteed to **"persons"** under **Article 21** of the Constitution in a manner that invalidates duly enacted plenary legislation passed under **Entry 17 of List III** with Presidential Assent under **Article 254(2)**.
        3. **Judicial Deference to Legislative Determination of Cultural Heritage:** Whether **Jallikattu** (celebrated during the **Pongal** harvest festival) constitutes an integral part of the cultural heritage of Tamil Nadu is a mixed socio-historical question belonging to the **legislative domain**, not the judicial evidentiary forum; once the elected State Legislature has recorded that the sport is a centuries-old cultural tradition linked to indigenous breed conservation, the Court cannot substitute its subjective cultural assessment to strike down the statute under **Article 14**, though it directed District Collectors and Superintendents of Police to remain **strictly and personally responsible** for enforcing the anti-cruelty arena rules.

## III. Sovereign Bioprospecting and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (Amended 2023)

### 6. The Biological Diversity Act, 2002: Three-Tier Institutional Grid, PBRs, and ABS Architecture

Prior to the **1992 UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)**, global genetic resources were legally treated as the *"common heritage of mankind,"* enabling multinational pharmaceutical and agribusiness corporations from the Global North to freely extract wild germplasm, landraces, and ethnobotanical knowledge from megadiverse Global South nations, splice or synthesize the active compounds, and fence them behind exclusive **Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS)** patents without sharing a single dollar of royalty with the custodian communities—a structural exploitation termed **biopiracy**. Following high-profile legal battles fought by India to revoke foreign patents on traditional knowledge—including the **US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO)** patent on the wound-healing properties of **Turmeric (*Curcuma longa*)** (revoked in **1997** by CSIR), the **European Patent Office (EPO)** patent on the antifungal properties of **Neem (*Azadirachta indica*)** (revoked in **2000**), and the **RiceTec patent on Basmati rice**—India pioneered the **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)** in **2001** (translating codified Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, and Sowa Rigpa texts into 5 international languages for patent examiners) and enacted the **Biological Diversity Act, 2002 (BDA 2002)** to operationalize **Article 15 of the CBD** (sovereign rights of states over their biological resources).

The **BDA 2002** operates through a decentralized **three-tier institutional grid** that bifurcates regulatory scrutiny based on the nationality and ownership structure of the user entity:

*   **Tier 1 — National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) (Sections 3, 4, 6, 8, 19, 20):** Established in **2003** and headquartered in **Chennai, Tamil Nadu**, the **NBA** is a statutory autonomous body that acts as the sovereign gatekeeper against international biopiracy. Under **Section 3**, **no foreign national, Non-Resident Indian (NRI), foreign corporation**, or Indian company having foreign control (as recalibrated in 2023) can obtain any biological resource occurring in India or associated knowledge for **research, commercial utilization, or bio-survey and bio-utilization** without the **prior approval of the NBA**. Furthermore, **Section 4** prohibits any person (including an Indian citizen) from transferring the results of any research relating to Indian biological resources to any foreign entity without NBA approval (exempting collaborative research projects approved by the Central Government under **Section 5** and routine publication of academic research papers). Under **Section 21**, the NBA ensures **equitable Access and Benefit Sharing (ABS)** across six statutory modes:
    1. Grant of **joint ownership of Intellectual Property Rights** to the NBA or identified traditional knowledge holders;
    2. **Transfer of technology**;
    3. Location of **production, research, and development units** in areas that improve the living standards of the benefit claimers;
    4. Association of **Indian scientists**, benefit claimers, and local people with R&D and bio-survey activities;
    5. Setting up of a **venture capital fund** for aiding the cause of benefit claimers;
    6. Payment of **monetary compensation** and non-monetary benefits into the **National Biodiversity Fund (Section 27)**, from which **95% of accrued monetary benefits** are channeled down to the concerned **BMCs / benefit claimers** under the **ABS Guidelines, 2014 (revised 2025)**.
*   **Tier 2 — State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs) (Sections 7, 22–24):** Constituted by every State Government (whereas in Union Territories, the **NBA** exercises the powers of an SBB via Union Territory Biodiversity Councils), the **SBB** regulates **Indian citizens** and purely Indian companies/associations registered in India. Under **Section 7**, no Indian citizen or Indian corporate body can obtain any biological resource for **commercial utilization** (or bio-survey for commercial utilization) except after giving **prior intimation to the concerned State Biodiversity Board**. While pure scientific research by Indian citizens within India requires no SBB approval, commercial extraction by Indian industries triggers SBB scrutiny, empowering the SBB under **Section 24** to prohibit or restrict any activity that violates conservation sustainability or equitable benefit-sharing.
*   **Tier 3 — Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs) and People’s Biodiversity Registers (PBRs) (Section 41):** Under **Section 41(1)**, every local body at the Gram Panchayat, Taluk/Mandal, Zila Parishad, Municipality, and Municipal Corporation level must constitute a **Biodiversity Management Committee (BMC)** within its area for promoting conservation, sustainable use, and documentation of biological diversity—including the preservation of habitats, conservation of **landraces, folk varieties and cultivars, domesticated stocks and breeds of animals**, and microorganisms. Under **Rule 22 of the Biological Diversity Rules, 2004**, the primary statutory mandate of every BMC is to prepare and periodically update a **People’s Biodiversity Register (PBR)** in consultation with local people. With over **2,77,000+ BMCs** and **2,67,000+ PBRs** constituted nationwide (increasingly digitized onto a national e-PBR portal), the **PBR** serves as a legally admissible documentary repository of local ecological baselines and oral ethnobotanical knowledge, establishing **prior art** against fraudulent patent claims and identifying the exact local "benefit claimers" when the NBA or SBB negotiates an ABS agreement. Additionally, excluded from the regulatory net of the BDA under **Section 40** are **"Normally Traded Commodities" (NTCs)**—over 420 common agricultural, horticultural, and forestry commodities notified by the Central Government when traded strictly in bulk as raw commodities rather than for extracting genetic/biochemical leads.

The **2023 Amendment Act** restructures the **BDA 2002** across four critical axes, generating intense debate between industrial bio-economy proponents and conservation jurists:

*   **Redefining the Foreign Corporate Filter (Amended Section 3(2)(c)):** Under the original **2002 Act**, **Section 3(2)(c)** required prior **NBA approval** for any body corporate registered in India which had *"any non-Indian participation in its share capital or management."* Consequently, if an Indian pharmaceutical or agri-biotech start-up raised even **1% equity** from a foreign venture capital fund or had a single NRI on its board, or was listed on the stock exchange with Foreign Institutional Investor (FII) holdings, it was legally classified as a "foreign entity" and subjected to the onerous Section 3 NBA clearance regime rather than the simpler Section 7 SBB regime. The **2023 Amendment** replaces this threshold with the phrase **" company controlled by a foreigner within the meaning of clause (42) of section 2 of the Companies Act, 2013."** Thus, Indian-incorporated companies with minority foreign investment that remain under Indian management control are now treated as **domestic Indian entities**, massively easing foreign direct investment (FDI) into India's bio-economy.
*   **Exemption of Codified Traditional Knowledge, Registered AYUSH Practitioners, and Cultivated Medicinal Plants (Amended Section 7 and Section 40):** Under the original **Section 7**, only local people and communities of the area, including *vaids* and *hakims* practicing indigenous medicine, were exempted from giving prior intimation to SBBs. However, SBBs across states had begun serving notices demanding **ABS royalties (0.1% to 0.5% of annual gross ex-factory sale price)** from domestic **AYUSH manufacturers** even when they used medicinal plants grown on private agricultural lands or relied on public-domain texts like the *Charaka Samhita*. The **2023 Amendment** expands **Section 7** to completely exempt from prior SBB intimation and ABS payment obligations:
    1. Users of **codified traditional knowledge**;
    2. **Registered AYUSH practitioners** who have been practicing indigenous medicine for sustenance and livelihood; and
    3. **Cultivated medicinal plants and their products** (encouraging *ex-situ* farm cultivation of medicinal flora to reduce harvesting pressure on wild forest populations, subject to obtaining a **Certificate of Origin** for cultivated plants from the local **BMC** as prescribed under the **Biological Diversity Rules, 2024**).
    *   *Ecological Critique:* Conservation biologists warn that distinguishing between **wild-harvested** and **farm-cultivated** medicinal herbs (such as *Ashwagandha*, *Shatavari*, or *Kutki*) in raw bulk supply chains is practically impossible without molecular barcoding, creating a regulatory loophole where contractors may strip wild forest populations and pass them off as "cultivated" via fraudulent certificates, while the blanket exemption for "codified traditional knowledge" leaves it undefined whether large corporate AYUSH conglomerates (with multi-thousand-crore turnovers) qualify for zero-ABS exemption alongside village *vaids*.
*   **Two-Stage Recalibration of Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) Approvals (Amended Section 6):** Under the original **Section 6**, no person (whether Indian or foreign) could apply for any IPR in or outside India for any invention based on research on an Indian biological resource without obtaining the **prior approval of the NBA** (or before the sealing of the patent). Because the vast majority of academic and start-up patents never reach commercial production, requiring full ABS negotiation at the patent-filing stage created a severe backlog. Under the **amended Section 6**:
    *   **Foreign entities** covered under **Section 3(2)** must still obtain the **prior approval of the NBA** before the grant of any IPR.
    *   **Indian citizens and domestic Indian entities**, however, are only required to **register** their IPR application with the **NBA** prior to the grant of the patent, and must obtain formal **NBA approval and execute an ABS agreement only at the time of actual commercialization** of the patented invention.
*   **Complete Decriminalization of Offenses (Amended Section 55 and Section 55A):** Previously, **Section 55** treated violations of Sections 3, 4, 6, and 7 as **cognizable and non-bailable criminal offenses** punishable with **imprisonment for up to 5 years** (or 3 years for SBB violations) and fines up to ₹10 lakh. Aligned with the **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023** philosophy of eliminating criminal imprisonment for regulatory compliance failures to remove fear among scientists and entrepreneurs, the **2023 Amendment** **completely removes all imprisonment provisions** from the BDA. Instead, **amended Section 55** introduces a graded civil financial penalty regime ranging from **₹1,00,000 to ₹50,00,000**, and where the contravention continues, an additional penalty that may extend up to **₹1,00,000,000 (₹1 crore)**, determined after an inquiry conducted by an **Adjudicating Officer** not below the rank of **Joint Secretary** to the Government of India (or Secretary to the State Government) under **Section 55A**, with appeals lying to the **National Green Tribunal (NGT)**. Additionally, the **Member-Secretary** of the NBA has been elevated to the Principal Executive Officer, and representation in the NBA has been expanded to **16 ex-officio members** (including the **Ministry of AYUSH**) and **4 institutional representatives**.

## IV. Multilateral Biodiversity Architecture: CBD COP16, CITES COP20, CMS, and Global Genetic Commons

### 8. The UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), Cartagena & Nagoya Protocols, Kunming-Montreal GBF, and CBD COP16 (Cali Fund for DSI)

Opened for signature at the **1992 Rio Earth Summit (UNCED)** and entering into force on **December 29, 1993** (with **196 Parties**—near-universal ratification, with the **United States** notably remaining the sole UN member state that signed but never ratified the treaty; Secretariat located in **Montreal, Canada** under **UNEP**), the **United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)** is a legally binding framework treaty anchored on **three co-equal statutory objectives (Article 1)**: (1) the **conservation of biological diversity**, (2) the **sustainable use** of its components, and (3) the **fair and equitable sharing of the benefits** arising out of the utilization of genetic resources.

To enforce these three pillars across biotechnology risks, physical bioprospecting, spatial ecosystem targets, and genomic databases, the CBD operates through two binding legal protocols and the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (KM-GBF)**:

*   **The Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (Adopted 2000, Entered into Force September 11, 2003):** Grounded in **Principle 15 (Precautionary Principle)** of the Rio Declaration and **Article 19(3) of the CBD**, the **Cartagena Protocol** governs the safe handling, transport, and transboundary movement of **Living Modified Organisms (LMOs)** resulting from modern biotechnology that may have adverse effects on biological diversity and human health (excluding pharmaceuticals for humans addressed by other international organizations). It bifurcates import regulation into two procedures:
    *   **Advance Informed Agreement (AIA) Procedure:** Applies prior to the first intentional transboundary movement of **LMOs intended for intentional introduction into the environment** of the Party of import (such as genetically modified seeds for planting or GM fish for release). The exporter must notify the Competent National Authority of the importing country (in India, the **Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee - GEAC** under the **EPA 1986**), which conducts a scientific risk assessment and issues an explicit written consent before shipment.
    *   **LMOs Intended for Direct Use as Food or Feed, or for Processing (LMO-FFP):** Bulk agricultural shipments (such as GM soybean or corn grain for oil crushing) are exempt from the bilateral AIA procedure; instead, Parties making a domestic decision to approve an LMO-FFP for market release must notify all other Parties within **15 days** through the multilateral **Biosafety Clearing-House (BCH)**, and shipments must carry documentation stating that they *"may contain"* LMOs not intended for intentional planting.
    *   **Nagoya–Kuala Lumpur Supplementary Protocol on Liability and Redress (Adopted 2010, in force March 2018; ratified by India in 2014):** Establishes international rules and procedures for civil and administrative **response measures** (requiring operators to restore biodiversity to baseline conditions or reimburse state cleanup costs) in the event of ecological damage resulting from the transboundary movement of LMOs.
*   **The Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources and the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits (Adopted 2010 at COP10 in Nagoya, Japan; Entered into Force October 12, 2014):** Operationalizing the **third objective of the CBD**, the **Nagoya Protocol** creates a legally binding bilateral architecture to prevent biopiracy of physical genetic resources and **Traditional Knowledge associated with Genetic Resources (TKGR)**. Any user seeking access to a genetic resource must obtain the **Prior Informed Consent (PIC)** of the provider country (and the PIC or approval and involvement of **Indigenous and Local Communities** where they hold established rights over the resource or TK) and negotiate **Mutually Agreed Terms (MAT)** governing royalty and technology sharing. Once national permits (such as India's NBA approvals) are uploaded to the **ABS Clearing-House (ABSCH)**, they automatically become an **Internationally Recognized Certificate of Compliance (IRCC)**, requiring user-country checkpoints (such as European or Japanese patent offices and research agencies) to verify lawful acquisition.
*   **The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (KM-GBF, Adopted at COP15 in December 2022) and India’s Updated NBSAP (2024–2030):** Succeeding the expired **Aichi Biodiversity Targets (2011–2020)**—none of whose 20 targets were fully achieved globally—COP15 adopted the **KM-GBF**, structured around **4 Long-Term 2050 Goals (Goals A–D)** and **23 Action-Oriented 2030 Targets**:
    *   **Target 2 (30% Ecosystem Restoration):** Ensure that by **2030** at least **30%** of areas of degraded terrestrial, inland water, and marine and coastal ecosystems are under effective restoration.
    *   **Target 3 (The Flagship "30x30" Target):** Ensure and enable that by **2030** at least **30% of terrestrial and inland water areas, and of marine and coastal areas**, especially areas of particular importance for biodiversity and ecosystem functions, are effectively conserved and managed through ecologically representative, well-connected, and equitably governed systems of **Protected Areas (PAs)** and **Other Effective Area-based Conservation Measures (OECMs)**, recognizing Indigenous and traditional territories.
    *   **Target 6 (Invasive Alien Species):** Reduce the rates of introduction and establishment of other known or potential **invasive alien species by at least 50% by 2030**.
    *   **Target 7 (Pollution & Pesticides):** Reduce excess nutrients lost to the environment by at least **half**, and reduce the **overall risk from pesticides and highly hazardous chemicals by at least half** by 2030.
    *   **Target 18 & Target 19 (Subsidy Reform & Biodiversity Finance):** Identify by **2025**, and eliminate, phase out, or reform incentives, including **subsidies harmful for biodiversity**, by at least **$500 billion per year by 2030** (**Target 18**); and mobilize at least **$200 billion per year by 2030** from all sources for biodiversity (**Target 19**), including increasing international financial flows from developed to developing countries to at least **$20 billion per year by 2025** and **$30 billion per year by 2030**, channeled in part through the newly established **Global Biodiversity Framework Fund (GBFF)** housed under the **Global Environment Facility (GEF)** (launched at the 7th GEF Assembly in Vancouver in August 2023).
    *   **India’s Updated National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (NBSAP 2024–2030):** Submitted at **CBD COP16 on October 30, 2024** (making India one of only ~27% of Parties to submit a full updated NBSAP on time), India’s **NBSAP 2024–30** establishes **23 National Biodiversity Targets (NBTs)** directly mapped to the 23 KM-GBF targets across **six conservation domains** (area-based conservation; ecosystem resilience; threatened species recovery; agrobiodiversity across India's **22 agrobiodiversity hotspots**; sustainable use; and implementation mainstreaming). Formulated through a **"Whole-of-Government and Whole-of-Society"** approach integrating **23 Central Ministries**, State Biodiversity Boards, and BMCs, the NBSAP projects an average annual biodiversity financing requirement of **₹81,664 crore per year** between **2024–25 and 2029–30**, deploying both Protected Areas and **OECMs** to achieve India's domestic **30x30** commitment.

#### CBD COP16 (Cali, Colombia 2024 & Rome Resumed Session, February 2025): The "Cali Fund" for DSI and the Article 8(j) Subsidiary Body

While the **Nagoya Protocol (2010)** successfully regulated physical genetic samples (seeds, tissue, microbes carried across borders), synthetic biology and next-generation genomic sequencing created a massive technological loophole: researchers could sequence the DNA/RNA of a rare rain-forest plant or extremophile bacterium in situ, upload the **Digital Sequence Information (DSI)** to open-access public databases (such as GenBank, EMBL-EBI, and DDBJ), and allow pharmaceutical and biotech corporations in the Global North to download the sequence and chemically synthesize the gene or protein *in silico* without ever touching a physical biological sample or paying bilateral Nagoya ABS royalties—a phenomenon termed **digital biopiracy**.

Convened in **Cali, Colombia (October 21 – November 2, 2024)** under the theme *"Peace with Nature"* and concluded at its **resumed session in Rome, Italy (February 25–27, 2025)**, **CBD COP16** delivered four landmark multilateral breakthroughs:

*   **Operationalization of the Multilateral "Cali Fund" for Digital Sequence Information (DSI):** Building on the COP15 mandate to create a multilateral (rather than bilateral) mechanism for DSI so open scientific access is preserved while ending free-riding by commercial corporations, **COP16** adopted the operational modalities of the **Cali Fund for the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits from the Use of Digital Sequence Information on Genetic Resources** (officially launched in **Rome on February 25, 2025**, hosted by the **UN Multi-Partner Trust Fund Office**):
    *   **Contributing Sectors & Thresholds:** Large commercial enterprises operating in sectors that directly or indirectly benefit from the use of **DSI**—specifically **pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, agricultural and industrial biotechnology, nutraceuticals, and laboratory equipment/reagents** (exceeding at least two of three size thresholds: total assets of **$20 million**, sales of **$50 million**, or profits of **$5 million**, while exempting public academic research institutions)—are called upon to contribute **1% of their profits or 0.1% of their revenue** into the **Cali Fund**.
    *   **The 50% IPLC Allocation Lock:** In an unprecedented victory for environmental justice, COP16 mandated that **at least 50% of the total financial resources disbursed from the Cali Fund must be allocated directly to Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities (IPLCs)** (including women and youth) based on their **self-identified conservation needs**, either directly through indigenous institutions or via national focal points, while the remainder flows to developing countries to build bioinformatics capacity and implement their **NBSAPs**.
*   **Establishment of a Permanent Subsidiary Body on Article 8(j) (Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities):** For 26 years under the CBD, issues relating to **Article 8(j)** (respecting, preserving, and maintaining traditional knowledge and practices of indigenous and local communities) were relegated to an *ad hoc* temporary working group requiring periodic renewal. At **COP16 in Cali**, Parties took the historic decision to elevate the working group into a **permanent Subsidiary Body on Article 8(j)**—placing Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities on an equal permanent institutional footing with the CBD’s scientific body (SBSTTA) and implementation body (SBI), alongside formally recognizing the vital conservation role of **People of African Descent** (embodied across Latin America’s *Quilombola* and Afro-descendant territories).
*   **Marine EBSAs and the Rome Resumed Session Resource Mobilization Agreement (February 2025):** In Cali, COP16 broke an 8-year geopolitical deadlock to adopt a new scientific mechanism for identifying and updating **Ecologically or Biologically Significant Marine Areas (EBSAs)** in national and high-seas waters (directly feeding into MPA designations under the **2026 UN High Seas BBNJ Treaty**), alongside adopting a **Global Action Plan on Biodiversity and Health** (One Health approach). After the Cali session was suspended on November 2, 2024 due to loss of quorum during final finance negotiations, Parties reconvened at **FAO Headquarters in Rome (February 25–27, 2025)** and adopted the **Resource Mobilization Strategy (2025–2030)** to close the $700 billion annual global biodiversity finance gap, finalized the **KM-GBF Monitoring Framework** indicators, and established a roadmap starting at **COP17 (Yerevan, Armenia, 2026)** and concluding by **COP19 (2030)** to decide on a permanent, dedicated biodiversity financial mechanism under the direct authority of the CBD COP (addressing Global South critiques of donor-dominated GEF governance).

*   **CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, 1973):** Drafted following a **1963 IUCN resolution**, opened for signature in **Washington, D.C. in 1973** (hence known as the **Washington Convention**), and entering into force on **July 1, 1975** (administered by **UNEP** from **Geneva, Switzerland**, with **185 Parties** including India since 1976), **CITES** subjects international trade in specimens of over **40,900 species** (~6,610 animals and ~34,310 plants) to strict licensing across three Appendices:
    *   **Appendix I:** Lists species **threatened with extinction** which are or may be affected by trade. **International commercial trade in wild-caught specimens is strictly prohibited**; trade is permitted only in exceptional non-commercial circumstances (such as scientific research or zoo breeding exchange) and requires the prior grant and presentation of **both an Import Permit** (issued by the destination country) **and an Export Permit** (issued by the origin country), each backed by a Scientific Authority **Non-Detriment Finding (NDF)**.
    *   **Appendix II:** Lists species that are **not necessarily now threatened with extinction, but may become so unless trade in such specimens is subject to strict regulation** in order to avoid utilization incompatible with their survival (plus **"look-alike" species** listed so customs officers can control trade in threatened species). International commercial trade is allowed, but requires an **Export Permit** (or Re-Export Certificate) granted only after the exporting country's Scientific Authority issues a **positive NDF** confirming that harvesting will not harm wild populations (no Import Permit is required under CITES unless mandated by stricter domestic law).
    *   **Appendix III:** Contains species that are protected inside the borders of **at least one Party**, which has unilaterally asked other CITES Parties for assistance in controlling international trade. Trade requires an **Export Permit** if originating from the state that listed the species in Appendix III, or a **Certificate of Origin** if exported from any other Party. While CITES is **legally binding** on the Parties, it is **not self-executing**—it does not take the place of national laws, instead requiring each Party to enact domestic legislation (such as **Chapter VB and Schedule IV** of India’s **WPA Amendment Act, 2022**).
    *   **CITES COP19 (Panama City, November 2022) vs. CITES COP20 (Samarkand, Uzbekistan, November 24 – December 5, 2025):**
        *   At **CITES COP19 (Panama, 2022)**, two Indian freshwater turtle proposals were adopted: the Critically Endangered **Red-Crowned Roofed Turtle (*Batagur kachuga*)** and **Leith’s Softshell Turtle (*Nilssonia leithii*)** were **uplisted from Appendix II to Appendix I**, while the endemic **Jeypore Hill Gecko (*Cyrtodactylus jeyporensis*)** was included in **Appendix II**. Simultaneously, India secured a major trade relief for its ₹4,000-crore rural handicraft sector on **North Indian Rosewood / Shisham (*Dalbergia sissoo*)** (listed in Appendix II), obtaining a weight-based annotation exemption allowing exports of finished Shisham items weighing up to **10 kg per shipment** (excluding net packaging weight) without CITES permits, and India’s **WCCB Operation Turtshield** against illegal freshwater turtle trafficking received global commendation. Earlier in **2024**, following a updated NDF by **BSI** and **ICFRE**, CITES also lifted its Review of Significant Trade (RST) suspension on India’s export of **Red Sanders (*Pterocarpus santalinus*)** and set sustainable export quotas for cultivated **Agarwood (*Aquilaria malaccensis*)**.
        *   Convened in **Samarkand, Uzbekistan from November 24 to December 5, 2025**, **CITES COP20** marked the **50th anniversary** of the entry into force of the Convention, reviewing **51 species listing proposals** and adopting **39 proposals** covering over **120+ threatened species**:
            1. **Historic Elasmobranch (Shark & Ray) Uplistings to Appendix I:** Building on COP19's Appendix II listings, **COP20** adopted unprecedented protection for **74 shark and ray species**, including **uplisting 11 species to Appendix I** (imposing a complete global commercial trade ban on fins, gill plates, and meat) for the **Whale Shark (*Rhincodon typus*)** (protected in Schedule I of India's WPA), the **Oceanic Whitetip Shark (*Carcharhinus longimanus*)**, all **Manta and Devil Rays (family *Mobulidae*)**, and critically endangered **Wedgefishes and Giant Guitarfishes**, alongside tightening Appendix II controls on deep-water **Gulper Sharks** (heavily exploited for squalene liver oil used in cosmetics).
            2. **India’s Successful Defense of Guggul (*Commiphora wightii*):** The European Union submitted a proposal at **COP20** to include **Guggul / Indian Bdellium-tree (*Commiphora wightii*)**—an arid-zone thorny shrub native to Rajasthan and Gujarat whose oleo-gum-resin (*Guggulu*) is a foundational anti-inflammatory and lipid-lowering ingredient in **Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia**—in **CITES Appendix II**. **India successfully opposed and defeated the EU proposal**, demonstrating that domestic conservation, sustainable tapping guidelines by the National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB), and farm cultivation were actively restoring populations, that international trade was not the primary driver of wild decline, and that an Appendix II listing would impose disproportionate permit burdens on traditional **AYUSH** supply chains.
            3. **Rejection of Ivory/Rhino Horn Trade & Other Species Decisions:** **COP20** overwhelmingly rejected Southern African proposals to reopen international commercial trade in **elephant ivory** and **black and white rhinoceros horn**, rejected attempts to weaken protection for **giraffes**, uplisted **Okapis**, **Golden-bellied Mangabeys**, **Home’s Hinge-back Tortoise**, and all four **Galápagos Marine and Land Iguanas** to **Appendix I**, included **Striped Hyenas (*Hyaena hyaena*)** and **Dorcas Gazelles** in **Appendix II**, deleted the recovered South African **Bontebok (*Damaliscus pygargus pygargus*)** from Appendix II, and relaxed trade annotations on the **Saiga Antelope (*Saiga tatarica*)** in Kazakhstan following its population rebound.
*   **TRAFFIC (The Wildlife Trade Monitoring Network):** Established in **1976** as a strategic joint program of the **World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF)** and the **International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)** (headquartered in **Cambridge, United Kingdom**, with its **TRAFFIC India** office operating in partnership with WWF-India since **1991**), **TRAFFIC** is a non-governmental scientific and investigative organization—not an intergovernmental treaty—that works closely with the **CITES Secretariat** and national enforcement agencies (such as India's **WCCB**). It investigates illicit wildlife supply chains, publishes seizure analytics (on tiger parts, pangolins, rhino horns, timber, and marine products), and deploys sniffer-dog squads (*"Super Sniffers"*) across Indian railway hubs and forest divisions.
*   **CMS (Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals / Bonn Convention, 1979) and CMS COP14 (Samarkand, February 2024):** Signed in **Bonn, Germany in 1979** and entering into force in **1983** under the aegis of **UNEP** (with **133 Parties**; India has been a Party since **1983** and hosted **CMS COP13 at Gandhinagar, Gujarat in February 2020** with the **Great Indian Bustard**—"Gibi"—as the mascot), the **CMS** is the sole global UN convention specializing in the conservation of migratory species, their habitats, and their migration routes across national jurisdictional boundaries. It operates via two Appendices:
    *   **CMS Appendix I:** Lists migratory species that are **threatened with extinction** throughout all or a significant proportion of their range (including India's **Great Indian Bustard, Asian Elephant, Bengal Florican, Snow Leopard, Siberian Crane, and Ganges River Dolphin**). Range States are legally obligated to strictly prohibit the "taking" of such animals, conserve and restore their critical habitats, and eliminate physical obstacles to migration (such as linear infrastructure and power lines).
    *   **CMS Appendix II:** Lists migratory species that have an **unfavourable conservation status** and require international agreements for their conservation and management. Under **Article IV**, CMS serves as a framework convention under which Range States conclude either **legally binding Treaties/Agreements** (such as EUROBATS, ACCOBAMS, AEWA, or the Gorilla Agreement) or **less formal, non-binding Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs)**. India is a signatory to four pivotal **CMS MoUs**: (1) **Siberian Crane MoU (1998)**, (2) **Marine Turtles of the Indian Ocean and South-East Asia (IOSEA) MoU (2007)**, (3) **Dugong MoU (2008)**, and (4) **Birds of Prey (Raptors) MoU (2016)**.
    *   **CMS COP14 (Samarkand, Uzbekistan, February 12–17, 2024):** Convened under the slogan ***"Nature Knows No Borders"*** (the first global biodiversity COP held in Central Asia), **CMS COP14** released the landmark **First-Ever State of the World’s Migratory Species Report**, which revealed that **44% of CMS-listed species are experiencing population declines**, **22% (more than 1 in 5) are threatened with extinction**, and **97% of CMS-listed migratory fish species** face extinction risk due to overexploitation and habitat fragmentation. **COP14** added **14 new species** to the CMS Appendices (including the **Eurasian Lynx, Pallas’s Cat / Manul, Peruvian Pelican, Lahille’s Bottlenose Dolphin, Sand Tiger Shark, Blackchin Guitarfish, and Bull Ray**), launched the **Global Partnership on Ecological Connectivity (GPEC)** to safeguard transboundary wildlife corridors, and adopted the **India-championed Central Asian Flyway (CAF) Initiative** (establishing a coordinating institutional mechanism supported by India across **30 Range States** stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the Indian Ocean to protect over **600 migratory waterbird, raptor, and landbird species**).
*   **International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (ITPGRFA / International Seed Treaty, 2001):** Adopted by the **31st Session of the FAO Conference in November 2001** and entering into force on **June 29, 2004** (headquartered in **Rome, Italy**; India is a contracting party), the **ITPGRFA** harmonizes agricultural crop diversity with the **UN CBD**. Because agricultural staple crops result from millennia of multi-country pedigree cross-breeding, bilateral Nagoya tracking of individual parent seeds is unworkable. The Seed Treaty resolves this through three unique mechanisms:
    *   **The Multilateral System (MLS) of Access and Benefit-Sharing (Articles 10–13):** Establishes a pooled global gene bank covering **64 of the world’s most vital food and forage crops (Annex I)**—which together account for **80% of human calorie intake from plants** (including rice, wheat, maize, potato, chickpea, pigeon pea, sorghum, and finger millet)—facilitating fast-track germplasm exchange for research, breeding, and training via a standardized **Standard Material Transfer Agreement (SMTA)**. If a recipient commercializes a new plant variety derived from the MLS and restricts others from using it for further research and breeding (via patents), they must pay a mandatory **equitable share of monetary benefits (1.1% or 0.77% of sales)** into the Treaty’s multilateral **Benefit-Sharing Fund** supporting smallholder farmers in developing countries.
    *   **Farmers’ Rights (Article 9):** For the first time in a binding international treaty, **Article 9** formally recognizes the enormous historical and ongoing contribution of local and indigenous farmers in conserving, improving, and making available plant genetic resources, calling on national governments to protect traditional agricultural knowledge, ensure equitable benefit sharing, participate in national decision-making, and protect the **right of farmers to save, use, exchange, and sell farm-saved seed and propagating material**. India is the global pioneer in operationalizing Article 9 domestically through the **Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers’ Rights (PPV&FR) Act, 2001**, whose **Section 39(1)(iv)** guarantees that an Indian farmer is entitled to **save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share, or sell his farm produce including seed of a variety protected under the Act** in the same manner as he was entitled before the Act, with the sole proviso that the farmer shall not sell **branded seed** of a protected variety.
    *   **Global Crop Diversity Trust (Crop Trust) & Svalbard Global Seed Vault:** Article 15 integrates the *ex-situ* seed collections of the **Consultative Group on International Agricultural Research (CGIAR)** centers, backed financially by the **Crop Trust** and physically safeguarded inside the permafrost backup facility of the **Svalbard Global Seed Vault (Spitsbergen, Norway)**—complemented in India by the **National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (ICAR-NBPGR)** in New Delhi and India’s high-altitude permafrost seed vault at **Chang La, Ladakh** (established by DRDO-DIHAR and NBPGR at 17,300 feet).
*   **IPBES (Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services, 2012):** Established in **April 2012** in Panama City by 94 governments (including **India** as a founding member) and headquartered in **Bonn, Germany** (with **UNEP** providing secretariat services across **147+ member states**), **IPBES** is an **independent intergovernmental body** (not a UN treaty or subsidiary organ, though authorized by the UNGA/UNEP) that functions for biodiversity exactly as the **IPCC** functions for climate change. Operating across four functions—scientific assessments, policy support, capacity building, and knowledge generation while systematically integrating **Indigenous and Local Knowledge (ILK)** alongside peer-reviewed western science—IPBES produced the landmark **2019 Global Assessment Report** (documenting that **1 million species face extinction** driven in descending order by: 1. Land and sea use change, 2. Direct exploitation, 3. Climate change, 4. Pollution, and 5. Invasive alien species), the **2023 Invasive Alien Species Assessment** (quantifying annual global economic damage from biological invasions at over **$423 billion**), and at its **11th Plenary (IPBES-11) in Windhoek, Namibia (December 2024)**, adopted two monumental synthesis reports: the **Nexus Assessment** (mapping the indivisible interlinkages and trade-offs across **Biodiversity, Water, Food, Health, and Climate Change**, proving that siloed climate solutions like monoculture tree plantations destroy biodiversity and water tables) and the **Transformative Change Assessment** (identifying the structural economic, governance, and cultural shifts required to halt biodiversity loss).

### 10. Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 5: From Biotic & Genetic Governance to Atmospheric Ecotoxicology

Whereas **Parts 1 through 4** have deconstructed the open thermodynamic architecture of ecosystems, spatial biogeography, keystone megafauna recovery missions, and the multi-tiered domestic and multilateral legal regimes (**WPA 1972/2022, IFA 1927, FRA 2006, BDA 2002/2023, CBD COP16, and CITES COP20**) erected to insulate biological and genetic diversity against habitat fragmentation and commercial overexploitation, the integrity of every terrestrial and aquatic biome ultimately depends on the chemical equilibrium of its abiotic envelope. When anthropogenic combustion of fossil fuels, vehicular internal-combustion engines, thermal power plants, industrial stacks, and agrarian biomass burning inject massive fluxes of reactive chemical species into the lower troposphere, they overwhelm atmospheric dispersion capacity and initiate non-linear photochemical cascades. **Part 5 (Atmospheric Pollution, Ecotoxicology, Photochemical Smog, and Statutory Air Quality Regimes)** shifts the analytical lens from biotic conservation jurisprudence to atmospheric physics and chemical ecotoxicology—systematically unpacking the alveolar pathobiology of **PM2.5** and **carboxyhemoglobin**, the stoichiometric contrast between reducing **Classical London Smog** and oxidizing **Photochemical Los Angeles Smog (Tropospheric Ozone and Peroxyacyl Nitrates)**, the winter boundary-layer temperature inversions trapping pollutants across the **Indo-Gangetic Plain**, and the statutory abatement machinery of the **Air Act 1981, NAAQS, CAQM (GRAP), and the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP)**.
