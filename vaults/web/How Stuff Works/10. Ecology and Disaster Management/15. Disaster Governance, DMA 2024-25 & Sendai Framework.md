# Part 15: Institutional Disaster Governance, the Disaster Management (Amendment) Act 2024/2025, Mitigation Financing, and the Sendai Framework

## I. Epistemological Evolution and the Statutory Architecture of the Disaster Management Act, 2005

### 1. The Paradigm Shift: From Ex-Post Relief Codes to Ex-Ante Holistic Risk Governance

#### The Thermodynamic and Institutional Imperative of Proactive Risk Reduction
Disasters are not exogenous, stochastic "Acts of God" operating outside human agency; they are the materialization of accumulated physical hazard energy intersecting with socio-ecological exposure and structural vulnerability in the absence of absorptive coping capacity. For over a century, disaster administration across the Indian subcontinent remained trapped within the colonial epistemology of the **Famine Codes of the 1880s** and post-independence **Scarcity Manuals**. Under this archaic regime, the state apparatus remained dormant while tectonic strain accumulated along Himalayan thrusts, urban floodplains underwent impervious concretization, and coastal bioshields were degraded. Institutional action was triggered exclusively *ex-post*—after kinetic, hydro-meteorological, or anthropogenic energy had already ruptured human settlements—reducing disaster management to ad-hoc charity, gratuitous doles, temporary relief camps, and revenue remissions administered by State Relief Commissioners and the **Ministry of Agriculture**.

This relief-centric equilibrium collapsed under the cumulative thermodynamic and economic shockwaves of the **1993 Latur Earthquake**, the **1999 Odisha Super Cyclone**, the **2001 Bhuj Earthquake**, and the **2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami**. Empirical econometric audits demonstrated that every dollar invested in *ex-ante* structural mitigation and early warning systems averts between four and seven dollars in *ex-post* reconstruction losses, whereas unmitigated post-disaster relief perpetually drains sovereign capital budgets without lowering baseline vulnerability. Recognizing this fiscal and humanitarian unsustainability, the **J.C. Pant High Powered Committee (1999)** and the post-Tsunami legislative consensus catalyzed the enactment of the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** (enacted by Parliament on **December 23, 2005**, invoking **Entry 23 — "Social Security and Social Insurance, Employment and Unemployment"** of the **Concurrent List** in the **Seventh Schedule** of the Constitution). Simultaneously, nodal administrative responsibility for all disasters (except drought, pest attack, hailstorm, and cold wave/frost, which remained with the Ministry of Agriculture) was transferred to the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)**.

#### Operationalizing the Holistic Disaster Management Continuum
Under **Section 2(e) of the Disaster Management Act, 2005**, disaster management is statutorily defined not as a singular post-event intervention, but as a continuous and integrated process of planning, organizing, coordinating, and implementing measures across six interlocking phases:

*   **Prevention of Danger or Threat of Any Disaster:** Eliminating the genesis of hazard exposure through land-use prohibitions—such as enforcing **Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ-I)** no-development buffers, prohibiting residential siting inside **Major Accident Hazard (MAH)** chemical exclusion zones, and halting construction across active riverine floodways.
*   **Mitigation or Reduction of Risk:** Executing structural and non-structural engineering interventions that attenuate the severity or consequences of a hazard before it strikes—including seismic base-isolation retrofitting, **High-Density Polyethylene (HDPE)** siphoning of glacial lakes, slope bio-engineering in the Western Ghats, and mangrove shelterbelt restoration.
*   **Capacity Building and Preparedness:** Institutionalizing scientific forecasting, **Common Alerting Protocol (CAP)** multi-hazard early warning networks, standard operating procedures (SOPs), community **Aapda Mitra** training, and strategic stockpiling of emergency medical and search-and-rescue equipment.
*   **Prompt Response to Any Threatening Disaster Situation:** Mobilizing rapid aerodynamic, maritime, and terrestrial deployment of the **National Disaster Response Force (NDRF)** and **State Disaster Response Forces (SDRFs)** during the critical **Golden Hour** to execute mass evacuation, urban search and rescue (USAR), and **Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear (CBRN)** containment.
*   **Assessing the Severity or Magnitude of Effects:** Deploying **Post-Disaster Needs Assessments (PDNA)**, satellite synthetic aperture radar (SAR) inundation mapping, and statutory post-disaster audits to quantify mortality, infrastructure destruction, and ecological damage with empirical precision.
*   **Evacuation, Rescue, Relief, Rehabilitation, and Reconstruction:** Transitioning seamlessly from immediate life-sustaining relief (potable water, temporary shelters, minimum standards of relief) to long-term socio-economic rehabilitation and **"Build Back Better" (BBB)** capital reconstruction that embeds multi-hazard resilience into replacement infrastructure.

| Structural Dimension | Colonial & Pre-2005 Relief Paradigm | Post-2005 & 2025 Proactive Risk Governance Regime |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Epistemological Foundation** | Disasters treated as exogenous, unpredictable natural calamities requiring post-event state charity. | Disasters analyzed as a function of $\text{Risk} = \frac{\text{Hazard} \times \text{Exposure} \times \text{Vulnerability}}{\text{Capacity}}$ amenable to ex-ante engineering reduction. |
| **Statutory & Legal Basis** | Executive **Famine Codes (1880s)**, state **Scarcity Manuals**, and executive orders without statutory penalties. | Statutory mandate under the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** and **Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025**. |
| **Nodal Administrative Ministry** | **Ministry of Agriculture** (reflecting an agrarian, drought-and-famine institutional bias). | **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)** as the nodal ministry for overall disaster management (Agriculture retains drought/pest/hail/frost). |
| **Institutional Architecture** | Ad-hoc **Relief Commissioners** at the state level and revenue collectors at the district level. | Permanent three-tier statutory hierarchy (**NDMA, SDMA, DDMA**) plus municipal **UDMAs**, **NCMC**, **HLC**, **NIDM**, and **NDRF**. |
| **Fiscal & Financing Architecture** | **Calamity Relief Fund (CRF)** and **National Fund for Calamity Relief (NFCR)** restricted strictly to post-disaster gratuitous relief. | **NDRMF** and **SDRMF** with a statutory **80:20 bifurcation** into **Response Funds (NDRF/SDRF)** and dedicated **Mitigation Funds (NDMF/SDMF)**. |
| **Planning & Mainstreaming** | Zero integration with capital infrastructure budgeting or urban master plans. | Mandatory integration of disaster resilience into all central/state ministerial development plans (**Section 36/39**) and **CDRI** standards. |

By anchoring disaster governance in statutory law rather than executive discretion, the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** transformed disaster risk reduction from an episodic welfare expenditure into a permanent constitutional and administrative obligation of the Indian state.

### 2. The Three-Tier Institutional Matrix under the Disaster Management Act, 2005

#### Co-Operative Federalism vs. Unified Command in Crisis Governance
Managing high-impact disasters across a federal polity requires resolving a fundamental institutional tension: while constitutional authority over land use, municipal drainage, policing, and public health resides predominantly with State Governments and local bodies, mega-disasters routinely overwhelm sub-national fiscal, technical, and logistical capacities. The **Disaster Management Act, 2005** resolves this tension by constructing a synchronized three-tier institutional matrix—at the **National**, **State**, and **District** levels—that fuses centralized policy standardization and rapid paramilitary deployment with decentralized, ground-level execution.

#### Structural Anatomy of the National, State, and District Authorities
*   **The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA — Sections 3 to 7):** Established as the apex statutory body for disaster management in India, the **NDMA** is chaired *ex-officio* by the **Prime Minister of India** and comprises up to **nine members** nominated by the Chairperson, one of whom may be designated as the **Vice-Chairperson** (holding the rank and status of a Union Cabinet Minister, while other members hold the rank of Union Minister of State; in recent administrative practice, high-level domain members and a Member-Secretary of Secretary rank drive daily operations). Under **Section 6**, the **NDMA** lays down national policies on disaster management, issues binding guidelines to central ministries and state authorities for integrating disaster prevention and mitigation into developmental plans, recommends the provision of funds for mitigation, and coordinates bilateral/multilateral assistance to foreign countries affected by major disasters.
*   **The National Executive Committee (NEC — Sections 8 to 10):** To translate **NDMA** policies into inter-ministerial executive action, **Section 8** constitutes the **NEC**, chaired *ex-officio* by the **Union Home Secretary** (the Secretary to the Government of India in charge of the Ministry or Department having administrative control of disaster management). Its membership comprises the Secretaries to the Government of India in the Ministries/Departments of Agriculture, Atomic Energy, Defence, Drinking Water Supply, Environment and Forests, Finance (Expenditure), Health, Power, Rural Development, Science and Technology, Space, Telecommunications, Urban Development, Water Resources, and the **Chief of the Integrated Defence Staff of the Chiefs of Staff Committee**. The **NEC** acts as the coordinating and monitoring body for disaster management at the national level, evaluates preparedness across all governmental tiers, and coordinates response during threatening disaster situations.
*   **State Disaster Management Authorities (SDMAs — Sections 14 to 19) and State Executive Committees (SECs — Sections 20 to 24):** Mirroring the national architecture, every State Government must establish an **SDMA** chaired *ex-officio* by the **Chief Minister of the State** (or the **Lieutenant Governor/Administrator** in Union Territories), comprising up to **eight members** appointed by the Chief Minister and the **Chairperson of the State Executive Committee (the Chief Secretary)** serving as both *ex-officio* member and Chief Executive Officer of the **SDMA**. The **SDMA** lays down the State Disaster Management Policy, approves department-level mitigation plans, and reviews vulnerability reduction measures. It is assisted by the **SEC**, chaired *ex-officio* by the **Chief Secretary of the State** alongside four other Secretaries of the State Government, which coordinates ground-level execution, issues operational directions to district authorities, and manages emergency procurement and relief disbursement.
*   **District Disaster Management Authorities (DDMAs — Sections 25 to 34):** The operational fulcrum of field-level disaster governance is the **DDMA**, constituted in every district of the state. To synthesize bureaucratic command with democratic local-government accountability, **Section 25** mandates a dual-leadership structure:
    *   **Chairperson:** The **District Collector** (or **District Magistrate** or **Deputy Commissioner**, as the case may be) serves as the *ex-officio* Chairperson.
    *   **Co-Chairperson:** The **elected representative of the local authority**—specifically, the **Chairperson of the Zila Parishad**—serves as the *ex-officio* Co-Chairperson. Crucially, under the statutory proviso to **Section 25(2)**, in any **Tribal Area** governed by the **Sixth Schedule to the Constitution**, the **Chief Executive Member (CEM) of the District Council of the Autonomous District** serves as the *ex-officio* Co-Chairperson of the **DDMA**.
    *   **Composition and Powers:** The **DDMA** comprises up to seven members, including the **Chief Executive Officer of theDDMA** (typically an Additional District Magistrate), the **Superintendent of Police (SP)**, and the **Chief Medical Officer (CMO)** of the district. Under **Sections 30 and 34**, the **DDMA** acts as the district planning, coordinating, and implementing body; during a threatening disaster situation, it possesses sweeping statutory powers to requisition private or public vehicles, buildings, and equipment, control traffic, order mandatory evacuations, demolish unsafe structures, and direct any district department to deploy resources immediately.

This three-tier design ensures that national scientific standards formulated by the **NDMA** cascade directly through state-level fiscal and administrative channels (**SDMA/SEC**) into legally enforceable district-level field operations (**DDMA**).

### 3. Specialized Knowledge and Tactical Response Pillars: NIDM, 16 NDRF Battalions, and Aapda Mitra

#### Bridging Scientific Pedagogy, Paramilitary Precision, and Community First-Response
Institutional committees cannot physically extract survivors from collapsed reinforced-concrete slabs, neutralize leaking chlorine cylinders, or model probabilistic seismic hazard curves. Consequently, Chapter VII and Chapter VIII of the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** establish two specialized operational wings—the **National Institute of Disaster Management (NIDM)** for intellectual and pedagogical capacity building, and the **National Disaster Response Force (NDRF)** for specialist tactical response—supplemented by the community-level **Aapda Mitra** volunteer corps.

#### Statutory Mandate and Operational Mechanics of NIDM, NDRF, and Aapda Mitra
*   **The National Institute of Disaster Management (NIDM — Sections 42 & 43):** Upgrading the earlier National Centre for Disaster Management (established in 1995), **Section 42** constitutes **NIDM** as a statutory body corporate under the Ministry of Home Affairs, currently operating across multiple campuses (including **New Delhi/Rohini** and its Southern Campus in **Vijayawada, Andhra Pradesh**). Guided by a broad-based general body chaired by the **Union Home Minister** (with the Vice-Chairperson of NDMA as Vice-President) and managed by an **Executive Director**, **NIDM** is statutorily mandated to:
    *   Develop training modules, undertake research and documentation in disaster management, and organize training programs for civil servants, disaster managers, urban planners, and engineers.
    *   Formulate and implement a comprehensive **National Human Resource Plan** for disaster management.
    *   Provide technical assistance to state-level **Administrative Training Institutes (ATIs)** and State Disaster Management Centres to build sub-national pedagogical capacity.
*   **The National Disaster Response Force (NDRF — Sections 44 & 45):** Constituted under **Section 44** "for the purpose of specialist response to a threatening disaster situation or disaster," the **NDRF** operates under the general superintendence, direction, and control of the **NDMA**, while its operational command is vested in the **Director General of the NDRF (DG-NDRF)** appointed by the Central Government.
    *   **The 16-Battalion Multi-Hazard Deployment Grid:** Expanded progressively from an initial 8 battalions in 2006 to **16 specialized battalions** (plus 28 **Regional Response Centres - RRCs** for rapid zero-hour mobilization), **NDRF** personnel are drawn on deputation from India's **Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs)**—comprising **4 battalions from the Border Security Force (BSF)**, **4 from the Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF)**, **4 from the Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP)**, **2 from the Central Industrial Security Force (CISF)**, and **2 from the Sashastra Seema Bal (SSB)**. These 16 battalions are strategically located across high-vulnerability seismic, cyclonic, and riverine nodes including **Guwahati (Assam)**, **Haringhata/Kolkata (West Bengal)**, **Mundali/Cuttack (Odisha)**, **Arakkonam (Tamil Nadu)**, **Sudumbare/Pune (Maharashtra)**, **Gandhinagar (Gujarat)**, **Bathinda (Punjab)**, **Ghaziabad (Uttar Pradesh)**, **Bihta/Patna (Bihar)**, **Kondapavuluru/Vijayawada (Andhra Pradesh)**, **Varanasi (Uttar Pradesh)**, **Doimukh/Itanagar (Arunachal Pradesh)**, **Ladhowal/Ludhiana (Punjab)**, **Nurpur (Himachal Pradesh)**, **Gadarpur/Udham Singh Nagar (Uttarakhand)**, and **Samba/Jammu (J&K)**.
    *   **Internal Battalion Engineering & CBRN Specialization:** Each **NDRF battalion** has a sanctioned strength of **1,149 personnel** organized into **18 self-contained specialist Search and Rescue (SAR) teams** of **45 personnel each**. Unlike conventional infantry units, each team integrates structural engineers, cutting-and-breaching technicians, electricians, deep-water divers, paramedics, and trained canine squads, backed by specialized **Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear (CBRN)** reconnaissance and decontamination units. Adhering to **UN International Search and Rescue Advisory Group (INSARAG)** heavy/medium USAR standards, the **NDRF** pioneered the doctrine of **proactive pre-positioning** ahead of landfalling tropical cyclones (achieving near-zero mortality during **Cyclone Biparjoy** in 2023 and **Cyclone Dana** in 2024) and executed international humanitarian missions including **Operation Dost** during the **2023 Türkiye-Syria Earthquake** and **Operation Karuna** in Myanmar.
*   **Community Volunteerism — The Aapda Mitra and Yuva Aapda Mitra Schemes:** Because external paramilitary forces require transit time to reach cut-off Himalayan valleys or inundated deltaic islands, survival during the initial **Golden Hour** depends entirely on local communities. Implemented by the **NDMA** in partnership with **SDMAs**, the Centrally Sponsored **Aapda Mitra Scheme** trains **1,00,000 community volunteers** across the **350 most multi-hazard-prone districts** of India (expanded further through **Yuva Aapda Mitra** integrating **NCC, NSS, NYKS**, and **Bharat Scouts & Guides** youth cohorts). Volunteers undergo standardized field training in basic search and rescue, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), rope rescue, flood evacuation, and crowd management, and are covered by life/medical insurance while districts receive **Emergency Essential Resource Reserves (EERR)** stocked with inflatable boats, life jackets, wood-cutters, and solar searchlights.

By linking community first-responders (**Aapda Mitra**) with state forces (**SDRF**) and apex specialist battalions (**NDRF**), India's tactical response matrix eliminates the fatal temporal gap between hazard strike and technical intervention.

## II. The Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2024/2025 Overhaul: Urbanization of Risk and Statutory Rationalization

### 4. Structural Deficits of the 2005 Act and the Legislative Genesis of the 2024/2025 Amendments

#### Empirical Stress-Testing of the 2005 Architecture (2005–2024)
While the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** established a foundational statutory framework, two decades of high-frequency climate extremes, rapid urban agglomeration, and complex systemic crises exposed acute structural, jurisdictional, and operational bottlenecks within the original statute. First, the **2013 Task Force chaired by Dr. P.K. Mishra** documented pervasive institutional friction between the **NDMA** and the **NEC** (and correspondingly between **SDMAs** and **SECs**): under the original 2005 Act, the **NEC** was tasked with preparing the **National Plan** to be approved by the **NDMA**, creating an anomalous governance inversion where an implementation committee drafted the strategic blueprint for the policy authority. Second, the **NDMA** lacked statutory autonomy to frame service regulations, determine staffing cadres, or engage technical domain experts without prolonged bureaucratic delays, frequently leaving the apex authority under-staffed.

Third, the invocation of the **Disaster Management Act, 2005** across India in **March 2020** to manage the **COVID-19 pandemic**, alongside recurrent catastrophic urban floods in **Chennai (2015, 2023)**, **Bengaluru (2022)**, **Mumbai**, and **Delhi (2023)**, revealed a glaring metropolitan governance vacuum. The 2005 Act designed the **DDMA** around a rural administrative template headed by the **District Collector**. However, in sprawling metropolitan cities governed by **Municipal Corporations**, the primary drivers of disaster vulnerability—stormwater drainage networks, solid waste clogging, building-byelaw enforcement, urban heat islands, and subterranean utility corridors—fall exclusively under the jurisdiction of the **Municipal Commissioner**, while traffic and law-and-order coordination rest with the **Police Commissioner**. Relying on a rural-oriented **DDMA** fractured metropolitan command during urban emergencies. Finally, two of India's most powerful operational decision-making bodies—the **National Crisis Management Committee (NCMC)** and the **High-Level Committee (HLC)**—operated purely via executive notifications outside the statutory text of the 2005 Act.

#### Enactment of the Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2024/2025
To resolve these structural deficits without encroaching upon the federal autonomy of the States, the **Disaster Management (Amendment) Bill, 2024** was introduced in the Lok Sabha on **August 1, 2024**, passed by the Lok Sabha on **December 12, 2024**, approved by the Rajya Sabha on **March 25, 2025**, received Presidential Assent on **March 29, 2025**, and formally came into force on **April 9, 2025** as the **Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025 (Act No. 10 of 2025)**.

*   **Precision Statutory Lexicon (Amendment of Section 2):** To align domestic municipal law with the **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction** and **IPCC AR6** risk terminology, the Amendment Act expanded **Section 2** by inserting explicit statutory definitions for **`"disaster database"`**, **`"disaster risk"`**, **`"evacuation"`**, **`"exposure"`**, **`"hazard"`**, **`"vulnerability"`**, **`"High Level Committee"`**, **`"National Crisis Management Committee"`**, and **`"Urban Authority"`**.
*   **Mainstreaming Multi-Hazard Risk Assessment:** By codifying `disaster risk` as the potential loss of life, injury, or destroyed or damaged assets which could occur to a system, society, or community in a specific period of time, determined probabilistically as a function of **hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and capacity**, the 2024/2025 overhaul legally obligates all central and state authorities to conduct continuous, forward-looking probabilistic risk assessments rather than static historical hazard mapping.

### 5. Urban Disaster Management Authorities (UDMAs): Closing the Municipal Governance Vacuum

#### The Biophysical and Infrastructure Mechanics of Metropolitan Vulnerability
India's urban centres represent hyper-dense concentrations of human populations, vertical concrete assets, economic GDP output, and hazardous industrial infrastructure. Unlike agrarian districts where hazards manifest across dispersed floodplains or rainfed watersheds, metropolitan disasters are characterized by cascading systemic failures: high-intensity cloudburst rainfall exceeding $100\text{ mm/hour}$ hits impervious asphalt surfaces (runoff coefficient $>0.85$), overwhelms encroached natural stormwater channels (*Rajakaluves* or *Nullahs*), floods subterranean metro tunnels and basement electrical transformers, paralyzes telecommunications, and triggers simultaneous sewage contamination and vehicular gridlock. Managing such hyper-localized, rapid-onset urban cascades requires an authority that exercises direct statutory command over municipal engineering, town planning, fire services, and metropolitan policing.

#### Statutory Structure and Mandate of UDMAs under Section 41A
The **Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2024/2025** inserts a dedicated chapter/provision—**Section 41A**—empowering State Governments to constitute a separate **Urban Disaster Management Authority (UDMA)**:

*   **Geographic Jurisdiction and Statutory Carve-Outs:** Under **Section 41A(1)**, the State Government may, by notification, constitute an **Urban Disaster Management Authority (UDMA)** for the **State Capital** and for every city having a **Municipal Corporation**. Two explicit statutory exceptions are carved out: the **National Capital Territory (NCT) of Delhi** and the **Union Territory of Chandigarh** are excluded from Section 41A because their multi-jurisdictional governance structures are already directly integrated into specialized Union Territory/National Capital administrative frameworks. Once a **UDMA** is notified for a municipal corporation area, that urban territory is decoupled from the standard rural **DDMA** for localized urban disaster planning and execution, eliminating jurisdictional turf wars.
*   **Tri-Partite Command Composition (Section 41A(2)):** To unify municipal engineering, revenue administration, and metropolitan law enforcement within a single command table, the **UDMA** comprises:
    *   **Chairperson:** The **Municipal Commissioner** of the Municipal Corporation (*ex-officio*).
    *   **Vice-Chairpersons:** Both the **District Collector** and the **Commissioner of Police** (or head of the urban police district) serve as *ex-officio* Vice-Chairpersons.
    *   **Members:** Such other officers of the Municipal Corporation and the State Government (representing urban development, public health engineering, fire services, electricity distribution, and town planning) as may be prescribed by the State Government.
*   **Core Statutory Functions of the UDMA:** The **UDMA** is empowered to prepare and implement the **Urban Disaster Management Plan** for the city (in alignment with the National and State Plans and approved by the **SDMA**), identify vulnerable slum clusters and structurally dilapidated buildings, ensure that all municipal infrastructure projects comply with disaster mitigation standards, coordinate pre-monsoon de-silting of stormwater drains, operationalize city-level **Emergency Operations Centres (EOCs)**, and direct urban line departments during floods, heatwaves, industrial leaks, or structural collapses.

By elevating the **Municipal Commissioner** to the statutory chairmanship of the **UDMA** alongside the **District Collector** and **Police Commissioner**, the 2024/2025 Amendment aligns the **74th Constitutional Amendment Act (1992)** municipal governance framework with modern urban disaster risk management.

#### Key Statutory Provisions of the 2024/2025 Institutional Overhaul
*   **Transfer of Plan-Preparation Duty from NEC/SEC to NDMA/SDMA (Sections 6, 11, 18, & 23):** Under the original 2005 Act, the **NEC** was responsible for preparing the **National Plan** (under Section 11) and submitting it to the **NDMA** for approval, while the **SEC** prepared the **State Plan** (under Section 23) for approval by the **SDMA**. The 2024/2025 Amendment abolishes this circuitous two-step loop: **NDMA** and **SDMAs** are now directly empowered to prepare the **National Disaster Management Plan** and **State Disaster Management Plans**, respectively, while the **NEC** and **SECs** focus exclusively on inter-departmental coordination, execution, and monitoring.
*   **Conferring Statutory Status on the National Crisis Management Committee (NCMC — Section 8A):** Historically constituted via Cabinet Secretariat executive orders, the **NCMC** is now elevated to a statutory body under **Section 8A**. Chaired *ex-officio* by the **Cabinet Secretary** (the highest civil servant of the Union) and comprising Secretaries of key ministries/departments depending on the nature of the crisis, the **NCMC** functions as the **nodal statutory body for dealing with major disasters that have serious or national ramifications**. The **Chairperson of the NCMC** is statutorily empowered to issue binding operational directions to the **NEC** and coordinate inter-state and civil-military emergency operations.
*   **Conferring Statutory Status on the High-Level Committee (HLC — Section 8B):** The **HLC** is accorded statutory backing under **Section 8B** to govern central disaster fiscal releases. Chaired by the **Minister in charge of the Ministry or Department of the Central Government having administrative control of disaster management (the Union Home Minister)**, its statutory members include the **Union Minister of Finance**, the **Union Minister of Agriculture**, and the **Vice-Chairman of NITI Aayog**. Based on empirical damage assessments submitted by **Inter-Ministerial Central Teams (IMCTs)** and recommendations of the Sub-Committee of the **NEC**, the **HLC** approves additional financial assistance to disaster-affected States from the **National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF)**.
*   **Mandatory National and State Disaster Databases (Amended Sections 6 & 18):** To eliminate data fragmentation across agencies, the Amendment mandates the creation and maintenance of a dynamic **National Disaster Database** by the **NDMA** and corresponding **State Disaster Databases** by **SDMAs**. These statutory digital repositories must continuously record:
    *   Types and severity of disasters alongside spatial hazard, exposure, and vulnerability risk assessments;
    *   Allocation of funds under response and mitigation windows and granular expenditure tracking;
    *   Preparedness and mitigation plans, beneficiary compensation registries, and infrastructure damage inventories.
*   **Expanded Regulatory, Audit, and Staffing Powers of the NDMA (Sections 3A, 6, & 75):** The **NDMA** is newly empowered to periodically take stock of the entire spectrum of disaster risks across the country, conduct **preparedness audits** and **post-disaster audits** of states and central ministries, prescribe **minimum standards of relief** (covering food, drinking water, medical cover, sanitation, and ex-gratia assistance for loss of life or livelihood), provide technical assistance to state authorities, and—under **Section 3A**—appoint officers, employees, and domain experts/consultants with prior Central Government approval and frame regulations governing its internal administration.
*   **Statutory Constitution of State Disaster Response Forces (SDRFs) and Section 60A Harmonization:** While several states had raised **SDRF** units through executive orders modeled on the central **NDRF**, the 2024/2025 Amendment inserts explicit statutory provisions empowering every **State Government to constitute a State Disaster Response Force (SDRF)** for specialist response, define its functions, and prescribe the terms and conditions of service for its personnel. Furthermore, **Section 60A** empowers both the Central and State Governments to issue general or special directions to any authority or person to prevent or mitigate disaster impacts, harmonizing central-state emergency command powers.

## III. Disaster Risk Financing Architecture (15th and 16th Finance Commissions) and Early Warning Systems

### 7. The 15th and 16th Finance Commission Fiscal Revolution: NDRMF, SDRMF, and the 80:20 Response-Mitigation Bifurcation

#### Operationalizing Sections 46–48 of the DM Act through Constitutional Fiscal Federalism
A statutory disaster plan without ring-fenced fiscal appropriations is an unenforceable paper mandate. Under India's constitutional architecture, vertical and horizontal transfers of disaster management resources between the Union and the States are governed by the quinquennial awards of the **Finance Commission** constituted under **Article 280 of the Constitution**, interfacing directly with **Chapter IX (Sections 46 to 50) of the Disaster Management Act, 2005**. Although **Section 46** mandated the **National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF)**, **Section 47** mandated the **National Disaster Mitigation Fund (NDMF)**, and **Section 48(1)** mandated State Governments to establish both the **State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF)** and the **State Disaster Mitigation Fund (SDMF)**, a major fiscal anomaly persisted for fifteen years (2005–2020): successive governments operationalized only the response funds (**NDRF** and **SDRF**), leaving the statutory mitigation funds (**NDMF** and **SDMF**) unconstituted on the grounds that sectoral ministries already executed developmental schemes.

This structural omission starved pre-disaster engineering projects of dedicated capital until the **15th Finance Commission (2021–26, chaired by N.K. Singh)**—and subsequently reinforced and scaled up by the **16th Finance Commission (2026–27 to 2030–31, chaired by Dr. Arvind Panagariya, report submitted November 17, 2025 and accepted by the Union Government on February 1, 2026)**—executed a foundational overhaul of India's disaster risk financing architecture.

#### Mechanics of the 80:20 NDRMF/SDRMF Bifurcation and the 16th Finance Commission Award (2026–31)
*   **Creation of Overarching Risk Management Funds (NDRMF & SDRMF) and the 80:20 Split:** The **15th Finance Commission** established two overarching umbrella funds—the **National Disaster Risk Management Fund (NDRMF)** at the Union level and the **State Disaster Risk Management Funds (SDRMF)** at the State level—and instituted a mandatory **80:20 statutory bifurcation** that the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** has retained and expanded:
    *   **80% Allocation to Response Funds (NDRF and SDRF):** Dedicated to managing the immediate and post-event stages of the disaster continuum, structured across three functional sub-windows:
        1.  **Response and Relief (40% of total SDRMF/NDRMF):** Search and rescue operations, immediate gratuitous relief, temporary shelter, drinking water, medical care, and ex-gratia payouts.
        2.  **Recovery and Reconstruction (30% of total SDRMF/NDRMF):** Medium-to-long-term restoration of damaged housing and public infrastructure adhering to **"Build Back Better"** resilience norms following a scientific **Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA)**.
        3.  **Preparedness and Capacity Building (10% of total SDRMF/NDRMF):** Acquisition of specialist SAR equipment for **SDRFs**, training of **Aapda Mitra** volunteers, establishment of Emergency Operations Centres, and public awareness campaigns.
    *   **20% Allocation to Dedicated Mitigation Funds (NDMF and SDMF):** Operationalizing **Section 47** and **Section 48(1)(c)** of the **DM Act, 2005** (accounted for in the **Public Account** under Reserve Fund bearing interest in **Major Head 8121 — General and Other Reserve Funds**), this 20% corpus is strictly ring-fenced for **ex-ante structural and non-structural mitigation projects**. Under the **Operational Guidelines for Administration of SDMF and NDMF (2026–31)** issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs under **Section 62 of the DM Act**, **NDMF** and **SDMF** finance targeted national and state-level mitigation missions including urban flood management, GLOF risk reduction, landslide mitigation, forest fire control, lightning safety, and coastal cyclone cabling.
*   **Quantitative Corpus and Federal Cost-Sharing under the 16th Finance Commission (2026–27 to 2030–31):** Accepting the **16th Finance Commission** report on **February 1, 2026**, the Union Government notified the five-year disaster risk financing corpus for **2026–31**:
    *   **State-Level Corpus (SDRMF):** A combined allocation of **₹2,04,401 crore** across 28 States (a substantial increase from the ₹1,28,124 crore allocated during 2021–26). Applying the **80:20 ratio**, **₹1,63,521 crore** is earmarked for the **State Disaster Response Funds (SDRF)** and **₹40,880 crore** for the **State Disaster Mitigation Funds (SDMF)**.
    *   **Federal Cost-Sharing Norms (75:25 and 90:10):** Reflecting cooperative fiscal federalism, the Union Government contributes **75%** and the State Governments contribute **25%** toward the **SDRMF** (both **SDRF** and **SDMF**) for **General Category (non-Northeastern and non-Hilly) States**, whereas for **North-Eastern and Himalayan States**, the Union contributes **90%** and the States contribute **10%**. Across the ₹2,04,401 crore 16th FC state corpus, the Central share is **₹1,55,915.85 crore** and the aggregate States' share is **₹48,485.15 crore**.
    *   **Central-Level Corpus (NDRMF):** An additional **₹79,406 crore** (100% Union-funded) is allocated to the central **NDRMF** corpus (**NDRF** + **NDMF**) for supplementing state funds during disasters of "severe nature" and executing pan-India multi-state mitigation projects.
    *   **Enhanced Sub-Window Flexibility:** Addressing state grievances that rigid sub-window caps under the 15th FC left funds unspent in one sub-window while another faced deficits, the **16th Finance Commission** granted States operational flexibility to reallocate resources within the **SDRF** across response, relief, recovery, and reconstruction sub-windows based on ground exigencies.
*   **Horizontal Allocation Methodology and the Disaster Risk Index (DRI) Debate:** Until the 14th Finance Commission, state-wise disaster allocations were determined purely by historical expenditure averages—a regressive formula that penalized fiscally weaker states and failed to account for changing climate risk. The **15th Finance Commission** introduced a composite **Disaster Risk Index (DRI)** (scoring states across hazard probability, population/asset exposure, and socio-economic poverty vulnerability) blended with historical expenditure, area, and population. Under the **16th Finance Commission**, while hazard sensitivity and composite risk parameters guide allocations, policy analysts emphasize that overweighting raw population or geographical area relative to hazard intensity can under-fund sparsely populated yet ecologically fragile Himalayan states (such as Sikkim, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand) facing disproportionate cryospheric and seismic capital destruction.

### 8. The 12 Centrally Notified Disasters, the 10% State Flexi-Fund, and Parametric Risk Transfer

#### The Statutory Gatekeeping of Eligible Hazards
Access to the **State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF)** and the **National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF)** is not open-ended; expenditure can be legally debited from the main corpus only when a calamity falls within the list of **12 Centrally Notified Disasters** approved by the Government of India on the recommendations of the Finance Commissions:

1.  **Cyclone**
2.  **Drought**
3.  **Earthquake**
4.  **Fire**
5.  **Flood**
6.  **Tsunami**
7.  **Hailstorm**
8.  **Landslide**
9.  **Avalanche**
10. **Cloudburst**
11. **Pest Attack**
12. **Frost and Cold Wave**

#### The 10% Flexi-Fund for State-Notified Local Disasters and Emerging Climate Contradictions
Because India's agro-climatic diversity generates lethal regional hazards that do not appear on the central list of 12—such as **Lightning strikes** (which cause over 2,500 deaths annually across Bihar, Odisha, Jharkhand, and Madhya Pradesh, exceeding mortality from cyclones and floods combined), **Extreme Heatwaves**, **Coastal and River Bank Erosion** (in Assam, West Bengal, and Kerala), **Snakebite envenomation**, **Boat capsizing**, and **Human-Wildlife Conflict**—the Finance Commission guidelines incorporate a crucial federal safety valve: the **10% State-Specific Local Disaster Flexi-Fund**.

*   **Operational Mechanics of the 10% Flexi-Fund:** A State Government may utilize **up to 10% of the annual funds allocation of its SDRF** to provide immediate relief to victims of natural disasters that it considers to be "disasters" within the local context and which are not included in the centrally notified list of 12 disasters. To trigger this window, the State Government must formally notify the specific local disasters, establish clear, transparent state-level norms and guidelines for expenditure with the approval of the **State Executive Committee (SEC)**, and adhere strictly to the ceiling rates of assistance prescribed under **SDRF/NDRF** norms.
*   **The Structural Debate over Expanding the Central List of 12:** Coastal and eastern states have repeatedly petitioned successive Finance Commissions to include **Lightning**, **Heatwaves**, and **River/Coastal Erosion** in the central list of 12 notified disasters so that 100% of the SDRF/NDRF corpus (rather than a 10% ceiling) becomes accessible. Both the 15th and 16th Finance Commissions declined to expand the central list of 12 response disasters, reasoning that:
    *   The **10% local disaster flexi-fund** (substantially enlarged in absolute rupee terms by the ₹2,04,401 crore 16th FC corpus) is adequate for immediate ex-gratia relief for lightning and heatwave fatalities; and
    *   Structural interventions such as **coastal/river bank erosion barriers**, **urban heat-island cooling**, and **lightning arresters** are fundamentally *mitigation and capital infrastructure works* that should be financed through the **20% State/National Disaster Mitigation Funds (SDMF/NDMF)** and sectoral climate missions rather than emergency relief funds.
*   **Beyond Sovereign Budgetary Relief — The 4-Layer Risk-Layering Strategy:** Recognizing that sovereign budgets alone cannot absorb tail-end climate catastrophes without derailing fiscal deficit targets, the Finance Commission and **NDMA** have instituted a four-tiered **Disaster Risk Layering Framework** transitioning from public bailouts to market-based risk transfer:
    *   **Layer 1 (High-Frequency, Low-Severity Events):** Absorbed by routine **SDRF** and **SDMF** budgetary allocations.
    *   **Layer 2 (Moderate-Severity Regional Events):** Financed via **NDRF** supplementary grants (approved by the statutory **HLC**) and contingent credit lines from Multilateral Development Banks.
    *   **Layer 3 (Low-Frequency, High-Severity Catastrophes):** Transferred to domestic and international insurance/reinsurance pools via **Parametric Disaster Insurance** (where payouts are triggered automatically within 48 hours when an objective physical threshold—such as wind speed $>180\text{ km/h}$, rainfall $>250\text{ mm/24h}$, or Peak Ground Acceleration $>0.3g$—is crossed, bypassing months of manual crop/property loss inspection, as pioneered by Nagaland's parametric insurance scheme).
    *   **Layer 4 (Extreme Sovereign Tail-Risk):** Securitized in global capital markets via **Catastrophe Bonds (Cat Bonds)** and regional sovereign risk pools.

### 9. Last-Mile Early Warning Architectures: Common Alerting Protocol (CAP) and the Sachet Cell-Broadcast Ecosystem

#### Overcoming the Telecommunication Bottleneck of Point-to-Point Alerting
Scientific hazard forecasting by technical agencies has minimal life-saving value if the warning fails to reach the exposed population within the lead-time window before kinetic impact. Historically, early warnings in India suffered from two fatal structural bottlenecks: first, scientific agencies issued bulletins in non-standardized technical formats (PDFs, faxes, emails) that required manual interpretation by district collectors; second, mass dissemination via conventional **Short Message Service (SMS)** relies on **unicast (point-to-point) routing**, which sequentially queues millions of individual messages through mobile switching centres—causing severe network congestion and delivery delays of several hours during an unfolding cyclone or tsunami.

To eliminate both bottlenecks, the **NDMA** partnered with the **Centre for Development of Telematics (C-DOT)** to deploy the pan-India **Integrated Alert System (Sachet)**, anchored on the **International Telecommunication Union (ITU-T Recommendation X.1303) Common Alerting Protocol (CAP)** and **Cell Broadcast Technology (CBT)**.

#### Engineering Mechanics of CAP, Sachet, and Cell Broadcast Technology
*   **The ITU-T X.1303 Common Alerting Protocol (CAP) Standard:** **CAP** is an open, non-proprietary **XML-based data format** for exchanging public warnings and emergencies across all hazard types and all communication networks. Every **CAP alert message** encapsulates four mandatory standardized metadata parameters alongside precise **GIS latitude-longitude polygon coordinates**:
    1.  **Event Type & Category:** Geophysical, Meteorological, Hydrological, Fire, CBRN, or Infrastructure failure.
    2.  **Urgency:** Immediate (responsive action should be taken immediately), Expected (within the next hour), Future, or Past.
    3.  **Severity:** Extreme (extraordinary threat to life or property), Severe, Moderate, or Minor.
    4.  **Certainty:** Observed (determined to have occurred or to be ongoing), Likely (probability $>50\%$), or Possible.
*   **Integration of Alert Generating Agencies (AGAs) with SEOCs:** Under the **Sachet** architecture, India's primary scientific forecasting institutions—the **India Meteorological Department (IMD)**, **Central Water Commission (CWC)**, **Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS)**, **Geological Survey of India (GSI)**, **Defence Geoinformatics Research Establishment (DGRE)**, and **Forest Survey of India (FSI)**—feed automated, geo-referenced hazard polygons directly into the central and state **Sachet** servers. **State Emergency Operations Centres (SEOCs)** verify and disseminate these alerts in **vernacular regional languages** tailored strictly to the citizens physically located inside the threatened geographical polygon (preventing panic in unaffected districts).
*   **Cell Broadcast Technology (CBT) vs. Unicast SMS:** While location-based SMS provided initial coverage under Sachet (sending over 6,000 crore geo-targeted alerts across India), **NDMA** and **C-DOT** upgraded the network in **2023–2026** with **Cell Broadcast Technology (CBT)** across all major telecom service providers (Jio, Airtel, Vi, BSNL). Unlike SMS, **CBT** is a **one-to-many (point-to-area) radio broadcast technology** operating on a dedicated broadcast control channel of mobile towers (**Base Transceiver Stations - BTS**):
    *   **Zero Congestion & Sub-Second Latency:** Because the alert is broadcast simultaneously to every mobile handset connected to the selected cell towers within the hazard polygon—regardless of whether 1,000 or 10 million phones are in the area, and without requiring the system to look up individual mobile numbers—it generates **zero network congestion** and delivers the warning within seconds, making it indispensable for ultra-short-lead-time hazards like **earthquakes, tsunamis, flash floods, and lightning**.
    *   **Attention-Forcing Acoustic & Visual Override:** Cell Broadcast alerts trigger a standardized high-decibel emergency siren and vibration pattern on the user's smartphone (even if the phone is set to "Silent" or "Do Not Disturb" mode) accompanied by a pop-up screen reading out the warning in the local language for visually impaired or non-literate citizens.
*   **Deep-Sea and Shadow-Zone Redundancy (NavIC and GEMINI):** Because terrestrial cellular towers reach only $10\text{–}12\text{ km}$ off the coast, **Sachet** integrates with **ISRO's NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation)** and **GAGAN** satellites as well as **INCOIS GEMINI (GAGAN Enabled Mariner's Instrument for Navigation and Information)** receivers, transmitting CAP cyclone and tsunami warnings up to **$300\text{ nautical miles}$** into the high seas while simultaneously triggering coastal **Early Warning Dissemination System (EWDS)** acoustic siren towers.

## IV. Multilateral Disaster Governance: Sendai Framework (2015–2030), CDRI/IRIS, Build Back Better, and the PM's 10-Point Agenda

### 10. The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030): Global Targets and Priority Architecture

#### The Multilateral Trajectory from Yokohama (1994) and Hyogo (2005) to Sendai (2015–2030)
International disaster governance evolved through three foundational United Nations conferences coordinated by the **United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR, formerly UNISDR)**. The **Yokohama Strategy and Plan of Action for a Safer World (1994)** first challenged fatalistic perceptions of natural disasters by emphasizing socio-economic vulnerability. Following the 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami, the **Hyogo Framework for Action (HFA, 2005–2015): Building the Resilience of Nations and Communities to Disasters** catalyzed the creation of national statutory platforms (directly inspiring India's **DM Act, 2005**). However, mid-term and final audits of the Hyogo decade revealed that while global mortality from hydro-meteorological hazards declined due to early warnings, absolute economic asset losses soared beyond $\$1.5\text{ trillion}$ because rapid urbanization and capital investments continued to accumulate in high-hazard zones without ex-ante risk governance.

Adopted at the **Third UN World Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction in Sendai, Miyagi, Japan (March 14–18, 2015)** and endorsed by the UN General Assembly, the **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030)** serves as the first major global pact of the post-2015 development agenda (operating in direct synergy with the **2015 Paris Climate Agreement** and the **2030 Sustainable Development Goals**). The **Sendai Framework** executes a decisive conceptual pivot: from **managing disasters** (responding to events) to **managing disaster risk** (preventing the creation of new risk, reducing existing risk, and strengthening economic, social, health, and environmental resilience) across **natural and man-made hazards as well as related environmental, technological, and biological hazards and risks**.

#### Deconstructing the Seven Global Targets (A–G) and Four Priorities for Action
*   **The Seven Quantitative Global Targets (Targets A to G):** Unlike the qualitative commitments of Yokohama and Hyogo, the **Sendai Framework** establishes **seven measurable global targets** evaluated via the **Sendai Framework Monitor**:
    *   **Four Reduction Targets (Targets A to D — to be achieved by 2030 compared to the 2005–2015 decade):**
        1.  **Target A (Mortality):** Substantially reduce global disaster mortality by 2030, aiming to lower the average per 100,000 global mortality rate in the decade 2020–2030 compared to the period 2005–2015.
        2.  **Target B (Affected Population):** Substantially reduce the number of affected people globally by 2030, aiming to lower the average global figure per 100,000 in the decade 2020–2030 compared to 2005–2015.
        3.  **Target C (Economic Losses):** Reduce direct disaster economic loss in relation to global Gross Domestic Product (GDP) by 2030.
        4.  **Target D (Critical Infrastructure & Basic Services):** Substantially reduce disaster damage to critical infrastructure and disruption of basic services, among them health and educational facilities, including through developing their resilience by 2030.
    *   **Three Increase/Enabler Targets (Targets E to G):**
        5.  **Target E (National & Local DRR Strategies):** Substantially increase the number of countries with national and local disaster risk reduction strategies by 2020 (fulfilled early by India through the **National Disaster Management Plan - NDMP 2016**, revised in **2019**, alongside state and district plans).
        6.  **Target F (International Cooperation):** Substantially enhance international cooperation to developing countries through adequate and sustainable support to complement their national actions for implementation of the Framework by 2030.
        7.  **Target G (Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems):** Substantially increase the availability of and access to multi-hazard early warning systems and disaster risk information and assessments to people by 2030 (directly operationalized globally via the UN **"Early Warnings for All" (EW4All)** initiative and domestically via **Sachet CAP**).
*   **The Four Priorities for Action:** Every chapter of India's **National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP)** is structurally mapped across the **Sendai Framework's Four Priorities**:
    *   **Priority 1 — Understanding Disaster Risk:** Disaster risk management must be based on an understanding of disaster risk in all its dimensions of vulnerability, capacity, exposure of persons and assets, hazard characteristics, and the environment (statutory basis for India's **National and State Disaster Databases** under the 2025 Amendment Act).
    *   **Priority 2 — Strengthening Disaster Risk Governance to Manage Disaster Risk:** Clear vision, plans, competence, guidance, and coordination within and across sectors at national, regional, and local levels (reflected in the **DM (Amendment) Act, 2025** creation of **UDMAs**, statutory **NCMC/HLC**, and direct plan-making powers for **NDMA/SDMAs**).
    *   **Priority 3 — Investing in Disaster Risk Reduction for Resilience:** Public and private investment in disaster risk prevention and reduction through structural and non-structural measures are essential to enhance the economic, social, health, and cultural resilience of persons, communities, countries, and their assets (operationalized via the **20% NDMF/SDMF** mitigation funds and **CDRI**).
    *   **Priority 4 — Enhancing Disaster Preparedness for Effective Response and to "Build Back Better" in Recovery, Rehabilitation, and Reconstruction:** The steady growth of disaster risk means there is a need to strengthen disaster preparedness for response, take action in anticipation of events, integrate disaster risk reduction in response preparedness, and ensure that capacities are in place for effective recovery and reconstruction at all levels, utilizing the post-disaster window as a critical opportunity to **"Build Back Better"**.

### 11. Engineering Resilient Infrastructure and Recovery: "Build Back Better" and CDRI / IRIS

#### The Engineering Mechanics of "Build Back Better" (BBB)
When a cyclone, flood, or earthquake destroys housing stock, bridges, power grids, or hospitals, reconstructing those assets to their exact *pre-disaster engineering specifications* merely recreates the identical structural vulnerability that failed during the hazard event—locking the state into a perpetual **sawtooth cycle of asset destruction and fiscal depletion**. Enshrined in **Priority 4 of the Sendai Framework** and operationalized in India through the **30% Recovery and Reconstruction sub-window of the SDRF/NDRF** alongside the **Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA)** methodology, the **"Build Back Better" (BBB)** doctrine utilizes the post-disaster capital infusion window to elevate the structural, spatial, and socio-economic resilience baseline of the affected region above its pre-disaster state.

#### Operational Pillars of "Build Back Better" and the CDRI / IRIS Global Architecture
*   **Three Structural Pillars of "Build Back Better" (BBB):**
    1.  **Build Back Stronger (Engineering & Structural Hardening):** Upgrading replacement assets to withstand higher return-period climate and geophysical stresses—such as reconstructing flood-damaged bridges with higher hydraulic freeboard and scour-resistant deep foundations, replacing overhead coastal distribution poles with underground ring-fenced electrical cables, and enforcing ductile detailing under **BIS IS 1893** and **IS 13920** in post-earthquake housing (as executed after the **2001 Bhuj** and **2015 Nepal/Sikkim** earthquakes).
    2.  **Build Back Faster (Pre-Arranged Fiscal & Institutional Protocols):** Utilizing pre-agreed **PDNA** damage estimation templates, digital direct benefit transfers (DBT), and pre-negotiated procurement contracts under the **30% Recovery & Reconstruction SDRF window** so that communities recover livelihoods rapidly without falling into predatory debt traps.
    3.  **Build Back More Inclusively & Spatially Safer (Non-Structural & Land-Use De-Risking):** Prohibiting in-situ reconstruction inside high-hazard landslide runout paths (such as the post-2024 **Wayanad Mundakkai-Chooralmala** rehabilitation townships) or active riverine floodways, granting joint house-title ownership to women, and restoring ecological buffers (mangroves and urban wetlands) alongside grey infrastructure.
*   **The Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI):** Recognizing that developing economies will invest tens of trillions of dollars in new infrastructure by 2050—and that **Sendai Target D** cannot be achieved unless new capital assets are climate- and disaster-proofed at the design stage—Prime Minister Narendra Modi launched the **Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI)** at the **UN Climate Action Summit in New York on September 23, 2019** (the second major international intergovernmental initiative headquartered in India after the **International Solar Alliance**).
    *   **Legal Status and Governance:** Headquartered in **New Delhi**, **CDRI** was accorded the legal status of an **"Independent and International Legal Entity"** by the Union Cabinet in **June 2022** under the **United Nations (Privileges and Immunities) Act, 1947**, enabling it to deploy international experts and manage global trust funds autonomously. Co-chaired by India (**Principal Secretary to the PM / NDMA leadership**) and a rotating international co-chair, **CDRI** brings together over **40 national governments**, **7 multilateral organizations** (including the World Bank, ADB, UNDP, and UNDRR), academic institutions, and private-sector infrastructure developers.
    *   **Technical Mandate and Global Flagships:** Rather than acting as a brick-and-mortar construction contractor, **CDRI** functions as a global knowledge, standard-setting, and technical-assistance platform across five critical infrastructure sectors: **Power, Telecommunications, Transport (aviation, ports, roads, rail), Water, and Health/Social Infrastructure**. It publishes the biennial flagship **Global Infrastructure Resilience Report (GIRR)** (quantifying global multi-hazard Probable Maximum Loss across infrastructure assets), operates the **Infrastructure Resilience Academic Exchange (IRAX)** to embed disaster-resilient engineering curricula into global universities, and channels multi-donor grant finance through the **Infrastructure Resilience Accelerator Fund (IRAF)** (a UN Multi-Partner Trust Fund established in 2022).
*   **Infrastructure for Resilient Island States (IRIS):** Launched jointly by **India, the United Kingdom, and Australia** at **UNFCCC COP26 in Glasgow (November 2021)** as the first dedicated flagship program operationalized under the **CDRI IRAF**, **IRIS** addresses the existential vulnerability of **Small Island Developing States (SIDS)** across the Indo-Pacific, Caribbean, and Indian Ocean. Because a single Category-5 tropical cyclone or storm surge can wipe out $50\%\text{ to }200\%$ of a small island nation's GDP in hours, **IRIS** provides non-debt-creating technical assistance, space-based early warning systems, and climate-resilient design retrofits for SIDS ports, airports, hospitals, and renewable micro-grids.

### 12. The Prime Minister's 10-Point Agenda on Disaster Risk Reduction and the Outgoing Structural Synthesis

#### The Strategic Doctrine of the Prime Minister's 10-Point Agenda (2016–2026)
Articulated by the Prime Minister of India during the **Asian Ministerial Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction (AMCDRR) in New Delhi in November 2016**, the **Prime Minister's 10-Point Agenda on Disaster Risk Reduction** serves as the overarching strategic doctrine unifying India's domestic statutory reforms, fiscal allocations, space-and-telecom technologies, and international diplomacy with the **Sendai Framework (2015–2030)**. Rather than treating disaster management as an isolated silo of the Home Ministry, the **10-Point Agenda** embeds risk reduction across every dimension of public policy:

1.  **All Development Sectors Must Imbibe the Principles of Disaster Risk Management:** Every capital investment—whether a highway tunnel in the Himalayas, a coastal port, a smart-city drainage network, or a rural housing scheme (**PM Awas Yojana**)—must incorporate multi-hazard risk screening and structural resilience at the project conceptualization and appraisal stage (**Sections 36 and 39 of the DM Act, 2005**).
2.  **Work Towards Risk Coverage for All—From Poor Households to SMEs to Multi-National Corporations to Nation States:** Expanding financial risk transfer across the socio-economic pyramid through universal financial inclusion (**PM Jan Dhan Yojana** for direct ex-gratia transfers), crop insurance (**PM Fasal Bima Yojana**), micro-enterprise accident/property cover (**PM Suraksha Bima Yojana**), and sovereign parametric disaster insurance.
3.  **Encourage Greater Involvement and Leadership of Women in Disaster Risk Management:** Transforming women from passive victims of disasters into active leaders and first-responders by integrating **Self-Help Groups (SHGs)** into community early warning, shelter management, and **Aapda Mitra / Yuva Aapda Mitra** cohorts, alongside training women masons (*Rani Mistris*) in earthquake-resistant construction.
4.  **Invest in Risk Mapping Globally to Improve Understanding of Nature and Disaster Risks:** Standardizing probabilistic multi-hazard zoning across borders—operationalized domestically through the **National and State Disaster Databases** (mandated by the **DM Amendment Act, 2025**), the **Vulnerability Atlas of India (BMTPC)**, and **CDRI's Global Infrastructure Resilience Report**.
5.  **Leverage Technology to Enhance the Efficiency of Our Disaster Risk Management Efforts:** Deploying space-based synthetic aperture radar (**ISRO RISAT/EOS**), **X-band Doppler Weather Radars**, **CWC FloodWatch**, **GSI Bhusanket**, and **C-DOT's Sachet Cell-Broadcast CAP** network for real-time hazard forecasting and zero-latency public alerting.
6.  **Develop a Network of Universities to Work on Disaster Issues:** Mobilizing coastal universities for cyclone/tsunami modeling, Himalayan universities for glaciology, GLOF, and seismic engineering, and dryland agricultural universities for drought resilience under the **NIDM India Universities and Institutions Network for Disaster Risk Reduction (IUINDRR)** and **CDRI's IRAX**.
7.  **Utilize the Opportunities Provided by Social Media and Mobile Technologies for Disaster Risk Reduction:** Transforming ubiquitous smartphone penetration into a two-way crisis communication grid through **Sachet CAP mobile broadcasts**, the **Damini Lightning App**, **Mausam App**, and crowd-sourced flood/landslide damage reporting.
8.  **Build on Local Capacities and Initiatives to Enhance Disaster Risk Reduction:** Institutionalizing community-first survival capacity through the **Aapda Mitra** volunteer scheme across 350+ hazard-prone districts, **UNESCO-IOC Tsunami Ready** coastal villages (in Odisha and Gujarat), and preservation of vernacular seismic architectures (*Dhajji-Dewari*, *Kath-Kuni*, *Koti Banal*, *Ikra*).
9.  **Ensure That the Opportunity to Learn from a Disaster is Not Wasted:** Institutionalizing mandatory **post-disaster scientific audits** (statutorily empowered under the **DM Amendment Act, 2025**), **NIDM** case-study documentation, and **Post-Disaster Needs Assessments (PDNA)** after every major event (such as the **2023 South Lhonak GLOF**, the **2023 Silkyara Tunnel Collapse**, and the **2024 Wayanad Landslide**) to continuously upgrade engineering codes and SOPs.
10. **Bring About Greater Cohesion in International Response to Disasters:** Elevating global diplomatic and operational synergy through the **Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI)**, **IRIS**, the **G20 Disaster Risk Reduction Working Group** (established under India's 2023 G20 Presidency), **BIMSTEC / SCO joint NDRF exercises**, and rapid humanitarian assistance and disaster relief (**HADR**) missions such as **Operation Dost** and **Operation Karuna**.
