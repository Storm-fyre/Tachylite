# Part 11: Constitutional Amendment Mechanics (Article 368), the Basic Structure Doctrine, Judicial Review, and Public Interest Litigation

## I. The Architecture of Constitutional Amendment (Part XX, Article 368)

### 1. Constituent Power vs. Constituted Legislative Authority: The Three-Tier Amendment Matrix

#### Flexible vs. Rigid Constitutionalism and the Constituent Assembly Consensus

Every written constitution confronts a foundational paradox of sovereign temporality: how to bind future majorities to an enduring social contract without petrifying the state against historical evolution. In comparative public law, this tension is mediated through the distinction between **Primary Constituent Power** (*pouvoir constituant originaire*)—the plenary, extra-legal sovereignty exercised by the **Constituent Assembly** in framing the original text—and **Derivative Constituent Power** (*pouvoir constituant institué*)—the circumscribed competence vested in a constituted organ to revise that text according to prescribed rules. Speaking before the Constituent Assembly on **November 4, 1948**, and **September 17, 1949** (**CAD Vol. VII & IX**), **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** rejected both the extreme rigidity of the **United States Constitution (Article V)**—which requires a two-thirds congressional supermajority plus ratification by three-fourths of the states—and the unbridled parliamentary plasticity of the unwritten **British Constitution**, where any fundamental constitutional statute may be repealed by an ordinary legislative majority.

Instead, **Part XX (Article 368)** of the Constitution of India institutionalizes a calibrated, asymmetrical **Three-Tier Amendment Matrix**. By graduating the procedural threshold in direct proportion to the structural sensitivity of the constitutional norm being altered, the framers sought to reconcile parliamentary responsiveness with federal and fundamental rights entrenchment:

*   **Tier 1 (Simple Parliamentary Majority Outside Article 368):** Facilitates administrative, territorial, and institutional adaptability by allowing Parliament to modify designated constitutional provisions through the ordinary legislative process, explicitly exempting such alterations from the technical definition of an amendment under **Article 368**.
*   **Tier 2 (Special Majority of Each House Under Article 368(2)):** Protects the core normative architecture of the Constitution—most notably **Part III (Fundamental Rights)** and **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)**—against transient partisan majorities by mandating a dual supermajority within both chambers of the Union Legislature.
*   **Tier 3 (Special Majority Plus Ratification by Half of the State Legislatures Under the Proviso to Article 368(2)):** Safeguards the federal compact and the institutional balance between the Union and the States by interposing a sub-national veto gate, prohibiting unilateral parliamentary alteration of entrenched federal articles.

#### Tier 1: Amendment by Simple Parliamentary Majority Outside the Ambit of Article 368

A substantial corpus of constitutional provisions contains internal self-executing clauses—typically phrased as *"until Parliament by law otherwise provides"*—which authorize Parliament to alter the textual operation of the Constitution through an ordinary statute enacted by a simple majority of members present and voting (**Article 100(1)**). Such laws expressly declare that they *"shall not be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of Article 368"*. Consequently, they bypass the special majority requirements of **Part XX**, remain amenable to a **Joint Sitting** under **Article 108** in the event of a bicameral deadlock, and are subject to the full spectrum of Presidential vetoes under **Article 111** as well as ordinary **Article 13(2)** judicial review.

*   **Internal Territorial Re-Engineering (Articles 2, 3, and 4):** Under **Article 2** (admission or establishment of new States) and **Article 3** (formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries, or names of existing States), Parliament exercises plenary power to redraw the political map of the Union. **Article 4(1)** empowers Parliament to make supplemental and consequential amendments to the **First Schedule** (names and territories of States/UTs) and the **Fourth Schedule** (allocation of seats in the Rajya Sabha), while **Article 4(2)** explicitly stipulates that no such law shall be deemed an amendment under **Article 368** (upheld in **Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay, 1960**; though ceding sovereign Indian territory to a foreign nation lies outside **Articles 2–4** and mandates an **Article 368** amendment, as settled in **In Re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves, 1960** and executed via the **100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015**).
*   **Abolition or Creation of State Legislative Councils (Article 169):** Where the Legislative Assembly of a State passes a resolution by a special majority (majority of total membership plus two-thirds present and voting), Parliament may by law provide for the abolition or creation of the **Vidhan Parishad** (Legislative Council) by a simple majority. **Article 169(3)** insulates such a law from **Article 368**.
*   **Autonomous Governance and Scheduled Areas (Fifth and Sixth Schedules):** Under **Paragraph 7 of the Fifth Schedule** and **Paragraph 21 of the Sixth Schedule**, Parliament may by ordinary law amend, vary, or repeal any provision of the respective Schedules governing Scheduled and Tribal Areas without invoking **Article 368**.
*   **Systemic Institutional and Procedural Norms:** Simple-majority modifications also govern **Article 11** (regulation of citizenship), **Article 73(2)** (continuance of pre-constitutional state executive powers), **Article 100(3)** (parliamentary quorum), **Article 105(3)** (codification of parliamentary privileges), **Article 106** (salaries and allowances of MPs), **Article 120(2)** (use of English in Parliament after 15 years), **Article 124(1)** (increasing the number of Supreme Court Judges), **Article 135** (conferment of Federal Court jurisdiction on the Supreme Court), **Article 137** (rules for Supreme Court review), **Article 239A** (creation of local legislatures for Union Territories), **Article 343(3)** (use of English or Devanagari numerals for official purposes), **Article 348(1)** (language of the Supreme Court and High Courts), and the **Second Schedule** (emoluments, allowances, and privileges of constitutional dignitaries).

#### Tier 2: Special Majority of Each House Under Article 368(2)

For all constitutional provisions neither exempted under Tier 1 nor entrenched under Tier 3, **Article 368(2)** prescribes a dual-pronged **Special Majority** in each House of Parliament:
1.  **The Absolute Majority Prong:** A majority of the **total membership** of that House (irrespective of vacancies or absentees—i.e., at least 273 votes in a 543-member Lok Sabha and 123 votes in a 245-member Rajya Sabha).
2.  **The Functional Supermajority Prong:** A majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**.

The expression *"present and voting"* excludes members who are present in the chamber but abstain from voting. Under **Rules 155 to 158 of the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha**, although a rigorous judicial interpretation in **Shankari Prasad (1951)** noted that the constitutional mandate applies at the final passage of the Bill, parliamentary rules strictly enforce the dual special majority at **all effective stages** of the Bill—namely, the motion that the Bill be taken into consideration, the motion that the Bill as reported by the Select/Joint Committee be taken into consideration, the clause-by-clause consideration vote, and the final motion that the Bill be passed.

#### Tier 3: Special Majority Plus Federal Ratification Under the Proviso to Article 368(2)

When a Constitutional Amendment Bill seeks to make any change in the federal architecture of the Republic, the **Proviso to Article 368(2)** mandates that, in addition to being passed by the Tier 2 special majority in both Houses of Parliament, the amendment *"shall also require to be ratified by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States by resolutions to that effect passed by those Legislatures before the Bill making provision for such amendment is presented to the President for assent."* State Legislatures ratify such amendments via a **simple majority** of members present and voting; the Constitution prescribes no time limit within which States must ratify or reject the resolution, and once a State Legislature communicates its ratification, constitutional convention precludes its subsequent withdrawal.

The entrenched provisions enumerated under **Clauses (a) to (e) of the Proviso to Article 368(2)** are exhaustive:
*   **Clause (a) — Presidential Election & Executive Extent:** **Article 54** (Election of President), **Article 55** (Manner of election of President), **Article 73** (Extent of executive power of the Union), **Article 162** (Extent of executive power of a State), **Article 241** (High Courts for Union Territories), and **Article 279A** (Goods and Services Tax Council, inserted by the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016**).
*   **Clause (b) — Constitutional Judiciary:** **Chapter IV of Part V** (**Articles 124–147**: The Union Judiciary / Supreme Court), **Chapter V of Part VI** (**Articles 214–231**: The High Courts in the States), and **Chapter I of Part XI** (**Articles 245–255**: Legislative Relations between the Union and the States).
*   **Clause (c) — Legislative Lists:** Any of the three Lists (Union, State, or Concurrent) in the **Seventh Schedule**.
*   **Clause (d) — Federal Parliamentary Representation:** The representation of States in Parliament (including the **Fourth Schedule** and **Article 80 / Article 81** allocation formulas when altered via constitutional amendment rather than an **Article 4** reorganization).
*   **Clause (e) — The Amending Clause Itself:** Any of the provisions of **Article 368** itself (preventing Parliament from using a Tier 2 majority to dismantle the Tier 3 federal lock).

The Supreme Court has strictly policed any parliamentary attempt to circumvent the **Proviso to Article 368(2)**. In **Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhur (1992)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench invalidated **Paragraph 7 of the Tenth Schedule** (inserted by the **52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985**), because Paragraph 7 explicitly ousted the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (**Article 136**) and High Courts (**Articles 226 and 227**) over anti-defection disqualification orders without the Bill having been ratified by half of the State Legislatures under **Clause (b) of the Proviso to Article 368(2)**. Applying the **Doctrine of Severability**, the majority severed Paragraph 7 while upholding the remainder of the **Tenth Schedule**.

This federal-entrenchment jurisprudence culminated in the landmark 3-Judge Bench ruling in **Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)**, which adjudicated the validity of the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011**. The 97th Amendment had inserted **Part IXB (Articles 243ZH to 243ZT)** to dictate uniform regulations for **Co-operative Societies** across India (fixing board sizes, tenures, audit mechanisms, and supersession rules) without securing ratification by half of the State Legislatures. The Supreme Court held that:
*   Under **Entry 32 of List II (State List)** of the **Seventh Schedule**, the incorporation, regulation, and winding up of co-operative societies (other than Multi-State Co-operative Societies falling under **Entry 44 of List I**) is an exclusive State legislative subject.
*   Even though the **97th Amendment** did not textually alter the words of **Entry 32 of List II**, by enacting **Part IXB**, it substantially curtailed and fettered the plenary legislative power of State Legislatures under **Article 246(3)** and **Entry 32 of List II**.
*   Applying the **Doctrine of Pith and Substance** and the **Direct Impact Test** to the **Proviso to Article 368(2)**, any constitutional amendment that curtails the exclusive legislative competence of the States requires mandatory ratification by half of the State Legislatures, regardless of whether the text of the **Seventh Schedule** is directly modified.
*   Invoking the **Doctrine of Severability**, the Bench struck down **Part IXB** in its entirety insofar as it applied to State-level Co-operative Societies, while preserving its validity exclusively for **Multi-State Co-operative Societies** (**Article 243ZR**) and Union Territories (**Article 243ZS**).

### 2. Procedural Mechanics and Institutional Locks Under Article 368

#### Parliamentary Monopoly over Initiation and the Exclusion of State Legislatures

Under **Article 368(2)**, an amendment of the Constitution may be initiated **only** by the introduction of a Bill for the purpose in **either House of Parliament** (the **Lok Sabha** or the **Rajya Sabha**). Unlike the **United States Constitution (Article V)**, which empowers two-thirds of the state legislatures to petition Congress to call a national constitutional convention, State Legislatures in India possess zero power to initiate a Constitutional Amendment Bill (even their resolution under **Article 169** for a Legislative Council is merely an enabling request that does not legally bind Parliament to enact the law). Furthermore, inside Parliament, an **Article 368** Bill may be introduced either by a **Minister** (Government Bill) or by a **Private Member** (Private Member's Bill—governed by examination by the **Committee on Private Members' Bills and Resolutions** in the Lok Sabha), and unlike a **Money Bill (Article 110)** or an **Article 3** State Reorganization Bill, it requires **no prior recommendation of the President** for its introduction.

#### Bicameral Co-Equality and the Prohibition of Article 108 Joint Sittings

Within the **Article 368** framework, the **Rajya Sabha** enjoys absolute institutional parity with the **Lok Sabha**. Every Constitutional Amendment Bill must be passed in **each House separately** by the prescribed special majority. If one House passes the Bill and the other House rejects it, or proposes amendments to which the originating House refuses to agree, or allows more than six months to elapse without passing it, the Bill reaches a terminal constitutional impasse and **lapses**. 

There is **no provision for a Joint Sitting of both Houses under Article 108** to resolve a deadlock over a Constitutional Amendment Bill. The rationale is twofold: first, the text of **Article 368(2)** explicitly mandates passage *"in each House"* by a special majority, whereas **Article 108(4)** operates via a simple majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting in a joint session; second, allowing a joint sitting would enable the numerically superior **Lok Sabha** (543 members) to overwhelm the **Rajya Sabha** (245 members, the federal chamber representing the States), thereby subverting the federal safeguard embedded in bicameral constituent deliberation. Historically, this bicameral check was visibly demonstrated when the **64th and 65th Constitutional Amendment Bills (1989)** (on Panchayati Raj and Nagarpalikas) and the **24th Constitutional Amendment Bill (1970)** (abolition of Privy Purses) were passed by the Lok Sabha with the requisite two-thirds majority but defeated on the floor of the Rajya Sabha by a fraction of a vote.

#### Mandatory Presidential Assent After the 24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

Prior to 1971, **Article 368** stated that a Bill passed by both Houses *"shall be presented to the President for his assent and upon such assent being given to the Bill, the Constitution shall stand amended"*. This phrasing left open the theoretical ambiguity of whether the President could exercise a **Suspensive Veto**, **Absolute Veto**, or **Pocket Veto** under **Article 111** over a Constitutional Amendment Bill. To extinguish any potential executive obstruction of Parliament's constituent will (and as part of the broader institutional restructuring following **Golaknath, 1967**), Parliament enacted the **24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**, which substituted the words: *"it shall be presented to the President who **shall give his assent** to the Bill and thereupon the Constitution shall stand amended"*. Consequently, the President is constitutionally bound to grant assent to a duly passed Constitutional Amendment Bill; the President can neither withhold assent nor return the Bill for reconsideration of the Houses.

## II. The Genealogy of the Basic Structure Doctrine: From Formal Positivism to Structural Teleology (1951–1973)

### 3. The Early Positivist Era and the "Law" vs. "Constituent Act" Dichotomy

#### Sankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India (1951) and the Immunity of Article 368 from Article 13(2)

Within months of the commencement of the Republic on **January 26, 1950**, the constitutional vision of agrarian social revolution collided head-on with property rights in **Part III**. When the Patna High Court (**Kameshwar Singh v. State of Bihar, 1951**) struck down the Bihar Land Reforms Act, 1950 for violating **Article 14**, the **Provisional Parliament** (consisting entirely of the unicameral Constituent Assembly sitting as the legislature prior to the first general elections of 1951–52) enacted the **1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951**. This amendment inserted **Article 31A** (immunizing agrarian estate acquisition laws from **Articles 14, 19, and 31**) and **Article 31B** along with the **Ninth Schedule** (creating a retrospective constitutional vault that immunized specified statutes from judicial review on the ground of violating any Fundamental Right).

In **Sankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India (1951)**, Zamindars challenged the **1st Amendment** before a unanimous 5-Judge Constitution Bench led by **Justice Patanjali Sastri**. The petitioners contended that under **Article 13(2)**, the *"State"* (which includes Parliament under **Article 12**) *"shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part [Part III]"*, and that the word *"law"* in **Article 13(2)** must include a Constitutional Amendment Act passed under **Article 368**. Applying strict **Austin-inspired legal positivism** and the principle of **Harmonious Construction**, the Supreme Court rejected the challenge and upheld the **1st Amendment**:
*   **Distinction Between Ordinary Legislative Power and Constituent Power:** Although the word *"law"* in **Article 13(2)** is wide enough in isolation to include a constitutional amendment, in the context of a written constitution a clear demarcation exists between an **ordinary law** made in exercise of legislative power (**Articles 245–246**) and a **constitutional law** made in exercise of constituent power (**Article 368**).
*   **Avoidance of Textual Redundancy:** **Article 368** is framed in general, unqualified terms without any exception in favor of **Part III**. Reading **Article 13(2)** as an absolute bar against amending Fundamental Rights would render **Article 368** otiose with respect to Part III. Harmonizing the two provisions required reading **Article 13(2)** as governing only ordinary legislative enactments, leaving **Article 368** with plenary, unfettered power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.

#### Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965) and the Judicial Seeds of "Basic Features"

For fourteen years, the *Sankari Prasad* consensus held firm. However, after Parliament enacted the **17th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1964**—which expanded the definition of *"estate"* in **Article 31A** to include *ryotwari* lands and added 44 additional state statutes into the **Ninth Schedule**—its constitutional validity was challenged before a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)**. By a **3:2 split** in reasoning (though unanimous on the result of upholding the **17th Amendment**), the façade of unlimited constituent power fractured:
*   **The Majority Ratio (Gajendragadkar C.J., Wanchoo and Raghubar Dayal JJ.):** Reaffirmed *Sankari Prasad* in full, holding that the pith and substance of the **17th Amendment** was to assist State Legislatures in executing agrarian reform rather than directly altering High Court jurisdiction under **Article 226** (thereby bypassing the **Proviso to Article 368**), and that *"law"* in **Article 13(2)** excludes constituent amendments under **Article 368**.
*   **Justice M. Hidayatullah’s Concurring Reservation:** Expressed profound misgivings that Part III Fundamental Rights could be serves up as "the playthings of a special majority." He observed that the framers would not have omitted Part III from the entrenched **Proviso to Article 368** if they intended that foundational civil liberties could be swept away more easily than executive or tax provisions.
*   **Justice J.R. Mudholkar’s Structuralist Seed:** Explicitly questioned whether making a change in a **"basic feature of the Constitution"** could be regarded merely as an *"amendment"* or whether it would amount to **rewriting a part of the Constitution**—an exercise lying wholly outside the ambit of **Article 368**. Pointing to the **Preamble** as the enduring epitome of these basic features, **Justice Mudholkar** imported into Indian jurisprudence the doctrinal terminology deployed two years earlier by Chief Justice Cornelius of the Supreme Court of Pakistan in **Fazlul Quader Chowdhry v. Mohd. Abdul Haque (1963)**.

#### I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967): The 11-Judge Reversal and Prospective Overruling

Emboldened by the concurring doubts in *Sajjan Singh*, petitioners in **I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)** challenged the **1st, 4th, and 17th Constitutional Amendment Acts** (alongside the Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act, 1953, placed in the **Ninth Schedule**). An 11-Judge Constitution Bench delivered a historic, sharply polarized **6:5 verdict** (**Chief Justice K. Subba Rao** writing for five judges and **Justice Hidayatullah** concurring separately; **Justices Wanchoo, Bhargava, Mitter, Bachawat, and Ramaswami** dissenting), which overruled both *Sankari Prasad (1951)* and *Sajjan Singh (1965)*:
*   **Location of the Substantive Power to Amend:** The majority focused on the original marginal note of **Article 368**, which read *"Procedure for amendment of the Constitution"*. **Subba Rao C.J.** held that **Article 368** did **not** contain the substantive *power* to amend the Constitution; it merely prescribed the procedural steps. Instead, the substantive power to amend the Constitution resided in Parliament's **residuary legislative competence** under **Article 245, Article 246, and Article 248 read with Entry 97 of List I (Union List)** of the **Seventh Schedule**.
*   **Equating Constitutional Amendments with Ordinary "Law" under Article 13(2):** Because an amendment was thus an exercise of legislative power under **Article 245** (which explicitly begins with the words *"Subject to the provisions of this Constitution"*), a Constitutional Amendment Act was a **"law"** within the meaning of **Article 13(2)**.
*   **Transcendental Immunity of Fundamental Rights:** Consequently, **Part III Fundamental Rights** were accorded a "transcendental and primordial" status; Parliament possessed **zero competence** to abridge or take away any Fundamental Right, even by unanimous vote under **Article 368**. (While **M.K. Nambyar**, counsel for the petitioners, argued the German doctrine of implied structural limitations, **Subba Rao C.J.** declined to base the judgment on implied Basic Structure limits, opting instead for a total textual lock on Part III via **Article 13(2)**).
*   **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling:** Recognizing that retroactively invalidating the **1st, 4th, and 17th Amendments** would trigger socio-economic chaos by unravelling 17 years of zamindari abolition and land redistribution across India, the 6-Judge majority imported the American doctrine of **Prospective Overruling** (**Linkletter v. Walker, 1965**). The Court declared that the **1st, 4th, and 17th Amendments** would continue to remain valid for the past and future, but from the date of the *Golaknath* judgment (**February 27, 1967**) onwards, Parliament would have no power to amend any provision of Part III so as to take away or abridge Fundamental Rights. The Court placed three strict conditions on Prospective Overruling: (1) it can be invoked only in constitutional matters; (2) it can be applied only by the **Supreme Court** (not High Courts); and (3) the scope of prospectivity is left to the discretion of the Court in each case.

### 4. Parliament’s Counter-Offensive and the Watershed of Kesavananda Bharati (1973)

#### The 24th and 25th Constitutional Amendment Acts (1971): Textual Re-Engineering of Articles 13, 31C, and 368

The *Golaknath* ruling precipitated an acute constitutional crisis between the Indira Gandhi executive/legislature and the Supreme Court, intensified when the Court struck down the **Bank Nationalization Ordinance/Act** in **R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970)** and invalidated the executive derecognition of **Privy Purses** in **Madhav Rao Scindia v. Union of India (1970)**. Campaigning on *Garibi Hatao* and securing a commanding two-thirds majority in the 1971 general elections, Parliament enacted a suite of constitutional amendments designed to dismantle every doctrinal pillar of *Golaknath*, *R.C. Cooper*, and *Madhav Rao Scindia*:

*   **The 24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971 (Neutralizing Golaknath):**
    1.  **Amended the Marginal Heading of Article 368:** Replaced *"Procedure for amendment of the Constitution"* with *"Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor"*.
    2.  **Inserted Article 368(1):** Explicitly vested constituent power in Parliament: *"Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution, Parliament may in exercise of its constituent power amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision of this Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down in this article."*
    3.  **Amended Article 368(2):** Made Presidential assent compulsory (*"it shall be presented to the President who shall give his assent to the Bill"*).
    4.  **Inserted Article 13(4) and Article 368(3):** Created a mutually reinforcing cross-exemption: **Article 13(4)** declared *"Nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment of this Constitution made under article 368"*, while **Article 368(3)** declared *"Nothing in article 13 shall apply to any amendment made under this article."*
*   **The 25th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971 (Neutralizing R.C. Cooper & Elevating Socialism):**
    1.  **Amended Article 31(2):** Replaced the word *"compensation"* (which the Court in **Bella Banerjee, 1954** and **R.C. Cooper, 1970** had interpreted as requiring a "just equivalent" market value) with the neutral word **"amount"**, and barred courts from questioning the adequacy of the amount.
    2.  **Inserted Article 31C:** Provided in its **First Part** that no law giving effect to the policy of the State towards securing the Socialist Directive Principles specified in **Article 39(b)** (distribution of ownership and control of material resources of the community to subserve the common good) or **Article 39(c)** (preventing concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment) shall be deemed void on the ground that it violates **Articles 14, 19, or 31**. Crucially, the **Second Part of Article 31C** appended a radical **Ouster Clause**: *"and no law containing a declaration that it is for giving effect to such policy shall be called in question in any court on the ground that it does not give effect to such policy."*
*   **The 29th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972:** Placed two Kerala land reform statutes (the Kerala Land Reforms (Amendment) Acts of 1969 and 1971) into the **Ninth Schedule**, directly triggering the litigation filed by **His Holiness Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru**, head of the Edneer Mutt in Kasaragod, Kerala.

#### Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (April 24, 1973): The 13-Judge 7:6 Structural Synthesis

To adjudicate the validity of the **24th, 25th, and 29th Amendments** and reconsider *Golaknath*, **Chief Justice S.M. Sikri** constituted the largest bench in Indian judicial history—a **13-Judge Constitution Bench** that heard arguments across 68 working days. Leading for the petitioners, **Nani Palkhivala** advanced a structuralist thesis deeply informed by the German legal scholar **Professor Dietrich Conrad** (whose 1965 Banaras Hindu University lecture on *"Implied Limitations of the Amending Power"* drew directly upon the Weimar Republic's collapse under Article 48/76 and the post-war **Eternity Clause [Ewigkeitsklausel] in Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law of 1949**). Conrad and Palkhivala argued that every constituted authority created by a constitution—including the amending body exercising derivative constituent power under **Article 368**—is bound by the inherent philosophical boundaries of the instrument that created it; a power to *amend* a constitution cannot be used to commit constitutional suicide by *destroying* its identity.

On **April 24, 1973**, the 13 Judges delivered 11 separate judgments spanning over 700 pages, producing a razor-thin **7:6 structural synthesis** pivoted around the decisive opinion of **Justice H.R. Khanna**:
*   **The Six "Pro-Limitation" Judges (Sikri C.J., Shelat, Grover, Hegde, Mukherjea, and Jaganmohan Reddy JJ.):** Held that **Article 368** is subject to implied structural limitations, and that the core essence of Fundamental Rights themselves forms an un-destroyable part of the Basic Structure.
*   **The Six "Pro-Plenary Power" Judges (Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi, and Chandrachud JJ.):** Held that under the amended **Article 368**, Parliament’s constituent power is plenary, sovereign, and subject to **no** implied substantive limitations whatsoever—empowering Parliament to amend or repeal any provision of the Constitution without exception.
*   **Justice H.R. Khanna’s Decisive Swing Ratio (Forming the 7:6 Majority):** **Justice Khanna** rejected *Golaknath* and broke with the Sikri bloc on one crucial point: he held that Fundamental Rights (specifically the Right to Property) are **not automatically immune** as a bloc, and that Parliament can abridge Fundamental Rights under **Article 368**. However, he aligned decisively with the Sikri bloc on the core philosophical principle, holding that the word **"amend"** in **Article 368** means to modify or improve within the broad contours of the existing document—it **cannot mean the destruction, abrogation, or emasculation of the "Basic Structure or Framework" of the Constitution**. What must survive every **Article 368** amendment is the **foundational identity** of the Constitution: the old Constitution must remain recognizable after the amendment without being replaced by a *new* Constitution.

The operative ratios of **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)** reshaped Indian public law across four axes:
1.  **Overruling of Golaknath and Validation of the 24th Amendment:** Even under the unamended **Article 368**, the power to amend resided within **Article 368** itself and not in residuary legislative power (**Article 248 / Entry 97 List I**). The **24th Amendment** merely made explicit what was previously implicit, and is wholly valid. The word *"law"* in **Article 13(2)** applies strictly to ordinary legislative acts and does not include constituent amendments under **Article 368**.
2.  **Entrenchment of the Basic Structure Doctrine:** While Parliament can amend any provision of the Constitution—including **Part III Fundamental Rights**—the power to *"amend"* under **Article 368** does not encompass the power to **abrogate, emasculate, or destroy the Basic Structure or essential features** of the Constitution.
3.  **Severance of Article 31C (25th Amendment):** The Court upheld the **First Part of Article 31C** (protecting laws that genuinely implement **Article 39(b) and (c)** from **Articles 14, 19, and 31**), but **struck down (7:6) the Second Part of Article 31C**, which made a legislature’s declaration conclusive and ousted judicial review. **Justice Khanna** held that **Judicial Review** is an integral element of the Basic Structure; Parliament cannot vest ordinary legislatures with an unreviewable blank check to amend the Constitution de facto by attaching a false declaration to an ordinary statute.
4.  **Illustrative (Non-Exhaustive) Enumeration of Basic Features:** Rather than locking the doctrine into a rigid catalog, the majority judgments offered illustrative structural pillars:
    *   ** Sikri C.J.:** (i) Supremacy of the Constitution; (ii) Republican and Democratic form of government; (iii) Secular character of the Constitution; (iv) Separation of powers between the legislature, executive, and judiciary; and (v) Federal character of the Constitution.
    *   **Shelat and Grover JJ.:** Added (vi) the mandate to build a welfare state contained in the **Directive Principles of State Policy**, and (vii) the **Unity and Integrity of the Nation**.
    *   **Hegde and Mukherjea JJ.:** Added (viii) Sovereignty of India, (ix) Democratic character of the polity, and (x) Essential features of individual freedoms secured to citizens.

## III. Consolidation and Operationalization of the Basic Structure Filter (1975–2026)

### 5. Emergency Autocracy, Constituent Adjudication, and the Harmonious Balance Doctrine

#### Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and the Strike-Down of the 39th Amendment (Article 329A(4))

In the immediate aftermath of **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**, the executive moved to overturn the 7:6 majority from within: the seniority convention was bypassed to appoint **Justice A.N. Ray** as Chief Justice of India over Justices Shelat, Hegde, and Grover, and in November 1975, Chief Justice Ray arbitrarily convened a 13-Judge Bench to review *Kesavananda Bharati*—only to dissolve it after two days following Nani Palkhivala’s blistering advocacy. Within days, however, a paradoxical turn of events forced the very judges who had dissented against the Basic Structure Doctrine in 1973 (**Ray C.J., Mathew, Beg, and Chandrachud JJ.**, sitting alongside **Khanna J.**) to wield the doctrine to save constitutional democracy.

On **June 12, 1975**, Justice Jagmohan Lal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court set aside Prime Minister **Indira Gandhi’s** 1971 election from Rae Bareli for corrupt electoral practices under the **Representation of the People Act, 1951**. After proclaiming an **Internal National Emergency under Article 352** on June 25, 1975, and while her appeal was pending before the Supreme Court, Parliament enacted the **39th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975**, inserting **Article 329A**. **Clause (4) of Article 329A** abolished the jurisdiction of all courts over the election of the Prime Minister and the Speaker, declared that the disputed election of the Prime Minister shall continue to be valid in all respects, and declared the High Court's order void.

In **Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)**, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench unanimously struck down **Article 329A(4)** as unconstitutional for violating the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution (while upholding Ms. Gandhi’s election on the merits via retrospective amendments to the ordinary **Representation of the People Act, 1951**):
*   **Justice H.R. Khanna:** Held that **Free and Fair Elections** are the lifeblood of **Democracy**, which is an unalterable part of the Basic Structure. Eliminating judicial resolution of electoral corrupt practices destroys democratic legitimacy.
*   **Justice K.K. Mathew:** Invoked the **Separation of Powers** and the **Rule of Law**. He held that constituent power under **Article 368** is a power to frame general, abstract norms—it is **not an adjudicative power** to pass a legislative **Bill of Attainder** or pronounce a judicial decree in a specific pending election dispute without investigating facts or applying any legal standard.
*   **Justice Y.V. Chandrachud:** Held that **Article 329A(4)** violated the **Rule of Law**, **Separation of Powers**, and the principle of **Equality (Article 14)** by arbitrarily placing specific high individuals above the law without rational justification.
*   **Chief Justice A.N. Ray:** Held that deciding a private election dispute via constituent fiat without applying any law violated the **Rule of Law** and judicial determination of election disputes.

By deploying the Basic Structure Doctrine to invalidate a concrete constitutional amendment, *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* cemented *Kesavananda Bharati* as binding law under **Article 141** and transformed erstwhile judicial skeptics (**Mathew and Chandrachud JJ.**) into its staunch expositors.

#### The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act (1976) and Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

Determined to extinguish Basic Structure judicial review permanently, the Emergency Parliament enacted the sweeping **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** (the "Mini-Constitution" based on the **Swaran Singh Committee** recommendations). Section 55 of the **42nd Amendment** inserted **Clauses (4) and (5) into Article 368**:
*   **Article 368(4):** *"No amendment of this Constitution (including the provisions of Part III) made or purporting to have been made under this article... shall be called in question in any court on any ground."* (Total ouster of judicial review).
*   **Article 368(5):** *"For the removal of doubts, it is hereby declared that there shall be no limitation whatever on the constituent power of Parliament to amend by way of addition, variation or repeal the provisions of this Constitution under this article."* (Total obliteration of implied Basic Structure limits).
*   Simultaneously, Section 4 of the **42nd Amendment** expanded **Article 31C** so that laws implementing **any or all Directive Principles in Part IV** (not merely **Article 39(b) and (c)**) were immunized from challenge under **Articles 14, 19, and 31**, thereby conferring blanket constitutional primacy upon non-justiciable Directive Principles over enforceable Fundamental Rights.

In **Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench (**Chandrachud C.J., Gupta, Untwalia, Kailasam, and Bhagwati JJ.**) delivered a monumental rebuke to parliamentary absolutism:
*   **Unanimous Invalidation of Article 368(4) and (5):** Writing for the Court, **Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud** articulated the foundational syllogism of constitutionalism: *"Since the Constitution had conferred a limited amending power on the Parliament, the Parliament cannot under the exercise of that limited power enlarge that very power into an absolute power. Indeed, **a limited amending power is one of the basic features of our Constitution**, and therefore, the limitations on that power cannot be destroyed. In other words, Parliament cannot, under Article 368, expand its amending power so as to acquire for itself the right to repeal or abrogate the Constitution or to destroy its basic and essential features. **The donee of a limited power cannot by the exercise of that power convert the limited power into an unlimited one.**"* **Clause (5)** was struck down for demolishing the Basic Structure of limited constituent power, and **Clause (4)** was struck down because stripping the courts of **Judicial Review** would reduce Part III Fundamental Rights to mere parchment promises and destroy the constitutional balance of powers.
*   **Invalidation of the 42nd Amendment Expansion of Article 31C (4:1 Majority, Bhagwati J. dissenting on Art. 31C):** The majority struck down the expansion of **Article 31C** to all Directive Principles, formulating the **Doctrine of Harmonious Balance between Part III and Part IV**: *"The Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV. To give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution. This harmony and balance between fundamental rights and directive principles is an essential feature of the basic structure of the Constitution... They are like two wheels of a chariot, one no less important than the other."* While the goals set out in **Part IV** are sublime, they must be achieved through permissible means without abrogating the liberty and equality guarantees of **Part III**.

#### Waman Rao (1981), S.R. Bommai (1994), and L. Chandra Kumar (1997): Expanding the Functional Reach of Basic Structure

Following *Minerva Mills*, three landmark Constitution Bench verdicts expanded the temporal, executive, and institutional scope of the Basic Structure filter:
*   **Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) (5-Judge Bench — The April 24, 1973 Watershed Line):** Faced with hundreds of agrarian and socio-economic statutes placed in the **Ninth Schedule (Article 31B)** across decades, **Chief Justice Chandrachud** drew a bright temporal line of demarcation at **April 24, 1973**—the exact date on which the **Kesavananda Bharati** judgment was pronounced. Applying the logic of **Prospective Overruling** and *stare decisis*, the Court held that all Constitutional Amendments inserting Acts and Regulations into the **Ninth Schedule prior to April 24, 1973** are valid and unconditionally immune from judicial scrutiny. Conversely, all Acts and Regulations included in the **Ninth Schedule on or after April 24, 1973** do not enjoy blanket immunity and are open to challenge on the ground that they damage or destroy the **Basic Structure**. (Furthermore, *Waman Rao* reaffirmed the validity of **Article 31A** and the unamended **First Part of Article 31C** covering **Article 39(b) and (c)**).
*   **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) (9-Judge Bench — Basic Structure as a Check on Executive Power):** Traditionally, the Basic Structure Doctrine operated strictly as a constraint on constituent legislative amendments under **Article 368**. In *S.R. Bommai*, the 9-Judge Bench transformed the doctrine into a direct substantive standard to adjudicate the validity of **Executive Action**—specifically Presidential Proclamations dismissing State Governments under **Article 356**. The Court declared that **Federalism**, **Secularism**, and **Parliamentary Democracy** are foundational pillars of the Basic Structure: States are not mere administrative appendages of the Union, and on the flip side, if a State Government acts in subversion of **Secularism** (e.g., following the December 1992 Babri Masjid demolition), it acts contrary to the Basic Structure, legally justifying the invocation of **Article 356**.
*   **L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) (7-Judge Bench — Inviolability of Articles 32 and 226/227 Judicial Review):** Examining the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** (which inserted **Part XIV-A: Articles 323A and 323B**) and the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**, a unanimous 7-Judge Bench led by **Chief Justice A.M. Ahmadi** held that the power of **Judicial Review** over legislative and administrative action vested in the **High Courts under Articles 226 and 227** and in the **Supreme Court under Article 32** is an integral, un-destroyable constituent of the **Basic Structure**. Consequently, the Bench struck down **Clause 2(d) of Article 323A** and **Clause 3(d) of Article 323B** to the extent they excluded the jurisdiction of High Courts and the Supreme Court, ruling that while Tribunals may act as **supplemental** courts of first instance, all tribunal decisions must remain subject to the scrutiny of a **Division Bench of the jurisdictional High Court under Articles 226/227**.

### 6. Modern Doctrinal Tests: Width, Identity, and the Ninth Schedule Impact Filter

#### M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006): The Width Test and Identity Test of Overarching Principles

As Basic Structure challenges proliferated, the Supreme Court needed an analytical methodology to distinguish permissible constitutional evolution from impermissible structural destruction. In **M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006)**, a unanimous 5-Judge Constitution Bench led by **Justice S.H. Kapadia** adjudicated a Basic Structure challenge to four Constitutional Amendments—the **77th (1995)**, **81st (2000)**, **82nd (2000)**, and **85th (2001) Amendment Acts**—which inserted **Article 16(4A)** (reservations in promotion with consequential seniority for SCs/STs), **Article 16(4B)** (the Carry-Forward rule bypassing the 50% ceiling for backlog vacancies), and a Proviso to **Article 335** (relaxing qualifying marks for SCs/STs).

To evaluate whether these amendments destroyed the Basic Structure of Equality, **Justice Kapadia** synthesized a two-tiered doctrinal standard:
1.  **Overarching Principles vs. Connecting Rules:** The Basic Structure of the Constitution is not found in the literal text of isolated articles or sub-clauses; rather, it consists of **overarching systematic principles** (such as **Egalitarian Equality**, **Secularism**, **Democracy**, **Rule of Law**, and **Separation of Powers**) that bind together different articles like an architectural spine. Individual clauses (like **Article 16(4)**) are merely enabling rules that operationalize the overarching principle (**Article 14** read with **Articles 15 and 16**).
2.  **The "Width Test" (Quantitative / Boundaries Inquiry):** The Court first examines the **width** (scope and ambit) of the amending power exercised by the impugned amendment. Does the amendment obliterate the quantitative or qualitative constitutional boundaries that keep the overarching principle intact? In *Nagaraj*, the Court held that the **Width Test** was satisfied only because the **77th, 81st, 82nd, and 85th Amendments** were purely *enabling provisions* that preserved the structural guardrails of **Indra Sawhney (1992)**—namely, the **50% ceiling limit** on current-year reservations, the exclusion of the **Creamy Layer**, the requirement of **quantifiable data** on backwardness and inadequacy of representation, and the preservation of overall **administrative efficiency under Article 335**.
3.  **The "Identity Test" (Qualitative / Continuity Inquiry):** Second, the Court applies the **Identity Test** to verify whether the pre-amendment constitutional identity survives post-amendment. Even if an amendment modifies a right, does it alter the **essential core or overarching identity** of the Constitution beyond recognition? Because the four amendments merely altered the means of achieving substantive equality for SCs and STs without destroying the overarching principle of the **Equality Code (Articles 14, 15, and 16)**, they passed both the **Width Test** and the **Identity Test**.

#### I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007): The 9-Judge Ninth Schedule Architecture and the Direct Impact and Effect Test

The most rigorous synthesis of **Part III Fundamental Rights**, **Article 31B (Ninth Schedule)**, and the **Basic Structure Doctrine** was delivered by a unanimous **9-Judge Constitution Bench** led by **Chief Justice Y.K. Sabharwal** in **I.R. Coelho (Dead) by LRs v. State of Tamil Nadu (January 11, 2007)**. Originally intended in 1951 as a narrow shield for 13 agrarian land-reform laws, the **Ninth Schedule** had ballooned via successive constitutional amendments to encompass **284 statutes**—many completely unrelated to agrarian reform, such as the Tamil Nadu 69% Reservation Act (inserted by the **76th Amendment, 1994**), election laws, monopolies statutes, and preventive detention laws.

The 9-Judge Bench laid down the definitive doctrinal framework governing the **Ninth Schedule**:
*   **Fundamental Rights as Integral Components of the Basic Structure:** Rejecting any artificial dichotomy between Part III Fundamental Rights and the Basic Structure, the Court held that the core Fundamental Rights—most notably the **Golden Triangle of Articles 14, 19, and 21**, alongside the principles underlying **Article 15**—are themselves foundational pillars of the Basic Structure. A constitutional amendment cannot use **Article 31B** to immunize a statute that destroys the Golden Triangle.
*   **Rejection of the Narrow "Essence of Rights" Test in Favor of the "Direct Impact and Effect Test" (Rights Test):** Clarifying a potential ambiguity in *M. Nagaraj*, the 9-Judge Bench explained that when testing a **Ninth Schedule** inclusion, the determinant is **not** a vague, subjective search for the abstract "essence" of an overarching principle divorced from the text of Part III. Instead, the Court must apply the **"Direct Impact and Effect Test"** (derived from **R.C. Cooper, 1970** and **Maneka Gandhi, 1978**): the validity of an **Article 368** amendment inserting a law into the **Ninth Schedule** must be judged not by its form or purported object, but by its **direct impact and consequence on Fundamental Rights**.
*   **The Two-Step I.R. Coelho Inquiry for Post-April 24, 1973 Ninth Schedule Laws:**
    1.  **Step 1 (Part III Violation Check):** Does the statute placed in the **Ninth Schedule** on or after **April 24, 1973** violate or abridge any Fundamental Right in **Part III** (specifically **Articles 14, 15, 19, or 21**)? If it does *not* violate any Part III right, the challenge ends and the law is valid.
    2.  **Step 2 (Basic Structure Impact Check):** If the statute *does* violate a Fundamental Right, does the **extent and direct impact** of that violation abrogate, damage, or destroy the **Basic Structure** of the Constitution as reflected in **Article 21 read with Articles 14 and 19**? If the answer is affirmative, the Constitutional Amendment inserting that statute into the **Ninth Schedule** is void to that extent, stripping the statute of its **Article 31B** protective shield and exposing it to invalidation under **Article 13(2)**.
*   **Pre-Existing Judicial Striking Down Bars Ninth Schedule Resurrection:** If the Supreme Court has already held that the violation of a Part III right by a specific statute damages the Basic Structure, Parliament cannot subsequently resurrect and immunize that statute by placing it into the **Ninth Schedule**.

#### Post-2015 Applications: The Fourth Judges Case (NJAC, 2015) to Property Owners Association (2024)

In the contemporary era (2015–2026), the Supreme Court has continued to refine the **Width, Identity, and Direct Impact Tests** across major Constitution Bench battles:
*   **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (Fourth Judges Case, 2015) (5-Judge Bench, 4:1):** The Court struck down the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** and the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014** in their entirety. Applying *M. Nagaraj* and *I.R. Coelho*, the 4-Judge majority (**Khehar, Lokur, Joseph, and Goel JJ.**; **Chelameswar J.** dissenting) held that **Judicial Independence** is an inviolable pillar of the Basic Structure, and that **Judicial Primacy** in the appointment of Supreme Court and High Court judges (**Articles 124(2) and 217(1)**) is inextricably integrated into Judicial Independence. By replacing the Collegium with a 6-member NJAC containing the Union Law Minister and two "eminent persons" (where any two members could veto a judicial nominee), the **99th Amendment** failed the **Width and Identity Tests** by enabling executive and political intrusion into judicial appointments.
*   **Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) (5-Judge Bench, 3:2):** The Court upheld the **103rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019**, which inserted **Articles 15(6) and 16(6)** to provide a **10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)** excluding SCs, STs, and OBCs. Applying the **Width and Identity Tests**, the 3-Judge majority (**Maheshwari, Trivedi, and Pardiwala JJ.**) held that carving out an economic criteria alongside caste-based affirmative action moderately shifts the *means* of egalitarian redistribution without destroying the overarching identity of the **Equality Code**, and that the 50% ceiling rule of *Indra Sawhney* is flexible rather than an immutable Basic Structure lock for Parliament's constituent power. Conversely, in dissent, **Bhat J. and Chief Justice U.U. Lalit** held that explicitly excluding the poorest SCs, STs, and OBCs from poverty-based affirmative action practiced constitutionally forbidden **exclusion and discrimination**, violating the core of the Equality Code.
*   **Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (November 2024) (9-Judge Bench):** Resolving a three-decade reference stemming from *Minerva Mills* and *Waman Rao*, the 9-Judge Bench led by **Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** unanimously settled the status of **Article 31C** after *Minerva Mills (1980)* had struck down Section 4 of the **42nd Amendment**. Rejecting the argument that striking down the **42nd Amendment’s** substitution had left a vacuum that obliterated **Article 31C** altogether, the 9-Judge Bench held that invalidating an unconstitutional amending clause automatically **revives the unamended constitutional text of Article 31C** (as originally inserted by the **25th Amendment, 1971** and upheld in **Kesavananda Bharati, 1973**). Thus, the unamended **First Part of Article 31C** shielding genuine **Article 39(b) and (c)** laws remains valid and operative, while the Bench simultaneously overruled **State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy (1977)** and **Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (1983)** (8:1) to hold that **not every privately owned property** automatically constitutes a *"material resource of the community"* under **Article 39(b)**.

## IV. Judicial Review, Public Interest Litigation (PIL), and the Separation of Powers Dialectic

### 7. The Tripartite Architecture of Judicial Review in India

#### Textual Anchors and the Distinction Between Constitutional, Legislative, and Administrative Review

Unlike the **United States Constitution**, where the power of judicial review over primary legislation was inferred from structural logic by Chief Justice John Marshall in **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**, the Indian Constitution embeds **Judicial Review** in explicit, interlocking textual provisions across multiple Parts. As **Chief Justice Patanjali Sastri** famously observed in **State of Madras v. V.G. Row (1952)**, as regards Fundamental Rights, the Indian Constitution assigns the Supreme Court the role of a *"sentinel on the qui vive"*—not out of any distrust of popular legislatures, but as a structural consequence of a written constitution backed by an express declaration of voidness.

The textual foundations of Judicial Review in India span a comprehensive constitutional grid:
*   **Article 13(1) and 13(2):** Declares all pre-constitutional and post-constitutional laws void to the extent of their inconsistency with or abridgment of **Part III Fundamental Rights**.
*   **Article 32 and Article 226:** Vests the **Supreme Court** (as a guaranteed Fundamental Right under **Article 32**) and the **High Courts** (under **Article 226** for Fundamental Rights and *"for any other purpose"*) with plenary writ jurisdiction (**Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto**).
*   **Article 245 and Article 246 (Read with the Seventh Schedule):** Subjects all Union and State legislation to the provisions of the Constitution and polices the federal distribution of legislative competence across Lists I, II, and III.
*   **Articles 131 to 136:** Establishes Original Federal Jurisdiction (**Article 131**), Constitutional, Civil, and Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction (**Articles 132–134**), and Plenary Special Leave Jurisdiction (**Article 136**).
*   **Article 227:** Vests every High Court with constitutional superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout its territorial jurisdiction.
*   **Article 372(1):** Subjects the continuance of all pre-constitutional laws in force to the other provisions of the Constitution.

#### Standards of Scrutiny: Competence, Part III Proportionality, and Manifest Arbitrariness

Because the democratic pedigree and sovereign character of State action vary across the three tiers of the pyramid, Indian constitutional jurisprudence enforces strictly calibrated **Standards of Judicial Scrutiny**:

*   **Tier 1 — Review of Constitutional Amendments (Article 368):** A Constitutional Amendment Act cannot be struck down for lack of legislative competence under the **Seventh Schedule**, nor can it be invalidated merely for abridging a Fundamental Right in isolation (**Article 13(4)**). It can be struck down **solely** on two grounds:
    1.  **Procedural Non-Compliance with Article 368(2) or its Proviso:** Failure to secure the requisite special majority in each House separately or failure to obtain ratification by half of the State Legislatures when an entrenched federal subject or State List power is curtailed (**Kihoto Hollohan, 1992**; **Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah, 2021**).
    2.  **Substantive Violation of the Basic Structure Doctrine:** Failure to satisfy the **Width Test**, the **Identity Test** (**M. Nagaraj, 2006**), or the **Direct Impact and Effect Test** (**I.R. Coelho, 2007**).
*   **Tier 2 — Review of Primary / Plenary Legislation (Statutes & Ordinances):** As affirmed in **State of A.P. v. McDowell & Co. (1996)** and expanded in **Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017)**, an ordinary Act of Parliament or a State Legislature starts with a **Presumption of Constitutionality** (**Charanjit Lal Chowdhury, 1950**; though weakened where facial classifications infringe core dignity rights in **Navtej Singh Johar, 2018**) and can be struck down on three distinct grounds:
    1.  **Lack of Legislative Competence / Federal Ultra Vires:** Violation of **Articles 245–254** and the **Seventh Schedule** (tested via **Pith and Substance**, **Colourable Legislation**, and **Repugnancy**).
    2.  **Violation of Part III Fundamental Rights or Other Express Constitutional Limitations (e.g., Article 301):** Where a statute restricts a Fundamental Right under **Articles 19 or 21**, it must satisfy the **Four-Pronged Proportionality Standard** codified in **Modern Dental College & Research Centre v. State of M.P. (2016)** and **K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (Puttaswamy I, 2017 9-Judge Bench)**: *(i) Legitimate State Aim; (ii) Rational Nexus / Suitability; (iii) Necessity (Least Restrictive Measure available); and (iv) Balancing (Proportionality Stricto Sensu between the gravity of the right infringement and the societal benefit).*
    3.  **Manifest Arbitrariness Under Article 14:** Overturning the restrictive dictum of *McDowell (1996)*, the Constitution Benches in **Shayara Bano (2017)**, **Navtej Singh Johar (2018)**, **Joseph Shine (2018)**, and **Association for Democratic Reforms (Electoral Bonds Case, February 2024)** established that plenary legislation can be struck down under **Article 14** if it suffers from **Manifest Arbitrariness**—defined as an enactment done capricious, irrationally, without adequate determining principle, or excessively and disproportionately. Crucially, however, a statute **cannot** be struck down merely because the Court thinks it is unwise, or because it violates an abstract "spirit of the Constitution" divorced from textual provisions or Part III rights (**Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India, 2006**; **State of Kerala v. Peoples Union for Civil Liberties, 2009** noting that while **Basic Structure** is the standard for **Article 368** amendments, ordinary statutes are tested directly against **Part III** and specific constitutional articles—though in **Madras Bar Association, 2014**, the Court also used Basic Structure separation of powers and judicial independence to invalidate tribunal statutes).
*   **Tier 3 — Review of Delegated Legislation and Administrative / Executive Action:** Subordinate legislation (rules, regulations, notifications) and executive orders face the widest net of judicial review. In addition to all grounds applicable to primary statutes, delegated legislation is void if it is **ultra vires the parent enabling Act**, suffers from **Excessive Delegation** of essential legislative policy (**In Re: Delhi Laws Act, 1951**), or is unreasonable. Administrative decisions are scrutinized under **Lord Diplock’s** classic tripartite framework in **Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for the Civil Service (GCHQ Case, 1984)**, adopted into Indian law in **Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994)**:
    1.  **Illegality:** Failure of the decision-maker to understand correctly the law regulating its decision-making power, acting *ultra vires*, fettering discretion, or acting with *mala fides* / colourable purpose.
    2.  **Irrationality (Wednesbury Unreasonableness):** A decision so outrageous in its defiance of logic or accepted moral standards that no sensible person who had applied their mind to the question could have arrived at it (**Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd. v. Wednesbury Corporation, 1948**)—increasingly supplanted by **Proportionality Review** whenever Fundamental Rights are engaged (**Om Kumar v. Union of India, 2001**).
    3.  **Procedural Impropriety:** Breach of statutory procedural rules or violation of the **Principles of Natural Justice**—*Nemo judex in causa sua* (rule against bias) and *Audi alteram partem* (right to a fair hearing and reasoned speaking orders: **A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India, 1969**; **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, 1978**)—alongside the doctrine of **Legitimate Expectation**.

### 8. Public Interest Litigation (PIL) and the Democratization of Access to Justice

#### From Anglo-Saxon Locus Standi to Social Action Litigation: Hussainara Khatoon (1979) and S.P. Gupta (1981)

For the first three decades of the Republic, writ litigation under **Articles 32 and 226** remained captive to the colonial Anglo-Saxon doctrine of **Locus Standi** (standing to sue). Under this individualistic private-law paradigm, only a **"person aggrieved"**—an individual who had personally suffered a direct, tangible legal injury to a vested right—could knock on the doors of the Constitutional Courts (with the sole traditional exceptions of **Habeas Corpus** and **Quo Warranto**). In a society marked by acute structural poverty, illiteracy, caste hierarchy, and bonded labor, this strict *locus standi* barrier effectively excluded the vast subaltern majority from constitutional justice; those whose **Article 14, 21, and 23** rights were most brutally violated lacked the economic resources or legal awareness to file formal writ petitions.

In the post-Emergency era (1977 onwards), seeking to reclaim institutional moral legitimacy after the catastrophic capitulation of **ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976)**, a vanguard of Supreme Court judges led by **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** and **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** engineered a structural revolution in access to justice: **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**—which legal theorist **Professor Upendra Baxi** famously re-christened **Social Action Litigation (SAL)** to distinguish its focus on state lawlessness and subaltern survival from American civic-regulatory public interest litigation.

The doctrinal evolution of relaxed **Locus Standi** unfolded across three foundational milestones:
*   **Seeds of Collective Standing (1976–1980):** In **Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdulbhai Faizullabhai (1976)** and **Fertilizer Corporation Kamgar Union v. Union of India (1981)**, **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** argued that narrow procedural rules of standing developed for nineteenth-century individual property disputes could not govern collective socio-economic injuries in a third-world welfare state. In **Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration (II) (1980)**, **Justice Krishna Iyer** accepted a letter scribbled by a prisoner from Tihar Jail alleging brutal torture of a fellow convict by a jail warder as a formal **Article 32** writ petition.
*   **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) (The First Iconic PIL):** Based on a series of investigative articles published in the *Indian Express* by R.F. Rustamji detailing how thousands of impoverished undertrial prisoners were languishing in Bihar jails for periods far exceeding the maximum statutory sentence they could have received if convicted, advocate **Pushpa Kapila Hingorani** filed a **Habeas Corpus** petition under **Article 32** without holding any *vakalatnama* (power of attorney) from the prisoners. A Bench led by **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** entertained the third-party petition, declared the **Right to a Speedy Trial** and **Free Legal Aid** to be an implicit Fundamental Right under **Article 21**, and ordered the immediate release of over **40,000 undertrial prisoners** across India.
*   **S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (First Judges Case, 1981) (7-Judge Bench Codification of PIL Standing):** Writing the definitive constitutional charter of PIL, **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** held that the language of **Article 32(1)** (*"The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed"*) does not prescribe *who* may move the Court or *what* form "appropriate proceedings" must take. **Justice Bhagwati** established two distinct categories of expanded standing:
    1.  **Representative Standing (On Behalf of the Disadvantaged):** Where a legal wrong or legal injury is caused to a person or a determinate class of persons by reason of violation of any constitutional or legal right, and such person or determinate class is—by reason of **poverty, helplessness, disability, or socially or economically disadvantaged position**—unable to approach the court for relief, **any member of the public or social action group acting bona fide** can maintain an application under **Article 32 or Article 226** seeking judicial redress on their behalf (**People's Union for Democratic Rights [PUDR] v. Union of India, 1982**; **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, 1984**).
    2.  **Citizen Standing (Against Diffuse Public Injury / Breach of Public Duty):** Where a public authority breaches a constitutional or statutory duty resulting in a **diffuse public injury** where no single individual suffers a distinct, exclusive harm (e.g., executive interference with judicial independence, environmental degradation, or systemic corruption), any citizen having *"sufficient interest"* and acting *bona fide* (and not as a "meddlesome interloper or busybody" for personal gain or political motivation) has *locus standi* to enforce the rule of law.

#### Epistolary Jurisdiction, Non-Adversarial Fact-Finding, and Continuing Mandamus (Vineet Narain 1997, Godavarman)

In **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)**, **Justice P.N. Bhagwati** defended a suite of radical procedural innovations anchored in the text of **Article 32(1)** (*"appropriate proceedings"*) and **Article 32(2)** (which empowers the Supreme Court to issue *"directions or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of..."*—wider than traditional English prerogative writs):

*   **Epistolary Jurisdiction:** Dispensing with technical pleadings, court fees, and affidavits, the Court institutionalized **Epistolary Jurisdiction**—empowering the Supreme Court and High Courts to treat ordinary letters, postcards, telegrams, or even *suo motu* newspaper reports regarding bonded laborers, custodial deaths, or ecological disasters as formal writ petitions under **Articles 32 and 226** (subsequently regulated through the Supreme Court’s **PIL Cell** screening guidelines).
*   **Transition from Adversarial to Collaborative / Inquisitorial Fact-Finding:** Because a public-spirited petitioner filing a PIL on behalf of stone-quarry bonded laborers (**Bandhua Mukti Morcha, 1984**) or mentally ill women inmates (**Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra, 1983**) cannot match the evidentiary machinery of the State in an adversarial trial, the Court appointed **Socio-Legal Fact-Finding Commissions** (comprising district judges, journalists, social scientists, legal academics, and technical experts) along with senior **Amicus Curiae** counsel to visit sites, interview victims, and submit objective investigative reports to the Court under **Order XLVI of the Supreme Court Rules**.
*   **The Innovation of "Continuing Mandamus":** Under traditional administrative law, a writ of **Mandamus** is a one-time dispositive command directing a public official to perform a statutory duty, upon the issuance of which the court becomes *functus officio* (its role concludes). However, when confronted with systemic institutional inertia, bureaucratic stonewalling, or politically sensitive investigations into high-level corruption, a final dispositive judgment was routinely frustrated by executive non-compliance. To overcome this, the Supreme Court in **Vineet Narain v. Union of India (Jain Hawala Case, 1997)** (3-Judge Bench led by **Chief Justice J.S. Verma**) forged the remedial instrument of **Continuing Mandamus**:
    1.  Instead of disposing of the writ petition with a final judgment, the Court **keeps the writ petition pending on its docket** over months or years.
    2.  The Court issues **periodic interim directions** and requires investigative or administrative agencies (such as the **Central Bureau of Investigation [CBI]** and the **Enforcement Directorate [ED]** in *Vineet Narain*) to submit regular **sealed-cover status reports** directly to the Bench, insulating investigating officers from political interference by the executive while strictly refraining from adjudicating the guilt of the accused on the merits.
    3.  In **T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1996–present)**, the Supreme Court expanded **Continuing Mandamus** into environmental and forest governance—redefining the statutory meaning of *"forest"* under the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, creating the **Central Empowered Committee (CEC)** and **CAMPA** funds, and exercising ongoing supervisory jurisdiction over forest clearances nationwide for nearly three decades (Similarly deployed in **M.C. Mehta v. Union of India** for Taj Trapezium/CNG conversion, **Prakash Singh v. Union of India, 2006** for police reforms, and **PUCL v. Union of India, 2001** for the Right to Food).

### 9. Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Overreach: The Separation of Powers Boundary

#### The Structural Tension Between Article 142 Complete Justice and Article 50 Separation of Powers

While **Public Interest Litigation** and expansive **Article 21** interpretation transformed the Supreme Court of India into arguably the most powerful constitutional court in the democratic world, this trajectory has generated severe structural friction with the **Doctrine of Separation of Powers** (**Article 50**, recognized as an unalterable pillar of the **Basic Structure** in **Kesavananda Bharati, 1973** and **Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975**). In Indian constitutional theory, the boundary between legitimate **Judicial Activism** and unconstitutional **Judicial Overreach** turns on whether the judiciary is operating within a true constitutional or statutory vacuum to protect Fundamental Rights, or whether it is usurping functions textually assigned to the Legislature and the Executive.

*   **Legitimate Judicial Activism (Filling Legislative Vacuums Under Articles 32 and 142):** Under **Article 142(1)**, the Supreme Court is empowered to pass *"such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it"*. When there is a total **legislative vacuum** and a Fundamental Right is being systematically violated, the Court has legitimately framed interim binding guidelines under **Articles 32, 141, and 142** that remain in force *only until* the competent legislature enacts a statute occupying the field:
    1.  **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997):** Framed workplace sexual harassment guidelines enforcing **Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g), and 21** read with **CEDAW** (**Article 51(c)**), explicitly stating they would hold the field until Parliament enacted the **POSH Act, 2013**.
    2.  **D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997):** Issued 11 mandatory arrest and interrogation safeguards against custodial torture under **Articles 21 and 22**, subsequently codified by Parliament into Sections 41A–41D of the CrPC (now **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita [BNSS], 2023**).
    3.  **Laxmi Kant Pandey v. Union of India (1984):** Regulated inter-country adoptions to prevent child trafficking under **Articles 21 and 23** until statutory adoption rules were enacted.
    4.  **Common Cause v. Union of India (2018 & 2023):** Established procedural guidelines for Advance Medical Directives (Living Wills) and passive euthanasia under **Article 21**.
*   **Constitutional Limits on Article 142 (Supreme Court Bar Association, 1998):** Even when invoking **Article 142**, the Court is not above the law. As a 5-Judge Constitution Bench held in **Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998)** (reaffirmed by the 5-Judge Bench in **Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan, 2023**), the power to do *"complete justice"* under **Article 142** is a curative, supplementary power—it **"cannot be used to build a new edifice where none existed earlier, by ignoring express statutory provisions dealing with a subject and thereby to achieve something indirectly which cannot be achieved directly."** An **Article 142** order cannot contravene fundamental principles of public policy or override express, mandatory substantive bars in a valid statute.
*   **Crossing the Rubicon into Judicial Overreach (Judicial Legislation & Polycentric Policy Usurpation):** Judicial Activism degenerates into **Judicial Overreach** when courts disregard institutional competence, fiscal constraints (**Article 266** legislative control over the purse), and democratic accountability to micro-manage polycentric executive policy or enact permanent judicial legislation—such as mandating the playing of the National Anthem in cinema halls (**Shyam Narayan Chouksey, 2016**, later modified), banning the sale of liquor within 500 meters of all national and state highways (**State of Tamil Nadu v. K. Balu, 2016**), taking over the day-to-day administration of the BCCI or municipal solid-waste management, or directing the inter-linking of rivers (**In Re: Networking of Rivers, 2012**). As **Professor Lon Fuller** famously cautioned, adjudicative courts are institutionally ill-equipped to resolve *"polycentric problems"* (complex spider-webs of resource allocation and scientific trade-offs) through bipolar judicial decrees.

#### Pathologies of PIL Abuse and the Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) Self-Restraint Framework

By the early 2000s, the relaxation of *locus standi* had also been hijacked by unscrupulous litigants who weaponized the PIL jurisdiction for corporate rivalry, blackmail, political vendettas, service disputes, and personal publicity—prompting the Supreme Court to warn against PILs mutating into **"Publicity Interest Litigation," "Private Interest Litigation," "Political Interest Litigation," or "Paisa Income Litigation"** (**Dattaraj Nathuji Thaware v. State of Maharashtra, 2005**; **Ashok Kumar Pandey v. State of West Bengal, 2004**).

In **Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass (2007)**, a 2-Judge Bench (**Justices A.K. Mathur and Markandey Katju**) issued a sharp institutional call for **Judicial Restraint**, emphasizing that under the **Separation of Powers**, judges must not act like emperors or attempt to run the government: if there is a law, judges must enforce it, but they cannot create new posts, direct budgetary allocations, or usurp legislative and executive functions merely because the other branches are perceived to be sluggish.

To purge the dockets of abusive petitions while preserving genuine subaltern remedies, the Supreme Court in **State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010)** synthesized the evolution of PIL into **Three Distinct Historical Phases** and laid down **mandatory pan-India guidelines** for all High Courts and the Supreme Court:
*   **The Three Phases of PIL Evolution (Balwant Singh Chaufal Taxonomy):**
    1.  **Phase I (Late 1970s to 1980s — Protection of the Marginalized):** Focused on enforcing the **Article 21, 22, 23, and 24** Fundamental Rights of child laborers, bonded laborers, prisoners, undertrials, pavement dwellers, and women in protective homes who lacked access to courts.
    2.  **Phase II (1990s — Ecological Preservation):** Focused on the protection and preservation of ecology, environment, forests, marine life, wildlife, rivers, and historical monuments via **Article 21** read with **Articles 48A and 51A(g)** (**M.C. Mehta** and **Godavarman** series).
    3.  **Phase III (Late 1990s to 2000s — Probity, Transparency, and Good Governance):** Focused on maintaining probity, transparency, and integrity in governance and public offices (**Vineet Narain, 1997**; **2G Spectrum Case, 2012**; **Coal Block Allocation Case, 2014**).
*   **The Eight Mandatory Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) Directives Against Frivolous PILs:**
    1.  All High Courts must frame uniform rules to encourage genuine PILs and strictly discourage frivolous or motivated PILs.
    2.  Before entertaining a PIL, the Court must **prima facie verify the credentials of the petitioner**.
    3.  The Court must be prima facie satisfied regarding the **correctness and substantiation of the contents** of the petition.
    4.  The Court must ensure that substantial **public interest** is genuinely involved.
    5.  The Court must ensure that the petition aims at redressing **genuine public harm or public injury**, with priority accorded to petitions impacting the poor and marginalized.
    6.  The Court must ensure that there is **no personal gain, private motive, or oblique political/commercial consideration** behind the PIL.
    7.  In **service matters** (appointments, promotions, transfers, disciplinary inquiries), **no PIL is maintainable** whatsoever (except a strict writ of **Quo Warranto** challenging statutory eligibility to hold a public office: **Hari Bansh Lal v. Sahodar Prasad Mahto, 2010**).
    8.  Courts must ruthlessly dismiss petitions filed by busybodies for extraneous motives by imposing **exemplary monetary costs** to deter abuse of the judicial process.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 12: From Apex Constitutional Adjudication to the Grassroots Trial Hierarchy and Specialized Tribunals

While **Part 11** has mapped how the Supreme Court and High Courts exercise apex constituent and supervisory power through **Article 368 Basic Structure review**, **Articles 32 and 226 writ jurisdiction**, and **Public Interest Litigation**, the vast quantitative weight of the Indian justice delivery system rests below the constitutional courts. For the ordinary citizen, the Rule of Law is experienced first at the level of the **Subordinate Judiciary (Part VI, Chapter VI, Articles 233–237)**, the post-July 2024 criminal trial architecture under the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023**, the **Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)** ecosystem (**Lok Adalats, Arbitration, and the Mediation Act, 2023**), and the sprawling network of **Administrative and Specialized Tribunals (Part XIV-A, Articles 323A and 323B)** whose very constitutional survival was reshaped by the Basic Structure ruling in **L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)**. **Part 12** systematically deconstructs this foundational trial, arbitral, and tribunal architecture.
