# Part 14: The Federal Architecture II: Fiscal Federalism, Mineral and GST Jurisprudence, the Finance Commission, and Borrowing Constraints

## I. Constitutional Architecture of Fiscal Federalism and Taxing Sovereignty (Part XII, Articles 264–271)

### 1. The Rule of Law in Taxation (Article 265) and the Taxonomy of Public Exactions

The constitutional architecture of Indian fiscal federalism in **Part XII (Articles 264–300A)** is engineered around an intentional structural asymmetry: the decoupling of high-yield, buoyant revenue-raising powers (vested predominantly in the Union) from expenditure-intensive socio-economic and developmental obligations (vested predominantly in the States). Rooted in the fiscal blueprint of the **Government of India Act, 1935** and the **Sarkar Committee Report (1947)** on financial provisions of the Union Constitution, the framers sought to prevent the economic balkanization of the domestic market while constructing institutional transfer mechanisms to correct the resulting **vertical fiscal imbalance** (mismatch between Union and State revenue-to-expenditure ratios) and **horizontal fiscal imbalance** (inter-state disparities in fiscal capacity and per-capita cost of public service delivery). At the apex of this constitutional edifice stands **Article 265**, which operationalizes the Magna Carta and Westminster principle of parliamentary control over the public purse by mandating that *"no tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law."* Under **Article 366(28)**, the constitutional definition of *"taxation"* is expansive, encompassing *"the imposition of any tax or impost, whether general or local or special."*

#### Constitutional Mandate of "Authority of Law" under Article 265

In constitutional jurisprudence, the phrase *"authority of law"* in **Article 265** imposes a four-fold substantive and procedural restraint on the taxing sovereign, transforming taxation from an arbitrary prerogative of the Crown into a strictly bounded legislative function:

*   **Plenary Legislative Enactment vs. Executive Fiat**: The term *"law"* in **Article 265** refers exclusively to a valid statutory enactment of a competent legislature (or an Ordinance promulgated under **Article 123** or **Article 213**), barring the imposition or collection of any compulsory fiscal exaction by mere executive notification, administrative circular, or departmental instruction (**Chhotabhai Jethabhai Patel v. Union of India, 1962**; **Bimal Chandra Banerjee v. State of Madhya Pradesh, 1970**).
*   **Dual Constraint on "Levy" and "Collection"**: Both the substantive charging mechanism (levy/assessment) and the procedural recovery mechanism (collection/enforcement) must rest on explicit statutory authority. In **Mafatlal Industries Ltd. v. Union of India (1997)**, a **9-Judge Constitution Bench** held that taxes collected without the authority of law violate **Article 265**, though claims for restitution are governed by the equitable **Doctrine of Unjust Enrichment** (requiring the claimant to demonstrate that the incidence of the illegal tax was not passed on to third-party consumers).
*   **Subjection to Part III Fundamental Rights and Basic Structure**: Unlike the pre-constitutional prerogative of taxation, a taxing statute under **Article 265** is a *"law"* within the meaning of **Article 13** and must conform to **Article 14** (non-arbitrariness and reasonable classification, as established in **Kunnathat Thathunni Moopil Nair v. State of Kerala, 1961**, where a flat-rate land tax ignoring land productivity was struck down as confiscatory), **Article 19(1)(g)** (freedom of trade and profession), **Article 27** (prohibition of taxes for promotion of any particular religion), **Article 285** and **Article 289** (mutual immunity of Union and State property from taxation), and **Article 301** (freedom of trade, commerce, and intercourse).
*   **Strict Construction of Fiscal Statutes**: As crystallized by the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Commissioner of Customs (Import), Mumbai v. Dilip Kumar & Company (2018)**, a taxing statute must be interpreted strictly according to its plain textual language without equity or intendment: any ambiguity in the charging section must be resolved in favor of the assessee (citizen), whereas any ambiguity in a statutory exemption notification must be strictly construed in favor of the Revenue (State).

#### Jurisprudential Demarcation: Tax vs. Fee vs. Cess vs. Surcharge

Because the **Seventh Schedule** rigidly separates general taxing entries from regulatory fee entries (specifically **Entry 96 of List I**, **Entry 66 of List II**, and **Entry 47 of List III**), and because **Articles 270 and 271** accord radically different revenue-sharing treatment to general taxes vis-à-vis Union cesses and surcharges, Supreme Court jurisprudence has developed diagnostic tests to prevent the Union or States from disguising one fiscal instrument as another:

*   **Tax vs. Fee and the Evolution of Quid Pro Quo**: In the foundational **7-Judge Constitution Bench** ruling in **Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt (1954)**, the Supreme Court defined a **Tax** as a compulsory exaction of money by public authority for public purposes enforceable by law, not serving as payment for any specific service rendered, and merged into the **Consolidated Fund** (**Article 266(1)**) for general public expenditure without any *quid pro quo* to the taxpayer. Conversely, a **Fee** was defined as a charge for a special regulatory or compensatory service rendered to a specific class of individuals, requiring a demonstrable correlation (*quid pro quo*) between the levy collected and the expenses incurred in rendering the service. Subsequent Constitution Benches in **Sreenivasa General Traders v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1983)** and **Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana (2016, 9-Judge Bench)** diluted the rigid arithmetic test of *quid pro quo*, holding that the correlation between a regulatory/compensatory fee and the service rendered need only be **"broad, general, and reasonable"** rather than exact or individualized, provided the levy is not wholly usurped for general revenue purposes.
*   **Purpose-Specific Cess (Article 270(1) Exclusion)**: A **Cess** is a **"tax on tax"** or an earmarked tax levied for a specific, statutorily ring-fenced administrative or developmental purpose (such as the Health and Education Cess, Clean Environment Cess, or Road and Infrastructure Cess). Jurisprudentially, a cess is an exercise of taxing power under a substantive taxing entry (or the Union's residuary taxing power under **Article 248** read with **Entry 97 of List I**), not a fee. However, under the express exclusion in **Article 270(1)**, *"any cess levied for specific purposes under any law made by Parliament"* is excluded from the **Divisible Pool** of taxes shared with the States. First principles of public finance dictate that proceeds of a central cess must first enter the **Consolidated Fund of India** (**Article 266(1)**) and subsequently be transferred via parliamentary appropriation (**Article 114**) to a dedicated reserve fund in the **Public Account of India** (**Article 266(2)**) to prevent diversion to general Union expenditure.
*   **Union Surcharge (Article 271)**: Under **Article 271**, Parliament is empowered to increase any of the duties or taxes referred to in **Articles 269 and 270** (except the Goods and Services Tax under **Article 246A**) by a **Surcharge** for purposes of the Union, and the *"whole proceeds of any such surcharge shall form part of the Consolidated Fund of India."* Unlike a cess, which requires a statutorily earmarked purpose, a surcharge is an unconditional additional levy on existing tax liability (typically triggered above specified income or corporate profit thresholds) whose proceeds are retained 100% by the Union without sharing with the States and without purpose-specific ring-fencing.

### 2. Strict Separation of Taxing Entries and the Five-Category Revenue Distribution Matrix (Articles 268–271)

Unlike the **Seventh Schedule's** allocation of general legislative subjects—where **List III (Concurrent List)** creates a shared zone of Union and State competence subject to **Article 254** repugnancy—the Indian Constitution enforces a **strict, hermetically sealed separation of taxing powers** between the Union and the States. Relying on the foundational ratio of the **11-Judge Constitution Bench** in **M.P.V. Sundararamier & Co. v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1958)**, the Supreme Court established that the entries in the Legislative Lists of the **Seventh Schedule** are divided into two distinct groups: **general regulatory entries** and **specific taxing entries**. In **List I (Union List)**, **Entries 1 to 81** enumerate general subjects of legislative competence, while **Entries 82 to 92C** (with several entries omitted by the **101st Amendment**) enumerate specific taxes; in **List II (State List)**, **Entries 1 to 44** enumerate general subjects, while **Entries 45 to 63** enumerate specific State taxes.

#### Asymmetrical Tax Assignment and the Absence of Concurrent Taxation (Seventh Schedule)

The structural jurisprudence of **Sundararamier (1958)**, reaffirmed by the **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in **State of West Bengal v. Kesoram Industries Ltd. (2004)** and **Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India (2024)**, establishes three ironclad canons of constitutional tax interpretation:

1.  **Power to Tax is Not Incidental to Power to Regulate**: Although a legislative entry must be given its widest amplitude covering all ancillary and subsidiary matters, the power to levy a tax is an independent sovereign attribute and **cannot be deduced as an incidental or ancillary power** from a general regulatory entry. For example, the power to legislate on *"mines and mineral development"* under **Entry 54 of List I** does not include the power to tax mineral rights, which is separately carved out as a taxing entry under **Entry 50 of List II**.
2.  **Absence of Taxing Entries in the Concurrent List (Subject to Article 246A)**: **List III (Concurrent List)** contains **zero taxing entries** (containing only **Entry 35** on principles of motor vehicle taxation and **Entry 47** on fees in respect of List III matters). Prior to the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016**, which inserted **Article 246A** outside the Seventh Schedule to enable simultaneous Union and State taxation of goods and services, concurrent taxation was constitutionally unknown in India, designed to eliminate double taxation and overlapping fiscal jurisdictions.
3.  **Exclusive Union Residuary Taxing Competence**: Under **Article 248(2)** read with **Entry 97 of List I**, the exclusive power of making any law imposing a tax not mentioned in either **List II (State List)** or **List III (Concurrent List)** vests solely in Parliament (upheld in **Union of India v. Harbhajan Singh Dhillon, 1971 (7-Judge Bench)** in relation to Wealth Tax on agricultural land, and utilized to introduce Service Tax in 1994 prior to the **88th** and **101st Amendments**).

#### The Five-Tiered Constitutional Revenue Assignment (Articles 268, 269, 269A, 270, and 271)

To bridge the vertical gap between the Union's expansive taxing entries and the States' expenditure responsibilities, **Part XII, Chapter I (Articles 268 to 271)** establishes a five-category matrix governing who levies, who collects, and who appropriates tax revenues:

*   **Category I — Duties Levied by the Union but Collected and Appropriated by the States (Article 268)**: Covers stamp duties mentioned in the Union List (**Entry 91 of List I**, i.e., stamp duties on bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes, bills of lading, letters of credit, policies of insurance, transfer of shares, and debentures). Parliament legislates the uniform rate across India to ensure commercial uniformity, but each State collects and retains 100% of the revenue generated within its territory (outside the Consolidated Fund of India). The **101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016)** deleted *"duties of excise on medicinal and toilet preparations"* from **Article 268**, subsuming them into GST, and previously the **88th Constitutional Amendment Act (2003)** had inserted **Article 268A** (Service Tax), which was also repealed and subsumed by the **101st Amendment**.
*   **Category II — Taxes Levied and Collected by the Union but Assigned Wholly to the States (Article 269)**: Covers taxes on the **inter-state sale or purchase of goods** and taxes on the **inter-state consignment of goods**, strictly limited after the **101st Amendment** to goods excluded from the GST regime (namely petroleum products under **Article 279A(5)** and alcoholic liquor for human consumption). These taxes are levied and collected by the Government of India, do not form part of the Consolidated Fund of India, and are assigned entirely to the collecting/originating States in accordance with principles of formulation enacted by Parliament (the **Central Sales Tax Act, 1956** formulated under **Article 269(2)** and **Article 286**).
*   **Category III — Levy and Collection of Goods and Services Tax in Course of Inter-State Trade or Commerce (Article 269A)**: Inserted by the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016)**, **Article 269A(1)** mandates that **Integrated Goods and Services Tax (IGST)** on supplies in the course of inter-state trade or commerce (including imports into the territory of India under the Explanation to **Article 269A(1)**) shall be levied and collected by the Government of India and **apportioned** between the Union and the destination State in the manner provided by Parliament by law on the recommendations of the **GST Council**. Crucially, the amount apportioned to a State does not form part of the Consolidated Fund of India, and the Union's apportioned share of IGST enters the **Article 270 Divisible Pool** for further vertical devolution to the States.
*   **Category IV — Taxes Levied and Collected by the Union and Distributed between the Union and the States (Article 270 — The Divisible Pool)**: Under **Article 270(1)**, all taxes and duties referred to in the **Union List**—**except** duties and taxes referred to in **Articles 268, 269, and 269A**, **surcharges** on taxes and duties referred to in **Article 271**, and **any cess levied for specific purposes** under any law made by Parliament—shall be levied and collected by the Government of India and distributed between the Union and the States as prescribed by the President by order after considering the recommendations of the **Finance Commission** (**Article 280**). Following the **101st Amendment**, **Article 270(1A)** and **(1B)** explicitly include **Central GST (CGST)** levied under **Article 246A(1)** and the Union's apportioned share of **IGST** under **Article 269A(1)** within this **Divisible Pool**.
*   **Category V — Surcharges on Certain Duties and Taxes for Purposes of the Union (Article 271)**: Empowers Parliament to levy a surcharge on any tax or duty under **Articles 269 and 270** (explicitly **excluding GST under Article 246A** after the **101st Amendment**), with 100% of the proceeds credited to the Consolidated Fund of India for exclusive Union appropriation without sharing with the States.

#### The 80th Constitutional Amendment Act (2000) and the Single Divisible Pool

Prior to the **80th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000**, **Article 270** and **Article 272** operated a fragmented, tax-by-tax sharing regime where only Income Tax (excluding Corporation Tax) was mandatorily shared under **Article 270**, Union Excise Duties were permissively shared at Parliament's discretion under **Article 272**, and **Corporation Tax** (**Entry 85 of List I**) and **Customs Duties** (**Entry 83 of List I**) were wholly excluded from the divisible pool. This created perverse fiscal incentives for the Union to maximize non-shareable Corporation Tax and Customs Duties while neglecting shareable Income Tax. Acting on the **Alternative Scheme of Devolution** recommended by the **10th Finance Commission (chaired by K.C. Pant)**, Parliament enacted the **80th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000** (with retrospective effect from April 1, 1996):

*   **Omission of Article 272 and Substitution of Article 270**: The **80th Amendment** repealed **Article 272** and substituted **Article 270** to pool **all** Central taxes in the Union List (including Corporation Tax and Customs Duties) into a **Single Comprehensive Divisible Pool**, ensuring that States share symmetrically in the aggregate buoyancy of the entire central tax system.
*   **Computation of "Net Proceeds" under Article 279**: Under **Article 270(2)** read with **Article 279(1)**, the vertical devolution percentage (e.g., **41%**) applies not to the **Gross Tax Revenue (GTR)** of the Union, but to the **"Net Proceeds"**—which is arrived at by deducting: (a) taxes under **Articles 268, 269, and 269A** (State IGST share); (b) all Union **cesses and surcharges** under **Articles 270(1) and 271**; (c) taxes attributable to **Union Territories**; and (d) the **cost of collection**. Under **Article 279(1)**, the *"net proceeds"* of any tax or duty are ascertained and certified by the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)**, whose certificate is constitutionally **final**.

## II. Sub-Surface Fiscal Sovereignty: The Mineral Rights and Royalty Jurisprudence (MADA v. SAIL, 2024)

### 3. The 35-Year Doctrinal Conflict over Mineral Taxation (Entry 54 List I vs. Entries 49 & 50 List II)

For thirty-five years between 1989 and 2024, Indian fiscal federalism was fractured by an intense constitutional battle over who controls the fiscal surplus generated by sub-surface mineral wealth. While **Article 297** vests all lands, minerals, and other things of value underlying the ocean within the territorial waters, continental shelf, or exclusive economic zone in the Union, **Article 294** and **Article 295** vest sub-surface onshore mineral resources located within the territories of the States in the respective **State Governments** as proprietors of the soil and sub-soil. However, the **Seventh Schedule** subjects mineral regulation and taxation to a complex interlocking scheme across **List I** and **List II**:

*   **Entry 54 of List I (Union List)**: *"Regulation of mines and mineral development to the extent to which such regulation and development under the control of the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest."* Pursuant to this entry, Parliament enacted the **Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 (MMDR Act)**, wherein **Section 2** declares that it is expedient in the public interest for the Union to take under its control the regulation of mines and the development of minerals, and **Section 9** empowers the holder of a mining lease to pay **royalty** (and **Section 9B/9C** District Mineral Foundation and National Mineral Exploration Trust contributions) at rates specified in the **Second Schedule**, which only the Central Government can enhance or reduce once in three years.
*   **Entry 23 of List II (State List)**: *"Regulation of mines and mineral development subject to the provisions of List I with respect to regulation and development under the control of the Union."*
*   **Entry 49 of List II (State List)**: *"Taxes on lands and buildings."* (An unconditioned, plenary State taxing entry).
*   **Entry 50 of List II (State List)**: *"Taxes on mineral rights subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development."*

#### The Statutory Denudation Thesis: From Hingir-Rampur (1961) and India Cement (1989) to Kesoram Industries (2004)

The constitutional conflict centered on two foundational questions: First, is a mining **royalty** collected under **Section 9 of the MMDR Act, 1957** a *"tax"*, such that the fixation of royalty by Parliament constitutes a *"limitation"* that completely ousts the States' power to tax mineral rights under **Entry 50 of List II**? Second, can States value mineral-bearing land by reference to the value of minerals extracted or royalty paid in order to levy a tax on *"lands and buildings"* under **Entry 49 of List II**?

*   **The Early Equilibrium (*Hingir-Rampur Coal Co. v. State of Orissa*, 1961 — 5-Judge Bench)**: The Supreme Court held that a limitation on the State's taxing power under **Entry 50 of List II** can only be imposed by Parliament via an **express statutory prohibition** targeting taxation, and since the **MMDR Act, 1957** contained no provision expressly prohibiting States from taxing mineral rights, the field of mineral taxation under **Entry 50 of List II** had not been occupied by the Union.
*   **The Centralizing Anomaly (*India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu*, 1989 — 7-Judge Bench)**: Striking down a Tamil Nadu law levying a local cess on mining royalty, Justice Sabyasachi Mukharji, writing for a **7-Judge Constitution Bench**, concluded in paragraph 34 that **"royalty is a tax,"** and therefore a cess on royalty is a tax on royalty, which lies beyond the legislative competence of the State Legislature because **Section 9 of the MMDR Act, 1957** comprehensively occupies the field of mineral development and operates as a complete limitation under **Entry 50 of List II**. This ruling effectively stripped mineral-rich States (Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka, Rajasthan, West Bengal) of their constitutional taxing power under **Entry 50 of List II** and blocked them from using mineral value as a measure for land tax under **Entry 49 of List II**.
*   **The Semantic Rupture (*State of West Bengal v. Kesoram Industries Ltd.*, 2004 — 5-Judge Bench)**: A **5-Judge Constitution Bench** led by Chief Justice R.C. Lahoti attempted to contain **India Cement (1989)** by holding that the statement *"royalty is a tax"* in paragraph 34 of **India Cement** was an inadvertent **"typographical or clerical error"** and that the 7-Judge Bench actually meant to say *"cess on royalty is a tax."* Because a 5-Judge Bench could not constitutionally overrule a 7-Judge Bench under the doctrine of **Stare Decisis** (**Article 141**), the direct clash between **India Cement (1989)** and **Kesoram Industries (2004)** compelled a reference in **2011** to a **9-Judge Constitution Bench**.

#### The 9-Judge Constitution Bench Verdict in Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) v. Steel Authority of India (July–August 2024)

On **July 25, 2024**, the **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) & Anr. v. Steel Authority of India & Anr. (2024)**—by an **8:1 majority** authored by **Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** (for himself and Justices Hrishikesh Roy, Abhay S. Oka, J.B. Pardiwala, Manoj Misra, Ujjal Bhuyan, Satish Chandra Sharma, and Augustine George Masih), with **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** delivering a sole dissent—**expressly overruled *India Cement (1989)*** and allied precedents (**Orissa Cement, 1991**; **Mahanadi Coalfields, 1995**), restoring sub-surface fiscal sovereignty to the States:

*   **Mining Royalty is NOT a Tax**: The 8:1 majority held that **royalty** under **Section 9 of the MMDR Act, 1957** is **not a tax** or compulsory sovereign exaction under **Article 265**. Instead, royalty is a **contractual consideration** (*ex-contractu* payment) paid by a mining lessee to the lessor (whether the State as owner of sub-surface minerals or a private proprietor) for parting with the exclusive privilege of extracting and removing exhaustible minerals from the land. While a tax is imposed unilaterally by sovereign authority for general public purposes without *quid pro quo*, royalty flows from a **mining lease agreement** as economic compensation for the diminution of the lessor's capital asset (mineral reserves).
*   **Section 9 of the MMDR Act Does Not Fetter Entry 50 of List II**: Because royalty is a contractual/regulatory fee-like consideration rather than a tax, the fixation of royalty rates under **Section 9 of the MMDR Act** (enacted under the regulatory power of **Entry 54 of List I**) does **not** constitute a statutory *"limitation"* on the States' taxing power under **Entry 50 of List II**. The phrase *"any limitations imposed by Parliament by law"* in **Entry 50 of List II** empowers Parliament to restrict or prohibit State taxation of mineral rights through an **explicit statutory provision** in a mineral development law, which the **MMDR Act, 1957** has never enacted; however, **Entry 50 of List II** does **not** transfer the power to tax mineral rights to Parliament itself (in alignment with **Sundararamier, 1958**).

### 4. Sub-National Resource Autonomy and Fiscal Consequences of the MADA Doctrine

Beyond liberating **Entry 50 of List II** from the erroneous equation of royalty with tax, the **MADA v. SAIL (2024)** judgment fundamentally transformed sub-national property and resource taxation by insulating **Entry 49 of List II** (*"Taxes on lands and buildings"*) from any Parliamentary curtailment. Even if Parliament were to amend the **MMDR Act, 1957** in the future to impose an express statutory cap or prohibition on State mineral rights taxes under the conditional clause of **Entry 50 of List II**, the 9-Judge Bench held that such a Parliamentary limitation would remain **wholly inapplicable** to a State tax on mineral-bearing land enacted under **Entry 49 of List II**.

#### Plenary State Competence under Entry 49 (Land Tax) and Entry 50 (Mineral Rights Tax) of List II

The majority in **MADA (2024)** articulated three foundational propositions regarding the scope of **Entry 49 of List II** and its distinct operational sphere from **Entry 50 of List II**:

1.  **Comprehensive Conception of "Land" under Entry 49**: Applying the classical property law maxim *cujus est solum, ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos* (whoever owns the soil owns everything up to the heavens and down to the depths of the earth), the Court held that *"land"* in **Entry 49 of List II** is not confined to the surface epidermis used for agriculture or construction, but encompasses all **sub-soil strata, quarries, and mineral-bearing lands** as a single constitutional unit of taxation.
2.  **Distinction Between the "Subject of Tax" and the "Measure of Tax"**: Relying on the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** ratio in **Sainik Motors v. State of Rajasthan (1961)** and **Goodricke Group Ltd. v. State of West Bengal (1995)**, the Court held that as long as the **pith and substance** (nexus) of a State levy under **Entry 49 of List II** falls squarely on the **land** as a unit of ownership or occupation, the State Legislature possesses complete freedom to adopt the **annual yield, mineral output, pithead value of extracted minerals, or quantum of mining royalty paid** as a convenient economic **measure (yardstick)** to quantify the land's productivity and tax liability. Using mineral value or royalty as a *measure* to assess the value of mineral-bearing land does not convert a land tax under **Entry 49** into a tax on mineral rights under **Entry 50** or a tax on the regulatory domain of **Entry 54 of List I**.
3.  **Zero Subordination of Entry 49 to Entry 50 or Entry 54**: Unlike **Entry 50 of List II**, which explicitly contains the qualifying text *"subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development,"* **Entry 49 of List II** contains no constitutional subordination clause. Consequently, Parliament cannot invoke **Entry 54 of List I** to restrict, cap, or nullify a State's plenary power to tax mineral-bearing lands under **Entry 49 of List II**.

#### Prospective vs. Staggered Retrospective Recovery Order (August 14, 2024) and the Justice Nagarathna Dissent

The competing structural forces of **fiscal federalism and sub-national resource equity** versus **national macroeconomic stability and uniform industrial pricing** collided directly in the dissenting opinion of **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** and the subsequent consequential hearing on retrospective tax recovery:

*   **The Dissent of Justice B.V. Nagarathna (National Economic Unity & Cascading Inflation)**: Dissenting from the 8-Judge majority, Justice Nagarathna held that in the context of the statutory scheme of the **MMDR Act, 1957**, compulsory exaction of royalty under **Section 9** carries the character of a **tax/impost** within the wide definition of **Article 366(28)**, and the fixation of uniform national royalty rates by the Union under **Section 9** operates as an implied statutory limitation under **Entry 50 of List II**. She warned that allowing mineral-rich States to levy unbridled taxes on mineral rights (**Entry 50**) and mineral-bearing lands measured by mineral value (**Entry 49**) would trigger **"unhealthy fiscal competition,"** severe regional imbalances, a vertical spike in the cost of foundational industrial inputs (coal, iron ore, bauxite, limestone, power generation), and a loss of international competitiveness, potentially forcing industries to import minerals rather than source them domestically.
*   **The August 14, 2024 Consequential Order on Retrospective Tax Demands**: Following the July 25, 2024 verdict, the Union of India and public sector undertakings (led by SAIL, Coal India, and NMDC) petitioned the 9-Judge Bench to apply the **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling** (**I.C. Golaknath, 1967**), arguing that retrospective tax demands running into ₹1.5–2 lakh crore under State laws enacted since 1989 would bankrupt public sector mining corporations and disrupt the national economy. On **August 14, 2024**, the **9-Judge Bench** rejected pure prospective overruling—reasoning that State mineral tax statutes enacted prior to July 25, 2024 were never constitutionally void *ab initio*, but merely dormant during the reign of **India Cement**—and crafted a balanced, **staggered retrospective recovery architecture**:
    1.  **Cut-Off Date of April 1, 2005**: While States can enforce their valid mineral/land tax statutes for the past period, **no tax demand shall operate for any period prior to April 1, 2005** (shortly after the **Kesoram Industries** verdict of January 2004).
    2.  **12-Year Staggered Installment Moratorium (2026–2038)**: The payment of past tax arrears accrued between April 1, 2005 and July 25, 2024 by mining lessees/assessees shall be staggered over **12 annual installments**, commencing from **April 1, 2026**.
    3.  **Complete Waiver of Interest and Penalties**: All statutory **interest and penalties** levied under State legislation for non-payment of such taxes for the period prior to **July 25, 2024** were **wholly waived** for all assessees.

## III. Co-operative Fiscal Federalism and the Goods and Services Tax (GST) Regime (101st Amendment)

### 5. The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016) and Simultaneous Legislative Competence (Article 246A)

If **MADA v. SAIL (2024)** represents the judicial vindication of exclusive, competitive State taxing entries under the classical **Seventh Schedule** design, the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** (enacted via the **Three-Tier Special Majority plus State Ratification procedure** under the **Proviso to Article 368(2)** and brought into force on **July 1, 2017**) executed the single largest structural transformation of Indian fiscal federalism since 1950 by replacing the doctrine of **exclusivity of indirect taxation** with **simultaneous, pooled sovereignty**. Prior to 2017, indirect taxation was fractured along the constitutional boundary between **production/manufacture** (Central Excise Duty under **Entry 84 of List I**) and **services** (Union Service Tax under **Entry 92C/97 of List I**) on one side, and **intra-state sale of goods** (State VAT/Sales Tax under **Entry 54 of List II**), **Entry Tax** (**Entry 52 of List II**), and **Luxury/Entertainment Tax** (**Entry 62 of List II**) on the other. This artificial divide produced severe **tax cascading** (tax-on-tax without cross-utilization of input tax credit between Central CENVAT and State VAT), origin-based distortions via the Central Sales Tax (CST), and physical border check-posts impeding **Article 301** trade freedom.

#### Structural Rupture of the Seventh Schedule: Article 246A, Article 269A (IGST), and Subsumed Levies

To institute a unified, destination-based, value-added **Goods and Services Tax (GST)** across the entire supply chain from manufacture to final consumption, the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** engineered four structural modifications to the Constitution:

1.  **Insertion of Article 246A (Simultaneous Legislative Power)**: Bypassing **Article 246** and the **Seventh Schedule**, **Article 246A(1)** begins with a *non-obstante* clause (*"Notwithstanding anything contained in Articles 246 and 254"*) and confers **simultaneous competence** on both **Parliament** and the **Legislature of every State** to make laws with respect to **Goods and Services Tax** imposed by the Union (**Central GST / CGST**) or by such State (**State GST / SGST**) on all intra-state supplies of goods, services, or both. Crucially, the explicit exclusion of **Article 254** (Doctrine of Repugnancy) from **Article 246A(1)** means that in the realm of GST, a Union enactment **does not automatically prevail over or override** a conflicting State GST enactment; neither tier enjoys constitutional paramountcy over the other.
2.  **Exclusive Parliamentary Competence over Inter-State Supplies (Article 246A(2) and Article 269A)**: Under **Article 246A(2)**, Parliament retains **exclusive power** to make laws with respect to GST where the supply of goods, or of services, or both takes place in the course of **inter-state trade or commerce** (**Integrated GST / IGST**). Under the Explanation to **Article 269A(1)**, supply of goods or services in the course of **import into the territory of India** is deemed to be a supply in the course of inter-state trade or commerce, subjecting imports to IGST alongside Basic Customs Duty (**Entry 83 of List I**) to ensure a level playing field for domestic manufacturers.
3.  **Definitional Expansion in Article 366(12A), (26A), and (26B)**: The **101st Amendment** inserted **Article 366(12A)** defining *"goods and services tax"* as *"any tax on supply of goods, or services or both except taxes on the supply of the alcoholic liquor for human consumption,"* shifting the constitutional taxable event from *"manufacture"*, *"sale"*, or *"provision of service"* to the unified concept of **"supply"**. **Article 366(26A)** defines *"services"* expansively as *"anything other than goods."*
4.  **Wholesale Pruning and Recalibration of Seventh Schedule Entries**: In **List I**, the **101st Amendment** omitted **Entry 92** (taxes on sale/purchase of newspapers and advertisements therein) and **Entry 92C** (taxes on services), and narrowed **Entry 84** (Duties of Excise) Strictly to **six specified items** (five petroleum products plus tobacco and tobacco products). In **List II**, it **completely omitted Entry 52** (Entry Tax / Octroi into local areas) and **Entry 55** (Taxes on advertisements other than in newspapers/radio/TV), amended **Entry 62** to strip States of luxury, betting, and gambling taxes while retaining only entertainment and amusement taxes levied and collected strictly by **local bodies** (Panchayats/Municipalities), and narrowed **Entry 54** (State Sales Tax/VAT) strictly to the five excluded petroleum products and **alcoholic liquor for human consumption**.

#### Fiscal Enclaves Outside the GST Net: Petroleum Products and Potable Alcohol for Human Consumption

Despite its "One Nation, One Tax" design, the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016)** preserved critical revenue enclaves outside the GST framework to secure the political consent of the States—who feared total fiscal dependence on the Union if they surrendered their most price-inelastic, high-yield revenue streams:

*   **Constitutional Exclusion of Alcoholic Liquor for Human Consumption (Article 366(12A))**: Under **Article 366(12A)**, **alcoholic liquor for human consumption** is **constitutionally excluded** from the very definition of GST. Consequently, States retain exclusive legislative competence both to levy **State Excise Duty** on its manufacture/production under **Entry 51 of List II** and to levy **Sales Tax / VAT** on its intra-state sale under amended **Entry 54 of List II**. Bringing potable alcohol into the GST net would require a formal **Constitutional Amendment under the Proviso to Article 368(2)** ratified by at least half of the State Legislatures. (Conversely, **industrial alcohol / denatured spirit** unfit for human consumption is already subject to 18% GST on its supply, while the **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in **State of UP v. Lalta Prasad Vaish, October 2024** held that States possess exclusive regulatory and fee-levying competence over industrial alcohol under **Entry 8 of List II**).
*   **Deferred Inclusion of Five Petroleum Products (Explanation to Article 246A & Article 279A(5))**: Under the Explanation to **Article 246A** read with **Article 279A(5)**, five specified hydrocarbon products—**(1) Petroleum Crude, (2) High Speed Diesel, (3) Motor Spirit (Petrol), (4) Natural Gas, and (5) Aviation Turbine Fuel (ATF)**—are **included** within the constitutional architecture of **Article 246A**, but **Article 246A** shall take effect in relation to them **only from the date recommended by the GST Council**. Until the GST Council makes such a recommendation, these five petroleum products remain in a dual pre-GST regime: the Union levies **Central Excise Duty** under amended **Entry 84 of List I** (heavily loaded with non-shareable Road and Infrastructure Cesses and Special Additional Excise Duties), while the States levy *ad valorem* **State Sales Tax / VAT** under amended **Entry 54 of List II** (contributing cascaded tax effects across the logistics and manufacturing economy).
*   **Dual Taxation of Tobacco Products (Entry 84 of List I + GST)**: Under amended **Entry 84(f) of List I**, **tobacco and tobacco products** are uniquely subjected to **both** GST (under **Article 246A**) **and** Union Central Excise Duty (under **Entry 84 of List I**), enabling the Union to impose penal fiscal disincentives on demerit consumption.
*   **Other Excluded Taxes (Electricity, Real Estate, and Stamp Duty)**: Taxes on the consumption or sale of **electricity** (**Entry 53 of List II**), **stamp duties and registration fees** on immovable property transactions (**Entry 63 of List II** and **Entry 44 of List III**), and taxes on **vehicles** and **lands and buildings** (**Entries 57 and 49 of List II**) were left untouched in the **State List**, continuing to generate cascading input tax blocks for power-intensive industries and real estate infrastructure.

### 6. The GST Council (Article 279A), Weighted Voting Mechanics, and the Mohit Minerals Doctrine

Because **Article 246A(1)** grants simultaneous taxing power to the Union and 28 State Legislatures (plus 3 Union Territories with Legislatures) without any **Article 254** repugnancy rule to resolve conflicts, an uncoordinated exercise of **Article 246A** would immediately fracture the national market into 32 conflicting tax rates, definitions, and thresholds. To prevent this chaos, **Section 12 of the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** inserted **Article 279A**, establishing the **Goods and Services Tax Council (GST Council)** as a permanent constitutional joint-decision-making body charged with guided harmonization under **Article 279A(6)** (*"need for a harmonised structure of goods and services tax and for the development of a harmonised national market for goods and services"*).

#### Institutional Design and Asymmetric Veto Calculus under Article 279A(9)

Constituted by the President within 60 days of the commencement of the **101st Amendment**, the **GST Council** brings together the executive leadership of both federal tiers within an asymmetric weighted-voting matrix:

*   **Composition (Article 279A(2) & (3))**: (a) The **Union Finance Minister** as **Chairperson**; (b) The **Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance** as a Member; and (c) The **Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation** or any other Minister nominated by each **State Government** (including Union Territories with Legislatures—Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir—under **Article 366(26B)**) as Members. The Members from the States choose one amongst themselves to serve as the **Vice-Chairperson** of the Council (**Article 279A(3)**), and **one-half of the total number of Members** constitutes the **quorum** at its meetings (**Article 279A(7)**).
*   **Weighted Voting Architecture and Mutual Veto (Article 279A(9))**: Every decision of the GST Council shall be taken at a meeting by a **majority of not less than three-fourths (75%) of the weighted votes** of the members **present and voting**, in accordance with two constitutional principles:
    1.  The vote of the **Central Government** shall have a weightage of **one-third (33.33%)** of the total votes cast; and
    2.  The votes of all the **State Governments taken together** shall have a weightage of **two-thirds (66.67%)** of the total votes cast, distributed **equally** among all voting States regardless of population or GDP size (meaning Sikkim or Mizoram carries the exact same vote weight as Uttar Pradesh or Maharashtra—Currently $66.67\% \div 31 = 2.1505\%$ per State/UT with Legislature).
*   **Structural Veto Implications**: Because the decision threshold is fixed at **75% (three-fourths)** while the Union holds **33.33%**, **neither the Union nor the States can pass any resolution unilaterally**:
    *   **Absolute Union Veto**: Even if all 31 States/UTs vote unanimously in favor of a proposal, their combined weight is only **66.67%** (falling short of the **75%** threshold); thus, the **Union Government possesses a unilateral constitutional veto** over every GST Council decision.
    *   **Collective State Veto**: Conversely, if the Union (33.33%) backs a proposal, it still requires at least **41.67%** weighted support from the States (requiring **20 out of 31 States/UTs** if all are present and voting) to cross the **75%** threshold. Consequently, a coalition of **12 States/UTs** (representing $>25\%$ of the total weighted vote) can exercise a **collective blocking veto** against the Union.

#### Union of India v. Mohit Minerals (2022): "Unworkable Federalism" vs. Non-Binding Cooperative Persuasion

For the first five years of the GST regime, the Union Executive operated on the assumption that recommendations of the **GST Council** under **Article 279A(4)** were **constitutionally binding mandates** on Parliament and the State Legislatures, arguing that if any State could deviate from GST Council recommendations when enacting or amending its **SGST Act** under **Article 246A**, the entire edifice of "One Nation, One Tax" would collapse. In **Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (May 2022)**, a **3-Judge Bench** led by **Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** (while striking down an IGST levy on importers for ocean freight under a reverse-charge notification on the ground of composite supply double taxation) delivered a definitive constitutional exposition on the interplay between **Article 246A** and **Article 279A**:

*   **Recommendations under Article 279A(4) Have Only "Persuasive Value"**: Interpreting the deliberate textual distinction in the Constitution between mandatory actions and *"recommendations"* (comparing **Article 279A(4)** with Finance Commission recommendations under **Article 280**), the Supreme Court held that the recommendations of the GST Council are the product of a collaborative dialogue and carry **high persuasive value**, but are **NOT constitutionally binding** on either **Parliament** or the **State Legislatures** in the exercise of their primary plenary legislative power under **Article 246A**.
*   **Preservation of Simultaneous Legislative Sovereignty (Article 246A)**: To treat the GST Council's recommendations as binding commands that automatically override or fetter legislative competence under **Article 246A** would invert the constitutional hierarchy by subordinating **32 elected legislatures** to an **executive body** (the GST Council, composed of Finance Ministers) in which the Union Executive enjoys a unilateral one-third veto.
*   **Distinction Between Primary Legislation (Article 246A) and Subordinate Legislation (CGST/SGST Acts)**: Crucially, the Court clarified that while GST Council recommendations do not bind Parliament or State Legislatures when enacting or amending **primary legislation** under **Article 246A**, where the **CGST Act, 2017**, **IGST Act, 2017**, or **SGST Acts** explicitly stipulate that the Government may issue **subordinate notifications** (such as fixing tax rates under Section 9 or granting exemptions under Section 11) *"on the recommendations of the Council,"* the GST Council's recommendation serves as a **mandatory statutory *sine qua non*** for the exercise of that delegated executive power.
*   **Cooperative vs. Uncooperative Federalism**: Rejecting the Union's vision of coercive, monolithically uniform federalism, the Court held that Indian federalism is a dialogue in which States possess constitutional space under **Article 246A** to contest, negotiate, and exercise **"uncooperative federalism"** as a democratic check against centralizing fiscal hegemony, noting that under **Article 279A(11)**, the GST Council has not yet established a permanent judicial/quasi-judicial mechanism to adjudicate disputes between the Union and States arising out of its recommendations.

#### The GST Compensation Cess Architecture, Pandemic Back-to-Back Loans, and the 2025–2026 Sunset Transition

To persuade manufacturing and high-consumption States (such as Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Gujarat) to surrender their autonomous **State VAT** and **Entry Tax** regimes in 2016, **Section 18 of the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** mandated that *"Parliament shall, by law, on the recommendation of the Goods and Services Tax Council, provide for compensation to the States for loss of revenue arising on account of implementation of the goods and services tax for a period of five years."*

*   **The 14% Compounded Guarantee and the Compensation Cess**: Enacted pursuant to **Section 18 of the 101st Amendment**, the **Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017** guaranteed every State a **14% compounded annual growth rate (CAGR)** over its **2015–16 base-year revenue** from subsumed taxes for the five-year transition period from **July 1, 2017 to June 30, 2022**. To finance this guarantee without drawing on the Consolidated Fund of India, **Section 8** levied a **GST Compensation Cess** on specified luxury and demerit ("sin") goods (such as automobiles, aerated waters, coal, pan masala, and tobacco products) over and above the peak 28% GST slab, credited into a non-lapsable **GST Compensation Fund** in the **Public Account of India** (**Article 266(2)**).
*   **The COVID-19 Revenue Shock and Back-to-Back Debt Servicing Extension (2020–2026)**: During the COVID-19 pandemic in FY 2020–21 and FY 2021–22, economic contraction caused SGST collections to plummet while Compensation Cess collections proved severely inadequate to meet the guaranteed 14% growth trajectory. Following intense federal contestation in the GST Council over whether the Union or the States should borrow to bridge the shortfall, the Union Government borrowed **₹1.10 lakh crore in 2020–21** and **₹1.59 lakh crore in 2021–22** (totaling **₹2.69 lakh crore**) through a Special Window and passed the funds to the States as **back-to-back loans** in lieu of GST compensation. While the constitutional mandate to pay *revenue loss compensation* to the States expired on **June 30, 2022**, the levy of the **GST Compensation Cess** was extended up to **March 31, 2026** exclusively to service and extinguish the principal and interest on the ₹2.69 lakh crore pandemic-era back-to-back debt.
*   **The 2025–2026 Sunset and Structural Merger into GST 2.0**: With robust post-pandemic collection buoyancy enabling the early retirement of the ₹2.69 lakh crore back-to-back loan liability ahead of the March 31, 2026 outer deadline, the **56th GST Council (September 2025)** and subsequent **Union Budget 2026–27** measures executed the final sunset of the **GST Compensation Cess**:
    1.  Effective **September 22, 2025**, the Compensation Cess on non-tobacco luxury and sin goods (automobiles, aerated beverages, coal, high-end motorcycles) was abolished and **merged directly into a consolidated 40% special GST rate** (up from 28% + Cess), ensuring that the revenue hitherto locked in an earmarked debt-servicing cess now flows half as **SGST** directly to the States and half as **CGST** into the **Article 270 Divisible Pool**.
    2.  Effective **February 1, 2026**, the residual Compensation Cess on **tobacco products and pan masala** was extinguished and replaced by revised **Central Excise Duties** (**Entry 84 of List I**) on cigarettes/tobacco and a specialized **Health Security and National Security Cess** on pan masala.

## IV. The Finance Commission (Article 280), Vertical-Horizontal Equity, and Discretionary Transfers

### 7. Constitutional Mandate and Devolution Mechanics of the Finance Commission (Articles 280–281)

Because the Indian Constitution deliberately centralizes the most buoyant direct and indirect tax instruments in the Union while assigning human capital, agrarian, health, public order, and physical infrastructure obligations to the States, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described the **Finance Commission** under **Article 280** as the institutional *"balancing wheel of Indian fiscal federalism."* Rather than inscribing rigid revenue-sharing percentages into the constitutional text—which would quickly become obsolete amidst shifting macroeconomic and demographic realities—the Constituent Assembly created an independent, periodic, **quasi-judicial constitutional body** to arbitrate both **vertical devolution** (the share of the Union's divisible pool transferred to all States collectively) and **horizontal devolution** (the inter-se formula distributing that pool among the 28 States individually).

#### Quasi-Judicial Institutional Architecture and Terms of Reference under Article 280(3)

Under **Article 280(1)**, the **President of India** is constitutionally mandated within two years from the commencement of the Constitution, and thereafter **at the expiration of every fifth year or at such earlier time as the President considers necessary**, to constitute a **Finance Commission** consisting of a **Chairman** and **four other members** appointed by the President. Pursuant to **Article 280(2)** and **Article 280(4)**, Parliament enacted the **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951**, prescribing that the Chairman shall be a person having experience in **public affairs**, and the four other members shall be selected from among persons who: (a) are, or have been, or are qualified to be appointed as **Judges of a High Court**; or (b) have special knowledge of the **finances and accounts of Government**; or (c) have had wide experience in **financial matters and in administration**; or (d) have special knowledge of **economics**. Under the 1951 Act, the Finance Commission exercises all the powers of a **Civil Court** under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 in summoning witnesses and requiring the production of public documents or fiscal records from any office.

Under **Article 280(3)**, it is the constitutional duty of the Finance Commission to make recommendations to the President as to four core fiscal pillars:

1.  **Article 280(3)(a) — Vertical and Horizontal Tax Devolution**: The distribution between the Union and the States of the **net proceeds of taxes** which are to be, or may be, divided between them under **Part XII, Chapter I (Article 270)**, and the allocation between the States of the respective shares of such proceeds.
2.  **Article 280(3)(b) — Principles of Grants-in-Aid**: The principles which should govern the **grants-in-aid of the revenues of the States** out of the **Consolidated Fund of India** (executed via **Article 275** statutory orders).
3.  **Article 280(3)(bb) and (c) — Augmentation of State Consolidated Funds for Local Bodies (73rd & 74th Amendments)**: Inserted by the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992**, these sub-clauses mandate the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures needed to **augment the Consolidated Fund of a State** to supplement the resources of the **Panchayats (Article 280(3)(bb))** and the **Municipalities (Article 280(3)(c))** in the State on the basis of the recommendations made by the **State Finance Commission** constituted under **Article 243-I** and **Article 243-Y**. (Notably, because many States chronically delay constituting their State Finance Commissions, successive Union Finance Commissions—and most stringently the **16th Finance Commission**—have made the timely constitution of SFCs and public disclosure of audited local body accounts a mandatory entry condition for receiving local body grants).
4.  **Article 280(3)(d) — Presidential Terms of Reference (ToR)**: *"Any other matter referred to the Commission by the President in the interests of sound finance"* (under which the Union Executive routinely refers Disaster Management financing under the DM Act 2005, FRBM fiscal deficit roadmaps, power sector reforms, and state-level fiscal consolidation conditions). Under **Article 281**, the President shall cause every recommendation made by the Finance Commission together with an **explanatory memorandum** as to the action taken thereon to be laid before each House of Parliament. While the recommendations of the Finance Commission are constitutionally **advisory** in text, an unbroken constitutional convention treats its core **vertical and horizontal tax devolution awards under Article 270** as binding on the Union Executive, which implements them via Presidential Order without modification.

#### Vertical Devolution Trajectory: From the 14th and 15th Finance Commissions (N.K. Singh) to the 16th Finance Commission (Dr. Arvind Panagariya, 2026–2031)

The trajectory of **vertical devolution** over the past three Finance Commissions reflects the shifting boundary between **untied statutory tax shares** and **conditional central transfers**:

*   **14th Finance Commission (2015–2020, Chaired by Dr. Y.V. Reddy)**: Executed a historic **10-percentage-point jump** in vertical devolution from **32%** (under the 13th FC) to **42%** of the divisible pool, triggered by the abolition of the **Planning Commission (2014)** and the elimination of the artificial distinction between **Plan and Non-Plan expenditure**, folding erstwhile Normal Central Assistance (Gadgil-Mukherjee formula grants) directly into untied tax devolution.
*   **15th Finance Commission (2020–2021 Interim & 2021–2026 Main Report, Chaired by N.K. Singh)**: Adjusted vertical devolution from **42% down to 41%** solely as a technical 1% recalibration following the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, which bifurcated the erstwhile State of Jammu & Kashmir (whose share had been ~0.85% of the divisible pool) into two **Union Territories** (J&K and Ladakh) whose expenditure responsibilities shifted to the Union Budget. Alongside the 41% tax share, the 15th FC awarded substantial **Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit (PDRD) Grants** (~₹2.94 lakh crore over 2021–26 to 17 States) under **Article 275**, ₹4.36 lakh crore in local body grants (linking 60% of urban/rural grants to water, sanitation, and property tax floor rates), and performance-based additional borrowing space of 0.5% of GSDP tied to power sector reforms.
*   **16th Finance Commission (2026–2031 Award Period, Chaired by Dr. Arvind Panagariya)**: Constituted on December 31, 2023, the **16th Finance Commission** submitted its report for the five-year cycle **2026–27 to 2030–31**, which was tabled in Parliament under **Article 281** on **February 1, 2026** and accepted by the Union Government:
    *   **Retention of 41% Vertical Devolution**: Rejecting the collective demand of several States to raise vertical devolution to **50%** (to compensate for the Union's heavy reliance on non-shareable cesses and surcharges), and simultaneously resisting central pressures to lower the share on account of higher Union debt-servicing and defense commitments, the **16th Finance Commission retained the States' vertical share in the divisible pool at 41%**.
    *   **Paradigm Shift from "Entitlement-Based" to "Compliance- and Performance-Driven" Grants**: In a decisive break from the 15th FC, the **16th Finance Commission completely discontinued Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit (PDRD) Grants**, as well as sector-specific and state-specific grants, reasoning that perennial revenue deficit grants create a moral hazard by rewarding fiscal profligacy and unchecked unconditional cash-transfer schemes. Instead, the **16th FC** recommended total grants-in-aid of **₹9.47 lakh crore for 2026–31**, heavily concentrated in **Local Body Grants (₹8.00 lakh crore**: ₹4.40 lakh crore for Rural Local Bodies and ₹3.60 lakh crore for Urban Local Bodies, including an **Urbanisation Premium of ₹10,000 crore** for peri-urban integration and **₹56,100 crore in Special Infrastructure Grants** for wastewater management in medium-sized cities, structured into 80% Basic Grants and 20% Performance Grants subject to strict constitutional constitution of local bodies, audited accounts disclosure, and timely SFC constitution) and **Disaster Management Grants (₹2,04,401 crore** for SDRF and SDMF on a 90:10 cost-sharing basis for North-Eastern/Himalayan States and 75:25 for other States).

### 8. Horizontal Devolution Friction, the North-South Fiscal Debate, and Divisible Pool Erosion

While vertical devolution pits the Union collectively against the States, **horizontal devolution** pits low-income, high-population northern and eastern States (such as Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and Jharkhand) against higher-income, demographically transitioned southern and western States (such as Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Gujarat). This structural cleavage revolves around the philosophical tension between **Redistributive Horizontal Equity** (allocating central resources to equalize per-capita public service delivery in poorer States) and **Fiscal Efficiency and Contribution** (avoiding the penalization of States that successfully invested in human development, fertility stabilization, and industrialized GDP growth).

#### The Horizontal Equity-Efficiency Matrix: Income Distance, 2011 Census Mandate, Demographic Performance, Forest/Ecology, and the 16th FC's GDP Contribution Criterion

Every horizontal devolution formula constructed under **Article 280(3)(a)** balances three baskets of criteria—**Need** (Population and Area), **Equity** (Income Distance), and **Efficiency/Performance** (Demographic Performance, Forest/Ecology, Tax Effort, and Economic Output):

1.  **Income Distance (15th FC: 45% $\rightarrow$ 16th FC: 42.5%)**: Represents the dominant equity equalizer in Indian fiscal federalism. Computed as the distance between a State's per-capita Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) and the benchmark average per-capita GSDP of the top three high-income large States, States with lower per-capita income receive a proportionally higher share to enable them to provide comparable standards of health, education, and policing without imposing confiscatory tax rates on their poorer populations. To slightly temper the redistributive skew against industrialized States, the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** trimmed the weight of Income Distance from **45% to 42.5%**.
2.  **The 1971 vs. 2011 Census Mandate and Demographic Performance (Population 2011: 15% $\rightarrow$ 17.5%; Demographic Performance: 12.5% $\rightarrow$ 10%)**: From the **7th Finance Commission (1979)** to the **13th Finance Commission (2010–15)**, Finance Commissions were mandated to use **1971 Census** population figures so that States achieving rapid fertility reduction (Total Fertility Rate below replacement level of 2.1) would not suffer a reduction in their population-based fiscal share. The **14th FC** split the weight between **1971 Population (17.5%)** and **2011 Population (10%)**. However, the Presidential **Terms of Reference (ToR)** of the **15th Finance Commission** mandated the exclusive use of **2011 Census** population data (15% weight), sparking fierce protests from Southern States that they were being fiscally penalized for achieving national population control targets while States with high fertility growth in the Gangetic belt were rewarded. To neutralize this penalty, the **15th FC** introduced the **"Demographic Performance"** criterion (**12.5% weight**), measured by the **inverse of the Total Fertility Rate (TFR)** scaled against the 1971 population baseline. In its **2026–31 award**, the **16th Finance Commission** adjusted **Population (2011 Census)** to **17.5%** and **Demographic Performance** to **10.0%**.
3.  **Area (15th FC: 15% $\rightarrow$ 16th FC: 10%) and Forest & Ecology (15th FC: 10% $\rightarrow$ 16th FC: 10%)**: **Area** compensates geographically large States (and smaller States subject to a 2% floor cap) for the higher unit cost of administrative and transport infrastructure, reduced by the **16th FC** from **15% to 10%**. **Forest and Ecology (10% weight)**—first introduced by the **14th FC (7.5%)** and retained at **10%** by both the **15th and 16th FCs**—serves as an **"ecological fiscal transfer"** compensating Himalayan, North-Eastern, and central tribal States for the economic opportunity cost of maintaining dense and moderately dense forest cover as a national carbon sink.
4.  **The 16th FC's Watershed Innovation — "Contribution to GDP" (10% Weight)**: Historically, Southern and Western States protested that for every ₹100 of central tax contributed from their territory, states like Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Maharashtra received back only ₹15 to ₹36 through horizontal devolution, whereas Bihar and Uttar Pradesh received ₹180 to ₹270. Addressing this North-South fiscal disparity directly, the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** dropped the minor **2.5% "Tax and Fiscal Efforts"** parameter of the 15th FC and introduced a landmark **10.0% weight for "Contribution to GDP"**—marking a structural shift in Indian fiscal federalism toward explicitly rewarding States that drive national economic output, industrial agglomeration, and capital formation.

#### Structural Erosion of the Divisible Pool: Non-Shareable Cesses and Surcharges (Article 271)

A central structural grievance presented by the States before both the **15th and 16th Finance Commissions** is the hollowing out of the **41% vertical devolution headline** through the Union's escalating reliance on **non-shareable cesses (Article 270(1)) and surcharges (Article 271)**:

*   **Divergence Between Nominal (41%) and Effective Devolution (~31–33%)**: While the **14th FC** increased nominal vertical devolution from **32% to 42%** (and the **15th and 16th FCs** pegged it at **41%**), the share of Union cesses and surcharges in the Union's **Gross Tax Revenue (GTR)** nearly doubled—rising from roughly **10.4% of GTR in 2011–12** to between **18% and 20% of GTR** during the 2020–2026 period (leaving the net divisible pool at only ~80–81% of GTR). Consequently, an nominal **41%** share of an **80%** divisible pool translates into an **effective vertical transfer of only ~32.8% of the Union's Gross Tax Revenue**—virtually identical to the pre-14th FC era.
*   **Rate Substitution on Petroleum Excise**: States have specifically highlighted that when global crude oil prices declined or stabilized, the Union frequently reduced the **Basic Excise Duty** on petrol and diesel (which enters the **Article 270 Divisible Pool** and is shared 41% with States) while simultaneously increasing the **Road and Infrastructure Cess** and **Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess** (which are 100% retained by the Union), thereby capturing fuel tax buoyancy exclusively for the Union exchequer. Furthermore, **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** audit reports have repeatedly flagged instances where billions of rupees collected via earmarked cesses (such as the erstwhile Secondary and Higher Education Cess, Clean Energy Cess, and Universal Service Obligation levy) were retained in the **Consolidated Fund of India** to compress the Union's headline fiscal deficit rather than being fully transferred to their statutory reserve funds in the **Public Account**.

#### Statutory Grants (Article 275) vs. Discretionary Executive Transfers and Centrally Sponsored Schemes (Article 282)

The funds retained by the Union through cesses and surcharges frequently return to the States not as untied statutory entitlements under **Article 270** or **Article 275**, but as conditional, executive-controlled transfers under **Article 282**:

*   **Article 275 (Statutory Grants-in-Aid of Revenues)**: Empowers Parliament by law (or the President by order until Parliament provides, strictly **after considering the recommendations of the Finance Commission** under **Article 275(2)**) to charge sums on the **Consolidated Fund of India** each year as grants-in-aid of the revenues of such States as Parliament may determine to be in need of assistance. In addition, the **First Proviso to Article 275(1)** mandates automatic statutory grants to meet the costs of schemes of development for promoting the welfare of **Scheduled Tribes** or raising the level of administration of **Scheduled Areas (Fifth Schedule)** and **Tribal Areas of Assam (Sixth Schedule)**.
*   **Article 282 (Discretionary Expenditure for Any Public Purpose)**: Positioned under the heading *"Miscellaneous Financial Provisions,"* **Article 282** provides that *"The Union or a State may make any grants for any public purpose, notwithstanding that the purpose is not one with respect to which Parliament or the Legislature of the State, as the case may be, may make laws."* Originally conceived by the Constituent Assembly (modeled on **Section 150(2) of the Government of India Act, 1935**) as a narrow residuary safety valve for unforeseen emergencies or famine relief, **Article 282** was transformed during the Planning Commission era—and continues today via **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)** and **Central Sector Schemes**—into the primary constitutional conduit for massive discretionary Union transfers on subjects falling exclusively within the **State List (List II)** (such as public health, rural housing, drinking water, agriculture, and sanitation).
*   **Jurisprudential Validation and Federal Distortion (*Bhim Singh v. Union of India*, 2010 — 5-Judge Bench)**: In **Bhim Singh v. Union of India (2010)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** upheld the constitutionality of the **MPLADS (Members of Parliament Local Area Development Scheme)** under **Article 282**, holding that **Article 282** is a wide, independent source of spending power not fettered by the legislative competence boundaries of **Article 246 / Seventh Schedule** or the Finance Commission mechanics of **Article 275**. However, from a fiscal federalism standpoint, extensive reliance on **Article 282 Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS)** introduces three structural distortions:
    1.  **Erosion of State Budgetary Autonomy**: Because CSS require **co-financing (matching shares)** by the States (typically **60:40** for General States and **90:10** for North-Eastern and Himalayan States following the **2015 Sub-Group of Chief Ministers on Rationalisation of CSS chaired by Shivraj Singh Chouhan**), substantial portions of the States' own tax revenues and untied 41% devolution are locked into matching Union policy priorities.
    2.  **One-Size-Fits-All Rigidities**: Uniform pan-India CSS guidelines often mismatch the divergent developmental stages of States (e.g., an aging demographic in Kerala requiring geriatric and tertiary healthcare versus a young demographic in Bihar requiring primary immunization and elementary schooling).
    3.  **Single Nodal Agency (SNA) / SNA-SPARSH Treasury Friction**: Recent Public Financial Management System (PFMS) reforms mandating **"just-in-time"** fund releases to Single Nodal Accounts have curtailed the float funds previously held in State treasuries while imposing strict conditionalities on scheme branding and compliance.

## V. Sub-National Debt Governance, Article 293 Borrowing Constraints, and Federal Litigation

### 9. Constitutional Regulation of Public Borrowing (Articles 292–293) and Fiscal Responsibility Regimes

When tax revenues (own-tax plus **Article 270** devolution) and grants (**Articles 275 and 282**) fall short of public expenditure, a government must resort to **public borrowing** (fiscal deficit financing). In **Part XII, Chapter II (Articles 292 and 293)**, the Constitution establishes a sharply asymmetrical regime governing sovereign and sub-national debt, grounding the Union's macroeconomic control over the national debt market, external balance of payments, and inflation in explicit constitutional text.

#### Asymmetric Borrowing Powers: Union Plenary Borrowing (Article 292) vs. Territorial and Consent Limits on States (Article 293)

A textual comparison of **Article 292** and **Article 293** reveals four structural constraints imposed exclusively on the States:

*   **Plenary Union Borrowing Power (Article 292)**: Under **Article 292**, the executive power of the Union extends to borrowing upon the security of the **Consolidated Fund of India** (both **domestically and externally** from foreign governments, multilateral institutions, or international bond markets) within such limits, if any, as may from time to time be fixed by Parliament by law, and to the giving of guarantees. Notably, apart from the flexible deficit targets in the **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003**, Parliament has never enacted a rigid statutory debt ceiling under **Article 292** capping the absolute quantum of Union borrowing.
*   **Territorial Restriction on State Borrowing (Article 293(1))**: Under **Article 293(1)**, the executive power of a State extends to borrowing **only *"within the territory of India"*** upon the security of the **Consolidated Fund of the State** within such limits, if any, as may from time to time be fixed by the Legislature of such State by law. States are **constitutionally barred from borrowing directly outside India** (from foreign markets or external agencies); all external Official Development Assistance (ODA) loans for State projects from the World Bank, ADB, or JICA must be borrowed by the Union Government under **Article 292** (**Entry 37 of List I**: *Foreign loans*) and passed on to the States on a back-to-back basis.
*   **Union Loans and Guarantees to States (Article 293(2))**: The Government of India may, subject to such conditions as may be laid down by or under any law made by Parliament, make loans to any State or give guarantees in respect of loans raised by any State, and any sums required for the purpose of making such loans shall be charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**.
*   **Mandatory Central Consent for Indebted States (Article 293(3) & (4))**: Under **Article 293(3)**, **"A State may not without the consent of the Government of India raise any loan if there is still outstanding any part of a loan which has been made to the State by the Government of India or by its predecessor Government, or in respect of which a guarantee has been given by the Government of India or by its predecessor Government."** Furthermore, under **Article 293(4)**, **"A consent under clause (3) may be granted subject to such conditions, if any, as the Government of India may think fit to impose."** Because virtually every State in India has outstanding central loans (including pre-2005 Block Loans, externally aided back-to-back loans, or 50-year interest-free capital expenditure loans under the Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment), **Article 293(3) and (4)** vest the Union Ministry of Finance (Department of Expenditure) with continuous, overarching veto and regulatory power over the annual **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)** of every State.

#### The FRBM Architecture, Net Borrowing Ceilings (NBC), and the Inclusion of Off-Budget State PSE Borrowings

Acting on the recommendations of the **12th Finance Commission (2005–10, chaired by Dr. C. Rangarajan)**, which linked central debt consolidation and interest relief to the enactment of State Fiscal Responsibility legislation, all 28 States enacted their own **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Acts**, anchoring their annual fiscal deficit to a norm of **3.0% of Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP)**. Following the **N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee Report (2017)**, the **15th Finance Commission** set a pandemic recovery glide path (4.0% of GSDP in 2021–22, 3.5% in 2022–23, and **3.0% of GSDP from 2023–24 to 2025–26**, plus an additional 0.5% tied to power sector reforms), and the **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** reinforced the strict **3.0% of GSDP fiscal deficit cap** alongside an overarching target to reduce consolidated general government debt toward the sustainable anchor of 60% of GDP (40% Union, 20% States).

To circumvent the **3.0% of GSDP** borrowing limit fixed by the Union under **Article 293(3)–(4)** without transparently reflecting higher deficits in their budgets, several State Governments increasingly resorted to **Off-Budget Borrowings (OBBs)**—creating Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs), State Infrastructure Boards, or State Public Sector Enterprises (such as Kerala's **KIIFB** — Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board and **KSSPL** — Kerala Social Security Pension Ltd., or similar corporations in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Punjab) to borrow thousands of crores from banks and bond markets on the strength of unconditional State Government guarantees, with the debt serviced directly through earmarked State budget appropriations or assigned State motor-vehicle/petroleum cesses.

In **March 2022**, the Union Ministry of Finance exercised its conditional consent power under **Article 293(3) and (4)** (supported by recommendations of the **15th Finance Commission** and sub-national debt audits by the **CAG**) to issue a landmark directive: **any borrowing by a State Public Sector Enterprise (SPSE), corporation, or Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) whose principal and/or interest is serviced out of the State Budget, or through the assignment of State taxes, cesses, or royalties, shall be treated as borrowing by the State itself and deducted directly from the State's annual Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC) of 3% of GSDP**.

### 10. The Article 293(3) Federal Litigation: State of Kerala v. Union of India (2024 Reference to a 5-Judge Constitution Bench)

The Union's decision to include off-budget borrowings of State statutory boards (**KIIFB** and **KSSPL**) within the State's **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)** under **Article 293(3)–(4)** triggered an unprecedented constitutional confrontation. In **2023**, the **State of Kerala** invoked the Supreme Court's **Original and Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction under Article 131** (**Original Suit No. 1 of 2024: *State of Kerala v. Union of India***), challenging the Union Ministry of Finance's executive circulars and letters imposing a strict Net Borrowing Ceiling and deducting KIIFB/KSSPL borrowings from Kerala's open-market borrowing space.

#### Competing Structural Claims: Macroeconomic Stability vs. Sub-National Fiscal Autonomy

Before the Supreme Court, the suit crystallized the fundamental philosophical tension of **Part XII, Chapter II**:

*   **The Sub-National Fiscal Autonomy Claim (State of Kerala)**:
    1.  **Plenary Legislative Power over "Public Debt of the State" (Entry 43 of List II)**: Under **Entry 43 of List II**, the *"Public debt of the State"* belongs to the **exclusive legislative competence of the State Legislature**. Once the Kerala State Legislature has enacted the **Kerala Fiscal Responsibility Act, 2003** under **Article 293(1)** and **Entry 43 of List II**, the Union Executive cannot use an administrative circular under **Article 293(3)** to override or nullify a valid State statutory ceiling.
    2.  **Narrow Creditor-Protection Scope of Article 293(3)–(4)**: Kerala argued that the purpose of **Article 293(3)** is solely to protect the Union's financial interest as a **creditor** (ensuring that a State can repay its outstanding debt to the Government of India), **not** to vest the Union Executive with sweeping macroeconomic superintendence over a State's entire fiscal policy or its independent statutory corporations.
    3.  **"Liabilities of the State" vs. "Public Debt"**: Under **Article 293(1)**, only borrowings secured directly upon the **Consolidated Fund of the State** (**Article 266(1)**) constitute *"loans raised by the State."* Off-budget liabilities in the **Public Account of the State** (**Article 266(2)**) or corporate borrowings by autonomous statutory entities like **KIIFB** (backed only by contingent State guarantees) cannot be equated with direct State borrowing under **Article 293(3)**.
*   **The National Macroeconomic Stability Claim (Union of India / Attorney General)**:
    1.  **Indivisibility of Sovereign Credit Rating and the National Debt Pool**: The Union contended that India possesses a single integrated pool of domestic household financial savings. Unchecked borrowing by any State (whether on-budget or disguised via off-budget SPVs) crowds out private investment, drives up G-Sec and State Development Loan (SDL) interest rates across the entire economy, fuels national inflation, and threatens India's **sovereign credit rating**—since under India's federal setup, the Union and the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** serve as implicit guarantors preventing any State from defaulting.
    2.  **Substance Over Form (Doctrine of Lifting the Corporate Veil)**: When a State SPV like **KIIFB** or **KSSPL** has zero independent revenue stream and its debt is serviced 100% through State budgetary allocations or statutorily escrowed State taxes/cesses, the borrowing is, in **pith and substance**, a direct fiscal liability of the State Consolidated Fund designed to bypass **Article 293(3)** discipline (a position explicitly endorsed by the **16th Finance Commission Report in February 2026**, which called for strictly ending all off-budget borrowings across States).

#### The April 2024 Supreme Court Interim Order (Balance of Convenience Test) and the 5-Judge Constitution Bench Reference Questions

On **April 1, 2024**, a **2-Judge Bench** comprising **Justices Surya Kant and K.V. Viswanathan** delivered a dual ruling in **State of Kerala v. Union of India (2024)**—first rejecting Kerala's plea for an interim injunction to borrow an additional ₹26,226 crore for FY 2023–24, and second referring the entire constitutional suit under **Article 145(3)** to a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** as a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution (noting that **Article 293** had never been authoritatively interpreted by a Constitution Bench since 1950):

*   **Rejection of Interim Relief on the Three-Pronged Injunction Test**: Applying the classical triple test for interim injunctions (**Prima Facie Case**, **Balance of Convenience**, and **Irreparable Injury**), the Bench held that all three prongs tilted decisively in favor of the **Union of India**:
    1.  **Prima Facie Case**: *Prima facie*, the language of **Article 293(3) and (4)** is wide enough to empower the Union to regulate State borrowings and include off-budget liabilities serviced from the State exchequer within the Net Borrowing Ceiling, especially since Kerala had previously accepted the 15th Finance Commission's borrowing framework to avail extra borrowing space.
    2.  **Balance of Convenience**: The mischief that would ensue to the **national economy** if States were permitted via interim judicial orders to breach their fiscal deficit ceilings and borrow indiscriminately from the market far outweighs the temporary fiscal hardship to a single State, particularly when **fiscal hardship is self-inflicted due to the State's own financial mismanagement** or off-budget accounting practices.
    3.  **Absence of Irreparable Injury**: Because any borrowing quota denied in one financial year can be adjusted or compensated in subsequent years if the State eventually succeeds in the final suit, no irreparable legal injury is caused by refusing an interim bailout.
*   **Four Constitutional Questions Referred to the 5-Judge Constitution Bench (Article 145(3))**:
    1.  What is the true import and scope of **Article 293(1), (3), and (4)**, and does the power to borrow under **Article 293(1)** read with **Entry 43 of List II** confer an enforceable constitutional **right** on a State to demand unregulated borrowing from the Union, or is it strictly subject to fiscal federalism norms and Union macroeconomic regulation under **Article 293(3)–(4)**?
    2.  Does the expression *"raise any loan"* in **Article 293(3)** encompass **off-budget borrowings** raised by State-owned corporations/SPVs (where the principal and/or interest is serviced out of the Consolidated Fund of the State or assigned State revenues) and liabilities arising out of the **Public Account of the State (Article 266(2))**?
    3.  What is the nature and Permissible Scope of the **"conditions"** that the Government of India may impose under **Article 293(4)** while granting consent under **Article 293(3)**, and can the Union apply a uniform formula fixed on the recommendations of the **Finance Commission** without conducting an individualized creditor-risk assessment for each loan?
    4.  Is the fiscal policy decision of the Union fixing the **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)** on the advice of the **Finance Commission** and the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** amenable to judicial review under **Article 131**, and if so, what are the doctrinal parameters of such review?

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 15: From Fiscal Centralism to Emergency Coercion and Asymmetrical Federalism

The constitutional dialectics of **Part 14** demonstrate that Indian federalism is neither a static compact of autonomous sovereigns nor a unitary command economy, but a contested institutional equilibrium operating across two distinct registers: in the domain of **original constitutional tax entries**, the **9-Judge Bench in *MADA v. SAIL* (2024)** and the **3-Judge Bench in *Mohit Minerals* (2022)** have fortified sub-national legislative and resource autonomy under **Entries 49 and 50 of List II** and **Article 246A**; conversely, in the domain of **revenue distribution, horizontal equalization, and public debt governance**, **Articles 270, 271, 280, 282, and 293(3)–(4)**—reinforced by the **16th Finance Commission's (2026–2031)** compliance-driven transfer architecture and the Supreme Court's **April 2024 ruling in *State of Kerala v. Union of India***—vest the Union with formidable fiscal leverage to enforce national macroeconomic discipline. When normal fiscal, legislative, and administrative coordination breaks down, or when the Union confronts existential security shocks or deep sub-national cultural diversity, the Constitution transitions from the cooperative and competitive mechanisms of **Parts XI, XII, and XIII** to its most extreme centripetal and centrifugal instruments. In **Part 15 (Emergency Provisions, Union Territories, and Asymmetrical Federalism)**, we examine how the Constitution converts the federal structure into a unitary command architecture during **National, State, and Financial Emergencies (Part XVIII, Articles 352–360)**—including **Article 360**, which empowers the President during a **Financial Emergency** to suspend the normal **Part XII** fiscal distribution scheme, mandate the reduction of salaries (including those of Supreme Court and High Court Judges), and reserve all State **Money Bills and Financial Bills** for Presidential consideration—while simultaneously accommodating sub-national distinctiveness through the **NCT of Delhi regime (Article 239AA)**, the constitutional closure of **Article 370 (*In Re: Article 370*, Dec 2023)**, the special safeguards of **Articles 371–371J**, and the autonomous indigenous governance architectures of the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules (Article 244)**.
