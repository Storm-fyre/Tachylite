# Part 10: The Higher Judiciary: Jurisdiction, Collegium Appointments, Judicial Independence, and Accountability

## I. Unified Judicial Architecture and the Jurisdictional Matrix of the Higher Judiciary

### 1. Constitutional Foundations of the Integrated Pyramid (Articles 124–147 and Articles 214–231)

#### Structural Divergence from Dual Federal Judiciaries

The Indian constitutional design resolves the classic tension between federal division of legislative power and uniform rule-of-law adjudication by constructing a single, integrated judicial hierarchy. Whereas classical federations such as the United States operate a dual judicial architecture—in which federal courts adjudicate federal questions and state supreme courts remain the final arbiters of state constitutional and statutory law—the Indian Constitution establishes an indivisible judicial pyramid anchored in **Part V, Chapter IV (Articles 124–147)** for the **Supreme Court of India** and **Part VI, Chapter V (Articles 214–231)** for the **High Courts**. During the Constituent Assembly Debates, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar** explicitly rejected a dual court system to prevent jurisdictional fragmentation, declaring that the Indian Federation, though dual in polity, is unitary in its judicial system, administering both Union and State laws through a seamless chain of command culminating in the Supreme Court.

*   **Continuity of the Colonial Federal Court and High Courts:** Under **Article 374**, the judges and pending proceedings of the **Federal Court of India** (established under the **Government of India Act, 1935**) automatically transitioned into the **Supreme Court of India** upon its inauguration on January 28, 1950, with **Article 135** vesting the Supreme Court with the jurisdiction and powers previously exercisable by the Federal Court under any existing law. Simultaneously, **Article 214** mandates that there shall be a **High Court for each State**, while **Article 231** (substituted by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**) empowers Parliament by law to establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and a Union Territory, read with **Article 230**, which allows Parliament to extend the jurisdiction of a High Court to, or exclude it from, any Union Territory.
*   **Constitutional vs. Parliamentary Competence over Judicial Strength:** Under **Article 124(1)**, the Constitution originally fixed the Supreme Court's strength at a Chief Justice of India (**CJI**) and not more than seven other Judges until Parliament by law prescribed a larger number. Exercising this plenary legislative power under the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956** (as last amended in **2019**), Parliament expanded the sanctioned strength to **34 Judges** (the **CJI plus 33 Judges**). Conversely, in a crucial structural asymmetry reflecting executive flexibility at the state tier, **Article 216** vests the power to determine the strength of each High Court not in Parliament or the State Legislature, but in the **President of India**, who appoints such number of Judges as he may from time to time deem it necessary to appoint.
*   **Plenary Legislative Enhancement of Jurisdiction (Articles 138–140):** While Parliament cannot curtail the core constitutional jurisdiction of the Supreme Court without offending the **Basic Structure Doctrine**, it possesses expansive authority under **Article 138(1)** to confer further jurisdiction and powers with respect to any of the matters in the **Union List (List I)**, under **Article 138(2)** to enlarge jurisdiction by special agreement between the Government of India and any State, under **Article 139** to confer powers to issue prerogative writs for purposes *other* than Fundamental Rights, and under **Article 140** to confer supplemental powers enabling the Court to exercise its constitutional authority more effectively.

#### Institutional Composition, Seat of the Court, and Ad Hoc/Retired Judicial Deployment (Articles 124–130, 214–224A)

To ensure institutional resilience against case backlogs, quorum deficits, and geographical barriers to justice, the Constitution embeds elastic personnel and territorial mechanisms within both the Supreme Court and the High Courts. These provisions balance the permanence of constitutional tenure against the operational exigencies of judicial administration.

*   **Qualifications and the "Distinguished Jurist" Clause (Articles 124(3) and 217(2)):** Under **Article 124(3)**, a person is qualified for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court if they are a citizen of India and: (a) have been for at least **5 years** a Judge of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession; or (b) have been for at least **10 years** an advocate of a High Court or two or more such Courts in succession; or (c) are, in the opinion of the President, a **distinguished jurist**. Under **Article 217(2)**, qualification for a High Court Judge requires Indian citizenship and at least **10 years** of holding a judicial office in the territory of India or **10 years** as an advocate of a High Court. Crucially, the Constitution prescribes **no minimum age** for appointment to either the Supreme Court or a High Court, and unlike **Article 124(3)(c)**, **Article 217(2)** contains **no "distinguished jurist" category** for High Court appointments (an omission that the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** briefly rectified before the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** deleted it).
*   **Acting Chief Justice, Ad Hoc Judges, and Retired Judges (Articles 126–128 and 223–224A):** When the office of the **CJI** is vacant or the CJI is unable to perform his duties, the President appoints another Judge of the Court as **Acting Chief Justice** under **Article 126** (mirrored for High Courts in **Article 223**). If at any time there is a lack of **quorum** of permanent Supreme Court Judges to hold or continue any session, **Article 127** empowers the **CJI**, with the **previous consent of the President** and after **consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned**, to request in writing a duly qualified High Court Judge to attend the sittings of the Supreme Court as an **Ad Hoc Judge**. Under **Article 128**, the **CJI**, with the **previous consent of the President**, may request any retired Judge of the Supreme Court, Federal Court, or a duly qualified retired High Court Judge to sit and act as a Judge of the Supreme Court (provided the retired judge consents). At the High Court level, **Article 224(1)** empowers the President to appoint duly qualified persons as **Additional Judges** for a period not exceeding **two years** to clear temporary business arrears, **Article 224(2)** provides for **Acting Judges** when a permanent judge is absent, and **Article 224A** allows a High Court Chief Justice, with the previous consent of the President, to request retired High Court Judges to sit and act (activated by the Supreme Court in *Lok Prahari v. Union of India*, **2021** to tackle chronic High Court pendency).
*   **Seat of the Supreme Court and Regional Bench Debates (Article 130):** **Article 130** stipulates that the Supreme Court shall sit in **Delhi** or in such other place or places as the **Chief Justice of India may, with the approval of the President, from time to time, appoint**. Because the word used is "may," the Supreme Court cannot be compelled by a writ of mandamus or parliamentary directive to establish regional benches outside Delhi unless the **CJI** initiates the proposal and secures Presidential approval.

### 2. Exclusive Original Federal Jurisdiction (Article 131) and Constitutional Federal Litigation

#### Textual Architecture and Party Qualification Rules under Article 131

As the institutional umpire of Indian federalism, the Supreme Court is vested under **Article 131** with **Original and Exclusive Jurisdiction** in any dispute: (a) between the **Government of India and one or more States**; or (b) between the **Government of India and any State or States on one side and one or more other States on the other**; or (c) between **two or more States**, if and insofar as the dispute involves **any question (whether of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends**. "Original" signifies that the proceeding originates directly in the Supreme Court at the first instance via an original civil suit (regulated by the Supreme Court Rules), and "Exclusive" signifies that no other court in India—including a High Court under **Article 226**—possesses jurisdiction to entertain such inter-governmental disputes.

*   **Strict Party Filter and Exclusion of Non-Sovereign Entities:** The party-qualification threshold under **Article 131** is rigid. No private citizen, corporate body, municipal authority, or public sector undertaking (PSU)—even if wholly owned by the Union or a State—can be impleaded either as a plaintiff or as a defendant in an **Article 131** suit (*State of Bihar v. Union of India*, **1970**). When a State seeks relief against a Central PSU alongside the Union of India, the suit is non-maintainable under **Article 131** and must be pursued in ordinary civil courts or via writ petitions.
*   **"Legal Right" vs. Purely Political Controversies:** In *State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (**1977**) and *State of Karnataka v. Union of India* (**1977**), a 7-Judge Constitution Bench clarified that a "legal right" under **Article 131** is not confined to private-law proprietary rights; it encompasses the constitutional competence, legislative autonomy, and executive authority of a State Government vis-à-vis the Union. Even if a federal dispute between the Union and a State possesses political overtones (such as the appointment of a Central Commission of Inquiry against a sitting Chief Minister or the threatened invocation of **Article 356**), the suit is maintainable under **Article 131** so long as it raises a justiciable question regarding the constitutional scope of sovereign power.
*   **Constitutional Carve-Outs and Ousters from Article 131:** The **Proviso to Article 131** explicitly excludes disputes arising out of any treaty, agreement, covenant, engagement, *sanad*, or other similar instrument which, having been entered into or executed before the commencement of the Constitution, continues in operation (such disputes belong to the mandatory advisory domain of **Article 143(2)**). Furthermore, because **Article 131** opens with the phrase *"Subject to the provisions of this Constitution,"* its jurisdiction is overridden by specific constitutional ousters:
    *   **Article 262(2)** read with the **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956** (barring Supreme Court jurisdiction over inter-state river water allocation disputes referred to a statutory tribunal, though states have invoked **Article 131** to enforce non-water-sharing rights or **Article 136** against tribunal awards).
    *   **Article 280** (matters referred to the **Finance Commission**).
    *   **Article 290** (adjustment of certain expenses and pensions between the Union and the States by an arbitrator appointed by the CJI).
    *   **Article 257(4)** and **Article 258(3)** (determination of extra administrative costs payable by the Union to a State).

#### Challenging Central Plenary Statutes and Fiscal Borrowing Caps (Articles 131 vs. 293(3))

Over the last decade, **Article 131** has evolved into the primary battleground between executive/legislative centralization and asymmetrical federal autonomy, centers on two foundational controversies: whether a State can challenge the constitutional validity of a **Central Act** directly via an **Article 131** suit, and whether a State can invoke **Article 131** to contest fiscal borrowing ceilings imposed by the Union under **Article 293(3)**.

*   **Divergent Precedents on Challenging Central Statutes under Article 131:** In *State of Madhya Pradesh v. Union of India* (**2011**), a 2-Judge Bench held that the constitutional validity of a Central law cannot normally be challenged in an original suit under **Article 131**, asserting that the proper remedy is the extraordinary writ jurisdiction under **Article 32** or **Article 226**. However, in *State of Jharkhand v. State of Bihar* (**2014**), another 2-Judge Bench disagreed with the **2011** ruling, observing that an **Article 131** suit is the quintessential vehicle for testing whether a parliamentary enactment trenches upon a State's constitutional rights or legislative competence under the **Seventh Schedule**, and referred the conflict to a larger Constitution Bench. Following the enactment of the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019** and the three **Central Farm Laws (2020)**, the States of Kerala, Chhattisgarh, and Punjab filed original suits under **Article 131** challenging these central statutes on federal and Basic Structure grounds, keeping this jurisdictional reference at the forefront of federal jurisprudence.
*   **Fiscal Federalism and the 2024 Kerala Borrowing Caps Suit (*State of Kerala v. Union of India*, 2024):** In April **2024**, the Supreme Court invoked its **Article 131** jurisdiction in an unprecedented fiscal federalism suit filed by the State of Kerala challenging the Union Ministry of Finance's decision to impose a strict **Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)** of 3% of GSDP under **Article 293(3)** and to include off-budget borrowings made by Kerala's State-owned enterprises (such as KIIFB) within the State's public debt liabilities. While refusing interim relief to Kerala on the ground that the State had already over-borrowed against permissible fiscal limits, the 2-Judge Bench (Justices Surya Kant and K.V. Viswanathan) recognized that **Article 293** had never been authoritatively interpreted by the Supreme Court since 1950 and referred the suit under **Article 145(3)** to a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** to adjudicate whether fiscal decentralization is a facet of Indian federalism and whether the Union's consent power under **Article 293(3)** is plenary or subject to objective fiscal criteria.

### 3. Extraordinary Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32 vs. Article 226) and the Appellate Pyramid (Articles 132–136)

#### Asymmetrical Scope of Prerogative Writs: Fundamental Rights Enforcement vs. "For Any Other Purpose"

While **Part 5** of this treatise examined the substantive mechanics of the five prerogative writs (*Habeas Corpus*, *Mandamus*, *Prohibition*, *Certiorari*, and *Quo Warranto*), their structural distribution between the Supreme Court (**Article 32**) and the High Courts (**Article 226**) creates a counter-intuitive constitutional paradox: although the Supreme Court sits at the apex of the judicial hierarchy, the High Courts possess a **substantively wider writ jurisdiction** while the Supreme Court possesses a **territorially wider and constitutionally guaranteed writ obligation**.

*   **Guaranteed Fundamental Right vs. Discretionary Constitutional Power:** **Article 32** is itself situated within **Part III (Fundamental Rights)**. Consequently, when a citizen establishes a *prima facie* violation of a Fundamental Right, the Supreme Court cannot refuse to entertain the petition on technical grounds of alternative remedies or laches (*Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras*, **1950**), because seeking a remedy under **Article 32** is a guaranteed Fundamental Right in itself. Conversely, **Article 226** lies outside Part III; the High Court's power to issue writs is discretionary, and a High Court will ordinarily decline to exercise **Article 226** jurisdiction if an efficacious alternative statutory remedy exists (unless there is a breach of natural justice, a violation of Fundamental Rights, or an attack on the *vires* of a statute).
*   **"For Any Other Purpose" under Article 226:** The Supreme Court under **Article 32** can issue writs **solely** for the enforcement of the **Fundamental Rights** conferred by **Part III** (unless Parliament enlarges this power by law under **Article 139**, which it has not generally done). Under **Article 226(1)**, however, every High Court is empowered to issue writs not only for the enforcement of any of the Fundamental Rights conferred by Part III, **but also "for any other purpose"**—meaning the enforcement of any ordinary statutory right, common-law legal right, constitutional right outside Part III (such as **Article 300A** property rights or **Article 265** tax immunity), or administrative duty.
*   **Territorial Cause-of-Action Nexus (Article 226(2)):** While the Supreme Court's writ runs across the entire territory of India, a High Court's writ under **Article 226(1)** initially ran only against authorities located within its state boundaries. Following the **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963** (and the **42nd/44th Amendments**), **Article 226(2)** empowers a High Court to issue writs to any Government, authority, or person located **outside** its territorial jurisdiction, provided that the **cause of action, wholly or in part**, arises within the territorial limits of that High Court (*Kusum Ingots & Alloys Ltd. v. Union of India*, **2004**). Furthermore, in the 7-Judge Constitution Bench landmark **L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)**, the Supreme Court declared that both **Article 32** and **Article 226/227** judicial review constitute inviolable pillars of the **Basic Structure**, striking down clauses of the **42nd Amendment (Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d))** that sought to exclude High Court writ scrutiny over administrative tribunals.

#### Constitutional, Civil, and Criminal Appellate Pathways (Articles 132, 133, 134, and 134A)

Unlike the United States Supreme Court, whose appellate docket is confined to federal and constitutional questions, the Indian Supreme Court sits as a general court of final appeal across constitutional, civil, and criminal domains under **Articles 132 to 134A**, complemented by its power to transfer cases under **Article 139A**.

*   **Constitutional Appeals (Article 132):** An appeal lies to the Supreme Court from any judgment, decree, or final order of a High Court in India—whether in a **civil, criminal, or other proceeding**—if the High Court certifies under **Article 134A** that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of this Constitution**. Under **Article 145(3)**, every such case involving a substantial question of constitutional interpretation must be heard and decided by a **Constitution Bench of at least 5 Judges**.
*   **Civil Appeals (Article 133):** Originally, civil appeals under **Article 133** were tied to a pecuniary valuation threshold (₹20,000). The **30th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972** abolished the monetary limit so that rich and poor litigants stand on an equal footing. Under amended **Article 133(1)**, a civil appeal lies to the Supreme Court only if the High Court certifies under **Article 134A** that: (a) the case involves a **substantial question of law of general importance**; and (b) in the opinion of the High Court, the said question **needs to be decided by the Supreme Court**. Under **Article 133(3)**, no appeal lies to the Supreme Court from the judgment, decree, or final order of a **Single Judge** of a High Court (notwithstanding anything in Article 133, though Special Leave under **Article 136** remains unaffected).
*   **Criminal Appeals (Article 134):** An appeal lies to the Supreme Court from any judgment, final order, or sentence in a criminal proceeding of a High Court in two distinct ways:
    1.  **As a Matter of Right (Without High Court Certificate):** Under **Article 134(1)(a)**, where the High Court has on appeal **reversed an order of acquittal** of an accused person and **sentenced him to death**; or under **Article 134(1)(b)**, where the High Court has **withdrawn for trial before itself** any case from any court subordinate to its authority and has in such trial **convicted the accused person and sentenced him to death**. Furthermore, exercising its power under **Article 134(2)**, Parliament enacted the **Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970**, extending this automatic right of appeal to cases where the High Court reverses an acquittal or withdraws a trial and sentences the accused to **imprisonment for life or for a period of not less than 10 years**.
    2.  **By High Court Certificate:** Under **Article 134(1)(c)**, if the High Court certifies under **Article 134A** that the case is a fit one for appeal to the Supreme Court.
*   **Certificate for Appeal (Article 134A) and Transfer of Cases (Article 139A):** Inserted by the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978**, **Article 134A** streamlines the certification process across **Articles 132(1), 133(1), and 134(1)(c)** by mandating that the High Court may grant a certificate on its own motion (*suo motu*) or if an **oral application** is made by the aggrieved party **immediately after the passing or making of such judgment, decree, final order, or sentence**. Similarly, **Article 139A** (inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976** and modified by the **44th Amendment, 1978**) empowers the Supreme Court to withdraw to itself cases pending before one or more High Courts if they involve identical or substantially the same substantial questions of general importance, or under **Article 139A(2)** to transfer any case, appeal, or other proceeding from one High Court to another High Court if deemed expedient for the ends of justice.

#### Plenary Residual Discretion under Special Leave to Appeal (Article 136) and the National Court of Appeal Debate

Positioned outside the ordinary appellate gates of **Articles 132–134**, **Article 136** vests the Supreme Court with an extraordinary, overarching discretionary reservoir: *"Notwithstanding anything in this Chapter, the Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India."* The sole constitutional exception in **Article 136(2)** excludes any judgment, determination, sentence, or order passed or made by any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the **Armed Forces**.

*   **Plenary Scope and Procedural Bifurcation of Special Leave Petitions (SLPs):** Unlike **Articles 132–134**, **Article 136** is not confined to "final orders" or "High Courts"; it reaches **interlocutory orders** as well as final judgments of **any court or tribunal** (including industrial tribunals, income tax tribunals, and election tribunals, though excluding purely administrative bodies). An **SLP** operates in two distinct stages (*Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala*, **2000**): first, the discretionary **grant of leave** (where dismissal *in limine* by a non-speaking order does not attract the **Doctrine of Merger** or create binding precedent under **Article 141**); and second, once leave is granted, the conversion of the SLP into a regular **Civil or Criminal Appeal** (where the resulting order merges the lower court judgment into the Supreme Court's decree).
*   **Self-Imposed Restraint vs. Docket Explosion (*Pritam Singh* to *Mathai @ Joby*):** In its foundational ruling in **Pritam Singh v. The State (1950)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench held that the wide discretionary power under **Article 136** is to be exercised **sparingly and in exceptional cases only**, when special and exceptional circumstances are shown to exist and grave or substantial injustice has been done. However, over subsequent decades, liberal entertainment of SLPs against routine bail orders, rent control decrees, and service disputes transformed the Supreme Court from a Constitutional Court into a routine *court of error*, generating over 60,000–80,000 pending matters. In **Mathai @ Joby v. George (2010)**, a 2-Judge Bench referred to a 5-Judge Constitution Bench the question of laying down authoritative guidelines to restrict **Article 136** to constitutional and nationally significant questions; while the 5-Judge Bench in **2016** declined to frame rigid mathematical straitjackets on the ground that fettering **Article 136** would undermine its constitutional flexibility, it reaffirmed *Pritam Singh*'s mandate of exceptional restraint.
*   **The National Court of Appeal (NCA) Debate (*V. Vasanthakumar v. H.C. Bhatia*, 2016):** To resolve the tension between the Supreme Court's role as a dedicated **Constitutional Court** and its crushing burden as an **Appellate Court**, the **Law Commission of India** (in its **95th Report (1984)**, **125th Report (1988)**, and **229th Report (2009)**), building upon the Supreme Court's *obiter* observations in *Bihar Legal Support Society v. Chief Justice of India* (**1986**), recommended establishing a **National Court of Appeal (NCA)** with regional benches in Chennai, Mumbai, Kolkata, and Delhi to act as final courts of appeal for routine civil, criminal, and statutory matters, leaving the Supreme Court in New Delhi exclusively for constitutional and public law adjudication. In **V. Vasanthakumar v. H.C. Bhatia (2016)**, a 3-Judge Bench referred a batch of petitions to a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** to examine whether establishing a **National Court of Appeal** or regional Cassation Benches is constitutionally permissible or whether stripping the Supreme Court of its final appellate character would violate the **Basic Structure** under **Articles 130 and 136** (which the Union Government opposed as constitutionally impermissible).

### 4. Presidential Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143): Constitutional Mechanics and Binding Force

#### Discretionary Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143(1)) vs. Mandatory Pre-Constitutional Treaty Jurisdiction (Article 143(2))

Tracing its lineage to **Section 213 of the Government of India Act, 1935** and **Section 4 of the Judicial Committee Act, 1833 (UK)**—and standing in sharp contrast to the **United States Supreme Court** (which since 1793 has refused to tender advisory opinions to the President as a violation of the Article III "Case or Controversy" requirement) and the **High Court of Australia** (*In Re Judiciary and Navigation Acts*, **1921**)—the Indian Constitution codifies a robust **Advisory Jurisdiction** (paralleled by the **Supreme Court of Canada**) in **Article 143**. By operation of **Article 145(3)**, every Presidential Reference under **Article 143** must be heard by a **Constitution Bench of at least 5 Judges** in open court (**Article 145(4)**).

*   **The Two-Track Bifurcation of Article 143(1) and Article 143(2):** **Article 143** creates two structurally distinct pathways depending on the subject matter of the Presidential Reference:
    1.  **Article 143(1) (General Questions of Law or Fact):** If at any time it appears to the **President** (acting strictly on the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers under **Article 74**) that a **question of law or fact has arisen, or is likely to arise**, which is of such a nature and of such **public importance** that it is expedient to obtain the opinion of the Supreme Court upon it, he may refer the question to that Court for consideration, and the Court **"may, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report to the President its opinion thereon."** Because the verb used is **"may,"** the Supreme Court is **NOT constitutionally bound** to answer a reference under **Article 143(1)**; it can decline to tender an opinion if the question is purely political, speculative, vague, incapable of judicial determination, or an attempt to ask the Court to sit in appeal over its own prior binding judgment (*In Re: Special Courts Bill*, **1978**; *In Re: Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal*, **1991**; and *Dr. M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India*, **1994**, where the Court declined to answer the single-point Ayodhya Presidential Reference under **Article 143(1)** on whether a Hindu religious structure pre-existed the Babri Masjid).
    2.  **Article 143(2) (Pre-Constitutional Treaties and Covenants Excluded from Article 131):** Notwithstanding anything in the **Proviso to Article 131**, the President may refer a dispute of the kind mentioned in the said Proviso (i.e., disputes arising out of pre-1950 treaties, agreements, covenants, engagements, or *sanads*) to the Supreme Court for opinion, and the Supreme Court **"shall, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report to the President its opinion thereon."** Because the word used is **"shall,"** the Supreme Court is **constitutionally obligated** to render its opinion under **Article 143(2)** and cannot decline the reference.
*   **Jurisprudential Status, Precedential Value, and Non-Appellate Constraint:** Technically, an opinion tendered under **Article 143** is not a "judgment, decree, or order" enforceable by execution against parties, nor does it bind the President to act in a specific policy direction. However, as authoritative exposition of constitutional law by a **5-, 7-, or 9-Judge Constitution Bench**, the legal propositions declared in an **Article 143** opinion carry immense persuasive and precedential weight across all High Courts and subordinate courts in India (*In Re: Special Courts Bill*, **1978**). Crucially, in **In Re: Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1991)** and **In Re: Natural Resources Allocation (2G Presidential Reference, 2012)**, the Supreme Court settled that the Executive cannot utilize **Article 143(1)** as an appellate mechanism to seek a review or overruling of an existing binding decision of the Supreme Court between *inter partes* litigants, though the Court in a Reference may clarify or overrule general propositions of law for future application without disturbing the *res judicata* of the decided lis. Historically, landmark constitutional architecture has been forged via **Article 143(1)** references, including *In Re: The Delhi Laws Act* (**1951** on delegated legislation limits), *In Re: The Kerala Education Bill* (**1958** on **Article 30** minority rights and harmonious construction of Part III and Part IV), *In Re: The Berubari Union* (**1960** requiring an **Article 368** amendment for territorial cession), *In Re: Keshav Singh* (**1965** on **Article 194** legislative privileges vs. **Article 21** and judicial review), *In Re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998* (**Third Judges Case** expanding the Collegium), and *In Re: Natural Resources Allocation* (**2012** holding that auction is not the sole constitutional mandate under **Article 14** for alienating natural resources when optimizing common good under **Article 39(b)**).

## II. Precedential Finality, Curative Remedies, Contempt Power, and Complete Justice

### 5. Court of Record and the Jurisprudence of Contempt (Articles 129, 215, and the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971)

#### Inherent Plenary Power to Punish for Contempt and Statutory Regulation

To ensure that judicial decrees are not reduced to pious declarations and that public confidence in the administration of justice is insulated from intimidation or defiance, **Article 129** constitutes the **Supreme Court** and **Article 215** constitutes every **High Court** as a **"Court of Record"** having all the powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself. Derived from British common law, the status of a **Court of Record** entails two defining legal attributes:

1.  **Evidentiary Finality and Incontestability of Records:** The acts, judgments, and judicial proceedings of the Court are enrolled for perpetual memory and testimony; their truth cannot be questioned in any proceeding, and the Court possesses inherent authority to correct errors apparent on the face of its own record (*Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra*, **1966**).
2.  **Inherent Constitutional Power to Punish for Contempt:** Unlike subordinate courts, which derive contempt protections solely from statute, the contempt jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (**Article 129**) and High Courts (**Article 215**) is an **inbuilt constitutional power** (*sui generis*). While Parliament and State Legislatures possess concurrent legislative competence under **Entry 77 of List I** (contempt of the Supreme Court) and **Entry 14 of List III** (contempt of other courts) read with **Article 142(2)** to regulate procedure and maximum punishment via the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971**, no parliamentary statute can abrogate, extinguish, or denude the inherent constitutional jurisdiction of the Supreme Court or High Courts under **Articles 129 and 215**.

*   **Civil Contempt vs. Criminal Contempt (Contempt of Courts Act, 1971):** The **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** bifurcates contempt into two operational categories and prescribes a statutory ceiling of **simple imprisonment up to 6 months** or a fine up to ₹2,000, or both (with a 1-year limitation period under Section 20):
    *   **Civil Contempt (Section 2(b)):** Wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ, or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. Its purpose is primarily remedial and coercive—to compel compliance for the benefit of the successful litigant.
    *   **Criminal Contempt (Section 2(c)):** The publication (whether by words, spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representations, or otherwise) of any matter or the doing of any other act whatsoever which: (i) **scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court**; or (ii) **prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding**; or (iii) **interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner**. Under Section 15, criminal contempt of the Supreme Court or a High Court may be taken cognizance of by the Court **on its own motion (*suo motu*)**, or on a motion made by the **Attorney General for India / Solicitor General** (for the SC) or **Advocate General** (for an HC), or by any other person with the **written consent** of the Attorney General / Advocate General. Statutory defenses include innocent publication, fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings, fair criticism of a judicial act on merits, and **Truth as a valid defense** (inserted by the **Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act, 2006** under Section 13(b), provided it is in the **public interest** and the request for invoking the defense is *bona fide*, as examined in *In Re: Prashant Bhushan*, **2020**).
*   **Supreme Court's Power to Punish for Contempt of Subordinate Courts and High Courts (*Delhi Judicial Service Association*, 1991):** In *Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat* (**1991**), where police officers assaulted and handcuffed a Chief Judicial Magistrate, the Supreme Court held that because it stands at the apex of a unified national judiciary with general appellate and supervisory control under **Articles 129, 136, and 142**, the phrase *"contempt of itself"* in **Article 129** encompasses the power to take cognizance of and punish for **contempt of subordinate courts and High Courts** throughout India whenever the stream of justice is polluted.
*   **Constitutional Limits on Punishments in Contempt Jurisdiction (*Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India*, 1998):** In a landmark 5-Judge Constitution Bench ruling in **Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998)** (overruling its earlier decision in *In Re: Vinay Chandra Mishra*, **1995**), the Supreme Court drew a sharp constitutional boundary between punishing a contemnor for contempt under **Article 129** and regulating professional misconduct under the **Advocates Act, 1961**. The Constitution Bench held that while the Supreme Court can sentence an advocate to imprisonment or fine, or temporarily debar a contemnor advocate from appearing before the Court until he purges his contempt, **neither Article 129 nor Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to suspend or revoke an advocate's license to practice law**, because the statutory jurisdiction to punish for professional misconduct vests exclusively in the **Bar Council of India / State Bar Councils**.

### 6. Stare Decisis (Article 141), Review Petitions (Article 137), and Curative Jurisdiction

#### Binding Precedential Authority, Ratio Decidendi, Obiter Dicta, and Bench Strength Hierarchy (Article 141)

To maintain uniformity, certainty, and predictability across a continent-sized legal order, **Article 141** commands: *"The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India."* Supported by **Article 144**—which mandates that all authorities, civil and judicial, in the territory of India shall act in aid of the Supreme Court—**Article 141** constitutionalizes the common-law doctrine of **Stare Decisis** ("to stand by things decided").

*   **"All Courts" Excludes the Supreme Court Itself (*Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar*, 1955):** In its 7-Judge Constitution Bench verdict in **Bengal Immunity Company Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1955)**, the Supreme Court established that the expression *"all courts within the territory of India"* in **Article 141** refers to all courts *other* than the Supreme Court. Consequently, the Supreme Court is **not irrevocably bound by its own previous decisions** and remains free to depart from or overrule an earlier erroneous constitutional or statutory interpretation in exceptional circumstances when convinced that its prior view was manifestly wrong and injurious to the public interest (*Keshav Mills Co. Ltd. v. CIT*, **1965**).
*   **Ratio Decidendi vs. Obiter Dicta vs. Per Incuriam and Sub Silentio:** What binds courts across India under **Article 141** is the **Ratio Decidendi**—the underlying legal principle and reasoning strictly necessary for deciding the issues before the Court—and not mere factual findings or casual observations. While **Obiter Dicta** (incidental observations not necessary to the decision) of the Supreme Court carry high persuasive weight (especially when deliberately articulated by a Constitution Bench on a point argued before it), a decision loses its binding force under **Article 141** if it is rendered **Per Incuriam** (in ignorance of a relevant constitutional provision, statute, or binding larger-bench precedent) or **Sub Silentio** (when a particular point of law involved in the decision was not perceived by the court or present to its mind, as reaffirmed in *Synthetics and Chemicals Ltd. v. State of U.P.*, **1990**).
*   **Judicial Discipline and Co-ordinate/Larger Bench Hierarchy (*Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community*, 2005):** Under the rules of **Stare Decisis** codified by the 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v. State of Maharashtra (2005)** (and *Trimurthi Fragrances v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi*, **2022**):
    1.  The law laid down by a **larger Bench** of the Supreme Court is strictly binding on any subsequent Bench of **lesser or co-equal strength** (measured solely by the total number of judges on the Bench, regardless of whether the earlier judgment was unanimous or decided by a narrow majority such as 3:2 on a 5-Judge Bench vs. a unanimous 5:0 Bench).
    2.  A Bench of **lesser quorum** cannot disagree with or dissent from the view of the law taken by a Bench of **larger quorum**; in case of doubt, a smaller Bench can only invite the attention of the **Chief Justice of India** and request that the matter be placed before a Bench of **co-equal** strength to that which delivered the earlier ruling, which in turn may refer the matter to a still larger Bench.

#### Review Jurisdiction (Article 137) and the Creation of the Curative Petition (Rupa Ashok Hurra, 2002)

In any apex judicial architecture, an inherent tension exists between two competing imperatives: *Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium* (it is in the interest of the state that there should be an end to litigation—finality) and *Actus curiae neminem gravabit* (an act of the court shall prejudice no person—fallibility/substantive justice). The Constitution balances these forces first through **Article 137** (Review) and subsequently through the judicially crafted **Curative Petition**.

*   **Review Petitions under Article 137:** **Article 137** provides that subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament or any rules made under **Article 145**, the Supreme Court shall have power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it. Under **Order XLVII of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013** (read with **Order XLVII Rule 1 of the CPC**), a **Review Petition** must be filed within **30 days** of the judgment and is maintainable **only on three narrow grounds** (it is NEVER a re-hearing or a disguised appeal, as held in *Northern India Caterers v. Lt. Governor of Delhi*, **1980** and *Kamlesh Verma v. Mayawati*, **2013**):
    1.  Discovery of **new and important matter or evidence** which, after the exercise of due diligence, was not within the petitioner's knowledge or could not be produced at the time the decree was passed;
    2.  **Mistake or error apparent on the face of the record** (an error so manifest that it does not require a long-drawn process of reasoning on points where there may conceivably be two opinions); or
    3.  **Any other sufficient reason** (interpreted *ejusdem generis* as analogous to the first two grounds).
    Procedurally, a Review Petition is circulated **in chambers without oral arguments** to the exact same Judges (or remaining Judges) who delivered the judgment, except in **death penalty cases**, where a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Mohd. Arif @ Ashfaq v. Registrar, Supreme Court of India (2014)** held that **Article 21** mandates an **open-court oral hearing** before a minimum **3-Judge Bench** for all death-sentence review petitions.
*   **The Genesis and Strict Mechanics of the Curative Petition (*Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra*, 2002):** Following the dismissal of a Review Petition under **Article 137**, litigants frequently attempted to file a writ petition under **Article 32** challenging the validity of the Supreme Court's own final judgment. In **Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously resolved this dilemma through two landmark holdings:
    1.  **Absolute Bar on Article 32 Writs Against SC Judgments:** No writ of *Certiorari* can ever be issued by the Supreme Court under **Article 32** to a High Court or to a coordinate Bench of the Supreme Court, because superior courts of record when discharging judicial functions do not fall within the definition of "the State" under **Article 12** in a manner that would permit a judicial order to be quashed as a violation of Fundamental Rights unless the order is a complete nullity passed without jurisdiction.
    2.  **Creation of the Curative Petition under Articles 129, 137, and 142:** However, to prevent gross miscarriage of justice where the Court's final order—even after the dismissal of an **Article 137 Review Petition**—suffers from fundamental procedural pathology, the Supreme Court invoked its inherent powers under **Articles 129, 137, and 142** to innovate the **Curative Petition** as an extraordinary, last-resort constitutional remedy. To prevent floodgates of endless litigation, *Rupa Ashok Hurra* subjected the **Curative Petition** to an exceptionally stringent **two-tier substantive and procedural filter**:
        *   **Substantive Grounds (Exhaustive):** The petitioner must establish either: (a) a **violation of the principles of natural justice** (specifically, that he was not a party to the lis but the judgment adversely affected his interests, or being a party, he was not served with notice of the proceedings and the matter proceeded as if he had notice); OR (b) **judicial bias / failure to disclose conflict of interest** (where a Judge on the Bench failed to disclose his connection with the subject matter or the parties, giving rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias and adversely affecting the petitioner).
        *   **Procedural Safeguards:** (i) The petitioner must specifically aver that the grounds mentioned in the Curative Petition had been **taken in the Review Petition** and that it was **dismissed by circulation**; (ii) A **Senior Advocate** must certify that the above substantive requirements of *Rupa Ashok Hurra* have been fulfilled; (iii) The Curative Petition must be circulated in chambers to a Bench comprising the **three senior-most Judges of the Supreme Court** (the CJI and the next two senior-most Judges) **PLUS the Judges who passed the impugned judgment**, if available; (iv) Only if a **majority of this combined Bench** concludes that the matter needs hearing is it listed before the same Bench (as far as possible); and (v) The Court may at any stage impose **exemplary costs** on the petitioner if his plea lacks merit and is found to be vexatious.

### 7. Plenary Equity and "Complete Justice" under Article 142: Scope and Constitutional Limits

#### Evolution from Constructive Gap-Filling (Vishaka) to Substantive Statutory Restraint (Supreme Court Bar Association, 1998)

**Article 142(1)** vests the Supreme Court with a unique, equitable plenary power unmatched in comparative constitutional charts: *"The Supreme Court in the exercise of its jurisdiction may pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it, and any decree so passed or order so made shall be enforceable throughout the territory of India in such manner as may be prescribed by or under any law made by Parliament and, until provision in that behalf is so made, in such manner as the President may by order prescribe."* Designed to liberate the apex court from procedural technicalities when fashioning remedies, **Article 142** has sits at the epicenter of the separation-of-powers dialectic between **judicial equity** and **parliamentary legislative supremacy**.

*   **Constructive Legislative Gap-Filling in the Absence of Statute (*Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*, 1997):** When a vacuum exists in domestic statutory law that leaves Fundamental Rights unprotected, the Supreme Court has deployed **Article 142** read with **Articles 32 and 141** to promulgate binding interim regulatory frameworks until Parliament enacts legislation. In **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997)**, confronting the total absence of domestic legislation to prevent sexual harassment of women at workplaces—in violation of **Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g), and 21** read with **CEDAW** (under **Article 51(c)**)—a 3-Judge Bench laid down the binding **Vishaka Guidelines**, which operated as the law of the land for 16 years until Parliament enacted the **Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013**. Similar gap-filling exercises under **Article 142** governed inter-country adoptions (*Lakshmi Kant Pandey*, **1984**), police reforms (*Prakash Singh*, **2006**), passive euthanasia living wills (*Common Cause*, **2018/2023**), and the restitution of cancelled coal blocks (*Manohar Lal Sharma*, **2014**) or the 5-acre land restitution in the Ayodhya verdict (*M. Siddiq*, **2019**).
*   **The "Supplement, Not Supplant" Doctrine (*Prem Chand Garg*, 1963 and *Supreme Court Bar Association*, 1998):** Can the Supreme Court invoke **Article 142** to pass an order that directly overrides or violates a Fundamental Right or an express substantive statutory prohibition enacted by Parliament? In **Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner, U.P. (1963)** (5-Judge Bench) and **A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak (1988)** (7-Judge Bench), the Court held that an order under **Article 142** can never contravene Fundamental Rights under Part III. Subsequently, after a 3-Judge Bench in *Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India* (**1991**) suggested that **Article 142** could override ordinary statutory prohibitions unless based on fundamental public policy, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998)** authoritatively settled the law:
    *   The power under **Article 142** is a **curative and supplementary** constitutional power; it **cannot be used to "supplant" substantive law** applicable to the case or cause under consideration.
    *   **Article 142** cannot be invoked to **build a new edifice where none existed earlier** by ignoring express statutory provisions dealing with a subject and thereby achieving indirectly what cannot be achieved directly.

#### Public Policy and Irretrievable Breakdown of Marriage (Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan, 2023)

In May **2023**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023)** conducted the most comprehensive modern restatement of **Article 142(1)**, reconciling *Supreme Court Bar Association (1998)* with the demands of substantive equity in family and constitutional law.

*   **Departure from Procedural Statutory Timelines vs. Fundamental Public Policy:** The 5-Judge Constitution Bench in *Shilpa Sailesh* held that while the Supreme Court under **Article 142(1)** will not disregard a statutory prohibition grounded in **fundamental considerations of general and specific public policy**, the Court is **not rigidly fettered by procedural statutory requirements** when strict adherence to procedure would perpetuate injustice in a particular cause. Specifically, the Bench ruled that the Supreme Court can invoke **Article 142(1)** to:
    1.  Waive the mandatory **6-to-18-month cooling-off waiting period** prescribed for divorce by mutual consent under **Section 13B(2) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955**; and
    2.  Directly grant a decree of divorce on the ground of **"irretrievable breakdown of marriage"**—even though "irretrievable breakdown" is **not yet a statutorily recognized ground** under the **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955** and even if one of the spouses opposes the divorce—after evaluating holistic factors (duration of separation, multiple pending litigations, nature of allegations, and impossibility of reconciliation), while simultaneously quashing pending criminal or civil proceedings between the spouses across different courts and high courts.
*   **Bar on Direct Article 32 Petitions for Divorce:** Crucially, *Shilpa Sailesh (2023)* clarified that a party **cannot file a direct Writ Petition under Article 32** (or Article 226) solely seeking a divorce on the ground of irretrievable breakdown of marriage; **Article 142(1)** is exercisable only in a valid cause or matter (such as a Transfer Petition or SLP) pending before the Supreme Court. Furthermore, in **High Court Bar Association, Allahabad v. State of U.P. (February 2024)**, another **5-Judge Constitution Bench** invoked the principles of **Article 142** restraint to overrule *Asian Resurfacing of Road Agency v. CBI* (**2018**), holding that the Supreme Court cannot use **Article 142** to issue a blanket judicial legislation directing that interim stay orders granted by all High Courts and trial courts across India shall automatically stand vacated after six months without judicial application of mind.

## III. Judicial Appointments, the Four Judges Cases, and the Collegium-Executive Standoff

### 8. Constitutional Text of Appointments (Articles 124(2) and 217) and the Evolution of the Four Judges Cases

#### Textual Ambiguity of "Consultation" and the First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta, 1981)

No arena of Indian constitutional law reveals the raw struggle between **executive democratic supremacy** and **judicial institutional independence** more starkly than the interpretation of **Article 124(2)** (appointment of Supreme Court Judges) and **Article 217(1)** (appointment of High Court Judges). During the Constituent Assembly Debates on May 24, 1949, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** steered a deliberate middle course between two extremes: he rejected the **British/American models** of unchecked executive appointment or partisan legislative (Senate) confirmation as unsuited to Indian conditions, yet he simultaneously rejected **K.T. Shah** and **B. Pocker Sahib's** amendments requiring the **mandatory "concurrence"** of the Chief Justice of India, warning that giving the CJI an absolute individual veto—even though an eminent person, still subject to human failings and prejudices—would be a *"dangerous proposition"* transferring sovereign power without checks.

*   **The Textual Mandate of Articles 124(2) and 217(1):**
    *   Under **Article 124(2)**, every Judge of the Supreme Court shall be appointed by the **President** by warrant under his hand and seal **"after consultation with such of the Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts in the States as the President may deem necessary for the purpose,"** provided that in the case of appointment of a **Judge other than the Chief Justice**, the **Chief Justice of India shall always be consulted**.
    *   Under **Article 217(1)**, every Judge of a High Court shall be appointed by the **President** by warrant under his hand and seal **"after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State, and, in the case of appointment of a Judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of the High court."**
*   **Executive Primacy and Supersessions in the 1970s:** From 1950 to 1973, a constitutional convention prevailed whereby the senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court was appointed **CJI**, and appointments generally proceeded by consensus between the Executive and the CJI. This consensus shattered on April 25, **1973**—one day after the **13-Judge Kesavananda Bharati verdict**—when the Union Executive superseded the three senior-most Judges in the majority (**Justices J.M. Shelat, K.S. Hegde, and A.N. Grover**) to appoint **Justice A.N. Ray** as CJI, followed in January **1977** by the supersession of **Justice H.R. Khanna** (consequent upon his solitary dissent in *ADM Jabalpur*, **1976**) in favor of **Justice M.H. Beg**, alongside the mass punitive transfer of 16 High Court Judges during the Internal Emergency (**Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth, 1977**).
*   **The First Judges Case (*S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981 — 7-Judge Bench, 4:3 Majority):** Challenging a Law Ministry circular seeking consent from Additional High Court Judges for out-of-state transfers and the non-extension of certain Additional Judges, petitions came before a **7-Judge Constitution Bench** in **S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)**. While expanding *locus standi* for Public Interest Litigation and affirming the citizen's right to information under **Article 19(1)(a)** to inspect correspondence between the Law Minister, the High Court Chief Justice, and the CJI, the **4:3 majority** (led by Justice P.N. Bhagwati) ruled on appointments that:
    1.  The word **"consultation"** in **Articles 124(2) and 217(1)** means full and effective consultation, **NOT "concurrence."**
    2.  Under **Article 217(1)**, the three constitutional functionaries—the **Chief Justice of India**, the **Governor of the State**, and the **Chief Justice of the High Court**—stand on a **coordinate and equal footing**, and the CJI enjoys **no primacy** over the other consultees.
    3.  Because the **President** acts on the aid and advice of the democratically accountable **Council of Ministers** under **Article 74(1)**, **ultimate primacy and the final decision** in appointing and transferring judges vest in the **Union Executive**, which may override the CJI's opinion for cogent reasons.

#### Judicial Primacy and the Birth of the Collegium: Second Judges Case (SCARA, 1993) and Third Judges Case (1998)

Twelve years after *S.P. Gupta*, recognizing that unbridled executive primacy under **Article 74(1)** compromised the structural insulation of the judiciary, a **9-Judge Constitution Bench** was convened in **Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)** (**Second Judges Case**) to reconsider *S.P. Gupta*.

*   **The Second Judges Case (*SCARA v. Union of India*, 1993 — 9-Judge Bench, 7:2 Majority):** Authored by **Justice J.S. Verma**, the **7:2 majority** **overruled *S.P. Gupta (1981)*** on the point of executive primacy and purposively re-interpreted **Articles 124(2) and 217(1)** in light of **Article 50** (directive principle mandating the separation of the judiciary from the executive) and the **Basic Structure Doctrine** (judicial independence):
    1.  **"Consultation" Means "Concurrence" of the Institutional Judiciary:** In the context of judicial appointments, where the Executive is itself the largest litigant before the courts, the constitutional purpose of consulting the **CJI** is to eliminate political patronage and secure the best-qualified candidates. Therefore, the opinion of the **Chief Justice of India has primacy**, and **"consultation" with the CJI functionally means "concurrence."**
    2.  **Plurality / Collegium Requirement (CJI + 2 Senior-Most Judges):** To address Dr. Ambedkar's fear in the Constituent Assembly of vesting an absolute veto in a *single individual*, Justice Verma held that the CJI's opinion is not his personal individual view, but the **collective institutional opinion of the judiciary** formed via a **Collegium** consisting of the **Chief Justice of India and the two senior-most Judges of the Supreme Court** (and for High Courts, the High Court Chief Justice plus the two senior-most Judges of that High Court).
    3.  **Initiation and Seniority Convention:** No appointment of any Judge to the Supreme Court or any High Court can be made unless it is **in conformity with the opinion of the CJI**. Furthermore, the **initiation** of every proposal for appointment must originate from the **CJI** (for the SC) or the **High Court Chief Justice** (for an HC), and appointment to the office of the **Chief Justice of India** must strictly follow the **rule of seniority** (the senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court considered fit to hold the office).
*   **The Third Judges Case (*In Re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998* — 9-Judge Bench, Unanimous):** In 1998, friction arose between the Union Executive and then-CJI M.M. Punchhi over whether the CJI had properly consulted his senior colleagues and whether the Executive was bound when the CJI's opinion did not reflect a collective consensus. The President invoked **Article 143(1)** to refer nine specific questions to the Supreme Court. A **9-Judge Constitution Bench** (authored by Justice S.P. Bharucha) unanimously **reaffirmed and expanded the Collegium architecture** established in the *Second Judges Case*:
    1.  **Expansion of the Supreme Court Collegium to 5 Judges (CJI + 4):** For the appointment of **Supreme Court Judges**, the transfer of **High Court Chief Justices or Judges**, and the appointment of **High Court Chief Justices**, the **CJI** must consult a **Collegium of the four senior-most Puisne Judges of the Supreme Court** (making it a **5-member body: CJI + 4**). For the appointment of **High Court Judges**, the SC Collegium remains **3 members (CJI + 2 senior-most Judges)**, while the initiating High Court Collegium comprises the **HC Chief Justice + 2 senior-most HC Judges**.
    2.  **Consultation with the "Conversant Judge" and Written Records:** In addition to the Collegium, the CJI must consult the **senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court who hails from the High Court** from which the candidate is being recommended (or to which the candidate is connected); if that Judge is not part of the 5-member Collegium, his **written opinion** must be obtained and placed before the Collegium. All opinions of the Collegium members must be recorded **in writing** and transmitted to the Government of India.
    3.  **Veto Rules and Non-Binding Unilateral CJI Recommendations:** If **two or more Judges** of the 5-member Collegium express an **adverse opinion** against a candidate, the CJI should **not press** the recommendation. Crucially, if the CJI recommends a candidate **without adhering to the mandatory consultation norms and procedural discipline** of the Collegium, his recommendation is **NOT binding on the Government of India**. Furthermore, while the Executive can return a Collegium recommendation once for reconsideration on the ground of strong, verifiable material regarding antecedent or suitability, if the **5-member Collegium unanimously reiterates** the recommendation, the Executive is **constitutionally bound** to make the appointment.

#### The 99th Constitutional Amendment, NJAC Act, and the Fourth Judges Case (SCARA v. Union of India, 2015)

Critics of the Collegium system consistently pointed to its structural opacity, lack of a permanent secretariat or objective evaluation criteria, susceptibility to "kinship syndrome" and internal horse-trading, and the fact that India became the only constitutional democracy where judges appoint judges without formal executive or legislative checks. To dismantle the Collegium and restore a co-equal participatory mechanism, Parliament enacted the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** (passed unanimously by Parliament and ratified by 20 State Legislatures under the **Proviso to Article 368(2)**) alongside the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014**.

*   **Structural Architecture of the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act, 2014:**
    *   The **99th Amendment** amended **Articles 124(2), 127, 128, 217(1), 222, and 224** to replace Collegium consultation with recommendation by the **NJAC**, and inserted three new constitutional articles: **Article 124A** (composition of the NJAC), **Article 124B** (functions of the NJAC), and **Article 124C** (empowering Parliament by ordinary law to regulate the procedure of the NJAC and the manner of selection).
    *   Under **Article 124A(1)**, the **6-member NJAC** comprised: (1) the **Chief Justice of India** (Chairperson, *ex officio*); (2) **two other senior Judges of the Supreme Court** next to the CJI (*ex officio*); (3) the **Union Minister in charge of Law and Justice** (*ex officio*); and (4) **two "eminent persons"** to be nominated for a 3-year non-renewable term by a committee consisting of the **Prime Minister, the CJI, and the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha** (provided that one eminent person must belong to the SCs, STs, OBCs, Minorities, or Women).
    *   Crucially, **Section 5(2) and Section 6(6) of the NJAC Act, 2014** stipulated that the NJAC **"shall not recommend a person for appointment if any two members of the Commission do not agree for such recommendation."**
*   **The Fourth Judges Case (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India*, October 2015 — 5-Judge Bench, 4:1 Majority):** In a watershed **4:1 verdict** (**Justices J.S. Khehar, Madan B. Lokur, Kurian Joseph, and Adarsh Kumar Goel** in the majority; **Justice J. Chelameswar** dissenting), the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** struck down both the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** and the **NJAC Act, 2014** *in toto* as unconstitutional and void for violating the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**, and revived the **Collegium system** as it stood prior to the amendment:
    1.  **Judicial Primacy in Appointments as an Integral Facet of the Basic Structure:** Applying the **Width and Identity Tests** (*M. Nagaraj*, **2006**), the 4-Judge majority held that **Independence of the Judiciary** is an inviolable pillar of the **Basic Structure**, and as definitively interpreted by two 9-Judge Benches in **1993** and **1998**, **judicial primacy and institutional exclusivity in the selection of judges** is an indispensable prerequisite of judicial independence in India.
    2.  **Subversion of Judicial Primacy via the Two-Member Veto:** Because the 3 Judicial Members (CJI + 2 Judges) constituted only 50% (3 out of 6) of the NJAC, and because **any 2 members could veto a unanimous recommendation of all 3 Judicial Members** under the NJAC Act, the Union Law Minister and one politically nominated "eminent person"—or the two non-judicial "eminent persons" acting together (whose qualifications were left totally undefined by **Article 124A**)—could paralyze and override the collective wisdom of the CJI and senior-most Supreme Court Judges.
    3.  **Violation of Separation of Powers (Article 50) and Conflict of Interest:** Including the **Union Law Minister** as a voting member of the NJAC violated the **Separation of Powers** and created an institutional conflict of interest, since the Union of India is the largest litigant before the Supreme Court and High Courts, which would undermine public confidence in judicial impartiality. Furthermore, **Article 124C** unconstitutionally downgraded the appointment architecture by empowering Parliament to alter NJAC selection criteria and veto thresholds by **simple legislative majority** outside **Article 368**.
    4.  **The Solitary Dissent of Justice J. Chelameswar:** In a blistering dissent, **Justice J. Chelameswar** distinguished between the **Basic Feature** (*Independence of the Judiciary*) and the **instrumental mechanism** (*exclusive judicial primacy via the Collegium*). He held that **primacy of the judiciary in appointments is NOT the only constitutional means** to secure an independent judiciary, nor is a total exclusion of the Executive and civil society required by the **Basic Structure**. Highlighting the complete opacity, lack of accountability, and historical failures of the Collegium, Justice Chelameswar argued that checks and balances require executive and public participation so long as the Executive cannot unilaterally pack the Court. Following the main judgment, the 5-Judge Bench conducted a consequential hearing in December **2015** inviting suggestions to reform the Collegium along four parameters (**Transparency, Eligibility Criteria, Secretariat, and Complaints Mechanism**), directing the Union Government to finalize an updated **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** in consultation with the CJI.

### 9. Operational Mechanics of the Collegium, Transfers (Article 222), and Executive Pocket-Veto Friction

#### The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) Standoff, Selective Segregation, and Pocket Vetoes

Following the **2015 Fourth Judges Case**, the operational protocol for judicial appointments has remained caught in a protracted institutional tug-of-war between the **Supreme Court Collegium** and the **Union Executive** over the revision of the **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** and the timeline for notifying Collegium recommendations.

*   **The Unresolved MoP Standoff (National Security Veto vs. Institutional Primacy):** In **2016–2017**, the Union Government proposed inserting a clause in the revised **MoP** empowering the Executive to reject any candidate recommended by the Collegium on grounds of **"national security" and "public interest"** without being bound by a subsequent Collegium reiteration. The Supreme Court Collegium rejected an absolute executive veto on national security grounds (accepting only that the Government could place verifiable Intelligence Bureau (IB) / RAW inputs before the Collegium, leaving the final decision to the Collegium upon reiteration), while the Executive simultaneously resisted creating permanent, funded Collegium Secretariats outside executive control. While the Collegium took unilateral administrative steps toward transparency starting in October **2017** (and expanded during **2022–2026**) by publishing Collegium resolutions, reasons for appointments/transfers, and summaries of Government objections and IB inputs on the Supreme Court's official website, the revised **MoP** remains unfinalized due to deadlock over enforceable timelines.
*   **Executive "Pocket Vetoes" and Selective Segregation of Names:** Despite the binding constitutional mandate of the **Second and Third Judges Cases** that the Union Government **must** notify an appointment once a recommendation is **unanimously reiterated** by the Collegium, a systemic operational pathology emerged wherein the Executive neither formally returned files nor issued warrants of appointment—exercising a *de facto* **pocket veto** by sitting on recommended and even reiterated names for months or years. Even more critically, the Executive frequently engaged in **selective segregation**—splitting a single composite batch of recommendations sent by the Collegium to notify only favored names while holding back others, thereby altering the **inter-se seniority** of Judges on the Bench (which directly determines who becomes a future High Court Chief Justice or Chief Justice of India). In *Advocates Association Bengaluru v. Barun Mitra* (**2021–2024** contempt proceedings) and *Plaban Mukherjee* (**2021**), the Supreme Court repeatedly admonished the Union Government that selective segregation and indefinite sitting on reiterated names violates the law of the land under **Article 141** and subverts the **Third Judges Case**.

#### High Court Appointments (Article 217) and Punitive vs. Public Interest Transfers (Article 222)

The appointment of High Court Judges under **Article 217** and their inter-state transfer under **Article 222** involve a multi-tiered federal-judicial filtration mechanism designed to balance local knowledge against national integration and insulation from local pressures.

*   **The High Court Appointment Pipeline (Article 217):** The proposal for appointing a High Court Judge (either from the Bar quota or the Subordinate Judicial Service quota) is initiated by the **High Court Collegium** (the **Chief Justice of the High Court + 2 senior-most Judges of that High Court**) six months prior to a vacancy. The proposal is forwarded to the **Chief Minister and Governor of the State**, who transmit their views (along with state intelligence inputs) within six weeks to the **Union Minister of Law and Justice**. The Union Law Ministry compiles an **Intelligence Bureau (IB)** background report and places the entire dossier before the **Supreme Court Collegium (CJI + 2 senior-most SC Judges)**, which also obtains the written opinion of the **conversant Supreme Court Judge(s)** familiar with that High Court. If approved by the SC Collegium, the recommendation goes to the Prime Minister/President for formal warrant issuance (or, if returned once and **unanimously reiterated** by the SC Collegium, becomes mandatorily binding).
*   **Transfer of High Court Judges (Article 222):** Under **Article 222(1)**, the **President may, after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, transfer a Judge from one High Court to any other High Court** (with **Article 222(2)** providing a compensatory allowance determined by Parliament). The jurisprudence of **Article 222** evolved across three stages:
    1.  In **Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977)** (5-Judge Bench), the Court held that the **consent of the Judge being transferred is NOT required** under **Article 222**, provided the transfer is made purely in the **public interest** and **not by way of punishment**, after full and effective consultation with the CJI.
    2.  In the **Second Judges Case (1993)** and **Third Judges Case (1998)**, the Supreme Court fortified safeguards against punitive executive transfers by holding that **only the CJI can initiate a transfer proposal**, and the CJI's opinion—which is determinative—must be formed collectively by the **5-member Supreme Court Collegium (CJI + 4 senior-most Judges)** in addition to consulting the **Chief Justice of the High Court from which the Judge is being transferred**, the **Chief Justice of the High Court to which the Judge is being transferred**, and any **Supreme Court Judge(s)** conversant with the affairs of those two High Courts. While take-into-account personal representations of the Judge are considered by the 5-member Collegium, once a transfer is ordered in compliance with these consultative norms, **judicial review of a transfer order is completely excluded** except on the solitary ground that the transfer was made *without* the recommendation of the CJI and the 5-Judge Collegium.

## IV. Institutional Independence Safeguards, Internal Governance, and Judicial Accountability

### 10. Structural Safeguards of Judicial Independence and Post-Retirement Vulnerabilities

#### Constitutional Insulation: Tenure, Consolidated Fund Charge, and Jurisdictional Bar (Articles 121, 124–125, 146, 211, 217, 229)

Because the Higher Judiciary must adjudicate the legality of actions taken by the Executive (which controls the sword) and the Legislature (which controls the purse), the Constitution erects six interlocking institutional firewalls to insulate Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts from political coercion or financial reprisal.

*   **Security of Tenure and Age Determination Mechanics:** Once appointed, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts do **not** hold office during the "pleasure of the President" (unlike the Attorney General, Governors, or civil servants under **Article 310**). A Supreme Court Judge holds office until attaining the age of **65 years** (**Article 124(2)**), and a High Court Judge holds office until attaining the age of **62 years** (**Article 217(1)**, raised from 60 to 62 by the **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963**; the **114th Constitutional Amendment Bill, 2010** to align HC retirement age with SC Judges at 65 lapsed). Under **Article 124(2A)** (inserted by the **15th Amendment**), the age of a Supreme Court Judge shall be determined by such authority and in such manner as **Parliament may by law provide**, whereas under **Article 217(3)**, if any question arises as to the **age of a High Court Judge**, the question shall be decided by the **President after consultation with the Chief Justice of India**, and the decision of the President (which must conform to the CJI's advice per *Union of India v. Jyoti Prakash Mitter*, **1971**) shall be final.
*   **Fixed Service Conditions and Charge on the Consolidated Fund (Articles 112(3)(d), 125, 146(3), 202(3)(d), 221, 229(3)):** Under **Articles 125 and 221** (read with the **Second Schedule** and parliamentary statutes), the salaries, allowances, privileges, leave, and pensions of Supreme Court and High Court Judges are determined by Parliament by law and **cannot be varied to their disadvantage after their appointment** (except during a **Financial Emergency** proclaimed under **Article 360(4)(b)**). Crucially, under **Article 112(3)(d)(i) and Article 146(3)**, the salaries, allowances, and **pensions** of Supreme Court Judges and the administrative expenses of the Supreme Court are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India** (meaning they may be discussed in Parliament but are **not subject to the vote of Parliament**). At the High Court level, a vital **federal fiscal distinction** operates:
    *   Under **Article 202(3)(d)** and **Article 229(3)**, the **salaries and allowances** of High Court Judges and the administrative expenses of a High Court are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of the State**;
    *   However, under **Article 112(3)(d)(iii)**, the **pensions** of High Court Judges are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India** (not the State), because a High Court Judge may serve across multiple States via transfer under **Article 222** prior to retirement.
*   **Autonomy over Court Staff and Absolute Bar on Legislative Discussion of Judicial Conduct (Articles 121, 146, 211, 229):** Under **Article 146(1)** and **Article 229(1)**, appointments of officers and servants of the Supreme Court and High Courts are made exclusively by the **Chief Justice of India / Chief Justice of the High Court** (Free from executive interference). Most importantly, to shield judges from legislative intimidation, **Article 121** mandates that **no discussion shall take place in Parliament** with respect to the conduct of any Judge of the **Supreme Court or of a High Court** in the discharge of his duties, **except upon a motion for presenting an address to the President praying for the removal of the Judge**. Even more stringently, **Article 211** imposes an **absolute, unconditional bar on State Legislatures**: no discussion can *ever* take place in the Legislature of a State with respect to the conduct of any Judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court in the discharge of his duties (because State Legislatures have zero role in the removal of High Court or Supreme Court Judges).

#### Post-Retirement Practice Bar (Articles 124(7) and 220) vs. Executive Sinecures and Tribunal Appointments

While the Constitution strictly regulates the right of retired judges to practice as advocates before courts, a glaring structural vulnerability has emerged regarding **post-retirement executive appointments** to tribunals, commissions, and constitutional or political offices.

*   **Asymmetrical Post-Retirement Practice Bar (Article 124(7) vs. Article 220):**
    *   Under **Article 124(7)**, no person who has held office as a Judge of the **Supreme Court** shall **plead or act in any court or before any authority within the territory of India** (an absolute, total lifetime bar on legal practice before any court or tribunal in India, though rendering legal opinions in chambers or acting as an arbitrator is not barred).
    *   Under **Article 220** (as amended by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**), no person who, after the commencement of the Constitution, has held office as a **permanent Judge of a High Court** shall plead or act in any court or before any authority in India **except the Supreme Court and the other High Courts**. Thus, a retired permanent High Court Judge is barred from practicing before the High Court(s) where they served as a permanent judge and all subordinate courts/tribunals across India, **but CAN practice as an advocate in the Supreme Court and in any High Court where they never held office as a permanent Judge** (and **Article 220** does not apply at all to an Additional Judge who stepped down without being made permanent).
*   **The Post-Retirement Sinecure Controversy:** Neither **Article 124(7)** nor **Article 220** prohibits a retired Supreme Court or High Court Judge from accepting post-retirement appointments from the Union or State Executive—whether as Chairpersons of statutory tribunals (**NGT, NCLAT, NHRC, Lokpal, Law Commission**—many of which statutorily require a retired SC Judge or HC Chief Justice), Governors of States, or nominated Members of the Rajya Sabha under **Article 80(1)(a)**. As first warned by the **Law Commission's 14th Report (1958, chaired by M.C. Setalvad)**, the prospect of immediate post-retirement patronage at the hands of the Executive creates a structural incentive that can erode the perceived impartiality of judges on the verge of retirement, fueling recurring demands for either a **mandatory 2-year post-retirement cooling-off period** or raising the retirement age while equalizing pensionary benefits to full last-drawn salary.

### 11. Removal Mechanics of Higher Judiciary Judges (Articles 124(4)–(5), 218, and the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968)

#### "Proved Misbehaviour or Incapacity" and the Statutory Seven-Stage Impeachment Gauntlet

Whereas the **President of India** is impeached under **Article 61** for *"violation of the Constitution"* by a two-thirds majority of the **total membership** of each House, a Judge of the **Supreme Court** (under **Article 124(4)**) or a **High Court** (under **Article 217(1)(b) read with Article 218**) can be removed from office ** exclusively on two constitutional grounds—proved misbehaviour or incapacity**—through a hybrid judicial-parliamentary procedure governed by **Article 124(4)** and the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968** (enacted by Parliament under **Article 124(5)**).

*   **The Seven-Stage Statutory & Parliamentary Removal Process:**
    1.  **Stage 1: Initiation by Numerical Parliamentary Motion (Section 3(1), Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968):** A removal motion may be initiated in **either House of Parliament** (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha). If initiated in the **Lok Sabha**, it must be signed by not less than **100 Members** of that House; if initiated in the **Rajya Sabha**, it must be signed by not less than **50 Members** of that Council, and handed over to the **Speaker / Chairman**.
    2.  **Stage 2: Discretionary Admission by Presiding Officer:** The **Speaker / Chairman** may, after consulting such persons and examining such relevant material as he thinks fit, **either admit the motion or refuse to admit it** (unlike a No-Confidence Motion, the Presiding Officer exercises quasi-judicial screening discretion at the threshold).
    3.  **Stage 3: Constitution of the 3-Member Statutory Inquiry Committee (Section 3(2)):** If the motion is admitted, the Speaker/Chairman keeps the motion pending and constitutes a **3-member Judicial Inquiry Committee** to investigate the grounds of misbehaviour or incapacity, comprising: (a) the **Chief Justice of India or a sitting Judge of the Supreme Court**; (b) a **Chief Justice of a High Court**; and (c) a **distinguished jurist** in the opinion of the Speaker/Chairman. (If removal notices are given on the same day in both Houses, the Committee is formed jointly by the Speaker and Chairman only if the motion is admitted in both Houses).
    4.  **Stage 4: Framing of Charges, Trial-Like Investigation, and Report (Sections 3–4):** The 3-member Committee frames definite charges, communicates them to the Judge, affords the Judge a reasonable opportunity of cross-examining witnesses and presenting a defense (or orders a medical board examination in cases of physical/mental incapacity), and submits its **Report** to the Speaker/Chairman.
    5.  **Stage 5: The Binding Negative Filter of "Not Guilty" (Section 6(1)):** If the Report of the 3-member Committee contains a finding that the Judge is **not guilty** of any misbehaviour or does not suffer from any incapacity, **then no further steps shall be taken in either House of Parliament** and the motion pending in the House(s) shall not be proceeded with. Parliament cannot override an exoneration by the judicial committee.
    6.  **Stage 6: Dual-Criterion Special Majority in Both Houses in the Same Session (Article 124(4)):** If the Committee reports that the Judge **is guilty** of any misbehaviour or suffers from any incapacity, the motion together with the Report is taken up for consideration by the House(s). Under **Article 124(4)**, an address for removal must be passed by **each House of Parliament** supported by a **Special Majority** (a **majority of the total membership of that House AND a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**), and the address must be **presented to the President in the SAME session** for such removal.
    7.  **Stage 7: Presidential Order of Removal:** Once the address passed by both Houses in the same session is presented to the President, the **President passes an order removing the Judge**.
*   **Jurisprudential Nuances and the In-House Inquiry Procedure (*Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability*, 1991 and *C. Ravichandran Iyer*, 1995):** In **Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability v. Union of India (1991)** (5-Judge Bench, arising out of the impeachment proceedings against **Justice V. Ramaswami**, the first SC Judge against whom an Inquiry Committee found proved misbehaviour, though the removal motion failed in the Lok Sabha in May **1993** due to the ruling Congress party's abstention), the Supreme Court held that the statutory process up to the submission of the **3-member Inquiry Committee's Report** is a **statutory and judicial process** outside the walls of Parliament that **does NOT lapse upon the dissolution of the Lok Sabha** and is subject to judicial review (as further elaborated in *Sarojini Ramaswami v. Union of India*, **1992**, holding that the Judge is entitled to a copy of the Inquiry Report before the parliamentary debate). Because the **Article 124(4)** impeachment threshold is intentionally near-impossible (no Supreme Court or High Court Judge has ever been removed via completion of all stages; **Justice Soumitra Sen** of the Calcutta HC and **Justice P.D. Dinakaran** of the Sikkim HC resigned in **2011** before Lok Sabha completion), the Supreme Court in **C. Ravichandran Iyer v. Justice A.M. Bhattacharjee (1995)** (and adopted by the Full Court in **1997/1999** alongside the *Restatement of Values of Judicial Life*) established an **In-House Peer Inquiry Procedure**: for misconduct falling short of or pending impeachment, the **CJI** can constitute a **3-Judge In-House Committee** (two HC Chief Justices and one HC Judge) to investigate allegations, withdraw judicial work from the errant Judge, and advise the Judge to resign or take voluntary retirement, failing which the CJI forwards the report to the President and Prime Minister to initiate **Article 124(4)** removal proceedings (made public in *Additional District and Sessions Judge 'X' v. Registrar General, High Court of MP*, **2014**).

### 12. Internal Administrative Prerogatives and Transparency: Master of the Roster and RTI Jurisprudence

#### The Chief Justice as "Master of the Roster" (Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India, 2018)

While on the judicial side a Chief Justice of India or a High Court Chief Justice is *primus inter pares* ("first among equals") carrying a single vote identical to any companion Judge on a Bench, on the **administrative side** the Chief Justice exercises exclusive prerogative over the constitution of Benches and the allocation of cases. Following the unprecedented January **2018** press conference by the four senior-most Puisne Judges of the Supreme Court raising concerns over selective allocation of politically sensitive cases to junior benches, a central constitutional question arose: in light of the **Second and Third Judges Cases** (which interpreted "Chief Justice of India" in **Articles 124(2) and 217(1)** to mean the **5-Judge Collegium**), should the term "Chief Justice" in the Supreme Court Rules governing roster allocation also be read as the **Collegium** rather than the individual CJI?

*   **Exclusivity of the Master of the Roster Prerogative (*Campaign for Judicial Accountability*, 2017; *Asok Pande*, 2018; *Shanti Bhushan*, 2018):** Across three successive decisions—culminating in **Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India (July 2018)**—the Supreme Court (Justices A.K. Sikri and Ashok Bhushan) decisively rejected the plea to vest roster allocation in the Collegium:
    1.  **Textual and Functional Distinction from Appointment Power:** Under **Article 145** read with the **Supreme Court Rules, 2013** (and **State of Rajasthan v. Prakash Chand, 1998**), the **Chief Justice of India is the sole and exclusive "Master of the Roster."** He alone possesses the administrative authority to constitute Benches of the Court and allocate cases to those Benches; no other Judge or Bench can take up a matter unless specifically assigned by the CJI, and any order passed by a Bench taking cognizance of a matter without the CJI's assignment is void and without jurisdiction (*Campaign for Judicial Accountability and Reforms v. Union of India*, **2017** 5-Judge Bench).
    2.  **Inapplicability of the Collegium Reading to Daily Administration:** In **Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India (2018)**, the Court held that reading "Chief Justice of India" as a multi-judge "Collegium" in the *Second and Third Judges Cases* was confined strictly to the constitutional appointment of judges under **Articles 124(2) and 217(1)** to shield the judiciary from executive interference. Replicating a 5-judge Collegium requirement for day-to-day roster allocation would be administratively unworkable, paralyze the daily listing of thousands of urgent cases, and lead to constant internal deadlocks. However, Justice Sikri emphasized that the CJI's authority as **Master of the Roster** is a high constitutional trust to be exercised with fairness, institutional objectivity, and transparency (subsequently operationalized via automated digital listing software and subject-category rosters introduced between **2019 and 2024**).

#### Transparency vs. Judicial Independence under the Right to Information Act (Subhash Chandra Agarwal, 2019)

The tension between **judicial accountability/transparency** (grounded in the citizen's right to know under **Article 19(1)(a)** and the **Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005**) and **judicial independence/privacy** (grounded in the Basic Structure and **Article 21**) reached its constitutional zenith in **Central Public Information Officer, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (November 2019)**, decided by a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** (CJI Ranjan Gogoi and Justices N.V. Ramana, D.Y. Chandrachud, Deepak Gupta, and Sanjiv Khanna).

*   **The Office of the CJI as a "Public Authority" under the RTI Act, 2005:** Upholding the landmark **2010** Delhi High Court Full Bench judgment, the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously ruled that the **Supreme Court of India** and the **Office of the Chief Justice of India** are NOT two separate legal entities; they constitute a single integrated **"Public Authority" under Section 2(h) of the Right to Information Act, 2005** (established by or under the Constitution) and are fully amenable to the RTI regime.
*   **Reconciling Judicial Independence and Transparency:** Rejecting the binary view that transparency inherently undermines judicial independence, the 5-Judge Bench declared that **judicial independence and accountability are mutually reinforcing facets of the Rule of Law**: judicial independence is not a personal privilege of judges to shield administrative secrecy, but a constitutional responsibility owed to citizens.
*   **Application of the Proportionality Test to Collegium Deliberations and Judicial Assets (Sections 8(1)(e), 8(1)(j), and 8(2)):** While bringing the CJI's office under the **RTI Act**, the Constitution Bench held that the right to information is **not absolute** and must be balanced against the **Right to Privacy (Article 21 - *K.S. Puttaswamy I*, 2017)**, fiduciary confidentiality (**Section 8(1)(e)**), and personal information exemptions (**Section 8(1)(j)**), read with the **Public Interest Override (Section 8(2) and 11(1))** via the **Four-Pronged Proportionality Doctrine**:
    1.  **Declaration of Judicial Assets:** Information regarding the **whether Supreme Court and High Court Judges have declared their assets** to the Chief Justice is **NOT exempt** as personal information under Section 8(1)(j); disclosing compliance with asset declarations serves the larger public interest without infringing privacy.
    2.  **Collegium Deliberations (Input vs. Output Distinction):** A vital constitutional distinction exists between the **final outcome** of a Collegium resolution (which is disclosable) and the **confidential deliberative inputs, third-party intelligence reports, and candid exchanges** regarding the personal integrity, character, or suitability of candidates considered (especially those rejected) by the Collegium. Disclosing raw deliberative files as a matter of routine could destroy the **"candour"** of consultees and irreparably damage the reputation and privacy of third-party candidates; therefore, whenever an RTI request seeks Collegium appointment correspondence, the **Central Public Information Officer (CPIO)** must evaluate on a **case-by-case basis** using the **Proportionality Test** whether the **larger public interest** under **Section 8(2) and Section 11(1)** outweighs the protected interest in privacy and institutional candour.
