# Part 16: Grassroots Democratic Decentralization: PRIs (73rd Amendment), ULBs (74th Amendment), PESA, and Co-operative Societies (97th Amendment)

## I. Ideological Genealogy and Structural Evolution of Local Self-Government (Article 40 to the 1992 Constitutional Watershed)

### 1. The Constituent Assembly Dialectics: Gandhian Gram Swaraj vs. Ambedkarite Constitutional Modernism and Article 40

The constitutional architecture of local self-government in India emerged from an acute ideological cleavage within the Constituent Assembly (1946–1949) between two irreconcilable visions of sovereign state-building: **Mahatma Gandhi’s decentralized communitarianism (*Gram Swaraj*)** and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s modernist liberal constitutionalism**. In the Gandhian conception, the self-sufficient, autonomous **Village Panchayat** constituted the primary cellular unit of Indian democracy, from which power would ascend in "oceanic circles" toward taluka, district, provincial, and national tiers without hierarchical subordination. Conversely, the initial draft of the Constitution prepared by Constitutional Adviser **B.N. Rau** and the **Drafting Committee** chaired by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** completely omitted village panchayats from the operative machinery of the State, anchoring the republic instead in the individual citizen and a centralized parliamentary federation capable of dismantling feudal and caste hierarchies.

When the Draft Constitution was introduced on **November 4, 1948**, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar defended the exclusion of village republics by delivering a blistering sociological critique of the traditional Indian village, characterizing it as *"a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism"* that reproduced entrenched caste tyranny against Scheduled Castes and marginalized communities. For Ambedkar and **Jawaharlal Nehru**, social revolution (land reform, abolition of untouchability, and rapid industrial planning) required a centralized state apparatus insulated from capture by dominant agrarian castes. However, confronted by intense pushback from Gandhian assembly members—including **K. Santhanam**, **Shriman Narayan**, **T. Prakasam**, **N.G. Ranga**, and **M. Ananthasayanam Ayyangar**—and following intervention by Constituent Assembly President **Dr. Rajendra Prasad**, a structural compromise was brokered on **November 22, 1948**. Moved by **K. Santhanam**, this compromise bifurcated the constitutional status of local government across two non-self-executing domains:

*   **Non-Justiciable Directive Principle (Article 40, Part IV):** Placed inside the **Directive Principles of State Policy**, **Article 40** mandated that *"The State shall take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self-government."* Because **Article 37** rendered Part IV unenforceable by any court, local self-government possessed moral-political weight without constitutional compulsion.
*   **Exclusive State List Legislative Hegemony (Entry 5 of List II, Seventh Schedule):** In the federal distribution of legislative powers, **Entry 5 of List II (State List)** vested State Legislatures with exclusive plenary competence over *"Local government, that is to say, the constitution and powers of municipal corporations, improvement trusts, district boards, mining settlement authorities and other local authorities for the purpose of local self-government or village administration."*
*   **Absence of Constitutional Entrenchment (1950–1992):** By consigning local bodies to **Article 40** and **Entry 5 of List II**, the 1950 Constitution created a strictly **two-tier federal polity** (Union and States). Local bodies existed purely as statutory creatures of State Legislatures (**ultra vires** doctrine), lacking fixed tenures, independent electoral machinery, constitutionally guaranteed fiscal transfers, or protection against arbitrary supersession by State executives.

### 2. The Pre-Constitutionalization Institutional Trajectory: From Bureaucratic Community Development (1952) to the Singhvi Constitutional Blueprint (1986)

The four-decade transition from the non-justiciable aspiration of **Article 40** to the mandatory constitutional architecture of **Parts IX and IXA (1992)** unfolded through five distinct institutional phases—commonly categorized by political scientists as the phases of **Ascendancy (1959–1964)**, **Stagnation (1965–1969)**, **Decline (1969–1977)**, **Revival Debate (1977–1986)**, and **Constitutionalization (1986–1992)**—driven by successive high-powered committees that diagnosed the failure of bureaucratic developmentalism.

*   **The Community Development Programme (CDP, 1952) and National Extension Service (NES, 1953):** Launched on **October 2, 1952**, under the First Five-Year Plan with Ford Foundation assistance, the **Community Development Programme (CDP)** established Development Blocks headed by bureaucratic Block Development Officers (BDOs) and Village Level Workers (VLWs). Because the CDP operated as a top-down technocratic apparatus without elected grassroots institutions, it failed to generate popular participation, degenerating into a subsidy-distribution bureaucracy captured by rural elites.
*   **Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (January 1957):** Appointed by the Committee on Plan Projects of the **National Development Council (NDC)** to examine the failure of the CDP (1952) and NES (1953), the **Balwant Rai Mehta Committee** submitted its report in November 1957 and coined the foundational concept of **"Democratic Decentralization"** (subsequently termed *Panchayati Raj* by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru). Its structural recommendations established the first generation of PRIs:
    *   Establishment of an organically linked **three-tier Panchayati Raj structure**: directly elected **Gram Panchayat** at the village level, indirectly elected **Panchayat Samiti** at the block level, and **Zila Parishad** at the district level.
    *   Designation of the **Panchayat Samiti (Block level)** as the primary **executive body** for developmental planning, while the **Zila Parishad (District level)** functioned strictly as an **advisory, coordinating, and supervisory body** chaired by the **District Collector**.
    *   Genuine transfer of power, responsibility, and developmental resources to these statutory bodies. The NDC accepted these recommendations in January 1958, and **Nagaur district in Rajasthan** inaugurated India's first Panchayati Raj system on **October 2, 1959**, followed immediately by **Andhra Pradesh** in November 1959.
*   **Ashok Mehta Committee (December 1977):** Following the atrophy of first-generation PRIs caused by the centralization of agricultural programs (such as the Intensive Agricultural District Programme and Green Revolution technocracy), the Janata Party government appointed the **Ashok Mehta Committee** in December 1977. Submitting its report in August 1978 with **132 recommendations**, it conceptualized ushering in **second-generation "Political Panchayats"**:
    *   Replacement of the three-tier structure with a **two-tier system**: a **Zila Parishad** at the district level (designated as the **first point of decentralization** and primary executive planning unit below the State) and a **Mandal Panchayat** below it consisting of a cluster of villages covering a population of **15,000 to 20,000**.
    *   Open, official **participation of political parties** at all levels of Panchayat elections.
    *   Compulsory **powers of taxation** for PRIs to mobilize their own financial resources, alongside social justice committees and proportional SC/ST reservation.
    *   Creation of independent **Nyaya Panchayats** presided over by a qualified judge, kept separate from developmental panchayats.
    *   Appointment of a dedicated **Minister for Panchayati Raj** in the State Council of Ministers, conducting elections within 6 months of supersession in consultation with the **Chief Election Commissioner**, and according **constitutional recognition** to PRIs (which drove successful second-generation reforms in **West Bengal**, **Karnataka**, and **Andhra Pradesh** during the 1980s).
*   **G.V.K. Rao Committee (March 1985):** Appointed by the **Planning Commission** to review administrative arrangements for rural development and poverty alleviation (CAARD), the **G.V.K. Rao Committee** famously diagnosed that developmental administration had become divorced from elected local bodies, turning the process into **"grass without roots"** due to excessive **"bureaucratization of development."** It recommended making the **Zila Parishad** the pivotal unit for democratic management of all district development programs, creating the post of a **District Development Commissioner (DDC)** to act as the CEO of the Zila Parishad (subordinating all district line departments to the Zila Parishad), divesting the **District Collector** of developmental dominance, and holding regular PRI elections.
*   **L.M. Singhvi Committee (June 1986):** Appointed by the Rajiv Gandhi government on *"Revitalisation of Panchayati Raj Institutions for Democracy and Development,"* the jurist **L.M. Singhvi Committee** provided the direct jurisprudential blueprint for the 73rd Amendment:
    *   It declared that PRIs must be constitutionally recognized, protected, and preserved by inserting a **new Chapter in the Constitution of India** that entrenched regular, free, and fair elections.
    *   It conceptualized the **Gram Sabha** as the embodiment of **direct democracy** and the foundational bedrock of decentralized governance.
    *   It recommended establishing **Panchayati Raj Judicial Tribunals** in each State to adjudicate disputes regarding elections, dissolution, and functioning of PRIs, alongside **Nyaya Panchayats** for village clusters.
*   **Thungon Committee (1988), Gadgil Committee (1988), and Legislative Enactment (1989–1992):** The Parliamentary Sub-Committee headed by **P.K. Thungon (1988)** and the Congress Policy Committee headed by **V.N. Gadgil (1988)** finalized the structural consensus for a uniform three-tier system, a **5-year fixed tenure**, SC/ST and women reservations, a **State Finance Commission**, and a **State Election Commission**. Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi introduced the **64th and 65th Constitutional Amendment Bills (1989)**, which passed the Lok Sabha but failed to secure the two-thirds special majority in the Rajya Sabha due to federal resistance from Opposition-ruled States fearing central bypass of State Governments ("District Collector to PM" centralization). After V.P. Singh’s National Front government introduced a revised bill in September 1990 that lapsed upon Lok Sabha dissolution, the **P.V. Narasimha Rao government** purged the centralist provisions, introduced the **72nd (Panchayats) and 73rd (Municipalities) Amendment Bills** in September 1991, and enacted them as the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992** in December 1992. Following ratification by more than half of the State Legislatures, received Presidential assent on **April 20, 1993**: the **73rd Amendment Act** entered into force on **April 24, 1993** (*National Panchayati Raj Day*), and the **74th Amendment Act** entered into force on **June 1, 1993**.

## II. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 (Part IX, Articles 243 to 243-O) and the Jurisprudence of Rural Local Governance

### 3. Structural Architecture of Part IX: Gram Sabha (Article 243A), Three-Tier Uniformity (Article 243B–C), and Compulsory vs. Voluntary Federal Mandates

To reconcile the centralizing impulse of national democratic uniformity with the federal autonomy of States under **Entry 5 of List II**, the **73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** (**Part IX**, **Articles 243 to 243-O**, and the **Eleventh Schedule** encompassing **29 functional subjects**) engineered a dual-track normative architecture. It divided its provisions into **Compulsory (Mandatory) Provisions**—which use the peremptory auxiliary verb *"shall"* and must be incorporated into every State Panchayati Raj Act within one year of commencement under **Article 243N**—and **Voluntary (Discretionary) Provisions**—which use the permissive phrasing *"the Legislature of a State may, by law, provide"* to accommodate regional political, demographic, and fiscal variations.

*   **Constitutional Definitions and the Gram Sabha (Articles 243 and 243A):** **Article 243(b)** defines the **Gram Sabha** as *"a body consisting of persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village comprised within the area of Panchayat at the village level,"* where a "village" (**Article 243(g)**) is specified by the **Governor** by public notification (and may include a group of villages). Thus, unlike a Gram Panchayat (which is a representative executive committee of elected ward members), the **Gram Sabha** is a permanent constitutional forum of **direct participatory democracy** comprising every registered adult voter in the village. However, under **Article 243A**, while the *existence* of the Gram Sabha is compulsory, its *powers and functions* are left to the discretion of the **State Legislature**.
*   **Uniform Three-Tier Structure and the 20-Lakh Population Exception (Article 243B):** **Article 243B(1)** mandates the constitution in every State of **Panchayats at the village, intermediate, and district levels**, establishing institutional uniformity nationwide. However, **Article 243B(2)** carves out a structural exception: a State having a population **not exceeding twenty lakhs (2 million)** may opt not to constitute Panchayats at the **intermediate level**, allowing smaller States and Union Territories to operate a two-tier system.
*   **Composition and Dual Electoral Mechanics (Article 243C):** To prevent State Governments from packing Panchayats with nominated loyalists, **Article 243C** establishes strict electoral rules distinguishing between **territorial constituency members** and **Chairpersons**:
    *   **Direct Election of All Members at All Three Tiers (Article 243C(2)):** Every single territorial constituency seat across the **Village**, **Intermediate**, and **District** Panchayats **shall be filled by persons chosen by direct election** from territorial constituencies in the Panchayat area, maintaining a uniform ratio between the population of each constituency and the number of seats allotted to it throughout the State (**Article 243C(1)**).
    *   **Asymmetrical Election of Chairpersons (Article 243C(5)):** Under **Article 243C(5)(b)**, the **Chairpersons of Panchayats at the intermediate and district levels** *shall* be elected **indirectly** by, and from amongst, the elected members thereof. Conversely, under **Article 243C(5)(a)**, the **Chairperson of a Panchayat at the village level (Sarpanch/Pradhan/Mukhiya)** shall be elected in such manner (direct or indirect) as the **Legislature of a State may, by law, provide**.
    *   **Ex-Officio Legislative Representation and Voting Rights (Article 243C(3)–(4)):** The State Legislature *may* by law provide for the representation of Chairpersons of lower-tier Panchayats in the immediately higher tier, as well as the representation of **Members of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assembly (MPs and MLAs)** and **Members of the Rajya Sabha and State Legislative Council (MPs and MLCs)** registered as electors within the Intermediate or District Panchayat area. Under **Article 243C(4)**, all members of a Panchayat—whether directly elected or ex-officio—possess the right to vote in meetings, *except* that in elections and removal motions for the **Chairpersons of Intermediate and District Panchayats**, **only the directly elected territorial members** have the right to vote.

### 4. Affirmative Action at the Grassroots: Article 243D, Women's Representation, and the "Triple Test" Jurisprudence for OBC Reservations

Unlike the enabling clauses of **Articles 15(4) and 16(4)** in Part III or the time-bound legislative reservations under **Articles 330 and 332** in Part XVI, **Article 243D** (together with **Article 243T** for Municipalities) embeds a multi-axis, interlocking affirmative action matrix at the grassroots to dismantle the historical capture of village governance by upper-caste patriarchal elites.

*   **Proportional SC/ST Reservation in Seats and Chairpersonships (Article 243D(1) and (4)):** Under **Article 243D(1)**, seats *shall* be reserved for the **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** in every Panchayat, and the number of seats so reserved shall bear, as nearly as may be, the **same proportion to the total number of seats** to be filled by direct election in that Panchayat as the population of the SCs or STs in that Panchayat area bears to the total population of that area, and such seats may be allotted by **rotation** to different constituencies. Under the **First Proviso to Article 243D(4)**, offices of **Chairpersons** at the village or any other level shall also be reserved for SCs and STs in proportion to their **total population in the State** as a whole. (Note: Via the **83rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000**, which inserted **Article 243M(3A)**, **Arunachal Pradesh** is exempted from reserving seats for Scheduled Castes under Article 243D because its indigenous population is entirely Scheduled Tribe with zero notified SCs).
*   **Interlocking Horizontal and Vertical Reservation for Women (Article 243D(2)–(4)):** **Article 243D** effected the world's largest statutory feminization of political institutions—producing over **1.45 million elected women representatives (EWRs)** out of ~3.1 million Panchayat representatives across India—through three overlapping mandates:
    *   **Sub-Quota inside SC/ST Seats (Article 243D(2)):** Not less than **one-third (1/3rd)** of the total number of seats reserved for SCs and STs under Clause (1) *shall* be reserved for **women belonging to the SCs or STs**.
    *   **Overall Seat Quota (Article 243D(3)):** Not less than **one-third (1/3rd)** (including the number of seats reserved for SC/ST women) of the total number of seats to be filled by direct election in every Panchayat *shall* be reserved for **women**, allotted by rotation to different constituencies.
    *   **Executive Chairperson Quota (Article 243D(4)):** Not less than **one-third (1/3rd)** of the total number of offices of **Chairpersons** in the Panchayats at each level *shall* be reserved for **women**, allotted by rotation to different Panchayats at each level. Because the text specifies *"not less than one-third"* as a constitutional floor rather than a ceiling, **21 States and 2 Union Territories** (spearheaded by **Bihar in 2006**, followed by Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Kerala, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Tripura, West Bengal, Assam, Gujarat, Karnataka, Punjab, Sikkim, and Tamil Nadu) have enacted State legislation raising **women’s reservation in PRIs to 50%**.
*   **Constitutional Validity and Distinction of Grassroots Quotas (*K. Krishna Murthy v. Union of India*, 2010):** In **K. Krishna Murthy v. Union of India (2010)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by Chief Justice **K.G. Balakrishnan** unanimously upheld the constitutional validity of **Articles 243D(4), 243D(6), 243T(4), and 243T(6)** against challenges under the **Basic Structure Doctrine** (equality under **Article 14** and democracy). The Constitution Bench laid down three foundational jurisprudential principles:
    1.  **Conceptual Separation of Political vs. Educational/Employment Backwardness:** The Court held that the nature and purpose of reservations in local self-government (**Articles 243D and 243T**) are fundamentally distinct from reservations in higher education (**Article 15(4)**) and public employment (**Article 16(4)**). While Articles 15(4) and 16(4) distribute scarce state welfare benefits to remedy **social and educational backwardness**, Articles 243D and 243T are designed to remove barriers to **political participation and representation** at the grassroots. Consequently, social and educational backwardness cannot be automatically equated with political backwardness.
    2.  **Validity of Chairperson Reservations as "Posts in Themselves":** Rejecting the contention that reserving solitary Chairperson posts amounted to an unconstitutional 100% reservation of a single-post cadre, the Court ruled that all Chairperson offices across a State at a given tier constitute a unified pool, and rotating SC/ST and women reservations across that pool satisfies substantive equality under **Article 14**.
    3.  **The 50% Vertical Ceiling on Combined Reservations:** While **Article 243D(6)** permits a State Legislature to make any provision for reservation of seats or offices of Chairpersons in favor of **Backward Classes of citizens (OBCs)**, the 5-Judge Bench ruled that in non-Scheduled Areas, the aggregate vertical reservation for **SCs + STs + OBCs combined cannot exceed the 50% upper ceiling** (except in Fifth/Sixth Schedule tribal areas where ST population itself exceeds 50%).
*   **The Mandatory "Triple Test" for OBC Reservations (*Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v. State of Maharashtra*, 2021):** Building directly upon *K. Krishna Murthy (2010)*, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court led by Justice **A.M. Khanwilkar** in **Vikas Kishanrao Gawali v. State of Maharashtra (March 2021)**—and reiterated unconditionally in **Suresh Mahajan v. State of Madhya Pradesh (May 2022)**—struck down mechanical flat-rate (27%) statutory OBC reservations in local bodies and formulated the binding **"Triple Test"**. Before any State Government or State Election Commission can notify OBC reservations in PRIs or ULBs under **Article 243D(6) or Article 243T(6)**, it must sequentially satisfy three non-negotiable conditions:
    1.  **Prong 1 (Dedicated Empirical Commission):** Set up a **dedicated Commission** to conduct a contemporaneous, rigorous empirical inquiry into the nature and implications of **backwardness strictly qua local bodies** (specifically measuring political under-representation local-body-wise, rather than relying on general Mandal/Census socio-educational data) within the State.
    2.  **Prong 2 (Local-Body-Wise Proportionate Specification):** Specify the exact proportion of reservation required to be provisioned **local-body-wise** in light of the specific recommendations of the dedicated Commission, so as not to fall foul of over-breadth.
    3.  **Prong 3 (Absolute 50% Aggregate Vertical Cap):** In any case, such OBC reservation must be dynamically adjusted local-body-wise so that the **aggregate vertical reservation in favor of SCs + STs + OBCs taken together does not exceed 50%** of the total seats reserved in that specific local body. Where SC and ST proportional reservations under **Article 243D(1)** already consume 50% or more of the seats in a district/block, the permissible OBC reservation in that local body reduces to zero. Furthermore, in *Suresh Mahajan (2022)*, the Supreme Court held that if a State fails to complete the Triple Test before the expiry of the 5-year constitutional tenure under **Article 243E / 243U**, the State Election Commission **cannot delay elections**; it must immediately notify elections treating the unverified OBC seats as general (unreserved) seats.

### 5. Democratic Continuity, Electoral Disqualifications, and Institutional Autonomy: Articles 243E–243O and the Constitutional Jurisprudence of Eligibility

The pre-1992 vulnerability of local bodies stemmed from two executive pathologies: indefinite supersession without elections and partisan manipulation of local electoral machinery. **Articles 243E, 243F, 243I, 243K, and 243-O** construct an interlocking shield of democratic continuity and institutional independence, while simultaneously triggering fierce constitutional litigation over candidate eligibility criteria.

*   **Fixed Five-Year Tenure and the Remainder-Term Rule (Article 243E):** **Article 243E(1)** mandates that every Panchayat, unless sooner dissolved under any law for the time being in force, **shall continue for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting and no longer**. Under **Article 243E(3)**, an election to constitute a Panchayat **shall be completed**: (a) before the expiry of its 5-year duration, and (b) before the expiration of a period of **six months from the date of its dissolution**. Crucially, **Article 243E(4)** introduces a distinctive **Remainder-Tenure Rule** (which differs fundamentally from the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, where a mid-term dissolution triggers a fresh full 5-year term):
    *   Where the remainder of the period for which the dissolved Panchayat would have continued is **less than six months**, it shall not be necessary to hold any by-election for constituting the Panchayat for such fractional period (**Proviso to Article 243E(3)**).
    *   A Panchayat constituted upon the dissolution of a Panchayat before the expiration of its duration **shall continue only for the remainder of the period** for which the dissolved Panchayat would have continued had it not been so dissolved (**Article 243E(4)**). This neutralizes any political incentive for a newly elected State Government to dissolve opposition-controlled Panchayats in order to reset a fresh 5-year electoral clock.
*   **Minimum Age of 21 Years and the Disqualification Jurisprudence (Article 243F):** Under **Article 243F(1)**, a person shall be disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member of a Panchayat: (a) if he is so disqualified by or under any law for the time being in force for the purposes of elections to the **Legislature of the State** concerned, or (b) if he is so disqualified **by or under any law made by the Legislature of the State**. However, the **Proviso to Article 243F(1)** creates an explicit constitutional lowering of the age threshold: **no person shall be disqualified on the ground that he is less than twenty-five years of age, if he has attained the age of twenty-one years** (Thus: Minimum voting age = **18 years** under **Article 326**; Minimum contesting age for Panchayat/Municipality = **21 years** under **Articles 243F/243V**; Minimum contesting age for Lok Sabha/Vidhan Sabha = **25 years** under **Articles 84/173**). Under **Article 243F(2)**, any question of disqualification is referred for the decision of **such authority and in such manner as the Legislature of a State may, by law, provide** (unlike **Articles 103/192**, which vest the decision in the President/Governor acting on the opinion of the ECI).
*   **Formal Procedural Equality vs. Substantive Participatory Democracy (*Javed*, 2003 and *Rajbala*, 2015):** Exercising their plenary power under **Article 243F(1)(b)**, several State Legislatures (notably Haryana, Rajasthan, Odisha, Maharashtra, and Andhra Pradesh) enacted stringent socio-economic and demographic disqualifications for Panchayat candidates, triggering two major Supreme Court verdicts that revealed a deep jurisprudential tension between state developmental paternalism and substantive democratic inclusion:
    *   **Javed v. State of Haryana (2003):** A 3-Judge Bench led by Justice **R.C. Lahoti** upheld the constitutional validity of **Section 175(1)(q) of the Haryana Panchayati Raj Act, 1994**, which disqualified any person having **more than two living children** from contesting or holding office in a Panchayat. The Court rejected challenges under **Article 14** (holding that population stabilization is a legitimate state objective and candidates for local office constitute an intelligible class intended to serve as role models), **Article 21** (holding that the right to contest an election is neither a Fundamental Right nor a common-law right, but a **purely statutory right** subject to statutory conditions), and **Article 25** (holding that the personal law permission for polygamy does not make having more than two children an essential religious practice).
    *   **Rajbala v. State of Haryana (2015):** A 2-Judge Bench comprising Justices **J. Chelameswar** and **A.M. Sapre** upheld the **Haryana Panchayati Raj (Amendment) Act, 2015**, which imposed five cumulative eligibility barriers on Panchayat candidates: (1) minimum educational qualification (**Matriculation/Class 10** for general candidates; **Class 8** for women and SC male candidates; **Class 5** for SC women candidates), (2) absence of criminal charges framed for offences punishable with 10+ years imprisonment, (3) clearance of arrears owed to agricultural cooperative societies and rural banks, (4) clearance of electricity bill arrears, and (5) possession of a **functional toilet** at the candidate's residence. While the Court held that the classification between matriculates and non-matriculates bore a rational nexus to better administrative performance by Sarpanchs under **Article 14**, constitutional scholars heavily critiqued *Rajbala (2015)* for relying on formalist **intelligible differentia** rather than **substantive equality and proportionality**: the educational barrier overnight disenfranchised **over 68% of Scheduled Caste women and over 50% of all rural women in Haryana** from their right to contest grassroots elections, penalizing citizens for the State's own failure to provide universal schooling under **Articles 45 and 21A**.
*   **The State Election Commission (SEC) and *Kishan Singh Tomar* Jurisprudence (Article 243K and Article 243-O):** To insulate grassroots elections from the State Executive, **Article 243K(1)** vests the superintendence, direction, and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for, and the conduct of, all elections to the Panchayats in a single-member **State Election Commission** consisting of a **State Election Commissioner** appointed by the **Governor** (the same SEC also conducts Municipal elections under **Article 243ZA**).
    *   **High Court Judge Removal Safeguard (Proviso to Article 243K(2)):** Although the conditions of service and tenure of office of the State Election Commissioner are determined by the Governor subject to State law, the **Proviso to Article 243K(2)** establishes an ironclad constitutional tenure shield: the **State Election Commissioner shall not be removed from his office except in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of a High Court** (i.e., exclusively by an order of the **President of India** passed after an address by both Houses of Parliament supported by a special majority under **Article 217(1)(b) read with Article 124(4)** on proved misbehaviour or incapacity), and his conditions of service shall not be varied to his disadvantage after appointment.
    *   **Kishan Singh Tomar v. Municipal Corporation of the City of Ahmedabad (2006):** A **5-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by Chief Justice **K.G. Balakrishnan** held that the words *"superintendence, direction and control"* in **Article 243K** and **Article 243ZA** are **in pari materia** with **Article 324** (Election Commission of India). Consequently, the **State Election Commission enjoys identical plenary independence and status in the sphere of local body elections as the ECI enjoys for Parliamentary and Assembly elections**. State Governments are constitutionally bound to abide by SEC directions, provide requisite staff under **Article 243K(3)**, and cannot postpone elections on the pretext of incomplete delimitation or electoral roll revision except under extraordinary *force majeure* (natural calamities or breakdown of law and order).
    *   **State of Goa v. Fouziya Imtiaz Shaikh (2021):** Extending *Kishan Singh Tomar*, a 3-Judge Bench led by Justice **R.F. Nariman** struck down the Goa government's practice of giving additional charge of the office of **State Election Commissioner** to a serving **Law Secretary** of the State Government. Applying the Basic Structure principle of **institutional independence of electoral watchdogs**, the Supreme Court issued a mandatory direction across all States that **no serving bureaucrat or government officer holding an office of profit under the State can ever be appointed as a State Election Commissioner under Article 243K**; the appointee must be an wholly independent person untouched by executive subordination.
    *   **Constitutional Bar on Judicial Interference During Elections (Article 243-O):** Parallel to **Article 329(b)**, **Article 243-O** bars interference by courts in electoral matters, declaring that: (a) the validity of any law relating to the **delimitation of constituencies or the allotment of seats** made under **Article 243K** shall not be called in question in any court, and (b) no election to any Panchayat shall be called in question **except by an election petition** presented to such authority and in such manner as is provided for by or under any law made by the Legislature of a State.
*   **The State Finance Commission (SFC) and the Vertical-Horizontal Fiscal Loop (Article 243I and Article 280(3)(bb)/(c)):** Recognizing that functional devolution under **Article 243G** and taxing competence under **Article 243H** are meaningless without assured fiscal transfers, **Article 243I(1)** mandates that the **Governor of a State shall**, as soon as may be within one year from the commencement of the 73rd Amendment Act and thereafter at the expiration of **every fifth year**, constitute a **Finance Commission** to review the financial position of the Panchayats (and of the Municipalities under **Article 243Y**) and make recommendations to the Governor as to:
    1.  The distribution between the State and the Panchayats of the **net proceeds of the taxes, duties, tolls, and fees** leviable by the State (**Vertical Devolution**) and the allocation between the Panchayats at all levels of their respective shares (**Horizontal Devolution**);
    2.  The determination of the **taxes, duties, tolls, and fees which may be assigned to, or appropriated by**, the Panchayats;
    3.  The **grants-in-aid** to the Panchayats from the **Consolidated Fund of the State**; and
    4.  The measures needed to **improve the financial position** of the Panchayats.
    Under **Article 243I(4)**, the Governor *shall* cause every recommendation made by the SFC together with an **explanatory memorandum as to the action taken thereon** to be laid before the **Legislature of the State**. Crucially, the **73rd and 74th Amendments simultaneously amended Article 280 of the Constitution** by inserting **Sub-clauses (bb) and (c) into Article 280(3)**, mandating that the **Union Finance Commission** shall also make recommendations to the President on *"the measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of the Panchayats and the Municipalities in the State on the basis of the recommendations made by the Finance Commission of the State."*

## III. Asymmetrical Grassroots Autonomy (PESA, 1996) and Urban Local Governance (74th Amendment, Part IXA)

### 6. The Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act (PESA), 1996: Bhuria Committee Origins and Radical Customary Self-Rule

Recognizing that the standardized representative architecture of **Part IX**—premised on individual electoral competition, formal statutory procedures, and territorial constituencies—would fracture the communal land ownership, customary dispute resolution, and egalitarian social fabric of indigenous tribal communities, the framers of the 73rd Amendment inserted **Article 243M**. Under **Article 243M(1) and (2)**, nothing in Part IX applies automatically to:
1.  **Fifth Schedule Areas** referred to in **Article 244(1)** and **Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas** referred to in **Article 244(2)**;
2.  The States of **Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Mizoram** (**Article 243M(2)(a)**, which may extend Part IX to themselves only via a special-majority resolution of their respective State Legislative Assemblies);
3.  The **Hill Areas in the State of Manipur** for which District Councils exist, and the hill areas of the **District of Darjeeling in West Bengal** regarding the functions and powers of the **Darjeeling Gorkha Hill Council** (**Article 243M(2)(b) and (3)**).

However, **Article 243M(4)(b)** armed **Parliament** with an extraordinary asymmetric legislative competence: *"Parliament may, by law, extend the provisions of this Part to the Scheduled Areas and the tribal areas referred to in clause (1) subject to such exceptions and modifications as may be specified in such law, and no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368."*

*   **The Dileep Singh Bhuria Committee (1994–1995) and Enactment of PESA, 1996:** In June 1994, the Union Government constituted a 22-member committee of MPs and experts chaired by tribal parliamentarian **Dileep Singh Bhuria** to formulate the framework for extending Part IX to Scheduled Areas. Submitting its report in January 1995, the **Bhuria Committee** argued that tribal governance must be anchored in **participatory customary democracy (*"Hamare Gaon Mein Hamara Raj"*)** rather than formal representative institutions that subordinate the village community to state bureaucracy. Accepting these recommendations, Parliament enacted **The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA, Act No. 40 of 1996)** on **December 24, 1996**, extending Part IX with radical structural modifications across the **10 Fifth Schedule States**: **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Rajasthan**.
*   **The Radical Architecture of Section 4 of PESA, 1996:** Unlike ordinary Part IX areas where the **Gram Sabha (Article 243A)** holds only such residual powers as the State Legislature chooses to grant, **Section 4 of PESA, 1996** inverts the hierarchy by making the **Gram Sabha the sovereign nucleus of local self-governance**:
    *   **Organic Definition of "Village" by Habitation/Hamlet (Section 4(b)):** In Scheduled Areas, a village is not an arbitrary revenue boundary notified by a bureaucrat; a village *shall* ordinarily consist of a **habitation or a group of habitations or a hamlet or a group of hamlets comprising a community and managing its affairs in accordance with traditions and customs**.
    *   **Plenary Cultural and Resource Competence of the Gram Sabha (Section 4(d)–(f)):** Every **Gram Sabha** shall be competent to **safeguard and preserve the traditions and customs of the people, their cultural identity, community resources, and the customary mode of dispute resolution** (**Section 4(d)**). Furthermore, the Gram Sabha is empowered to **approve the plans, programmes, and projects for social and economic development** before such plans are taken up for implementation by the Panchayat at the village level (**Section 4(e)(i)**), is solely responsible for the **identification or selection of persons as beneficiaries under poverty alleviation and other programmes** (**Section 4(e)(ii)**), and every Village Panchayat must obtain from the Gram Sabha a **certification of utilisation of funds** for all such plans (**Section 4(f)**).
    *   **Guaranteed Scheduled Tribe Political Dominance (Section 4(g)):** Reservation of seats in the Scheduled Areas at every Panchayat tier shall be in proportion to the population of the communities, **provided that the reservation for the Scheduled Tribes shall not be less than one-half (50%) of the total number of seats**, and **all (100%) seats of Chairpersons of Panchayats at all levels shall be reserved for the Scheduled Tribes**. Moreover, the State Government may nominate persons belonging to such Scheduled Tribes as have no representation in the Intermediate or District Panchayat, up to a maximum of one-tenth of total elected members.
    *   **Mandatory Prior Consultation on Land Acquisition and Minor Minerals (Section 4(i)–(l)):** The **Gram Sabha or the Panchayats at the appropriate level SHALL be consulted** before making the **acquisition of land** in the Scheduled Areas for development projects and before re-settling or rehabilitating persons affected by such projects (**Section 4(i)**). Similarly, the prior recommendation of the Gram Sabha or the Panchayats at the appropriate level is **mandatory** prior to the grant of **prospecting licence or mining lease for minor minerals** (**Section 4(k)**) and for the grant of concession for the exploitation of minor minerals by auction (**Section 4(l)**), while planning and management of **minor water bodies** is entrusted to Panchayats (**Section 4(j)**).
    *   **Sovereign Ownership and Regulatory Powers (Section 4(m)):** While endowing Panchayats in Scheduled Areas with authority necessary to function as institutions of self-government, a State Legislature *shall* ensure that the Panchayats at the appropriate level and the **Gram Sabha** are endowed specifically with:
        1.  The power to **enforce prohibition or to regulate or restrict the sale and consumption of any intoxicant**;
        2.  The **ownership of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)**;
        3.  The power to **prevent alienation of land** in the Scheduled Areas and to take appropriate action to **restore any unlawfully alienated land** of a Scheduled Tribe;
        4.  The power to **manage village markets** by whatever name called;
        5.  The power to **exercise control over money lending** to the Scheduled Tribes;
        6.  The power to **exercise control over institutions and functionaries in all social sectors**; and
        7.  The power to **control local plans and resources for such plans including tribal sub-plans**.
    *   **Prohibition against Usurpation by Higher Tiers (Section 4(n)–(o)):** State legislation must contain safeguards to ensure that Panchayats at the higher level do not assume the powers and authority of any Panchayat at the lower level or of the **Gram Sabha** (**Section 4(n)**), and the State Legislature shall endeavor to follow the pattern of the **Sixth Schedule** to the Constitution while designing administrative arrangements at the district level in Scheduled Areas (**Section 4(o)**).
*   **Constitutional Synthesis of PESA, FRA 2006, and Fundamental Rights (*Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. Ministry of Environment and Forests / Niyamgiri Vedanta Case*, 2013):** The transformative legal force of **PESA, 1996** was cemented by a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court led by Justice **K.S. Radhakrishnan** in **Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. Ministry of Environment and Forests (April 2013)**—universally known as the **Niyamgiri Vedanta Case**. When the State mining corporation and Sterlite/Vedanta sought stage-II forest clearance to mine bauxite across the **Niyamgiri Hills in Rayagada and Kalahandi districts of Odisha**—which the **Dongria Kondh** (a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group) revered as their living deity *Niyam Raja*—the Supreme Court read **Section 4(d) of PESA, 1996** harmoniously with **Sections 3(1)(i), 5, and 6 of the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA)**, **Article 244(1) (Fifth Schedule)**, and **Articles 21 and 25 (Right to Life, Livelihood, and Freedom of Religion/Culture)**. The Court ruled that the **Gram Sabha** is the statutory and constitutional authority to adjudicate whether proposed mining violates the religious, cultural, and community habitat rights of tribal forest dwellers. Directing that the question be placed before **12 Dongria Kondh Gram Sabhas** in the presence of a Judicial Officer of the rank of District Judge as an observer, the Supreme Court held that the Gram Sabhas' decision was binding on the Ministry of Environment and Forests. When all **12 Gram Sabhas unanimously voted against bauxite mining** in July–August 2013, the Union Government cancelled the mining clearance—operationalizing India’s first constitutional **environmental and cultural referendum** under **PESA, 1996**.

### 7. The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 (Part IXA, Articles 243P to 243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule Architecture

Enacted concurrently with the 73rd Amendment and brought into force on **June 1, 1993**, the **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** inserted **Part IXA ("The Municipalities", Articles 243P to 243ZG)** and the **Twelfth Schedule (18 functional subjects)** to constitutionalize **Urban Local Bodies (ULBs)**. While mirroring Part IX in its guarantees of a **5-year tenure (Article 243U)**, **minimum contesting age of 21 years (Article 243V)**, **SC/ST/Women/OBC reservations (Article 243T)**, **State Finance Commission review (Article 243Y)**, **State Election Commission superintendence (Article 243ZA)**, and **bar on judicial interference (Article 243ZG)**, Part IXA introduces distinct structural mechanisms calibrated to urban spatial demography and multi-tiered municipal administration.

*   **Three-Fold Typology of Municipalities and the Five Gubernatorial Criteria (Article 243Q):** Unlike rural Panchayats—which operate as a vertically stacked three-tier hierarchy over the same geographical district—urban local bodies under **Article 243Q(1)** are horizontally differentiated, self-contained **single-tier municipal authorities** classified into three types depending on the urban scale of the settlement:
    1.  A **Nagar Panchayat** (by whatever name called) for a **transitional area**, that is to say, an area in transition from a rural area to an urban area (**Article 243Q(1)(a)**);
    2.  A **Municipal Council** for a **smaller urban area** (**Article 243Q(1)(b)**); and
    3.  A **Municipal Corporation** for a **larger urban area** (**Article 243Q(1)(c)**).
    Under **Article 243Q(2)**, a "transitional area," a "smaller urban area," or a "larger urban area" is specified by the **Governor by public notification** having regard to **five mandatory statutory criteria**: (1) the **population** of the area, (2) the **density of the population** therein, (3) the **revenue generated for local administration**, (4) the **percentage of employment in non-agricultural activities**, and (5) the **economic importance** or such other factors as he may deem fit.
*   **The Industrial Township Exception (Proviso to Article 243Q(1)):** The **Proviso to Article 243Q(1)** carves out a critical corporate/industrial exception: a Municipality under Clause (1) **may not be constituted** in such urban area or part thereof as the **Governor may, having regard to the size of the area and the municipal services being provided or proposed to be provided by an industrial establishment in that area and such other factors as he may deem fit, by public notification, specify to be an Industrial Township** (e.g., Jamshedpur, Bhilai, Rourkela, or specialized IT/SEZ townships where corporate entities provide civic services directly without an elected municipal council).
*   **Composition, Nominated Experts, and Ward Committees (Articles 243R and 243S):** Under **Article 243R(1)**, all seats in a Municipality shall be filled by persons chosen by **direct election from the territorial constituencies** in the Municipal area, and for this purpose each Municipal area shall be divided into territorial constituencies to be known as **Wards**. Under **Article 243R(2)**, the **Legislature of a State may, by law, provide** for:
    *   The representation in a Municipality of **persons having special knowledge or experience in Municipal administration** (nominated experts), **provided that such persons shall NOT have the right to vote in the meetings of the Municipality** (**Proviso to Article 243R(2)(a)(i)**—a crucial distinction from nominated Rajya Sabha members, who can vote on all matters except Presidential elections);
    *   The representation of **Members of the Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assembly** representing constituencies that comprise wholly or partly the Municipal area, and **Members of the Rajya Sabha and the State Legislative Council** registered as electors within the Municipal area;
    *   The representation of the **Chairpersons of Committees** constituted under **Article 243S(5)**; and
    *   The **manner of election of the Chairperson of a Municipality** (Mayor / Municipal President).
    To bridge the vast physical and administrative distance between citizens and large Municipal Corporations, **Article 243S(1)** mandates that there **shall be constituted Wards Committees**, consisting of **one or more Wards**, within the territorial area of a Municipality having a **population of three lakhs (300,000) or more**. The composition, territorial area, and manner of filling seats in Wards Committees are determined by the State Legislature by law (**Article 243S(2)**), with the Ward Councillor representing that Ward serving as Chairperson where a Ward Committee consists of a single Ward (**Article 243S(4)**).
*   **Functional Architecture of the Twelfth Schedule (Article 243W):** Parallel to **Article 243G**, **Article 243W** enables the State Legislature by law to endow Municipalities and Committees with powers and authority across the **18 functional items of the Twelfth Schedule**: (1) Urban planning including town planning; (2) Regulation of land-use and construction of buildings; (3) Planning for economic and social development; (4) Roads and bridges; (5) Water supply for domestic, industrial, and commercial purposes; (6) Public health, sanitation conservancy, and solid waste management; (7) Fire services; (8) Urban forestry, protection of the environment, and promotion of ecological aspects; (9) Safeguarding the interests of weaker sections of society, including the handicapped and mentally retarded; (10) Slum improvement and upgradation; (11) Urban poverty alleviation; (12) Provision of urban amenities and facilities such as parks, gardens, playgrounds; (13) Promotion of cultural, educational, and aesthetic aspects; (14) Burials and burial grounds, cremations, cremation grounds, and electric crematoriums; (15) Cattle pounds and prevention of cruelty to animals; (16) Vital statistics including registration of births and deaths; (17) Public amenities including street lighting, parking lots, bus stops, and public conveniences; and (18) Regulation of slaughter houses and tanneries.

### 8. Spatial-Economic Planning Integration and the Structural Pathologies of the Third Tier: DPCs (Article 243ZD), MPCs (Article 243ZE), and the "Three Fs" Crisis

One of the most farsighted structural innovations of the **74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** was its recognition that rapid urbanization erases the artificial boundary between rural hinterland and urban agglomeration. To replace top-down bureaucratic planning with bottom-up spatial-economic integration, **Part IXA** established two mandatory constitutional planning institutions—the **District Planning Committee (DPC, Article 243ZD)** and the **Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC, Article 243ZE)**—which represent the **only constitutional bodies in Part IXA that integrate both Part IX (Panchayats) and Part IXA (Municipalities)**.

*   **The District Planning Committee (DPC — Article 243ZD):** **Article 243ZD(1)** mandates that there **shall be constituted in every State at the district level a District Planning Committee** to **consolidate the plans prepared by the Panchayats and the Municipalities in the district** and to **prepare a draft development plan for the district as a whole**.
    *   **The Four-Fifths (4/5ths) Democratic Composition Mandate (Proviso to Article 243ZD(2)):** While the State Legislature by law determines the composition of the DPC and the manner of choosing its Chairperson, the **Proviso to Article 243ZD(2)** stipulates that **not less than four-fifths (80%) of the total number of members of such Committee shall be elected by, and from amongst, the elected members of the Panchayat at the district level (Zila Parishad) and of the Municipalities in the district, in proportion to the ratio between the population of the rural areas and of the urban areas in the district**.
    *   **Planning Criteria (Article 243ZD(3)):** Every DPC must have regard to: (i) matters of **common interest between the Panchayats and the Municipalities** including **spatial planning, sharing of water and other physical and natural resources, the integrated development of infrastructure and environmental conservation**, and (ii) the extent and type of available financial or other resources. The Chairperson of the DPC forwards the development plan to the State Government (**Article 243ZD(4)**).
*   **The Metropolitan Planning Committee (MPC — Article 243ZE):** Under **Article 243P(c)**, a **"Metropolitan area"** is defined as an area having a **population of ten lakhs (1 million) or more**, comprised in one or more districts and consisting of **two or more Municipalities or Panchayats or other contiguous areas**, specified by the Governor by public notification. **Article 243ZE(1)** mandates that there **shall be constituted in every Metropolitan area a Metropolitan Planning Committee** to prepare a draft development plan for the Metropolitan area as a whole.
    *   **The Two-Thirds (2/3rds) Democratic Composition Mandate (Proviso to Article 243ZE(2)):** The **Proviso to Article 243ZE(2)** stipulates that **not less than two-thirds (66.67%) of the members of such Committee shall be elected by, and from amongst, the elected members of the Municipalities and Chairpersons of the Panchayats in the Metropolitan area in proportion to the ratio between the population of the Municipalities and of the Panchayats in that area** (allowing up to 1/3rd representation for Central and State Government agencies, urban development authorities, and infrastructure parastatals under **Article 243ZE(2)(c)**).

Despite this intricate constitutional design, the empirical functioning of PRIs and ULBs over three decades since 1993 has been crippled by three mutually reinforcing structural pathologies—diagnosed across the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC, 6th Report on *Local Governance*)**, the **Mani Shankar Aiyar Committee on Leveraging Panchayati Raj (2013)**, the **Reserve Bank of India Report on Municipal Finances (2022–2024)**, and the **Ministry of Panchayati Raj (MoPR) Devolution Index Report (2024)**:

*   **Pathology 1: The Structural Crisis of the "Three Fs" (Functions, Finances, and Functionaries):** Because **Articles 243G, 243H, 243W, and 243X** are voluntary enabling clauses, State Governments have resisted genuine devolution across all three pillars:
    *   **Functions (Incomplete Activity Mapping):** Although the **Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects)** and **Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects)** list expansive domains, most States have failed to execute granular **Activity Mapping** (unbundling each subject into specific policy, regulatory, and execution sub-activities across the three tiers). Even where subjects are formally transferred on paper, parallel state line-departments retain operational control.
    *   **Finances (Vertical Dependency and Own-Source Revenue Collapse):** According to the **RBI Reports on Municipal Finances (2022–2024)** and the **2024 MoPR Devolution Index**, total municipal revenue in India stagnates at **less than 1% of GDP** (compared to 6.0% in South Africa and 7.4% in Brazil), while rural Gram Panchayats generate **less than 5% to 8% of their total expenditure from Own Source Revenue (OSR)**, relying for over **90% to 95%** of their funds on tied Central and State grants. The **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 (GST)** further eroded ULB fiscal autonomy by subsuming local **Octroi, Entry Taxes, Local Body Tax (LBT), Entertainment Tax, and Advertisement Taxes** without providing a constitutional guarantee of direct GST revenue-sharing to the third tier. Meanwhile, property tax remains severely under-collected due to outdated cadastral valuation and political reluctance to revise floor rates.
    *   **Functionaries (Cadre Subordination to State Bureaucracy):** Local bodies lack an autonomous cadre over whom elected representatives exercise disciplinary control (**hire, evaluate, transfer, and fire**). Panchayat Secretaries, Block Development Officers (BDOs), District Collectors, and Municipal Commissioners belong to State Civil Services or the **Indian Administrative Service (IAS, Article 312)**. Particularly in Municipal Corporations, the **Commissioner System** (inherited from the 1888 Mumbai Municipal Corporation model) vests all executive and financial authority in a state-appointed IAS Municipal Commissioner, reducing the elected **Mayor** to a ceremonial presiding officer with a 1-year or 2.5-year rotational term.
*   **Pathology 2: The "Sarpanch Pati" (*Pradhan Pati* / *Mukhiya Pati*) Proxy Syndrome:** In deeply patriarchal agrarian structures, the mandatory reservation of seats and Chairperson offices for women under **Article 243D(2)–(4)** frequently encounters elite circumvention through **"Sarpanch Pati"** (or *Pradhan Pati*) capture, wherein male husbands, fathers, or dominant caste patrons run the election campaign, chair unofficial panchayat meetings, negotiate with block bureaucrats, and sign financial instruments by proxy while the elected woman Sarpanch remains a nominal signatory. In **2023–2025**, following a Supreme Court directive in a public interest petition highlighting proxy governance, the **Ministry of Panchayati Raj (MoPR)** constituted the **Sushil Kumar Lohani Advisory Committee (2023–2025)** to institutionalize statutory penalties against male relatives officiating in place of Elected Women Representatives (EWRs), alongside capacity-building under the **Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan (RGSA)** and mandatory **Mahila Sabhas** prior to Gram Sabha meetings.
*   **Pathology 3: Parastatal Bypass, SPV Corporatization, and Parallel Constituency Funds:** Rather than empowering **MPCs (Article 243ZE)** and elected ULBs under the **Twelfth Schedule**, both Union and State Executives systematically bypass the third tier through three parallel architectures:
    1.  **State Parastatal Agencies:** State-controlled **Urban Development Authorities** (e.g., DDA, MMRDA, BDA), **Water Supply and Sewerage Boards**, **Housing Boards**, and **Slum Clearance Boards** monopolize town planning, land monetization, and trunk infrastructure (**Items 1, 2, 5, 6, and 10 of the Twelfth Schedule**), answering exclusively to the State Urban Development Ministry rather than the elected Municipal Corporation.
    2.  **Smart Cities Mission Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs):** Under the **Smart Cities Mission**, urban redevelopment projects were vested in corporate **Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs)** registered under the **Companies Act, 2013** (with 50:50 equity between the State Government and the ULB), headed by a bureaucrat CEO who exercises delegated municipal powers outside the democratic oversight of the elected Municipal Council and **Wards Committees (Article 243S)**.
    3.  **Parallel Legislative Discretionary Funds (MPLADS and MLALADS):** The allocation of Rs 5 crore per annum per MP under the **Members of Parliament Local Area Development Scheme (MPLADS)** (upheld by a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in *Bhim Singh v. Union of India, 2010* under **Article 282**) and equivalent **MLALADS** schemes allows MPs and MLAs to directly fund micro-level village roads, drains, and community halls—functions constitutionally assigned to the **Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules**—inverting the hierarchy between legislators and local councillors.

## IV. Co-operative Federalism and Economic Democracy: The 97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011 (Part IXB) and the *Rajendra N. Shah* Jurisprudence

### 9. Constitutionalizing the Co-operative Movement: Article 19(1)(c), Article 43B, and the Regulatory Architecture of Part IXB (Articles 243ZH to 243ZT)

Throughout the post-independence era, India’s vast network of over **8.5 lakh co-operative societies**—spanning rural agricultural credit (PACS), dairy (Amul), sugar mills, fertilizer production (IFFCO/KRIBHCO), urban co-operative banks, and housing—suffered from the same institutional pathologies that afflicted pre-1992 Panchayats: politicized supersession of elected boards by State Registrars of Co-operative Societies, indefinite postponement of board elections, lack of professional auditing, and partisan capture by state politicians. Following the recommendations of the **Chaudhary Brahm Perkash Committee (1991)** on a Model Co-operative Act and the **Vaidyanathan Committee (2005)** on reviving rural co-operative credit structure, Parliament enacted the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011** (which received Presidential assent on **January 12, 2012**, and entered into force on **February 15, 2012**).

The **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011** operated across three distinct Parts of the Constitution simultaneously to establish economic democracy alongside the political democracy of Parts IX and IXA:

*   **Pillar 1 — Fundamental Right to Form Co-operative Societies (Article 19(1)(c), Part III):** The Amendment expanded **Article 19(1)(c)** so that all citizens shall have the right *"to form associations or unions **or co-operative societies**,"* subject to reasonable restrictions in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India or public order or morality under **Article 19(4)**. By elevating the right to form a co-operative society from a mere statutory privilege to a justiciable **Fundamental Right under Article 19(1)(c)**, the Amendment protected citizens' autonomous right of voluntary association against arbitrary administrative refusal of registration.
*   **Pillar 2 — Directive Principle of State Policy on Co-operatives (Article 43B, Part IV):** It inserted a new Gandhian-Socialistic Directive Principle, **Article 43B**, mandating that: *"The State shall endeavour to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of co-operative societies."*
*   **Pillar 3 — Uniform Regulatory Code in Part IXB ("The Co-operative Societies", Articles 243ZH to 243ZT):** It inserted **Part IXB** right after Part IXA, prescribing mandatory governance standards for all co-operative societies:
    *   **Definitions and Incorporation (Articles 243ZH and 243ZI):** Defines **"board"**, **"multi-State co-operative society"**, **"office bearer"**, and **"Registrar"** (**Article 243ZH**), and empowers the State Legislature to make provisions for the incorporation, regulation, and winding up of co-operative societies based on the principles of voluntary formation, democratic member-control, member-economic participation, and autonomous functioning (**Article 243ZI**).
    *   **Number and Term of Board Members & Co-opted Functional Experts (Article 243ZJ):**
        1.  **Maximum 21 Directors + Reservations (Article 243ZJ(1)):** The board shall consist of such number of directors as may be provided by the State Legislature, **provided that the maximum number of directors of a co-operative society shall not exceed twenty-one (21)**; and **provided further that the Legislature shall provide for the reservation of one seat for the Scheduled Castes or the Scheduled Tribes and two seats for women on the board of every co-operative society** having individuals as members from such categories.
        2.  **Fixed 5-Year Tenure (Article 243ZJ(2)):** The term of office of elected members of the board and its office bearers shall be **five years from the date of election**, and the term of office bearers shall be coterminous with the term of the board.
        3.  **Up to 2 Co-opted Expert Directors (Article 243ZJ(3)):** The board shall co-opt persons to be members of the board **having experience in the field of banking, management, finance, or specialization in any other field** relating to the objects and activities undertaken by the co-operative society; **provided that the number of such co-opted members shall not exceed two in addition to twenty-one directors**, and **such co-opted members shall NOT have the right to vote in any election of the co-operative society or be eligible to be elected as office bearers of the board** (though functional directors of the society shall also be members of the board and are excluded for the purpose of counting the total of 21 directors).
    *   **Independent Election of Board Members (Article 243ZK):** Notwithstanding anything in any State law, the election of a board shall be conducted before the expiry of the term of the board, and the superintendence, direction, and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections to a co-operative society shall vest in such an authority or body as may be provided by the Legislature (**Article 243ZK**).
    *   **Strict Limits on Supersession and Suspension of Boards (Article 243ZL):** To stop arbitrary executive takeovers, **Article 243ZL(1)** stipulates that **no board shall be superseded or kept under suspension for a period exceeding six months** (except for co-operative banks other than multi-State co-operative banks, where the maximum period of supersession is **one year**). Crucially, the **Second Proviso to Article 243ZL(1)** establishes an absolute bar on state interference in financially independent co-operatives: **the board of any such co-operative society shall NOT be superseded or kept under suspension where there is NO Government shareholding or loan or financial assistance or any guarantee by the Government**.
    *   **Mandatory Financial Audit, Annual General Body Meetings, Right to Information, and Penalties (Articles 243ZM to 243ZT):** Accounts of every co-operative society must be audited at least once in each financial year **within six months of the close of the financial year** (**Article 243ZM**); the **Annual General Body Meeting (AGM)** must be convened within **six months of the close of the financial year** (**Article 243ZN**); every member has the right to inspect books, information, and accounts (**Article 243ZO**); every society must file returns within six months of the close of the financial year (**Article 243ZP**); legislatures must define offences and penalties (**Article 243ZQ**); **Article 243ZR** applies Part IXB with modification of nomenclature to **Multi-State Co-operative Societies** (substituting "Parliament" for "State Legislature" and "Central Registrar" for "Registrar"); **Article 243ZS** applies Part IXB to Union Territories; and **Article 243ZT** provides for continuance of existing laws for one year.

### 10. Federal Legislative Competence vs. Unilateral Constitutional Amendment: The Landmark Verdict in *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021)

While the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992** were meticulously submitted by Parliament to the State Legislatures for ratification by not less than one-half of the States under the **Proviso to Article 368(2)** before being presented to the President for assent, Parliament enacted the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011** using only a special majority of both Houses under **Article 368(2)**—completely bypassing State ratification. This procedural shortcut ignited a fundamental federal collision over the **Seventh Schedule**.

Under the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution, legislative competence over corporations and co-operative societies is sharply bifurcated between the Union and the States:
*   **Entry 43 of List I (Union List):** Empowers Parliament to legislate over *"Incorporation, regulation and winding up of trading corporations, including banking, insurance and financial corporations, **but not including co-operative societies**."*
*   **Entry 44 of List I (Union List):** Empowers Parliament to legislate over *"Incorporation, regulation and winding up of corporations, whether trading or not, **with objects not confined to one State**, but not including universities"* (under which Parliament regulates **Multi-State Co-operative Societies** via the **Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002**).
*   **Entry 32 of List II (State List):** Vests **State Legislatures** with **exclusive plenary legislative competence** over *"Incorporation, regulation and winding up of corporations, other than those specified in List I, and universities; unincorporated trading, literary, scientific, religious and other societies and associations; **co-operative societies**."*

When a public interest litigation challenged the validity of the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011** before the **Gujarat High Court**, the High Court in April 2013 struck down **Part IXB** for violating the federal ratification requirement of the **Proviso to Article 368(2)**. On appeal by the Union Government, a **3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court** comprising Justices **R.F. Nariman**, **B.R. Gavai**, and **K.M. Joseph** delivered its landmark judgment in **Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (July 20, 2021)**, establishing four foundational principles of federal constitutional amendment jurisprudence:

*   **Ratio 1 — Substantive Curtailment of a State List Entry Triggers the Proviso to Article 368(2) Even Without Textual Amendment:** The Union Attorney General argued that because the 97th Amendment did not textually alter the words of **Entry 32 of List II** or shift "co-operative societies" from the State List to the Union or Concurrent List, it did not *"seek to make any change in... any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule"* under **Clause (b) of the Proviso to Article 368(2)**. Writing for the Court, Justice **R.F. Nariman** rejected this formalistic argument through the **"Direct Impact and Effect"** doctrine: prior to the 97th Amendment, State Legislatures enjoyed unfettered plenary discretion under **Entry 32 of List II** to decide the size of a co-operative board, its tenure, reservations, audit timelines, and supersession conditions. By inserting **Part IXB (Articles 243ZI to 243ZQ and 243ZT)**, the 97th Amendment imposed rigid, binding constitutional constraints ("shall not exceed 21 directors", "term shall be 5 years", "shall not be superseded for more than 6 months") that directly curtailed and fettered the width of State legislative power under **Entry 32 of List II**. Whenever a Constitutional Amendment significantly curtails or restricts the exclusive legislative field of the States under List II—whether directly by altering the text of the Seventh Schedule or indirectly by inserting a parallel Part—it effectuates a *"change"* in the Seventh Schedule Lists, mandatorily requiring ratification by **not less than one-half of the State Legislatures under the Proviso to Article 368(2)**. Because the 97th Amendment lacked State ratification, **Part IXB (insofar as it applies to co-operative societies operating within a single State) is ultra vires the Constitution and void**.
*   **Ratio 2 — Application of the Doctrine of Severability to Save Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Article 243ZR) and Union Territories (Article 243ZS):** While all three judges agreed that Part IXB was unconstitutional regarding State co-operative societies, the Bench divided **2:1** on the **Doctrine of Severability**:
    *   **The Majority Opinion (Justices R.F. Nariman and B.R. Gavai):** Held that **Article 19(1)(c)** (Part III) and **Article 43B** (Part IV) do not fall under the entrenched clauses of the **Proviso to Article 368(2)** and remain wholly valid. Furthermore, because **Multi-State Co-operative Societies** (whose objects are not confined to one State) fall exclusively within **Parliament’s legislative competence under Entry 44 of List I (Union List)** and **Union Territories** fall under **Article 246(4)**, requiring State ratification for **Article 243ZR** and **Article 243ZS** was unnecessary. Applying the **Doctrine of Severability**, the majority struck down **Part IXB** *only insofar as it applies to State co-operative societies*, while declaring that **Part IXB remains fully operative and valid for Multi-State Co-operative Societies under Article 243ZR and for Union Territories having no legislature under Article 243ZS**.
    *   **The Partial Dissent (Justice K.M. Joseph):** While agreeing that Part IXB violated the **Proviso to Article 368(2)** regarding State co-operatives, Justice **K.M. Joseph** dissented on severability. Pointing out that **Article 243ZR** has no independent substantive text of its own and merely states that *"the provisions of this Part shall apply to the multi-State co-operative societies subject to the modification that any reference to 'Legislature of a State'... shall be construed as a reference to 'Parliament',"*, Justice Joseph held that once the parent provisions (**Articles 243ZI to 243ZQ**) are struck down as unconstitutional for want of ratification, they are "stillborn" and cannot be resurrected as a skeleton strictly to breath life into **Article 243ZR**. However, by a **2:1 majority**, **Part IXB survived exclusively for Multi-State Co-operative Societies and UTs**.
*   **Post-2021 Statutory and Institutional Trajectory (Ministry of Cooperation & the 2023 Amendment Act):** Immediately prior to the judgment, the Union Government in **July 2021** created a dedicated **Union Ministry of Cooperation** (*"Sahakar se Samriddhi"*). Operating strictly within the constitutional perimeter delineated by the *Rajendra N. Shah (2021)* majority verdict—where **Part IXB** binds **Multi-State Co-operative Societies** under **Entry 44 of List I** while **State Co-operative Societies** remain under **Entry 32 of List II**—Parliament enacted the **Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023** to align the **Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002** with **Part IXB**, establishing: (1) a statutory **Co-operative Election Authority** to conduct free and fair elections to multi-State boards (**Article 243ZK**), (2) a **Co-operative Ombudsman** to adjudicate member grievances, (3) a **Co-operative Rehabilitation, Reconstruction and Development Fund** financed by profitable multi-State societies to revive sick societies, and (4) concurrent audit and governance standards for multi-State entities.

### 11. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Third-Tier Democratic Decentralization and Co-operative Federalism to the Constitutional Public Services and Safeguards Architecture (Part 17)

The constitutional trajectory of **Parts IX, IXA, and IXB** reveals that democratic decentralization in India is perpetually mediated by the structural tension between **constituent centralization, State List legislative hegemony (Entries 5 and 32 of List II), and executive bureaucracy**. Even where the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992** and **PESA, 1996** have entrenched direct democracy through the **Gram Sabha** and guaranteed political representation to Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Backward Classes, and Women via **Articles 243D and 243T**, the operational efficacy of the third tier remains tethered to the third pillar of the "Three Fs": **Functionaries**. At the district, block, and municipal levels, elected Zila Parishad Chairpersons, Mayors, and Sarpanchs confront a permanent executive apparatus led by the **District Collector**, **Municipal Commissioner**, and **Divisional Forest Officer**—officers recruited, trained, and insulated under the constitutional steel frame of the **All-India Services** and **State Civil Services**.

Understanding why local self-governments struggle to assert administrative control over their executive functionaries—and how the Indian Constitution simultaneously insulates permanent civil servants from political victimization while enforcing affirmative action and linguistic pluralism across the state apparatus—requires moving directly from the grassroots territorial architecture of **Parts IX–IXB** to the administrative and social backbone of the Republic in **Part 17**: **Constitutional Public Services (Part XIV, Articles 308–314)**, the **Doctrine of Pleasure (Article 310)** balanced against the **procedural due process shield of Article 311 (*Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel*, 1985)**, the federal dual-control mechanics of the **All-India Services (Article 312)**, the institutional independence of **Public Service Commissions (Articles 315–323)**, the **Constitutional Commissions for Marginalized Sections (Part XVI, Articles 338, 338A, and 338B read with the 102nd and 105th Constitutional Amendment Acts)**, and **Official Language Jurisprudence (Part XVII, Articles 343–351 and the Eighth Schedule)**.
