# Part 15: Emergency Provisions, Union Territories (Part VIII), and Asymmetrical Federalism (Fifth and Sixth Schedules, Articles 370–371J)

## I. Constitutional Emergency Architecture: Sovereign Preservation vs. Federal & Fundamental Rights (Part XVIII, Articles 352–360)

### 1. National Emergency Jurisprudence and the 44th Amendment Structural Guardrails (Articles 352–354, 358–359)

The inclusion of emergency provisions in **Part XVIII (Articles 352–360)** of the Constitution represents the ultimate structural synthesis between **Weimar-inspired crisis executive powers** (filtered through the **Government of India Act, 1935**) and liberal-democratic constitutionalism. During the **Constituent Assembly Debates**, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** characterized the Indian federation as a flexible dual polity that operates as a federal system during normalcy but possesses the intrinsic constitutional capacity to convert into a unitary apparatus when the sovereignty, territorial integrity, or constitutional machinery of the Republic is imperiled. However, the institutional pathology of the **1975–1977 Internal Emergency** exposed how unfettered executive prerogative under **Article 352**—amplified by the **38th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975** (which immunized emergency proclamations from judicial review) and the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** (which permitted localized emergencies and extended legislative tenures)—could subvert parliamentary accountability and extinguish civil liberties. The **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** fundamentally re-engineered **Part XVIII**, erecting stringent procedural, parliamentary, and substantive locks to prevent executive authoritarianism while preserving the state's defensive capacity against existential crises.

#### Taxonomic Grounds, Cabinet Procedural Locks, and Parliamentary Ratification (Article 352)

Under **Article 352(1)**, the President is empowered to proclaim a **National Emergency** if satisfied that a grave emergency exists whereby the security of India or of any part of its territory is threatened, whether by **War**, **External Aggression**, or **Armed Rebellion**. By **Explanation to Article 352(1)**, an emergency proclamation may be issued even before the actual occurrence of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion if the President is satisfied that there is an *imminent danger* thereof. The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** amended **Article 352** to empower the President to proclaim a National Emergency in respect of the whole of India or **only a part of its territory**, and permitted the variation of a proclamation by a subsequent proclamation.

*   **Substitution of "Internal Disturbance" with "Armed Rebellion" (44th Amendment, 1978):** Recognizing that the vague, elastic phrase *"internal disturbance"* had been weaponized in June 1975 to suppress political dissent, the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** substituted it with **"armed rebellion."** This textual recalibration distinguishes between an External Emergency (grounded in **War** or **External Aggression**) and an Internal Emergency (grounded exclusively in **Armed Rebellion**), confining emergency powers to armed insurrections that threaten the survival of the state rather than ordinary law-and-order breakdowns.
*   **Mandatory Written Cabinet Recommendation (Article 352(3)):** To prevent a Prime Minister from unilaterally advising the President to proclaim an emergency without consulting the Cabinet—as occurred on the night of June 25, 1975—the **44th Amendment** inserted **Article 352(3)**. It mandates that the President shall not issue a Proclamation under **Article 352(1)** or a varying proclamation unless the decision of the **Union Cabinet** (explicitly defined here—for the only time in the entire Constitution—as the Council consisting of the Prime Minister and other Ministers of Cabinet rank appointed under **Article 75**) that such a Proclamation may be issued has been communicated to the President **in writing**.
*   **Parliamentary Ratification by Special Majority within One Month (Article 352(4)):** Originally, a proclamation under **Article 352** could remain in force for **two months** without parliamentary approval and required only a simple majority. The **44th Amendment** compressed the ratification window to **one month** from the date of issue and elevated the voting threshold to a rigorous **Special Majority** under **Article 352(6)**: a majority of the **total membership** of each House AND a majority of not less than **two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting**. If the proclamation is issued when the **Lok Sabha** has been dissolved, or if dissolution takes place during the one-month period without the Lok Sabha having approved it, the proclamation survives until **30 days** from the first sitting of the newly reconstituted Lok Sabha, provided the **Rajya Sabha** has in the meantime approved it.
*   **Six-Month Periodic Renewal (Article 352(5)):** Once approved by both Houses of Parliament, the Emergency remains in force for a period of **six months** from the date of the passage of the second resolution, and can be extended indefinitely only through successive parliamentary resolutions passed every **six months** by the same **Special Majority** (replacing the pre-1978 regime where an approved emergency continued indefinitely at the pleasure of the executive).
*   **Mandatory Revocation via Lok Sabha Simple Majority (Article 352(7)–(8)):** Whereas proclaiming or continuing an emergency requires a Special Majority of *both* Houses, the **44th Amendment** deliberately lowered the threshold for **revocation**. Under **Article 352(7)**, the President *must* revoke a proclamation if the **Lok Sabha** passes a resolution by a **simple majority** disapproving its continuation. Furthermore, under **Article 352(8)**, if not less than **one-tenth (1/10th) of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha** give a written notice of intention to move a disapproval resolution (addressed to the Speaker if the House is in session, or to the President if the House is not in session), a **special sitting of the Lok Sabha** must be held within **14 days** from the date on which such notice is received.
*   **Judicial Review of Presidential Satisfaction:** While the **38th Amendment (1975)** had inserted **Article 352(5)** to declare the President's satisfaction final, conclusive, and beyond judicial scrutiny, the **44th Amendment (1978)** deleted this immunization clause. In **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench affirmed that there is no bar to judicial review of the validity of a proclamation of emergency issued by the President under **Article 352(1)**; the Court is empowered to examine whether the proclamation was issued on **mala fide**, absurd, perverse, or wholly extraneous and irrelevant grounds.

#### Legislative, Executive, and Fiscal Unitarism During National Emergency (Articles 250, 353, 354)

Upon the operation of a Proclamation of Emergency under **Article 352**, the federal distribution of powers undergoes an immediate constitutional transformation into a unitary command structure without formally abolishing State Governments or State Legislatures:

*   **Plenary Executive Directions to States (Article 353(a)):** During normalcy, the Union's executive power to issue directions to States is restricted to specific matters under **Articles 256 and 257**. Under **Article 353(a)**, the executive power of the Union extends to the giving of directions to any State as to the **manner in which the executive power thereof is to be exercised** on *any* matter whatsoever, bringing the State executive completely under the administrative command of the Union without suspending it.
*   **Concurrent Parliamentary Competence over the State List (Articles 250 and 353(b)):** Under **Article 250(1)**, Parliament acquires the power to make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India with respect to **any of the matters enumerated in the State List (List II)**. While the legislative power of a State Legislature is not suspended, it is subordinated to the overriding plenary competence of Parliament. Any law made by Parliament under **Article 250** ceases to have effect on the expiration of a period of **six months** after the Proclamation of Emergency has ceased to operate, except as respects things done or omitted to be done before the expiration of the said period (**Article 250(2)**). Furthermore, while a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, the President may issue **Ordinances** on State List subjects under **Article 123** if Parliament is not in session.
*   **Cross-Territorial Spillover (Proviso to Article 353):** Inserted by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, the Proviso to **Article 353** stipulates that if a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation *only in a part of the territory of India*, the Union's executive power to give directions and Parliament's legislative power to make laws under **Article 353** shall also extend to **any other State** (where the emergency is not in operation) if and in so far as the security of India or any part of its territory is threatened by activities in or in relation to the part of the territory in which the Proclamation is in operation.
*   **Modification of Federal Fiscal Devolution (Article 354):** While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, the President may by order direct that all or any of the constitutional provisions relating to the **distribution of revenues between the Union and the States (Articles 268 to 279)** shall have effect subject to such exceptions or modifications as the President thinks fit. Every such order must be laid before each House of Parliament and cannot extend beyond the expiration of the **financial year** in which the Proclamation ceases to operate.
*   **Extension of Lok Sabha and State Assembly Tenures (Provisos to Articles 83(2) and 172(1)):** While a National Emergency is in operation, Parliament may **by law** extend the normal five-year tenure of the **Lok Sabha** (**Article 83(2)**) and of any **State Legislative Assembly** (**Article 172(1)**) for a period not exceeding **one year at a time** and not extending in any case beyond a period of **six months** after the Proclamation has ceased to operate.

#### The Fundamental Rights Suspension Matrix: Article 358 vs. Article 359

The tension between **state security coercion** and **substantive due process** reaches its apex in **Articles 358 and 359**, which govern the curtailment of **Part III Fundamental Rights** during a National Emergency. The **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** introduced two foundational structural safeguards to ensure that the catastrophic jurisprudence of **ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976)**—in which a 4:1 majority of the Supreme Court (with **Justice H.R. Khanna** authoring a historic sole dissent) held that even the right to life and personal liberty under **Article 21** stood extinguished during an emergency, barring *habeas corpus* petitions against unlawful detention—could never recur. In **Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)**, a **9-Judge Constitution Bench** formally and explicitly overruled **ADM Jabalpur**, holding that the right to life and personal liberty are primordial natural rights that pre-exist the Constitution and were merely recognized, not created, by **Article 21**.

*   **Article 358 (Automatic Suspension of Article 19 during External Emergency Only):** Under **Article 358(1)**, when a Proclamation of Emergency is issued on the grounds of **War** or **External Aggression**, the six democratic freedoms guaranteed under **Article 19** are **automatically suspended** across the whole of India or the specific part where the emergency operates, without requiring a separate Presidential Order. The **44th Amendment (1978)** imposed two vital restrictions on **Article 358**:
    1.  **Exclusion of "Armed Rebellion":** **Article 19** can **never** be suspended under **Article 358** if the National Emergency is proclaimed on the ground of **Armed Rebellion**.
    2.  **Mandatory Statutory Recital Requirement (Article 358(2)):** Only those laws which contain an **express recital** that such law is in relation to the Proclamation of Emergency in operation—and only executive actions taken under such a recited law—are protected from challenge under **Article 19**. Ordinary laws lacking this nexus recital remain fully vulnerable to judicial invalidation under **Article 19**. Once the emergency ceases, **Article 19** revives automatically, though legislative/executive actions validly taken during the emergency remain immunized from post-emergency liability.
*   **Article 359 (Targeted Suspension of Enforcement of Specified Rights, Excluding Articles 20 and 21):** Unlike **Article 358**, **Article 359** does not automatically suspend any Fundamental Right itself; rather, it empowers the President to issue a specific **Presidential Order** suspending the **right to move any court for the enforcement** of such Fundamental Rights as may be explicitly named in that Order. **Article 359** operates during both External Emergencies (War/External Aggression) and Internal Emergencies (Armed Rebellion), and every Presidential Order under it must be laid before each House of Parliament. The **44th Amendment (1978)** inscribed two unbreachable constitutional walls into **Article 359**:
    1.  **Absolute Non-Derogability of Articles 20 and 21:** The President is constitutionally stripped of the power to suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of the rights conferred by **Article 20** (protection in respect of conviction for offences) and **Article 21** (protection of life and personal liberty). Even at the height of wartime or armed rebellion, the writ of *habeas corpus* and substantive due process under **Articles 20 and 21** remain fully enforceable.
    2.  **Mandatory Recital Requirement (Article 359(1B)):** Mirroring **Article 358(2)**, only laws containing an express recital that they are in relation to the Emergency—and executive actions taken thereunder—enjoy immunity from judicial challenge under the Fundamental Rights specified in the Presidential Order.

### 2. Failure of Constitutional Machinery in States: Articles 356, 365, and the S.R. Bommai Jurisprudence

Whereas **Article 352** addresses macroscopic existential threats to the Republic, **Article 356**—popularly termed **President's Rule** or a State/Constitutional Emergency—is the most fiercely contested site of **executive centralization vs. federal autonomy** in Indian constitutional history. Modeled on **Section 93 of the Government of India Act, 1935**, **Article 356** was envisioned by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** in the Constituent Assembly as a dormant safety valve that would ideally remain a *"dead letter"* and be invoked only as an absolute last resort. Instead, invoked over 130 times since 1950, **Article 356** was repeatedly deployed by successive Union governments to topple opposition-ruled State Ministries, until the **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)** fundamentally subordinated the provision to the **Basic Structure Doctrine** of federalism and judicial review.

#### Textual Architecture of Articles 355, 356, and 365: Duty to Protect vs. Coercive Takeover

The constitutional mechanics of President's Rule operate through an interlocked triad of articles—**Articles 355, 356, and 365**—balancing the Union's protective obligations against its coercive intervention powers:

*   **The Protective Mandate (Article 355):** **Article 355** imposes a positive constitutional duty on the Union to protect every State against **external aggression and internal disturbance** and to ensure that the government of every State is carried on **in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution**. As interpreted in **Sarbananda Sonowal v. Union of India (2005)**, **Article 355** is both the source of the Union's protective duty and the constitutional justification for intervention under **Article 356**.
*   **Dual Triggers for Proclamation (Articles 356(1) and 365):** Under **Article 356(1)**, the President may issue a Proclamation if satisfied—either **"on receipt of a report from the Governor of a State or otherwise"**—that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State **cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution**. This satisfaction is structurally reinforced by **Article 365**, which stipulates that whenever any State has failed to comply with, or to give effect to, any **executive directions given by the Union** in the exercise of its executive power, it shall be lawful for the President to hold that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, thereby triggering **Article 356**.
*   **Transfer of Executive and Legislative Powers (Articles 356(1)(a)–(c) and 357):** By a Proclamation under **Article 356**, the President may:
    1.  Assume to himself all or any of the executive functions of the Government of the State and the powers vested in the Governor or any body/authority in the State *other than the State Legislature* (**Article 356(1)(a)**);
    2.  Declare that the powers of the **Legislature of the State** shall be exercisable by or under the authority of **Parliament** (**Article 356(1)(b)**). Under **Article 357(1)**, where such a declaration is made, it becomes competent for Parliament to confer on the **President** the power of the Legislature of the State to make laws, and to authorize the President to delegate such delegated power to any other authority (who passes *"President's Acts"* in consultation with a parliamentary committee of MPs from that State). Laws made during **Article 356** under **Article 357(2)** continue in force even *after* the Proclamation ceases to operate until altered, repealed, or amended by a competent State Legislature;
    3.  Take necessary incidental or consequential provisions, including suspending the operation of constitutional provisions relating to any body or authority in the State.
*   **Absolute Judicial Firewall (Proviso to Article 356(1)):** The Proviso to **Article 356(1)** explicitly prohibits the President from assuming to himself any of the powers vested in or exercisable by a **High Court**, or suspending in whole or in part the operation of any provision of the Constitution relating to **High Courts**. Thus, the State's constitutional judiciary remains completely insulated during President's Rule.
*   **Parliamentary Approval, 6-Month Intervals, and the 3-Year Cap with 1-Year Locks (Article 356(3)–(5)):** A Proclamation under **Article 356** must be approved by resolutions of **both Houses of Parliament** by a **simple majority** within **two months** from its issue (subject to the 30-day survival window if the Lok Sabha is dissolved). Once approved, it remains in force for **six months**, and can be renewed for six months at a time up to a maximum ceiling of **three years** (**Article 356(4)**). However, to curb prolonged central rule, the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** substituted **Article 356(5)**, mandating that a resolution approving the continuation of President's Rule **beyond one year** cannot be passed by Parliament unless **BOTH** of the following strict conditions are cumulatively satisfied:
    1.  A **Proclamation of National Emergency (under Article 352)** is in operation in the whole of India, or in the whole or any part of the State, at the time of the passing of such resolution; **AND**
    2.  The **Election Commission of India** certifies that the continuance in force of the Proclamation is necessary on account of difficulties in holding general elections to the Legislative Assembly of the State.

#### The 9-Judge Bench in S.R. Bommai (1994) and Restoration Jurisprudence (Rameshwar Prasad, Nabam Rebia, Harish Rawat)

In **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)**, a **9-Judge Constitution Bench** synthesized the recommendations of the **Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State Relations (1988)** into binding constitutional law, transforming **Article 356** from a political weapon into a judicially policed emergency power:

*   **Federalism and Secularism as Basic Structure:** The Bench declared that both **Federalism** and **Secularism** are integral pillars of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**. While **Article 356** could not be invoked merely because a different political party won a landslide victory in the Lok Sabha elections (striking down the dismissals in Karnataka, Meghalaya, and Nagaland), the Bench *upheld* the dismissal of the BJP-led State Governments in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Himachal Pradesh following the December 1992 demolition of the Babri Masjid, holding that a State Government acting against **Secularism** is failing to carry on governance in accordance with the Constitution.
*   **Substantive Judicial Review and Burden of Proof:** The President's satisfaction under **Article 356(1)** is not absolute; it is conditioned upon objective material. If a prima facie case is made out by the petitioner, the burden shifts to the Union Government to produce the underlying material before the Court to prove that relevant material existed. If the proclamation is founded on *mala fide*, perverse, or wholly extraneous material, the Court will strike it down, and the bar on inquiring into Ministerial advice under **Article 74(2)** does not prevent the Court from calling for the *material* on which that advice was based.
*   **The Floor Test Mandate:** Whenever a question arises as to whether the Council of Ministers enjoys the confidence of the Legislative Assembly, the **sole constitutional forum** to determine that majority is the **floor of the House**, not the subjective chamber assessment of the Governor at Raj Bhavan.
*   **Suspension Before Dissolution & Power of Judicial Restoration:** The President can only **suspend**—and **cannot dissolve**—the State Legislative Assembly until **both Houses of Parliament** have approved the Proclamation under **Article 356(3)**. Furthermore, even if Parliament has approved the Proclamation and the Assembly has been dissolved, if the Court subsequently holds the Proclamation unconstitutional, the Court possesses the plenary power to **restore the dismissed Council of Ministers and revive the dissolved Legislative Assembly** to its original status quo ante.
*   **Consolidation in Rameshwar Prasad (2006), Nabam Rebia (2016), and Harish Rawat (2016):**
    *   In **Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** declared the May 2005 presidential dissolution of the newly elected Bihar Legislative Assembly—executed before its very first sitting on the Governor's unverified report of alleged horse-trading to prevent a rival coalition from staking claim—to be unconstitutional and *mala fide*.
    *   In **Union of India v. Harish Rawat (2016)** (the Uttarakhand crisis) and **Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016)** (5-Judge Bench on the Arunachal Pradesh crisis), the Supreme Court actually exercised its **S.R. Bommai** restoration power in practice—ordering a court-monitored floor test in Uttarakhand, quashing the **Article 356** proclamation, and **reinstating the dismissed Chief Minister and Legislative Assembly** to office. The **Punchhi Commission (2010)** further recommended localizing emergency interventions to specific troubled districts for up to three months rather than dismissing the entire elected State Government.

### 3. Financial Emergency Mechanics and Fiscal Centralization (Article 360)

Completing the triad of **Part XVIII**, **Article 360** empowers the President to proclaim a **Financial Emergency** if satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the **financial stability or credit of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened**. While **Article 360** has **never been invoked** in independent India (even during the acute 1991 Balance of Payments crisis), its textual mechanics represent the total subordination of State fiscal autonomy to Union macroeconomic command.

#### Proclamation Thresholds, Infinite Operation, and Subordination of State Fiscal Autonomy

*   **Parliamentary Approval Without Periodic Renewal:** Like **Article 356**, a Proclamation of Financial Emergency under **Article 360(2)** must be approved by resolutions of **both Houses of Parliament** by a **simple majority** within **two months** from its issue (subject to the standard 30-day survival window if the Lok Sabha is dissolved). However, in stark contrast to both **Article 352** and **Article 356**, once a Financial Emergency is approved by Parliament, **there is no maximum duration prescribed and no requirement of repeated parliamentary approval every six months**; it continues in force **indefinitely** until revoked by the President through a subsequent proclamation (**Article 360(2)(a)**). While the **38th Amendment (1975)** had barred judicial review of the President's satisfaction under **Article 360**, the **44th Amendment (1978)** deleted that bar, restoring judicial review over *mala fide* grounds.
*   **Unitary Fiscal Command and Salary Reductions (Article 360(3)–(4)):** During the period any such Proclamation is in operation, the executive authority of the Union extends to the giving of directions to any State to observe such **canons of financial propriety** as may be specified in the directions (**Article 360(3)**). Under **Article 360(4)**, any such direction may specifically include:
    1.  A provision requiring the **reduction of salaries and allowances** of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of a **State**;
    2.  A provision requiring that **all Money Bills or other Financial Bills (Article 207)** shall be **reserved for the consideration of the President** after they are passed by the Legislature of the State;
    3.  Competence for the President to issue directions for the **reduction of salaries and allowances** of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of the **Union**, expressly including the **Judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts** (representing the sole constitutional exception to the guarantee against non-disadvantageous alteration of judicial salaries under **Articles 125 and 221**).

## II. Union Territories and the NCT of Delhi Constitutional Battleground (Part VIII, Articles 239–241)

### 4. Constitutional Taxonomy of Union Territories and Asymmetrical Governance (Articles 239, 239A, 240, 241)

While **Part VI** of the Constitution governs the full member-States of the federal Union, **Part VIII (Articles 239–241)**—consolidated by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**, which abolished the original classification of Part A, B, C, and D States—establishes the governance architecture for **Union Territories (UTs)**. Unlike full States, which enjoy an autonomous share of sovereign legislative and executive power under **Articles 162 and 246**, Union Territories are centrally administered federal enclaves carved out due to strategic geopolitical vulnerability, cultural distinctiveness, small territorial size, or status as the National Capital. As of the late-2026 empirical baseline, India comprises **8 Union Territories**, which are structurally stratified into three distinct constitutional tiers across **Articles 239, 239A, 239AA, and 240**.

#### Executive Administration, Presidential Regulation-Making Power (Article 240), and Puducherry/J&K Legislatures (Article 239A)

*   **Direct Presidential Administration via Administrators (Article 239):** Under **Article 239(1)**, save as otherwise provided by Parliament by law, every Union Territory shall be administered by the **President** acting, to such extent as he thinks fit, through an **Administrator** to be appointed by him with such designation as he may specify (designated as **Lieutenant Governor** in Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, and Andaman & Nicobar Islands, and as **Administrator** in Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, and Lakshadweep). Unlike a State Governor—who is a constitutional head distinct from the Union Executive (**Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak, 1979**)—an Administrator under **Article 239** acts directly as a delegate or agent of the President. Under **Article 239(2)**, the President may appoint the Governor of a State as the Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory, and when so acting, the Governor exercises his functions **independently of his State Council of Ministers**.
*   **Parliamentary Legislatures for Puducherry and Jammu & Kashmir (Article 239A):** Inserted by the **14th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962**, **Article 239A** empowers Parliament to create **by ordinary law** (which under **Article 239A(2)** is not deemed a Constitutional Amendment under **Article 368**) a body—whether elected or partly nominated and partly elected—to function as a **Legislature** for the Union Territory of **Puducherry**, or a **Council of Ministers**, or both. Exercising this statutory mechanism, Parliament enacted the **Government of Union Territories Act, 1963** for Puducherry, and subsequently replicated the **Article 239A** statutory model in **Section 13 of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** to create the unicameral Legislative Assembly for the **Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir**. Crucially, because a Legislature under **Article 239A** is a creature of an ordinary Parliamentary statute rather than a self-executing constitutional grant, its autonomy is structurally thinner than that of a full State.
*   **Plenary Presidential Regulation-Making Power (Article 240):** Under **Article 240(1)**, the President is vested with the plenary legislative power to make **Regulations** for the peace, progress, and good government of the Union Territories of:
    1.  **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**;
    2.  **Lakshadweep**;
    3.  **Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu** (merged via the 2019 Act);
    4.  **Puducherry** (subject to the **First Proviso to Article 240(1)**, which states that once a Legislature is created for Puducherry under **Article 239A**, the President shall **not** make any regulation for Puducherry unless the Legislature has been **dissolved or suspended**); and
    5.  **Ladakh** (incorporated via **Section 58 of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, which explicitly applied **Article 240** to the UT of Ladakh).
    Remarkably, under **Article 240(2)**, any Regulation so made by the President may **repeal or amend any Act made by Parliament** or any other law applicable to that Union Territory, and when promulgated by the President, has the **exact same force and effect as an Act of Parliament**.
*   **Ordinance Power of UT Administrators (Article 239B) and High Courts (Article 241):** Inserted by the **27th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**, **Article 239B** empowers the Administrator of Puducherry (and analogously J&K under the 2019 Reorganisation Act) to promulgate **Ordinances** during the recess of the Legislature, strictly subject to **prior instructions from the President** and prohibited whenever the Legislature is dissolved or suspended. Under **Article 241**, Parliament may by law constitute a **High Court** for a Union Territory or declare any court in such territory to be a High Court, or extend the jurisdiction of a State High Court to a UT (currently, **Delhi** and **Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh** have dedicated High Courts, while the remaining six UTs fall under adjoining State High Courts).

### 5. The National Capital Territory of Delhi: Article 239AA, the "Triple Chain of Accountability," and the NCCSA Regime

No constitutional provision in **Part VIII** has generated more intense institutional friction between representative democracy and federal centralism than **Article 239AA**, which governs the **National Capital Territory of Delhi (NCTD)**. Inserted by the **69th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1991** (alongside **Article 239AB**) on the recommendations of the **S. Balakrishnan Committee (1987)**, **Article 239AA** attempted to reconcile two competing imperatives: granting the residents of Delhi a democratically elected Legislative Assembly and responsible Council of Ministers, while preserving the Union Government's overarching control over the vital physical and sovereign infrastructure of the National Capital.

#### The 69th Amendment Architecture (Article 239AA) and the 2018 & May 2023 Constitution Bench Verdicts

*   **Structural Mechanics of Article 239AA:**
    1.  **Constitutional Entrenchment & Three Excluded Subjects (Article 239AA(3)(a)):** Unlike Puducherry's statutory assembly under **Article 239A**, the Legislative Assembly of NCTD is entrenched directly in the Constitution via a **Special Majority** amendment under **Article 368**. Under **Article 239AA(3)(a)**, the NCTD Legislative Assembly has the power to make laws for the whole or any part of the NCTD with respect to **any of the matters enumerated in the State List (List II) or in the Concurrent List (List III)**, *except* three explicitly excluded entries of the State List: **Entry 1 (Public Order)**, **Entry 2 (Police)**, and **Entry 18 (Land)** (along with entries 64, 65, and 66 of List II insofar as they relate to those three subjects).
    2.  **Plenary Parliamentary Primacy (Article 239AA(3)(b)–(c)):** Unlike full States—where Parliament cannot ordinarily legislate on the State List—**Article 239AA(3)(b)** explicitly preserves Parliament's plenary power to make laws on **any matter** (even State List subjects within Delhi's domain) for the NCTD. Under **Article 239AA(3)(c)**, if any law made by the Delhi Legislative Assembly is repugnant to a law made by Parliament (whether passed before or after the Delhi law), the **Parliamentary law prevails** unless the Delhi law was reserved for the consideration of the President and received Presidential assent (though Parliament may still subsequently amend, vary, or repeal even such an assented law).
    3.  **10% Ministerial Cap & The Proviso to Article 239AA(4):** While State Councils of Ministers are capped at **15%** of Assembly strength under **Article 164(1A)**, **Article 239AA(4)** caps the NCTD Council of Ministers at **10% of the total number of members in the Legislative Assembly** (i.e., maximum **7 Ministers**, including the Chief Minister, out of 70 MLAs). The **Proviso to Article 239AA(4)** stipulates that in the case of a **difference of opinion** between the **Lieutenant Governor (LG)** and his Ministers on *"any matter,"* the LG shall refer it to the **President** for decision and act according to the decision given thereon, while exercising power to take immediate action in urgent pending cases. Under **Article 239AB**, if the administration of the NCTD cannot be carried on in accordance with **Article 239AA**, the President may suspend its operation—serving as Delhi's specialized equivalent of **Article 356**.
*   **The First 5-Judge Constitution Bench Verdict (Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India, July 2018):** Addressing a prolonged administrative paralysis between the elected NCTD Government and the Lieutenant Governor, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** established three foundational constitutional propositions:
    1.  **NCTD as a Sui Generis Asymmetric Entity:** While NCTD is not a full-fledged State (per the 9-Judge ruling in **NDMC v. State of Punjab, 1997**), **Article 239AA** accords it a *sui generis* constitutional status situated on a higher pedestal than other UTs, rooted in **pragmatic and collaborative federalism** and representative democracy.
    2.  **Aid and Advice is Binding on All Non-Excluded Subjects:** The Lieutenant Governor is an administrative head, not a colonial Viceroy or an independent executive authority. Outside the three excluded domains (**Public Order, Police, and Land**), the executive power of the NCTD is **co-extensive with its legislative power**, and the LG is **bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers** without requiring his prior "concurrence" on every routine decision.
    3.  **Reading Down the Proviso to Article 239AA(4) ("Any Matter" != "Every Matter"):** Applying the doctrine of **Constitutional Morality** and **Constitutional Trust**, the Bench held that the phrase *"any matter"* in the Proviso to **Article 239AA(4)** cannot be mechanically construed to mean *"every matter."* The LG cannot refer routine administrative files to the President merely to obstruct the elected government; the referral power is an exceptional mechanism restricted to genuine differences that threaten national interests or constitutional compliance.
*   **The Second 5-Judge Constitution Bench Verdict (Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India, May 2023—The "Services" Ruling):** Following a split verdict by a 2-Judge Bench in 2019 on whether the NCTD Government or the Union controlled **Entry 41 of List II (State Public Services)**, a second **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously ruled in **May 2023** that the elected Government of NCTD possesses both **legislative and executive competence over "Services" (Entry 41 of List II)**, including the deployment, posting, and disciplinary control of All-India Service officers (IAS, DANICS) serving in the NCTD (excluding officers serving in the three excluded domains of Public Order, Police, and Land). Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud articulated the **"Triple Chain of Collective Accountability"** intrinsic to Westminster parliamentary democracy:
    1.  **First Link:** Permanent civil servants are accountable to the elected Cabinet Ministers;
    2.  **Second Link:** The Cabinet Ministers are collectively accountable to the elected Legislature; and
    3.  **Third Link:** The Legislature is periodically accountable to the Electorate.
    The Bench held that if an elected government has no control over the civil servants who execute its policies, the first link in the **Triple Chain of Accountability** is severed, reducing representative democracy under **Article 239AA** to a hollow shell.

#### The GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023 and the National Capital Civil Service Authority (NCCSA)

Crucially, in paragraph 95 of its **May 2023 Services Verdict**, the 5-Judge Bench noted that under **Article 239AA(3)(b)**, **Parliament retains plenary legislative competence** to enact a law on *any* subject in List II (including Entry 41, Services) for Delhi, and observed that at that time, *no Parliamentary statute existed* governing Services in NCTD. Invoking this exact constitutional opening within eight days of the judgment, the Union promulgated an Ordinance in May 2023 and subsequently enacted the **Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023** in August 2023, fundamentally altering the bureaucratic command structure of the National Capital:

*   **Creation of the National Capital Civil Service Authority (NCCSA—Section 45E):** The 2023 Amendment Act constituted a permanent statutory body—the **National Capital Civil Service Authority (NCCSA)**—empowered to make recommendations to the Lieutenant Governor regarding transfers, postings, vigilance, disciplinary proceedings, and prosecution sanctions of all **Group 'A' officers** and **DANICS** officers serving in the affairs of the Government of NCTD (except those dealing with Public Order, Police, and Land).
*   **Three-Member Composition & Bureaucratic Majority Mechanics:** The **NCCSA** comprises three ex-officio members:
    1.  The **Chief Minister of NCTD** as the **Chairperson**;
    2.  The **Chief Secretary of NCTD** as a **Member** (appointed by the Union Ministry of Home Affairs); and
    3.  The **Principal Home Secretary of NCTD** as the **Member-Secretary** (also appointed by the Union).
    Under **Section 45H**, all matters required to be decided by the **NCCSA** shall be decided by a **majority of votes of the members present and voting** (with a quorum of two members). Consequently, the two Union-appointed senior bureaucrats can outvote the elected Chief Minister by a **2:1 majority** inside the Authority.
*   **Appellate Primacy and "Sole Discretion" of the Lieutenant Governor:** Even if the **NCCSA** forwards a recommendation, the **Lieutenant Governor** may return it for reconsideration, and in the case of a continuing difference of opinion, the **decision of the Lieutenant Governor shall be final**. Furthermore, the 2023 Act expanded the **sole discretionary powers** of the LG under **Section 41** to encompass all powers conferred on him by any law enacted by Parliament, as well as the appointment of chairpersons and members of various statutory boards, commissions, and authorities in Delhi (currently under challenge before a 5-Judge Constitution Bench).

## III. Asymmetrical Federalism I: The Abrogation of Article 370 and Special Provisions for States (Articles 370–371J)

### 6. Constitutional Anatomy of Article 370 and the December 2023 Constitution Bench Verdict

Unlike symmetrical federations such as the United States or Australia—where every constituent state possesses identical constitutional powers and equal representation in the upper chamber—the Indian Constitution was deliberately engineered as an **asymmetrical federation**. To integrate over 550 princely states, accommodate linguistically and culturally distinct frontier regions, and protect indigenous tribal populations, the Constituent Assembly embedded specialized asymmetric bargains in **Part XXI ("Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions," Articles 369–392)** and the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules**. For seven decades, the apex manifestation of political asymmetry was **Article 370**, which governed the constitutional relationship between the Union of India and the former State of **Jammu and Kashmir** until its structural de-operationalization in **August 2019** and subsequent judicial validation by a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution (December 2023)**.

#### Historical Architecture of Article 370 and the August 2019 Constitutional Operation (C.O. 272, C.O. 273, and J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019)

Following Maharaja Hari Singh's execution of the **Instrument of Accession on October 26, 1947** (ceding authority over Defence, External Affairs, and Communications), **Article 370** (originally Draft Article 306A) was incorporated into **Part XXI** with the heading *"Temporary provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir"*:

*   **The Pre-2019 Mechanics of Article 370:**
    1.  **Exemption from Part VI (Article 370(1)(a)):** The provisions of **Article 238** (subsequently repealed and replaced by **Part VI** governing States) did not directly apply to Jammu and Kashmir, which framed its own separate State Constitution adopted on **November 17, 1956**.
    2.  **Restricted Parliamentary Competence & Presidential Application Orders (Article 370(1)(b) & (d)):** Parliament's legislative power for J&K was restricted to matters in the Union and Concurrent Lists declared by the President in **consultation** with the State Government to correspond to the Instrument of Accession, and such other matters specified by the President with the **concurrence** of the State Government. Under **Article 370(1)(d)**, the President was empowered to issue Constitutional Orders applying other provisions of the Indian Constitution to J&K subject to *"such exceptions and modifications"* as the President specified— similarly requiring **consultation** for Instrument-of-Accession matters and **concurrence** of the State Government for all other matters (further requiring Constituent Assembly ratification under **Article 370(2)** if concurrence was given before the Constituent Assembly convened). It was via **Article 370(1)(d)** that the **Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 1954** inserted **Article 35A**, empowering the J&K Legislature to define "permanent residents" and grant them exclusive rights to land, employment, and scholarships.
    3.  **The Termination Clause (Article 370(3)):** Crucially, **Article 370(3)** provided an explicit constitutional exit mechanism: the President may, by public notification, declare that **Article 370 shall cease to be operative** or shall be operative only with such exceptions and modifications and from such date as he may specify—**Provided that** the recommendation of the **Constituent Assembly of the State** referred to in clause (2) shall be necessary before the President issues such a notification.
*   **The Four-Step August 2019 Constitutional Operation:** On **August 5–6, 2019**, while Jammu and Kashmir was under **President's Rule (Article 356)** following the collapse of the elected coalition government in June 2018 and the Governor's dissolution of the Legislative Assembly in November 2018, the Union executed a four-step constitutional operation:
    1.  **Step 1—Issuance of Constitutional Order 272 (C.O. 272) under Article 370(1)(d):** With the concurrence of the Governor/President (exercising State Government powers under **Article 356**), the President issued the **Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 2019 (C.O. 272)**, superseding the **1954 Order** (thereby extinguishing **Article 35A**) and applying *all* provisions of the Constitution of India in their entirety to Jammu and Kashmir. Furthermore, **C.O. 272** added clause (4) to **Article 367** (the general interpretation clause of the Constitution) to stipulate that references to the *"Constituent Assembly of the State"* in the Proviso to **Article 370(3)** shall be read as references to the *"Legislative Assembly of the State."*
    2.  **Step 2—Parliamentary Recommendation under Article 356(1)(b) read with Article 370(3):** Because the J&K Legislative Assembly was dissolved and its legislative powers were vested in **Parliament** under **Article 356(1)(b)**, both Houses of Parliament passed a statutory resolution recommending that the President declare **Article 370** inoperative under **Article 370(3)**.
    3.  **Step 3—Presidential Declaration C.O. 273 under Article 370(3):** Acting on Parliament's recommendation, the President issued **Constitutional Order 273 (C.O. 273)** on August 6, 2019, declaring that all clauses of **Article 370** shall cease to be operative, substituting a single text stating that all provisions of the Constitution of India shall apply without any modifications or exceptions to the State of Jammu and Kashmir.
    4.  **Step 4—Enactment of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 under Article 3:** Exercising Parliament's power to alter state boundaries and status under **Article 3** (with Parliament itself expressing the views of the State Legislature under the Proviso to **Article 3** read with **Article 356(1)(b)**), Parliament enacted the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** (effective October 31, 2019), bifurcating the State into two Union Territories: the **Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir** (with a Legislative Assembly under **Article 239A**) and the **Union Territory of Ladakh** (without a Legislative Assembly, governed under **Article 240**).

#### In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution (December 2023): 5-Judge Bench Ratios on Sovereignty, Temporariness, and Article 356 Powers

In **In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution (December 11, 2023)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously upheld the abrogation of **Article 370** and the validity of **C.O. 272** and **C.O. 273**, formulating six landmark jurisprudential ratios (with **Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** writing for himself, **Justice B.R. Gavai**, and **Justice Surya Kant**, and **Justice Sanjiv Khanna** and **Justice S.K. Kaul** authoring concurring opinions):

*   **Ratio 1—Zero Residual Internal Sovereignty Post-1947:** Overruling the premise of **Prem Nath Kaul v. State of J&K (1959)** and affirming **Sampat Prakash v. State of J&K (1969)** and **Mohd. Maqbool Damnoo (1972)**, the Bench held that Jammu and Kashmir **did not retain any element of internal sovereignty** after Maharaja Hari Singh executed the Instrument of Accession in October 1947 and His Highness Yuvraj Karan Singh issued the **Proclamation of November 25, 1949** declaring that the Constitution of India would supersede all inconsistent constitutional provisions in the State. **Article 1** of the Constitution of India (*"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States"*) read with the First Schedule applied unconditionally to J&K, and the J&K Constitution of 1956 was strictly subordinate to the Constitution of India. **Article 370** was an expression of **asymmetrical federalism**, not shared or divided sovereignty.
*   **Ratio 2—Article 370 was Strictly Temporary and Transitional:** Analyzing its placement in **Part XXI** ("Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions"), its marginal note, and **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar's** speech in the Constituent Assembly, the Bench held that **Article 370** was always intended as a **temporary and transitional provision** crafted due to wartime conditions in 1947 and to facilitate the gradual constitutional integration of the State until a Constituent Assembly could be convened—never as a permanent constitutional firewall.
*   **Ratio 3—Survival of Presidential Power under Article 370(3) After 1957:** The petitioners argued that once the J&K Constituent Assembly dissolved itself in January 1957 without recommending the abrogation of **Article 370**, the Proviso to **Article 370(3)** became impossible to fulfill, rendering **Article 370** permanent by default. Rejecting this contention, the Bench held that when the J&K Constituent Assembly ceased to exist in 1957, **only the transitional Proviso to Article 370(3) ceased to operate**, while the **substantive main power of the President under Article 370(3) to unilaterally declare Article 370 inoperative survived intact**. Holding otherwise would freeze a temporary provision into perpetuity against the explicit text of Part XXI.
*   **Ratio 4—Invalidation of the Article 367 "Interpretative Amendment" Route, Yet Validation of Direct Presidential Action:** In a nuanced doctrinal ruling on constituent vs. constituted power, the Bench held that **paragraph 2 of C.O. 272**—which used **Article 370(1)(d)** to amend the interpretation clause (**Article 367**) so as to substitute "Legislative Assembly" for "Constituent Assembly" in **Article 370(3)**—was **ultra vires and unconstitutional**, because an interpretative clause (**Article 367**) cannot be used as a backdoor circuit to amend a substantive constitutional provision (**Article 370(3)**) without following **Article 368**. *However*, this flaw did not invalidate the abrogation, because under Ratio 3, the President possessed the **unilateral substantive power under Article 370(3)** to issue **C.O. 273** directly without needing any recommendation from the Constituent Assembly or Parliament! Furthermore, applying the *entire* Constitution of India to J&K via **C.O. 272** under **Article 370(1)(d)** achieved the exact same constitutional integration as **Article 370(3)**, and because applying the rest of the Constitution required no new redistribution of federal powers, the "concurrence" of the State Government given by the President during **Article 356** was legally valid.
*   **Ratio 5—Scope of President's and Parliament's Powers during Article 356:** Addressing whether irrevocable constitutional changes can be made while a State is under **President's Rule (Article 356)**, the Bench held that following **S.R. Bommai (1994)**, there is no constitutional dichotomy between "everyday administrative actions" and "irreversible policy/constitutional actions" once **Article 356** is validly imposed. Any exercise of legislative or executive power by Parliament or the President on behalf of the State under **Article 356(1)(a)–(b)** is valid unless the challenger proves that the action is *mala fide* or has no reasonable nexus to the object of the proclamation.
*   **Ratio 6—Bifurcation into UTs (Article 3), Statehood Restoration, and 2024 Assembly Elections:** The Bench upheld the carving out of the **Union Territory of Ladakh** under **Article 3(a)** read with **Explanation I** (which explicitly authorizes forming a new Union Territory by separating territory from any State). On the profound federal question of whether **Article 3** permits Parliament to extinguish the statehood of an entire State by converting it completely into Union Territories, the Bench kept the constitutional question open in view of the Solicitor General's categorical assurance that **J&K's statehood would be restored**, and issued a binding judicial mandamus directing the **Election Commission of India** to conduct elections to the **J&K Legislative Assembly** by September 30, 2024—which were duly conducted in **September–October 2024**, culminating in the installation of an elected Council of Ministers in the UT of Jammu and Kashmir.

### 7. The Tapestry of Special Provisions: Articles 371 to 371J and Sub-State/Customary Asymmetry

While **Article 370** has been integrated into the uniform constitutional mainstream, **Part XXI** houses a vibrant, fully active architecture of **asymmetrical federalism** across **Articles 371 to 371J**, covering **12 States** across Western, Southern, and Northeastern India. Far from undermining national unity, these provisions demonstrate the Constitution's capacity to deploy two distinct species of federal asymmetry: **(1) Customary and Resource Veto Federalism** in the Northeastern frontier states (**Articles 371A, 371B, 371C, 371F, 371G, 371H**), where indigenous identity, customary adjudication, and community ownership of land and sub-soil resources are insulated even against Acts of Parliament; and **(2) Intra-State Developmental and Employment Asymmetry** in mainland states (**Articles 371, 371D, 371E, 371I, 371J**), designed to redress regional economic backwardness within large linguistic states.

#### Sovereign Bargains in the Northeast: Articles 371A (Nagaland), 371G (Mizoram), 371B, 371C (Manipur), 371F (Sikkim), and 371H

*   **Article 371A—Nagaland (13th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1962):** Born out of the **16-Point Agreement of 1960** between the Government of India and the Naga People's Convention, **Article 371A** grants **Nagaland** the most sweeping legislative veto in the Indian Constitution. Under **Article 371A(1)(a)**, notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, **no Act of Parliament** in respect of the following four core domains shall apply to the State of Nagaland **unless the Legislative Assembly of Nagaland by a resolution so decides**:
    1.  Religious or social practices of the **Nagas**;
    2.  **Naga customary law** and procedure;
    3.  Administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Naga customary law; and
    4.  **Ownership and transfer of land and its resources** (a crucial constitutional shield that gives tribal communities and the Nagaland Assembly veto power over central mining and petroleum statutes).
    Furthermore, under **Article 371A(1)(b)**, the **Governor of Nagaland** is vested with a **special responsibility with respect to law and order** in the State so long as internal disturbances continue; in exercising this function, the Governor acts in his **individual judgment** after consulting the Council of Ministers, and his decision cannot be called into question on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in his individual judgment.
*   **Article 371G—Mizoram (53rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1986):** Enacted to implement the historic **Mizoram Peace Accord of 1986** signed with the Mizo National Front, **Article 371G** replicates the **Article 371A** legislative shield for **Mizoram**, stipulating that **no Act of Parliament** in respect of: (1) religious or social practices of the **Mizos**, (2) **Mizo customary law** and procedure, (3) administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Mizo customary law, and (4) **ownership and transfer of land** (notably omitting the words *"and its resources"* found in **Article 371A**) shall apply to the State of Mizoram unless the **Legislative Assembly of Mizoram by a resolution so decides**. It also mandates a minimum Legislative Assembly strength of **40 members**.
*   **Article 371B—Assam (22nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1969) & Article 371C—Manipur (27th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971):** Under **Article 371B**, the President may provide for the constitution and functions of a **committee of the Legislative Assembly of Assam** consisting of members elected from the **Tribal Areas** specified in the Sixth Schedule. Similarly, under **Article 371C**, the President has constituted a **Hill Areas Committee (HAC)** of the **Manipur Legislative Assembly** consisting of MLAs elected from the **Hill Areas** of the State to safeguard tribal legislative interests, requires the Governor to make an annual report to the President regarding the administration of the Hill Areas, and extends the executive power of the Union to giving directions to the State as to the administration of the said areas.
*   **Article 371F—Sikkim (36th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975):** Following Sikkim's historic referendum abolishing the Chogyal monarchy and transitioning from an "Associate State" under the **35th Amendment (1974)** (which had briefly inserted **Article 2A** and the **Tenth Schedule**) to a full constituent State under the **36th Amendment (1975)**, **Article 371F** established special rules:
    1.  The Sikkim Legislative Assembly shall consist of **not less than 30 members** (an exception to the standard 60-member minimum under **Article 170**);
    2.  Parliament is empowered to reserve seats in the Sikkim Assembly for different sections of the population—specifically validating the reservation of seats for **Sikkimese of Bhutia-Lepcha origin** and **one seat for the Sangha (Buddhist monasteries)** elected by an electoral college of monks. In **R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India (1993)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** upheld these unique ethnic and monastic seat reservations under **Article 371F(f)** as valid historical accommodations of political asymmetry that do not violate the Basic Structure of democracy and secularism;
    3.  The Governor of Sikkim has special responsibility for peace and an equitable arrangement for the social and economic advancement of different sections of the population, acting in his discretion subject to Presidential directions; and
    4.  Neither the Supreme Court nor any other court has jurisdiction in respect of any dispute arising out of any treaty or agreement relating to Sikkim entered into before its merger.
*   **Article 371H—Arunachal Pradesh (55th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1986):** Upon elevating Arunachal Pradesh from a UT to Statehood, **Article 371H** vested the **Governor of Arunachal Pradesh** with a **special responsibility with respect to law and order** in the State, wherein the Governor exercises his **individual judgment** after consulting the Council of Ministers (until the President directs that such special responsibility shall cease), and fixed a minimum Legislative Assembly strength of **30 members**.

#### Intra-State Developmental Asymmetry in Mainland States: Articles 371, 371D–E, 371I, and 371J (Kalyana-Karnataka)

*   **Article 371—Maharashtra and Gujarat (7th Amendment, 1956):** Empowers the President to provide for the establishment of **separate Development Boards** for **Vidarbha, Marathwada**, and the rest of Maharashtra, and for **Saurashtra, Kutch**, and the rest of Gujarat, ensuring an annual report is placed before the State Legislative Assembly, equitable allocation of developmental funds, and equitable arrangements providing adequate facilities for technical education, vocational training, and state service employment.
*   **Articles 371D and 371E—Andhra Pradesh and Telangana (32nd Amendment, 1973 & AP Reorganisation Act, 2014):** Enacted to resolve the *Mulki Rules* agitation through the Six-Point Formula, **Article 371D** (extended to **Telangana** by the **Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014**) empowers the President to issue orders providing **equitable opportunities and facilities for the people belonging to different parts of the State** in matters of **public employment and education** by organizing local cadres and reserving direct recruitment posts and educational seats for local candidates (creating an express constitutional exception to **Article 16(3)**). While **Article 371D(5)** originally empowered the State Government to annul or modify final orders of the Administrative Tribunal, a **5-Judge Bench** in **P. Sambamurthy v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1987)** struck down **Article 371D(5)** as violating the **Basic Structure** of Rule of Law and Judicial Review. **Article 371E** empowers Parliament by law to establish a **Central University** in Andhra Pradesh.
*   **Article 371I—Goa (56th Amendment, 1987):** Fixes the minimum strength of the Legislative Assembly of the State of Goa at **not less than 30 members**.
*   **Article 371J—Karnataka / Kalyana-Karnataka Region (98th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2012):** Inserted to redress acute historical underdevelopment in the **Hyderabad-Karnataka (renamed Kalyana-Karnataka)** region comprising **six backward districts** (Bidar, Kalaburagi/Gulbarga, Yadgir, Raichur, Koppal, and Ballari/Vijayanagara), **Article 371J** empowers the President to confer special responsibility on the **Governor of Karnataka** for:
    1.  Establishing a **separate Development Board** for the Hyderabad-Karnataka (Kalyana-Karnataka) region with an annual report placed before the State Legislative Assembly;
    2.  Ensuring the **equitable allocation of funds** for developmental expenditure over the said region; and
    3.  Providing for **equitable opportunities and facilities** for people belonging to the region in **education and vocational training** (by reserving a proportion of seats) and in **State public employment** (by organizing local cadres and reserving a proportion of posts for domicile residents of the region by birth or residence).

## IV. Asymmetrical Federalism II: Tribal Self-Governance under the Fifth and Sixth Schedules (Article 244)

### 8. The Fifth Schedule Architecture: Paternalistic Protection, Gubernatorial Legislation, and the Samatha Doctrine (Article 244(1))

To prevent the socio-economic subjugation, land alienation, and cultural erasure of indigenous tribal communities (**Adivasis**) by mainstream market and legal forces, **Part X of the Constitution (Article 244)** creates a bifurcated regime of **spatial-demographic asymmetry**. Crafted during the Constituent Assembly Debates by two specialized sub-committees—the **Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas (Other than Assam) Sub-Committee** chaired by **A.V. Thakkar** and the **North-East Frontier (Assam) Tribal and Excluded Areas Sub-Committee** chaired by **Gopinath Bordoloi**—**Article 244** divides tribal governance into two distinct paradigms: **(1) The Fifth Schedule (Article 244(1))**, which governs the administration and control of **Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes** in **10 Mainland States** through a protective, Governor-centric architecture supplemented since 1996 by the **PESA Act**; and **(2) The Sixth Schedule (Article 244(2))**, which establishes self-governing **Autonomous District Councils** in **4 Northeastern States**.

#### Scheduled Areas in 10 Mainland States, Tribal Advisory Councils (TAC), and the Governor's Paragraph 5 Plenary Power

Under **Article 244(1)**, the provisions of the **Fifth Schedule** apply to the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in any State *other than* the States of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. Currently, **10 Mainland States** have notified **Fifth Schedule Areas**: **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Rajasthan**.

*   **Presidential Declaration of Scheduled Areas (Paragraph 6) & Union Executive Power (Paragraph 3):** Under **Paragraph 6** of the Fifth Schedule, the **President** is exclusively empowered to declare any area to be a **Scheduled Area**, and may at any time by order—after consultation with the Governor of the State—direct that the whole or any specified part of a Scheduled Area shall cease to be a Scheduled Area, or increase the area of any Scheduled Area, or alter its boundaries on the reorganization of States. Under **Paragraph 3**, the **Governor** of each State having Scheduled Areas must **annually** (or whenever so required by the President) make a **report to the President** regarding the administration of the Scheduled Areas in that State, and the **executive power of the Union** extends to the giving of **directions to the State** as to the administration of the said areas.
*   **The Tribal Advisory Council (TAC—Paragraph 4):** There must be established in each State having Scheduled Areas—and, if the President so directs, also in any State having **Scheduled Tribes but NOT Scheduled Areas** (such as Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, and Uttarakhand)—a **Tribal Advisory Council (TAC)** consisting of **not more than 20 members**, of whom, as nearly as may be, **three-fourths (3/4ths) shall be the representatives of the Scheduled Tribes in the Legislative Assembly of the State** (provided that if the number of ST MLAs in the State Assembly is less than the number of seats to be filled by such representatives, the remaining seats shall be filled by other members of those tribes). It is the duty of the **TAC** to advise on such matters pertaining to the **welfare and advancement of the Scheduled Tribes** in the State as may be referred to them by the Governor.
*   **The Governor's Plenary Legislative & Non-Obstante Power (Paragraph 5):** The constitutional fulcrum of the Fifth Schedule is **Paragraph 5**, which transforms the Governor into a plenary legislative authority for Scheduled Areas:
    1.  **Power to Exclude or Modify Parliamentary and State Laws (Paragraph 5(1)):** Notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, the **Governor** may by **public notification** direct that **any particular Act of Parliament or of the Legislature of the State shall NOT apply** to a Scheduled Area or any part thereof in the State, or shall apply to a Scheduled Area or any part thereof subject to **such exceptions and modifications** as he may specify in the notification, and may give such a direction **retrospective effect**.
    2.  **Power to Make Peace and Good Government Regulations (Paragraph 5(2)–(4)):** The Governor may make **Regulations** for the peace and good government of any area in a State which is for the time being a Scheduled Area. In particular, and without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing power, such Regulations may:
        *   **Prohibit or restrict the transfer of land** by or among members of the Scheduled Tribes in such area;
        *   **Regulate the allotment of land** to members of the Scheduled Tribes in such area; and
        *   **Regulate the carrying on of business as money-lender** by persons who lend money to members of the Scheduled Tribes in such area.
        In making any such Regulation under **Paragraph 5(2)**, the Governor may **repeal or amend any Act of Parliament or of the Legislature of the State** or any existing law applicable to the area! However, two procedural checks apply to **Paragraph 5(2)** Regulations: the Governor must **consult the Tribal Advisory Council (TAC)** before making the Regulation (**Paragraph 5(5)**), and every Regulation must be submitted forthwith to the **President** and has no effect until **assented to by the President** (**Paragraph 5(4)**).
*   **Simplified Parliamentary Amendment (Paragraph 7):** Under **Paragraph 7**, Parliament may from time to time **by ordinary law** (by a simple majority) amend by way of addition, variation, or repeal any of the provisions of the **Fifth Schedule**, and no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of **Article 368**.

#### Judicial Protection of Tribal Land and Resources: Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997) and Chebrolu Leela Prasad Rao (2020)

*   **The Samatha Doctrine (Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh, 1997):** In this watershed judgment, the Andhra Pradesh Government had granted mining leases over tribal lands in a Fifth Schedule Area to private non-tribal corporations. Construing **Paragraph 5(2)** of the Fifth Schedule alongside the Andhra Pradesh Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation (which prohibited the transfer of immovable property in a Scheduled Area to any *"person"* who is not a member of a Scheduled Tribe), the Supreme Court held by a 2:1 majority that the word **"person" includes the State Government itself**. Consequently, **even the State Government is constitutionally prohibited from transferring or leasing out government or forest land in a Fifth Schedule Area to non-tribal private mining companies**. Mining in Scheduled Areas can only be undertaken either by a State instrumentality/corporation or by a **cooperative society composed solely of Scheduled Tribes**, and even then, **at least 20% of the net profits** must be set aside as a permanent fund for the ecological restoration and socio-economic development of the local tribal community.
*   **Limits on Paragraph 5(1) Modification Power (Chebrolu Leela Prasad Rao v. State of Andhra Pradesh, 2020):** Addressing the outer constitutional boundaries of the Governor's power under **Paragraph 5(1)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously struck down a Gubernatorial notification issued under **Paragraph 5(1)** that had mandated **100% reservation for Scheduled Tribe candidates** in all school teacher posts across Fifth Schedule Areas in Andhra Pradesh. The Bench formulated two vital constitutional principles:
    1.  **The Power to "Modify" Does Not Include Creating a New Law:** Under **Paragraph 5(1)**, the Governor is only empowered to direct that an existing Parliamentary or State Act shall not apply or shall apply with *exceptions and modifications*; he cannot use **Paragraph 5(1)** to enact a wholly new, self-standing policy creating 100% reservations without following the **Paragraph 5(2)** Regulation route (which requires TAC consultation and Presidential assent).
    2.  **Subordination to Part III Fundamental Rights:** The non-obstante clause (*"Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution"*) in **Paragraph 5(1)** only overrides the normal federal distribution of legislative powers in **Part XI (Article 245/246)**; it **does NOT override Part III Fundamental Rights**. A **100% reservation** completely extinguishes open-category competition, violates **Articles 14 and 16(4)**, and breaches the **50% ceiling rule** established in **Indra Sawhney (1992)**.

### 9. The Sixth Schedule Architecture: Autonomous District Councils ("Mini-States") and the Ladakh Frontier (Article 244(2))

Whereas the **Fifth Schedule** relies on paternalistic protection through the office of the Governor, the **Sixth Schedule (Articles 244(2) and 275(1))**—designed by the **Bordoloi Sub-Committee**—establishes a radical model of **democratic tribal self-rule**. Operating across the **Tribal Areas** of **four Northeastern States—Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram (AMTM)**—the Sixth Schedule creates **Autonomous Districts** and **Autonomous Regions** governed by elected **Autonomous District Councils (ADCs)** and **Autonomous Regional Councils (ARCs)**, which function as constitutional **"States within a State"** possessing fused legislative, executive, fiscal, and judicial powers.

#### Autonomous District and Regional Councils (ADCs/ARCs) in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram: Legislative, Judicial, and Fiscal Autonomy

*   **The 10 Autonomous District Councils Across Four States (Paragraph 20):** The Tribal Areas under the **Sixth Schedule** are organized into **10 Autonomous Districts** grouped in three parts under **Paragraph 20**:
    1.  **Part I—Assam (3 ADCs):** The **North Cachar Hills (Dima Hasao)** District, the **Karbi Anglong** District, and the **Bodoland Territorial Areas** District (**Bodoland Territorial Council - BTC**, created by the **90th Amendment, 2003**, which uniquely possesses up to **46 members**—40 elected, of whom 30 are reserved for STs, 5 for non-tribal residents, 5 open, and 6 nominated by the Governor—and enhanced legislative powers over more than 40 subjects);
    2.  **Part II—Meghalaya (3 ADCs):** The **Khasi Hills**, **Jaintia Hills**, and **Garo Hills** Autonomous Districts (covering virtually the entire State of Meghalaya);
    3.  **Part IIA—Tripura (1 ADC):** The **Tripura Tribal Areas** Autonomous District; and
    4.  **Part III—Mizoram (3 ADCs):** The **Chakma**, **Mara**, and **Lai** Autonomous Districts.
*   **Creation, Alteration, and Composition of ADCs (Paragraphs 1 and 2):** Under **Paragraph 1**, the **Governor** is empowered by public notification to create a new Autonomous District, increase or diminish the area of any Autonomous District, unite two or more Autonomous Districts, or alter their names/boundaries, and if there are different Scheduled Tribes in an Autonomous District, the Governor may divide the area into **Autonomous Regions**. Under **Paragraph 2**, each standard **Autonomous District Council (ADC)** consists of **not more than 30 members**, of whom **not more than 4 persons shall be nominated by the Governor** and the **remaining 26 shall be elected on the basis of adult suffrage** for a normal term of **five years** (unless dissolved earlier by the Governor on the recommendation of an inquiry commission under **Paragraph 16**). Each ADC and ARC is an incorporated body corporate having perpetual succession.
*   **Plenary Legislative Powers of ADCs (Paragraph 3):** Unlike Panchayats or Municipalities, which lack true legislative power, **ADCs** and **ARCs** under **Paragraph 3** possess constitutional **legislative power** to make laws with respect to:
    1.  The allotment, occupation, use, or setting apart of **land** (other than reserved forests);
    2.  The management of any **forest** not being a reserved forest;
    3.  The use of any canal or water-course for the purpose of agriculture;
    4.  The regulation of the practice of **jhum** or other forms of shifting cultivation;
    5.  The establishment of village or town committees or councils and their powers;
    6.  Any other matter relating to **village or town administration**, including village or town police and public health and sanitation;
    7.  The **appointment or succession of Chiefs or Headmen**;
    8.  The **inheritance of property**;
    9.  **Marriage and divorce**; and
    10. **Social customs**.
    All laws made by an ADC or ARC under **Paragraph 3** must be submitted forthwith to the **Governor** and **have no effect until assented to by the Governor**.
*   **Judicial Powers: Village and District Council Courts (Paragraphs 4 and 5):** Under **Paragraph 4**, the **ADCs** and **ARCs** are empowered to constitute **Village Councils or Courts** and **District/Regional Council Courts** for the trial of suits and cases **between parties all of whom belong to Scheduled Tribes** within such areas, to the **exclusion of any court in the State**, and to appoint suitable persons as members/presiding officers of such courts. The **High Court** exercises only such jurisdiction over these suits and cases as the **Governor may by order specify**. Under **Paragraph 5**, for the trial of offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment for **not less than five years** under the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) / Indian Penal Code** or any other law, the **Governor** may confer powers under the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) / Code of Criminal Procedure** and the **Code of Civil Procedure** upon the District Council or Regional Council or courts constituted by them.
*   **Executive, Fiscal, and Mineral Royalty Powers (Paragraphs 6, 8, 9, and 13):**
    1.  **Executive Powers (Paragraph 6):** The District Council may establish, construct, or manage **primary schools**, dispensaries, markets, cattle pounds, ferries, fisheries, roads, and waterways, and in particular may prescribe the **language and the manner in which primary education shall be imparted** in the primary schools within the district.
    2.  **Taxation Powers (Paragraph 8):** The District and Regional Councils have the power to assess and collect **land revenue** in accordance with the principles followed by the State Government, to levy and collect **taxes on lands and buildings**, and tolls on persons resident within such areas. In addition, the **District Council** exclusively has the power to levy and collect all or any of the following taxes within the Autonomous District: (a) taxes on **professions, trades, callings, and employments**; (b) taxes on animals, vehicles, and boats; (c) taxes on the **entry of goods into a market** for sale therein, and tolls on passengers and goods carried in ferries; and (d) taxes for the maintenance of schools, dispensaries, or roads.
    3.  **Share of Mineral Royalties (Paragraph 9):** In respect of any **licences or leases for the purpose of prospecting for, or the extraction of, minerals** granted by the State Government in respect of any area within an Autonomous District, the District Council is constitutionally entitled to receive **such share of the royalties** accruing each year from such licences or leases as may be agreed upon between the State Government and the District Council (and in case of a dispute, as determined by the **Governor** acting in his discretion).
    4.  **Separate District and Regional Funds & CAG Audit (Paragraph 7) and Budgetary Scrutiny (Paragraph 13):** Every Autonomous District and Region maintains a **District Fund** or **Regional Fund** audited by the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)**, and its estimated receipts and expenditure are shown separately in the State's Annual Financial Statement (**Article 202**). Furthermore, under the **First and Second Provisos to Article 275(1)**, mandatory statutory grants-in-aid are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** to meet the costs of development schemes in both Fifth Schedule Areas and Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas of Assam.
*   **Exclusion of Parliamentary and State Laws (Paragraph 12, 12A, 12AA, 12B):** The power to exclude or modify Acts of Parliament and of the State Legislature varies across the four Sixth Schedule States:
    1.  **In Assam (Paragraph 12):** No Act of the Assam Legislature on subjects within the ADC's legislative competence (or prohibiting/restricting consumption of non-distilled alcoholic liquor) applies unless the **District Council** so directs; for all other **Acts of Parliament or the State Legislature**, the **Governor** may direct that the Act shall not apply or shall apply with modifications.
    2.  **In Meghalaya and Tripura (Paragraphs 12A and 12AA):** Laws made by the **District Council** on its enumerated subjects are **subordinate to laws made by the State Legislature** of Meghalaya or Tripura (i.e., State law prevails in case of repugnancy); regarding **Acts of Parliament**, it is the **President** (not the Governor) who may direct that an Act of Parliament shall not apply or shall apply with modifications, while the **Governor** exercises that power for **Acts of the State Legislature** in Tripura.
    3.  **In Mizoram (Paragraph 12B):** No Act of the Mizoram Legislature on ADC subjects applies unless the **District Council** so directs; for **Acts of Parliament**, the **President** may direct non-application or modification, whereas for other **Acts of the State Legislature**, the **Governor** exercises that power.

#### Contemporary Contestations: The Ladakh Sixth Schedule Demand vs. Article 240 Regulatory Governance

Following the enactment of the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, which detached **Ladakh** from Jammu and Kashmir and constituted it as a **Union Territory without a Legislative Assembly** governed directly through a Lieutenant Governor and Presidential Regulations under **Article 240**, a sustained constitutional mobilization emerged in Ladakh demanding inclusion under the **Sixth Schedule (Article 244(2))** and restoration of an elected legislature/statehood:

*   **The Structural Vacuum Post-August 2019:** Prior to August 2019, **Article 370** and **Article 35A** prohibited non-permanent residents from purchasing land or securing government employment in Ladakh, while Ladakh elected 4 MLAs and 2 MLCs to the J&K State Legislature. Currently, grassroots governance in Ladakh is exercised through two statutory **Autonomous Hill Development Councils** established under the **Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Councils Act, 1997**—the **LAHDC Leh** and **LAHDC Kargil** (each comprising 30 councillors: 26 elected and 4 nominated). However, unlike constitutional **Autonomous District Councils (ADCs)** under the **Sixth Schedule**, the statutory **LAHDCs** possess purely executive and developmental planning powers over local land allotment and district budgets, and **lack constitutional legislative power (Paragraph 3)** or **customary judicial powers (Paragraph 4)** to enact binding statutes protecting land, fragile Himalayan ecology, mineral resources, and indigenous culture.
*   **Constitutional Pathways under Debate (Sixth Schedule vs. Article 371 vs. Article 240):** Because over **90–97% of Ladakh's indigenous population** across Leh and Kargil belongs to recognized **Scheduled Tribes** (Balti, Beda, Bot/Boto, Brokpa/Drokpa/Dard/Shin, Changpa, Garra, Mon, and Purigpa), the **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST)** in September 2019 formally recommended the inclusion of Ladakh under the **Sixth Schedule**. However, textually, **Article 244(2)** currently restricts the **Sixth Schedule** exclusively to the four Northeastern States of **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**—meaning that extending the Sixth Schedule to the Union Territory of Ladakh would require a **Constitutional Amendment Act under Article 368**. Conversely, the Union Ministry of Home Affairs has explored utilising plenary **Presidential Regulations under Article 240(1)(e)** (or special provisions analogous to **Article 371**) to institute domicile-based job reservations, gazetted cadre recruitment through a dedicated Public Service Commission mechanism, and land-transfer protections without altering the Northeastern geographic boundary of **Article 244(2)**.

### Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 16: From Macro-Federal Asymmetry to the Third Tier of Grassroots Democracy

Having examined the macro-structural boundaries of sovereign crisis management under **Part XVIII (Articles 352–360)**, the centrally administered federal enclaves of **Part VIII (Articles 239–241)**, and the protective and autonomous asymmetric bargains of **Part XXI (Articles 370–371J)** and the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules (Article 244)**, we encounter a profound institutional transition. While the **Sixth Schedule** created self-governing **Autonomous District Councils** in four Northeastern states, the vast majority of rural, tribal, and urban citizens across mainland India lacked constitutionalized local self-government for the first four decades of the Republic, with Gandhian **Gram Swaraj** confined to the non-justiciable realm of **Article 40**. **Part 16** systematically deconstructs how the **73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992 (Parts IX and IXA, Articles 243 to 243ZG)**, the **Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act (PESA), 1996** (which fused the Fifth Schedule with radical **Gram Sabha** sovereignty), and the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011 (Part IXB, Co-operative Societies)** operationalized the third tier of Indian federalism—transforming local democratic decentralization from statutory discretion into an entrenched constitutional mandate.
