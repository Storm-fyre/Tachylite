# Part 5: Fundamental Rights III: Exploitation, Freedom of Conscience, Minority Rights, Constitutional Remedies, and Martial Law (Articles 23–30, 32–35)

## I. The Right Against Exploitation: Horizontal Coercion, Economic Compulsion, and Child Labor (Articles 23–24)

### 1. Constitutional Prohibition of Human Trafficking, Begar, and Forced Labor (Article 23)

#### Horizontal Enforceability and the Anatomy of Article 23(1)–(2)
Unlike the classic liberal negative liberties in **Part III** that operate strictly vertically against State action as defined under **Article 12**, **Article 23** and **Article 24** were engineered by the **Constituent Assembly** to dismantle entrenched socio-economic hierarchies of feudal bondage, caste-based unpaid extraction, and industrial exploitation of children. In asemi-feudal agrarian order where private landlords (*zamindars*), moneylenders, and industrial contractors exercised coercive sovereignty over landless laborers, a purely vertical constitutional bar against the State would have left the material architecture of servitude intact. Consequently, **Article 23(1)** is framed in absolute, universal terms—operating both vertically against State authorities and horizontally against private individuals, corporations, and non-state actors (reaffirmed by the 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh, 2023**).

The textual and structural mechanics of **Article 23** rest on three foundational pillars:

*   **Prohibition of Trafficking, *Begar*, and Forced Labor (Article 23(1)):** The clause prohibits three distinct yet overlapping phenomena: (i) **traffic in human beings**, which encompasses the buying and selling of men, women, and children Like chattels, immoral trafficking for prostitution (regulated under the **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956** and **Sections 143–146 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**), and *Devadasi* dedication practices; (ii) ***begar***, an indigenous feudal institution wherein local landlords or colonial officials compelled villagers and marginalized castes to render involuntary service without remuneration; and (iii) **other similar forms of forced labor**, an *ejusdem generis* catch-all phrase designed to capture debt bondage (*vetti*, *hali*, *kamiauti*) where nominal wages or generational advances mask economic enslavement.
*   **Penal Enforcement Mandate under Article 35:** The second limb of **Article 23(1)** explicitly declares that any contravention of this provision *"shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law."* Read with **Article 35(a)(ii)**, the power to prescribe penal sanctions for violations of **Article 23(1)** is vested exclusively in **Parliament** and explicitly denied to State Legislatures, ensuring uniform national criminalization of human bondage.
*   **The Public Purpose Conscription Exception (Article 23(2)):** During the **Constituent Assembly Debates**, members led by **K.M. Munshi** and **B.R. Ambedkar** recognized that the nascent Republic must retain sovereign competence to mandate compulsory military conscription or civic social service during national crises. **Article 23(2)** saves the State's power to impose *"compulsory service for public purposes,"* whether remunerated or unremunerated, subject to an absolute non-discrimination caveat: in imposing such service, the State shall not make any discrimination on grounds only of **religion, race, caste, or class or any of them**. Crucially, **sex/gender** is omitted from the forbidden grounds of differentiation under **Article 23(2)**, permitting the State to classify citizens by gender in military conscription regimes. Further, in **State of Gujarat v. Hon'ble High Court of Gujarat (1998)**, the Supreme Court held that extracting hard labor from convicts sentenced to rigorous imprisonment does not violate **Article 23**, provided the prisoners are paid equitable wages after deducting reasonable maintenance expenses.

#### Jurisprudential Expansion: Economic Compulsion and Minimum Wages (PUDR Asiad Workers and Bandhua Mukti Morcha)
For the first three decades of the Republic, **Article 23** remained largely dormant because classical legal formalism equated "force" strictly with physical violence or statutory penal compulsion. In the early 1980s, the Supreme Court revolutionized **Article 23** by synthesizing it with **Article 21** (human dignity) and **Articles 39, 41, and 42** of the **Directive Principles of State Policy** to dismantle market-driven exploitation:

*   **PUDR Asiad Workers Case (*People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India*, 1982):** Addressing the exploitation of contract laborers engaged through intermediaries (*jamadars*) in construction projects for the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi, Justice **P.N. Bhagwati** established three foundational ratios:
    1.  **Horizontal Enforceability:** **Article 23** strikes at forced labor regardless of whether the extractor is the Union, a State, a public sector undertaking, or a private contractor. Moreover, when a private contractor pays less than the minimum wage on a public project, the Union or State (as the principal employer under the **Contract Labour Act, 1970**) incurs a direct constitutional obligation to halt the violation.
    2.  **Redefinition of "Force" via Economic Compulsion:** The word *"force"* in **Article 23(1)** cannot be restricted to physical coercion, gunpoint threats, or legal sanction. In a society plagued by acute poverty and structural unemployment, hunger and destitution operate as coercive forces. Where a person provides labor or service to another for remuneration which is **less than the statutory minimum wage**, the labor provided by them clearly falls within the scope and ambit of the words *"forced labour"* under **Article 23**, because no rational individual would willingly work for less than the statutory minimum wage unless driven by dire economic compulsion.
    3.  **Irrelevance of Contract or Consent:** A contract entered into by a destitute worker to accept sub-minimum wages is void *ab initio*; under the **Doctrine of Non-Waiver** (**Basheshar Nath v. CIT, 1959**), poverty-induced consent cannot validate a breach of **Article 23**.
*   **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) and the Bonded Labour Abolition Act, 1976:** Enacted by Parliament pursuant to **Article 23(1)** and **Article 35**, the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** unilaterally extinguished all bonded debts, abolished customary caste obligations to render service, freed mortgaged property of bonded laborers, and mandated District Magistrates and Vigilance Committees to identify, release, and rehabilitate bonded workers. In **Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984)**, involving stone-quarry workers in Faridabad working under inhumane conditions and debt advances, the Supreme Court held that whenever it is shown that a laborer is made to provide forced labor, the Court will draw a **rebuttable legal presumption** that they are doing so in consideration of an advance or other economic consideration received by them, thereby constituting **bonded labor** entitled to statutory rehabilitation unless the employer or State proves the contrary. In **Neeraja Chaudhary v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1984)**, the Court further ruled that mere physical release of a bonded laborer without simultaneous socioeconomic **rehabilitation** under the **1976 Act** violates both **Article 21** and **Article 23**, as destitution would inevitably drive the freed worker back into bondage.

### 2. Prohibition of Child Labor in Hazardous Employment (Article 24)

#### Constitutional Mandate and the Sivakasi Welfare Corpus (M.C. Mehta)
While **Article 23** targets coerced labor across all age groups, **Article 24** enacts an absolute, unqualified prohibition against the industrial exploitation of children: *"No child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment."* Like **Article 23**, **Article 24** operates both vertically against the State and horizontally against private employers, admitting of no "reasonable restrictions" or economic necessity defenses.

During the **Constituent Assembly Debates**, **Article 24** was intentionally read alongside the original **Article 45** (free and compulsory education until age 14) and **Article 39(e)–(f)** (protection of childhood and youth against moral and material abandonment). In **M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996)** (the **Sivakasi Case**), triggered by fatal explosions in the fireworks and matchstick factories of Sivakasi employing thousands of children, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court transformed **Article 24** from a prohibitory command into an affirmative structural remedy:

*   **The Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund:** The Court directed that any offending employer employing a child in contravention of **Article 24** and statutory prohibitions must pay a compensation of **Rs. 20,000 per child**, to be deposited into a **Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund**.
*   **State Employment or Financial Deposit Mandate:** Recognizing that abrupt removal of a child from employment without addressing family destitution fails to solve the structural crisis, the Court directed the State Government to either provide gainful employment to one adult member of the child's family or contribute an additional **Rs. 5,000** per child to the Welfare Fund, with the corpus interest paid monthly to the parents on the strict condition that the child is withdrawn from work and enrolled in a full-time school under **Article 21A**.

#### Statutory Architecture: The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016
To harmonize **Article 24** with the fundamental **Right to Free and Compulsory Education** for children aged 6 to 14 years under **Article 21A** (inserted by the **86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002**) and to enable India's ratification of **ILO Conventions 138 (Minimum Age)** and **182 (Worst Forms of Child Labour)**, Parliament enacted the **Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016**, fundamentally overhauling the original **1986 Act**:

*   **Complete Prohibition of Child Labor Below 14 Years:** Whereas **Article 24** textually prohibits employment below 14 years only in *"factories, mines, or hazardous employment,"* the **2016 Amendment Act** goes beyond the constitutional minimum floor to impose a **total statutory ban** on employing any **"Child"** (defined as a person who has not completed 14 years of age, or such age as specified under the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009**, whichever is higher) in **any occupation or process whatsoever**, whether hazardous or non-hazardous.
*   **Statutory Carve-Outs for Family Enterprises and Child Artists (Section 3):** The **2016 Amendment** introduces two controversial statutory exceptions where a child below 14 may work, strictly provided the work does not affect their school education: (i) where the child *"helps his family or family enterprise"* (defined as work, profession, manufactory, or business performed by biological or adoptive parents and siblings) in non-hazardous occupations **after school hours or during vacations**; and (ii) where the child works as an artist in the **audio-visual entertainment industry** (films, television, serials, advertisements) or sports activities (explicitly excluding the circus), subject to prescribed safety conditions. Critics and child-rights jurists point out that in India's caste-stratified informal economy, home-based piece-rate labor (such as *zari* embroidery, *beedi* rolling, carpet weaving, and bangle making) is routinely disguised as "family enterprises," threatening to dilute the educational mandate of **Article 21A**.
*   **Creation of the "Adolescent" Category (14 to 18 Years):** The **2016 Amendment** introduced a new statutory class—**"Adolescent"** (persons who have completed 14 years but not 18 years)—and banned their employment in **hazardous occupations and processes** (specifically reduced in the Schedule to three broad categories: **mines**, **inflammable substances or explosives**, and **hazardous processes** as defined under the **Factories Act, 1948**, subject to central government expansion).
*   **Cognizable Offences and Statutory Rehabilitation Fund (Section 14B):** Employment of children or adolescents in contravention of the Act is upgraded into a **cognizable offence** punishable with imprisonment between **6 months and 2 years** (up to **3 years** for repeat offenders) and/or a fine between **Rs. 20,000 and Rs. 50,000** (with parents/guardians exempted from punishment for the first offence in non-commercial infractions unless they permit commercial exploitation). Furthermore, **Section 14B** codifies the **M.C. Mehta Sivakasi (1996)** directive by establishing a statutory **Child and Adolescent Labour Rehabilitation Fund** in every district, into which all fines realized from employers Plus an additional state contribution of **Rs. 15,000 per rescued child/adolescent** are credited directly for the child's welfare.

While **Articles 23 and 24** deploy the coercive and regulatory power of the Republic to emancipate the physical body from feudal and industrial subjugation, the subsequent cluster of Fundamental Rights transitions to the emancipation of the mind and conscience—confronting the far more intricate constitutional dilemma of reconciling individual spiritual autonomy, deeply entrenched religious hierarchies, and the transformative social reform mandate of a secular welfare State.

## II. Freedom of Conscience, Principled Distance, and Religious Denominational Autonomy (Articles 25–28)

### 3. Individual Freedom of Conscience vs. State Regulatory Power (Article 25)

#### The Triple Subjection Clause and Social Welfare Reform (Article 25(1)–(2))
Unlike the French model of negative *Laïcité* or the First Amendment Jeffersonian "wall of separation" in the United States—both of which emerged from struggles against a monolithic Church—Indian constitutional secularism was forged in a deeply religious, multi-confessional society where religion frequently regulated civil status, caste hierarchy, and public space. Consequently, the Indian Constitution adopts what political theorist Rajeev Bhargava and the Supreme Court (in **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, 1994** and **St. Stephen's College, 1992**) characterize as **"Principled Distance"** or **ameliorative secularism**: the State has no established religion of its own, respects all faiths equally (*Sarva Dharma Sambhava*), yet retains plenary interventionist authority to eradicate caste exclusion, gender subordination, and secular maladministration embedded within religious traditions.

**Article 25** operationalizes this ameliorative architecture through a carefully calibrated internal hierarchy:

*   **The Triple Subjection Clause (Article 25(1)):** Every **"person"** (citizens and non-citizens alike) is equally entitled to **freedom of conscience** and the right freely to **profess, practice, and propagate religion**. However, unlike **Article 19**, which states the positive right first and relegates restrictions to subsequent sub-clauses, **Article 25(1)** opens with an explicit, cumulative subordination clause: *"Subject to public order, morality and health and to the other provisions of this Part..."* By subordinating **Article 25(1)** not only to public order, morality, and health, but also **"to the other provisions of this Part,"** the **Constituent Assembly** explicitly subordinated religious freedom to **Article 14** (equality), **Article 15** (non-discrimination), **Article 17** (abolition of untouchability), **Article 21** (dignity and bodily autonomy), and **Article 23** (ban on *Devadasi* trafficking).
*   **The Four-Fold Breakdown of Article 25(1):**
    1.  **Freedom of Conscience:** The absolute inner autonomy of an individual to mould their spiritual relation with God or to hold atheistic, agnostic, or rationalist convictions without external compulsion.
    2.  **Right to Profess:** The overt, public declaration of one's religious beliefs and faith without fear of state persecution or civic disability (including the wearing of *kirpans* by Sikhs, explicitly deemed by **Explanation I to Article 25** to be included in the profession of the Sikh religion).
    3.  **Right to Practice:** The performance of religious worship, rituals, ceremonies, and the exhibition of beliefs and ideas through overt acts (as held in **Bijoe Emanuel v. State of Kerala, 1986**, where Jehovah's Witness students who stood respectfully in silence without singing the National Anthem due to conscientious religious objection were protected under **Articles 19(1)(a) and 25(1)**).
    4.  **Right to Propagate:** The transmission, dissemination, and exposition of one's religious tenets to others for their edification, Stopping strictly short of coercive or fraudulent proselytization.
*   **Secular Regulation vs. Social Reform (Article 25(2)(a)–(b)):**
    *   **Article 25(2)(a) (Secular Activities Associated with Religion):** Empowered the State to regulate or restrict any **economic, financial, political, or other secular activity** which may be associated with religious practice (e.g., auditing temple/mosque accounts, fixing service conditions of temple employees, or regulating land tenures held by deities).
    *   **Article 25(2)(b) (Social Welfare, Reform, and Temple Entry):** Empowered the State to enact laws providing for **social welfare and reform** (such as abolishing polygamy among Hindus via the **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955**, upheld in **State of Bombay v. Narasu Appa Mali, 1951**) or the **throwing open of Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus** (dismantling caste-based temple exclusion). **Explanation II to Article 25** stipulates that for the purposes of sub-clause (b) of clause (2), the reference to **Hindus** shall be construed as including a reference to persons professing the **Sikh, Jaina, or Buddhist** religion.

#### Propagation vs. Proselytization: Rev. Stainislaus (1977) and State Anti-Conversion Statutes
During the **Constituent Assembly Debates** (December 1948), the inclusion of the word **"propagate"** in **Article 25(1)** (then Draft Article 19) was the fruit of a historic compromise with the Christian minority represented by **H.C. Mookherjee** and **Frank Anthony**, defended eloquently by **K.M. Munshi** and **T.T. Krishnamachari**, who clarified that propagation meant persuasion and intellectual exposition, not forcible conversion.

In the landmark 5-Judge Constitution Bench ruling in **Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977)**, the Supreme Court adjudicated the constitutional validity of the **Madhya Pradesh Dharma Swatantrya Adhiniyam, 1968** and the **Orissa Freedom of Religion Act, 1967**, which criminalized religious conversion effected by *"force, fraud, or allurement/inducement"*:

*   **The Ratio of Rev. Stainislaus (1977):** Speaking through Chief Justice **A.N. Ray**, the Constitution Bench drew a sharp doctrinal line between **propagation** and **conversion**:
    1.  **Exposition vs. Conversion:** What **Article 25(1)** grants is the right to transmit or spread one's religion by an exposition of its tenets, **not the right to convert another person to one's own religion**.
    2.  **Reciprocal Freedom of Conscience:** Because **"freedom of conscience"** under **Article 25(1)** belongs equally to every citizen, a purposive attempt to convert another person through force, fraud, or inducement directly impinges upon the target individual's own freedom of conscience. If a person purposely undertakes the conversion of another person to their religion, as distinguished from an effort to transmit or spread the tenets of their religion, that would impinge on the *"freedom of conscience"* guaranteed to all citizens of the country alike.
    3.  **Public Order Nexus:** The Bench gave a wide construction to the phrase *"Subject to public order"* in **Article 25(1)**, holding that forcible or fraudulent conversions generate communal tension and threaten public order, thereby validating State legislative competence under **Entry 1 of List II (Public Order)**.
*   **Contemporary State Anti-Conversion Statutes (2020–2026 Baseline):** Between 2020 and 2024, several states (including Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Karnataka, Haryana, and Uttarakhand) enacted stringent new anti-conversion statutes expanding the **Stainislaus** framework by: (i) criminalizing **"conversion by marriage"** or marriage solemnized solely for the purpose of unlawful conversion; (ii) requiring **30 to 60 days' prior declaration** before a District Magistrate prior to voluntarily changing one's religion, coupled with a magisterial police inquiry into the motives of the conversion; (iii) shifting the **burden of proof** onto the person who caused or facilitated the conversion; and (iv) making offences cognizable and non-bailable with enhanced sentences when the convert belongs to a Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe, or is a woman/minor. While High Courts (such as the **Gujarat High Court in 2021** and the **Madhya Pradesh High Court in 2022**) have stayed provisions requiring prior magisterial permission and criminalizing consensual inter-faith marriages as prima facie violating the **Right to Decisional Autonomy, Privacy, and Choice of Partner** under **Article 21** (**K.S. Puttaswamy I, 2017**; **Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M., 2018**; **Lata Singh, 2006**), a consolidated batch of petitions challenging the constitutional interplay between **Rev. Stainislaus (1977)** and **Puttaswamy (2017)** remains under active adjudication before the Supreme Court.

### 4. Collective Denominational Autonomy and Secular Administration (Article 26)

#### The Tripartite Test of a "Religious Denomination" (S.P. Mittal v. Union of India)
While **Article 25** protects the individual's spiritual conscience and practice, **Article 26** protects the collective institutional autonomy of **"every religious denomination or any section thereof."** Subject to **public order, morality, and health**, **Article 26** guarantees every religious denomination four distinct collective rights:
*   **Article 26(a):** To establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes (note that, just like **Article 30(1)**, the words *"establish and maintain"* must be read conjunctively, as held in **Azeez Basha, 1967** and **S.P. Mittal, 1983**—a denomination can claim the right to maintain only those institutions that it has itself established).
*   **Article 26(b):** To manage its own affairs in **matters of religion**.
*   **Article 26(c):** To own and acquire movable and immovable property.
*   **Article 26(d):** To administer such property **in accordance with law**.

To claim the corporate shield of **Article 26**, a group must first prove that it constitutes a **"religious denomination or a section thereof."** Synthesizing the 7-Judge Bench ruling in **Shirur Mutt (1954)** with the 5-Judge Constitution Bench ruling in **S.P. Mittal v. Union of India (1983)**, the Supreme Court established a strict **Tripartite Jurisprudential Test** that any claimant group must satisfy concurrently:
1.  **Common Faith:** It must be a collection of individuals who have a system of beliefs or doctrines which they regard as conducive to their spiritual well-being.
2.  **Common Organization:** It must possess a common institutional organization or corporate structure binding its adherents together.
3.  **Designation by a Distinctive Name:** It must be designated and recognized by a distinctive name differentiating it from the general religious community (for instance, the **Ramakrishna Mission**, the **Gowda Saraswath Brahmins**, the **Dawoodi Bohras**, and the **Ananda Marga** sect were recognized as religious denominations/sections, whereas in **S.P. Mittal (1983)**, the **Auroville Society** founded on the teachings of Sri Aurobindo was held **not** to be a religious denomination because Sri Aurobindo's philosophy was a universal spiritual synthesis rather than a distinct sect with a closed creed).

#### Interplay of Article 25(2)(b) and Article 26(b): Denominational Autonomy Subordinate to Social Reform
A major structural textual contrast separates **Article 25** from **Article 26**: while **Article 25(1)** is subject to *"public order, morality and health and to the other provisions of this Part,"* **Article 26** opens only with *"Subject to public order, morality and health,"* omitting the phrase *"and to the other provisions of this Part."* Furthermore, while **Article 26(c)–(d)** (administration of property) can be regulated *"in accordance with law"* and even acquired by the State under **Article 300A** so long as the core denomination is not extinguished (**Khajamian Wakf Estates v. State of Madras, 1970**), **Article 26(b)** (managing its own affairs in *"matters of religion"*) contains no statutory subordination clause.

This textual asymmetry triggered a foundational conflict in **Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore (1958)**: when the Madras Temple Entry Authorisation Act threw open the Sri Venkataramana Temple at Mulki to all Hindus (including Scheduled Castes) under **Article 25(2)(b)**, the **Gowda Saraswath Brahmin** trustees claimed that as a distinct denominational temple under **Article 26(b)**, their *Agamic* rules restricting inner-sanctum entry to their own sect superseded **Article 25(2)(b)**. Applying the **Doctrine of Harmonious Construction**, Justice **T.L. Venkatarama Aiyar** for the 5-Judge Bench ruled that **Article 26(b) must be read as subject to Article 25(2)(b)**. If denominational autonomy under **Article 26(b)** were allowed to override the social-reform and temple-entry mandate of **Article 25(2)(b)**, the constitutional promise of eradicating caste exclusion in public Hindu temples would be rendered largely otiose; however, the Court carved out minor ceremonial slots (such as strictly private sectarian rituals where the general public was altogether excluded) to prevent **Article 26(b)** from becoming wholly redundant.

### 5. The Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Doctrine and Constitutional Morality

#### Genealogy of the ERP Test: From Shirur Mutt (1954) to Durgah Committee, Tandava Dance, and Shayara Bano
Because **Article 25(2)(a)** permits the State to regulate *"economic, financial, political or other secular activity which may be associated with religious practice,"* while **Article 26(b)** protects a denomination's autonomy in *"matters of religion,"* the Supreme Court was forced to construct a doctrinal boundary separating what is genuinely **"religious"** from what is merely **"secular."** In the Constituent Assembly (December 2, 1948), explaining why the State could codify personal laws despite **Article 25**, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** observed that the religious conceptions in India are so vast that they cover every aspect of life from birth to death, and that the State ought to restrict the definition of religion to *"beliefs and such rituals as may be connected with ceremonials which are essentially religious."*

From this remark, the Supreme Court constructed the **Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Doctrine**, which evolved across four distinct jurisprudential phases from a shield protecting religious autonomy into an invasive judicial filter whereby secular judges act as ecclesiastical arbiters of theological scripture:

*   **Phase I: The Autonomy-Centric Origins in *Shirur Mutt* (1954):** In **The Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt (1954)**, a 7-Judge Constitution Bench led by Justice **B.K. Mukherjea** rejected the State's argument that "religion" under **Article 25** is confined strictly to personal internal belief while all outward rituals are secular. The Court held that a religion undoubtedly has its basis in a system of beliefs or doctrines, *"but it would not be correct to say that religion is nothing else but a doctrine or belief. A religion may not only lay down a code of ethical rules for its followers to accept, it might also prescribe rituals and observances, ceremonies and modes of worship which are regarded as integral parts of religion."* Crucially, in **Shirur Mutt**, the test was **internal and deferential**: what constitutes the essential part of a religion is primarily to be ascertained **with reference to the doctrines of that religion itself**.
*   **Phase II: Shift from "Essentially Religious" to "Essential TO Religion" (*Durgah Committee*, 1961):** In **Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali (1961)** (5-Judge Bench) and **Tilkayat Shri Govindlalji Maharaj v. State of Rajasthan (1963)**, Chief Justice **P.B. Gajendragadkar** fundamentally inverted **Shirur Mutt**. Instead of asking whether a practice was *essentially religious* in nature (as opposed to secular/economic), Gajendragadkar, C.J., held that for a religious practice to claim constitutional protection under **Articles 25 and 26**, it must be an **essential and integral part of the religion**, Empowering secular courts to sift through religious scriptures to strip away *"superstitious beliefs and extraneous, unessential accretions"* that had sprung up over time. Earlier, in **M.H. Quareshi v. State of Bihar (1958)** (5-Judge Bench), the Court had similarly examined Islamic texts (*Hedaya* and the Quran) to hold that sacrificing a cow on *Bakr-Id* is not an obligatory or essential practice of Islam since options of sacrificing a camel or seven goats exist.
*   **Phase III: The "Core Alteration / Obligatory Foundation" Test (*Tandava Dance*, 1983/2004 & *Ismail Faruqui*, 1994):** The ERP test reached its zenith of judicial rigidity in the **Ananda Marga Tandava Dance** litigation (**Acharya Jagdishwarananda Avadhuta v. Commissioner of Police, Calcutta, 1983 [I] and 2004 [II]**). Even though the Ananda Marga sect was recognized as a religious denomination under **Article 26**, the Supreme Court ruled that performing the *Tandava* dance in public processions carrying lethal weapons, skulls, and live snakes was **not an essential religious practice** because: (i) the sect was founded in 1955 whereas the *Tandava* dance was introduced by its spiritual head (*Anandamurti*) only in 1966; and (ii) in **Jagdishwarananda II (2004)**, the Court held that an essential practice means the **core beliefs upon which a religion is founded**—a practice so fundamental that **without it, the character of the religion itself would be altered into something else**, and which cannot be created or altered by subsequent human mandate. Similarly, in **Dr. M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India (1994)** (5-Judge Bench), the Court held that while offering prayer (*namaz*) is an essential practice of Islam, offering prayer **at a mosque** is not an essential part of Islam unless the particular mosque has a peculiar significance, since *namaz* can be offered anywhere even in the open—hence, the State has sovereign power to acquire a mosque under eminent domain.
*   **Phase IV: Application to Personal Laws and *Shayara Bano* (2017):** In **Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017)** (5-Judge Bench, 3:2 striking down instantaneous Triple Talaq or *Talaq-e-Biddat*), Justice **Rohinton Nariman** (joined by U.U. Lalit, J.) and Justice **Kurian Joseph** applied the **Tandava Dance** ERP threshold to hold that because *Talaq-e-Biddat* is considered sinful and irregular (*bad in theology*) even within the Hanafi school and is merely permissible (*mubah*) rather than obligatory (*farz/wajib*), its abolition does not alter the fundamental nature of Islam; thus, it enjoys zero protection under **Article 25(1)**.

#### The Anti-Exclusion Principle and Constitutional Morality: Sabarimala (2018), Hijab Split Verdict (2022), and the 9-Judge Reference
The internal contradictions of the ERP doctrine erupted into a historic jurisprudential confrontation in **Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018)** (the **Sabarimala Judgment**), where a 5-Judge Constitution Bench (by a 4:1 majority of CJI Dipak Misra, A.M. Khanwilkar, R.F. Nariman, and D.Y. Chandrachud, JJ.; Indu Malhotra, J. dissenting) struck down the customary exclusion of women in the menstruating age group (10 to 50 years) from the Lord Ayyappa Temple at Sabarimala:

*   **Denial of Separate Denominational Status:** Applying the **S.P. Mittal (1983)** test, the majority held that devotees of Lord Ayyappa do not constitute a separate **religious denomination** under **Article 26**, as they share no distinct creed or exclusive organization separate from general Hinduism and the temple is administered by the statutory Travancore Devaswom Board receiving state funds under **Article 290A**.
*   **Constitutional Morality:** The majority held that the word **"morality"** occurring in **Articles 25(1) and 26** cannot be equated with transient public/popular morality or sectarian religious taboos; rather, it signifies **Constitutional Morality**—the overarching normative values of justice, liberty, equality, dignity, and fraternity enshrined in the **Preamble** and **Part III**. Under **Article 25(1)**, a woman's individual right to worship is co-equal to a man's and cannot be effaced by a physiological attribute (menstruation) of the celibate (*Naishtika Brahmachari*) deity.
*   **Justice D.Y. Chandrachud's "Anti-Exclusion Principle" and Article 17 Expansion:** Critiquing the **ERP Doctrine** for forcing secular judges to assume a theological role for which they lack institutional competence, Justice Chandrachud proposed supplementing or replacing ERP with the **Anti-Exclusion Principle**: the State and courts must respect religious autonomy *except* where a religious practice results in the exclusion, subordination, or stigmatization of individuals in a manner that impairs their basic human dignity and access to public goods. Furthermore, drawing directly from the **Constituent Assembly Debates**, Chandrachud, J., gave an expansive reading to **Article 17** (*"Untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden"*), holding that **"in any form"** prohibits not only caste-based untouchability, but also menstrual/biological notions of "purity and pollution" used to stigmatize women as impure.
*   **Justice Indu Malhotra's Dissent:** In a formidable dissent, Justice Malhotra cautioned that judicial review of religious beliefs under **Article 14** rationality standards would destroy India's pluralistic secular fabric, particularly for minority communities. She argued that **Constitutional Morality** in a plural society precisely demands judicial tolerance of diverse, non-rational religious faiths and practices under **Articles 25 and 26**, unless the practice is pernicious, oppressive, or a social evil like *Sati* or child marriage.
*   **The Pending 9-Judge Constitution Bench Reference (*Kantaru Rajeevaru*, 2020):** In November 2019 and February 2020 (**Kantaru Rajeevaru v. Indian Young Lawyers Association**), a 5-Judge Review Bench took an extraordinary procedural step: while keeping the Sabarimala review petitions pending, it framed **seven overarching constitutional questions** and referred them to an authoritative **9-Judge Constitution Bench** to settle the interplay between **Articles 14, 15, 17, 25, and 26**, the scope of **"Constitutional Morality,"** the boundaries of judicial review under the **ERP Test** (**Shirur Mutt** vs. **Durgah Committee**), and the overlapping rights of women across four pending disputes: (i) female entry into **Sabarimala**; (ii) entry of Muslim women into **Mosques/Dargahs**; (iii) entry of Parsi women married to non-Parsis into the **Agiary (Fire Temple)** (**Goolrokh Gupta**); and (iv) the practice of **Female Genital Mutilation (*Khatna*)** in the Dawoodi Bohra community (**Sunita Tiwari**), alongside the 5-Judge referral of the **Dawoodi Bohra Excommunication Case** (**Syedna Taher Saifuddin, 1962**) in February 2023.
*   **The Hijab Split Verdict (*Aishat Shifa v. State of Karnataka*, October 2022):** The perils of the ERP test resurfaced in the 2-Judge Bench split verdict over the Karnataka Government order banning the wearing of the Islamic headscarf (*Hijab*) inside government Pre-University College classrooms with prescribed uniforms. While Justice **Hemant Gupta** upheld the ban—accepting the Karnataka High Court's finding that wearing the *Hijab* is not an essential religious practice of Islam and holding that secular uniform regulations promote formal equality—Justice **Sudhanshu Dhulia** struck down the ban, rejecting the ERP inquiry altogether as irrelevant to individual conscience and holding that forcing a girl to choose between her religious attire/conscience (**Article 25(1)**), privacy/dignity (**Article 21**), and freedom of expression (**Article 19(1)(a)**) on the one hand, and her **Right to Education** on the other, inflicts a disproportionate, unconstitutional burden on female literacy. Following the 1:1 split, the matter awaits constitution of a larger 3-Judge Bench.

### 6. Statutory Flashpoints: Sacred Spaces, Waqf Governance, Fiscal Neutrality, and Educational Secularism (Articles 27–28)

#### The Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991: M. Siddiq (Ayodhya, 2019) and the Gyanvapi/Mathura Litigation
Enacted at the height of the Ram Janmabhoomi movement, the **Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991** freezes the religious character of all places of public religious worship in India (specifically exempting only the **Ram Janmabhoomi–Babri Masjid** site in Ayodhya under **Section 5** and ancient monuments covered by the **Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act, 1958** under **Section 4(3)(a)**) exactly as it existed on **August 15, 1947**:
*   **Section 3:** Imposes an absolute bar on converting any place of worship of any religious denomination (or even a different section of the same religious denomination) into a place of worship of a different denomination or section.
*   **Section 4(1)–(2):** Declares that the religious character of a place of worship existing on **August 15, 1947** shall continue to be the same, and abates all pending suits, appeals, or legal proceedings regarding conversions alleged to have occurred prior to August 15, 1947, while barring the institution of any fresh suit or proceeding on such grounds.

In the unanimous 5-Judge Constitution Bench judgment in **M. Siddiq (D) Thr. LRs v. Mahant Suresh Das (2019)** (the **Ayodhya Verdict**), the Supreme Court elevated the **Places of Worship Act, 1991** to a direct legislative instrument enforcing the **Basic Structure Doctrine**:
*   **Constitutional Non-Retrogression and Secularism:** The Constitution Bench held that the **1991 Act** imposes a non-derogable obligation towards enforcing the State's commitment to **Secularism** (a core feature of the **Basic Structure**). Under the **Doctrine of Non-Retrogression**, the State cannot step backward from the guarantees of equality and fraternity; **Parliament** determined that the historical wrongs of medieval rulers cannot be remedied by modern courts through the confiscation of contemporary places of worship, as the **Constitution of 1950** represents a decisive watershed moment of equal citizenship.
*   **The Gyanvapi (Varanasi), Shahi Idgah (Mathura), and Sambhal Litigation (2022–2026 Baseline):** Despite the sweeping ratio of **M. Siddiq (2019)**, dozens of civil suits were instituted before district courts in **Varanasi (Gyanvapi Mosque)**, **Mathura (Shahi Idgah Mosque)**, and **Sambhal**, demanding Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) excavations and worship rights. Relying on a May 2022 oral observation of a 2-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court that *"ascertainment of the religious character of a place of worship is not barred by Sections 3 and 4 of the 1991 Act,"* trial courts and the Allahabad High Court allowed suits to proceed on the theory that determining what the "religious character" actually was on August 15, 1947 is a mixed question of fact and law requiring trial evidence and ASI surveys. However, recognizing that routine trial court survey orders were effectively eroding the statutory bar of **Section 4**, a 3-Judge Special Bench of the Supreme Court (headed by then CJI **Sanjiv Khanna** in **Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. Union of India, December 2024**) issued a **pan-India interim directive**: while the 3-Judge Bench hears the constitutional challenge to the **1991 Act**, no new suits challenging the character of places of worship shall be registered anywhere in India, and in pending suits (including Gyanvapi, Mathura, and Sambhal), no court shall pass any effective interim or final orders—including orders for **ASI surveys**—until further orders of the Supreme Court.

#### Secular Administration of Endowments: The Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025 (UMEED Act)
Under **Article 25(2)(a)** and **Article 26(d)**, the secular administration of charitable and religious endowments falls squarely within the legislative competence of **Parliament** and **State Legislatures** under **Entry 28 of List III (Concurrent List: Charities and charitable institutions, charitable and religious endowments and religious institutions)**. Exercising this power, Parliament enacted the **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025**—rechristening the principal 1995 statute as the **Unified Waqf Management, Empowerment, Efficiency and Development Act, 1995 (UMEED Act)**—which fundamentally restructured the legal regime governing Islamic endowments in India:

*   **Prospective Omission of "Waqf by User" and Five-Year Practicing Requirement:** The **2025 Amendment** omits the statutory concept of **"Waqf by user"** (where property acquires Waqf character solely through long-standing religious usage without a formal deed of dedication or *Waqfnama*) prospectively, while inserting a savings clause clarifying that existing "Waqf by user" properties already registered prior to the commencement of the **2025 Act** shall remain Waqf properties *unless* the property is in dispute or belongs to the Government. Furthermore, only a person who has been **practicing Islam for at least 5 years** and owns the property free of contrivance can dedicate property as a Waqf, and creation of a *Waqf-alal-aulad* (family Waqf) must not result in the denial of inheritance rights to female heirs.
*   **Repeal of Section 40 and Adjudication of Government Land Disputes:** The **2025 Act** completely **omits Section 40** of the 1995 Act, which had previously empowered the Waqf Board itself to conduct an inquiry and unilaterally decide whether a property belongs to the Waqf. Where a dispute arises as to whether a property claimed as Waqf is actually **Government property**, the power of inquiry is shifted away from the Waqf Survey Commissioner to a **senior State Government officer above the rank of District Collector**, upon whose report the revenue records are corrected directly.
*   **Pluralistic Composition of the Central Waqf Council and State Waqf Boards:** Grounding its intervention in **Article 25(2)(a)** and **Article 26(d)** on the premise that a statutory Waqf Board performs purely secular, financial, and administrative functions rather than religious rituals under **Article 26(b)**, the **2025 Act** mandates the inclusion of **at least two Muslim women**, representatives of backward classes among Muslims and non-Sunni/Shia sects (Bohras and Aghakhanis), and **two non-Muslim members** (excluding *ex-officio* members) on both the **Central Waqf Council** and **State Waqf Boards**.
*   **Restoration of High Court Appellate Review and the Limitation Act, 1963:** The **2025 Act** removes the statutory finality previously attached to decisions of the **Waqf Tribunal** (which had restricted aggrieved parties to narrow revisional jurisdiction), introducing a full **statutory right of appeal to the High Court within 90 days**, and withdraws the statutory exemption granted by the **1995 Act** so that the **Limitation Act, 1963** now applies squarely to all Waqf property claims. Constitutional challenges to the **UMEED Act, 2025** under **Articles 14, 25, and 26** (challenging the inclusion of non-Muslims on Waqf Boards as discriminatory when Hindu Endowment Acts restrict board membership to Hindus, and challenging the executive adjudication of government land titles) are currently under consideration before the Supreme Court.

To complete the structural framework of Indian secularism, **Articles 27 and 28** insulate the public exchequer and the public classroom from sectarian capture:

*   **Freedom from Payment of Taxes for Promotion of Any Particular Religion (Article 27):** *"No person shall be compelled to pay any taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination."* Two precise jurisprudential distinctions govern **Article 27**:
    1.  **Tax vs. Fee (*Shirur Mutt*, 1954):** **Article 27** prohibits the levy of a **tax** (a compulsory exaction for general public purposes without a *quid pro quo*), not a **fee**. The State is fully competent to levy a regulatory fee on religious endowments to defray the expenses of the secular government machinery supervising their administration.
    2.  **"Any Particular Religion" vs. Non-Discriminatory Secular Support (*Prafulla Kumar Swain*, *T.M.A. Pai*, and *Prafull Goradia v. Union of India, 2011*):** Unlike the US Establishment Clause, **Article 27** does not prohibit the State from spending general tax revenues for the non-discriminatory reconstruction of temples/mosques destroyed in riots, providing civic infrastructure and security for pilgrims (*Kumbh Mela*, *Amarnath Yatra*, or *Haj*), or granting financial aid to educational institutions run by all religious denominations equally, provided a substantial portion of tax proceeds is not specifically earmarked to promote **one particular religion** to the exclusion of others.
*   **Freedom as to Attendance at Religious Instruction in Educational Institutions (Article 28):** **Article 28** classifies educational institutions into **four distinct structural categories** (mapped in the schematic above). In **D.A.V. College v. State of Punjab (1971)** (5-Judge Bench) and **Ms. Aruna Roy v. Union of India (2002)**, the Supreme Court drew a crucial distinction between **"religious instruction"** and the **"academic study of religions"**: establishing a university department to conduct academic research into the life and philosophy of Guru Nanak (**D.A.V. College**) or introducing value-based education teaching the common moral truths of all world religions under the National Curriculum Framework (**Aruna Roy**) does not constitute dogmatic *"religious instruction"* prohibited by **Article 28(1)**, which only forbids imparting sectarian tenets, rituals, and catechism aimed at proselytizing students.

Having delineated the boundaries of individual conscience and denominational religious autonomy under **Articles 25–28**, the constitutional scheme pivots to **Articles 29 and 30**, where the framers reinforced India's pluralistic contract by vesting linguistic and religious minorities with affirmative institutional autonomy over their cultural heritage and educational institutions.

## III. Cultural and Educational Rights of Minorities: Pluralism, Regulatory Autonomy, and Statutory Incorporation (Articles 29–30)

### 7. Cultural Conservation and Non-Discrimination in Educational Access (Article 29)

#### Distinct Language, Script, or Culture (Article 29(1)) vs. Universal Citizen Non-Discrimination (Article 29(2))
During the **Constituent Assembly Debates**, after the **Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights and Minorities** headed by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** secured the historic renunciation of separate communal electorates in May 1949, **Articles 29 and 30** were enshrined as the bedrock constitutional guarantee assuring India's religious and linguistic minorities that majoritarian political democracy would never degenerate into cultural assimilation. However, despite the marginal heading *"Protection of interests of minorities,"* the text of **Article 29** intentionally extends far beyond numerical minorities:

*   **Right to Conserve Distinct Language, Script, or Culture (Article 29(1)):** *"Any section of the citizens residing in the territory of India or any part thereof having a distinct language, script or culture of its own shall have the right to conserve the same."*
    1.  **Citizenship Limitation & Majority Inclusion:** Unlike **Articles 25–28**, **Article 29** is restricted strictly to **citizens of India**. However, as Chief Justice **S.R. Das** explained in **In Re: The Kerala Education Bill (1958)** and the 11-Judge Bench reaffirmed in **T.M.A. Pai Foundation (2002)**, the use of the phrase **"Any section of the citizens"** in **Article 29(1)** covers **both minorities and the majority community** alike, provided that section possesses a distinct language, script, or culture.
    2.  **Absolute Character & Political Agitation:** Unlike **Article 19(1)(a)**, **Article 29(1)** is not subject to any express reasonable restrictions Clause. In **Jagdev Singh Sidhanti v. Pratap Singh Daulta (1964)** (5-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court held that the right to *"conserve"* a language under **Article 29(1)** includes the right of citizens to agitate politically for the protection of that language, overriding the corrupt-practice bar in **Section 123(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951**.
*   **Citizen Right Against Educational Admission Discrimination (Article 29(2)):** *"No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them."*
    1.  **Individual Citizen Right:** **Article 29(2)** is an individual right available to **every citizen** (whether belonging to a majority or minority community), not a collective group right. Notice that while **Article 15(1)** prohibits discrimination on grounds of *"religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth,"* **Article 29(2)** omits **sex** and **place of birth** (allowing single-sex colleges and institutional domicile preferences) while explicitly adding **language**.
    2.  **The *Champakam Dorairajan* Catalyst (1951):** In **State of Madras v. Smt. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)** (7-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court relied directly on **Article 29(2)** to strike down the Madras Communal G.O. that apportioned medical and engineering seats by caste/religion, which immediately triggered the **First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951**, inserting **Article 15(4)** to begin with the words: *"Nothing in this article or in clause (2) of article 29 shall prevent the State from making any special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes."*

### 8. The Architecture of Minority Educational Institutions (Article 30)

#### State-Level Unit Determination and the Eleven-Judge Synthesis (T.M.A. Pai Foundation, 2002)
Unlike **Article 29(1)**, **Article 30(1)** is confined exclusively to **"All minorities, whether based on religion or language,"** guaranteeing them the right to **establish and administer educational institutions of their choice**. **Article 30(1A)** (inserted by the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** when the Right to Property was deleted from Part III) ensures that if the State compulsorily acquires the property of a minority educational institution, the statutory compensation amount fixed by the State shall not restrict or abrogate the right guaranteed under **Article 30(1)** (mandating full market-value compensation so the minority can re-establish its institution). **Article 30(2)** prohibits the State, in granting financial aid, from discriminating against any educational institution on the ground that it is under the management of a religious or linguistic minority.

In the landmark 11-Judge Constitution Bench judgment in **T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002)**—the second-largest Bench in Supreme Court history after **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**—the Court definitively settled the unit of minority determination and the scope of **"of their choice"**:

*   **The State as the Unit for Both Linguistic and Religious Minorities:** Because the Constitution itself does not define the word **"minority,"** the 11-Judge Bench held that since reorganization of States in India has been effected along linguistic lines and **Education** was transferred from the State List to **Entry 25 of the Concurrent List** by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, the geographical and political unit for determining who constitutes a **linguistic OR religious minority** under **Article 30(1)** is the **State** in relation to the particular legislation being challenged, and **not the whole of India** (affirming **D.A.V. College v. State of Punjab, 1971**, where Hindus were recognized as a religious and linguistic minority in the State of Punjab). Only where a Central Act applies exclusively to a central institution or nationally must the unit be evaluated accordingly, though in **Bal Patil v. Union of India (2005)** and subsequent proceedings, the **T.M.A. Pai** state-unit rule remains the governing constitutional baseline.
*   **Scope of "Educational Institutions of Their Choice":** In **In Re: The Kerala Education Bill (1958)** (7-Judge Bench) and **St. Xavier's College v. State of Gujarat (1974)** (9-Judge Bench), affirmed in **T.M.A. Pai (2002)**, the Court held that **"of their choice"** means a religious or linguistic minority is **not restricted** to establishing institutions that teach only its own religion, theology, or language; it has the absolute constitutional right under **Article 30(1)** to establish secular general universities, medical colleges, engineering institutions, and schools imparting secular education.

#### Regulatory Autonomy, Aided vs. Unaided Matrix, and Quota Immunity (St. Stephen’s, Islamic Academy, P.A. Inamdar, Pramati)
Across four major Constitution Bench interventions—**St. Stephen's College v. University of Delhi (1992, 5-Judge)**, **T.M.A. Pai Foundation (2002, 11-Judge)**, **Islamic Academy of Education v. State of Karnataka (2003, 5-Judge)**, and **P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005, 7-Judge)**, culminating in **Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India (2014, 5-Judge)**—the Supreme Court constructed a differentiated regulatory architecture balancing **Article 30(1)** management autonomy against **Article 29(2)** and academic excellence:

*   **The Dual Test of Permissible Regulation (*Rev. Sidhajbhai Sabhai*, 1962 & *T.M.A. Pai*, 2002):** While the text of **Article 30(1)** contains no express restriction clauses like **Article 19(2)–(6)** or **Article 25(1)**, the right to administer is not absolute and **does not include the right to maladminister**. Any State or University regulation imposed on a minority institution must satisfy a **Dual Test**: (i) the regulation must be reasonable and regulative of the educational character of the institution, **conducive to making the institution an effective vehicle of education for the minority community** (such as minimum qualifications for teachers, syllabus standards, sanitation, and fair service rules); and (ii) it must not annihilate or displace the management autonomy of the minority Governing Body (for instance, in **Secy., Malankara Syrian Catholic College v. T. Jose, 2007**, the Court affirmed that a minority institution has broad autonomy in selecting its Principal/Headmaster as the key administrative anchor of its ethos, though in **Sk. Mohd. *   **Reconciling Article 29(2) and Article 30(1) in Aided Institutions (*St. Stephen's* Overruled in Part by *T.M.A. Pai*):** In **St. Stephen's College v. University of Delhi (1992)**, a 5-Judge Bench had imposed a rigid **50% ceiling** on minority student admissions in aided minority institutions, requiring the remaining 50% to be filled purely on merit by non-minority applicants under **Article 29(2)**. In **T.M.A. Pai (2002)**, the 11-Judge Bench overruled this rigid 50% cap as arbitrary: it held that while receiving State aid activates **Article 29(2)** so that an aided minority institution cannot completely bar non-minority students, **Article 29(2) cannot be read to obliterate Article 30(1)**. Therefore, the State Government may prescribe a flexible percentage of non-minority admissions based on the population of the minority in the State and the specific educational needs of that community.
*   **Unaided Institutions and the Abolition of Cross-Subsidy Quotas (*Islamic Academy* 2003 & *P.A. Inamdar* 2005):** After **T.M.A. Pai** abolished the *Unni Krishnan (1993)* scheme of "free seats" and "payment seats" in unaided private professional colleges, a 5-Judge Bench in **Islamic Academy of Education (2003)** directed states to constitute two permanent committees headed by retired High Court Judges to oversee admissions and prevent profiteering/capitation fees. Clarifying **T.M.A. Pai** further, a 7-Judge Bench in **P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005)** categorically ruled that the State **cannot impose its reservation policy or appropriate any quota of seats** in **unaided** minority (or non-minority) professional educational institutions, as doing so amounts to nationalization of seats without compensation. However, in **Christian Medical College Vellore Association v. Union of India (2020)**, a 3-Judge Bench upheld the mandatory applicability of the centralized **National Eligibility-cum-Entrance Test (NEET)** to aided and unaided minority medical colleges, holding that a single national merit examinationmerely ensures academic transparency and prevents profiteering without stripping the minority college of its right to admit students from the NEET merit list.
*   **Constitutional Immunity from Article 15(5), Article 15(6), and the RTE Act (*Pramati Educational Trust*, 2014):** To overturn **P.A. Inamdar (2005)** with respect to non-minority private colleges, Parliament enacted the **93rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2005**, inserting **Article 15(5)** to enable reservations in private aided and unaided educational institutions, while **explicitly exempting minority educational institutions referred to in Article 30(1)** (an exemption later mirrored in **Article 15(6)** for the **10% EWS Reservation** under the **103rd Amendment, 2019**). In **Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India (2014)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench not only upheld the **Article 15(5)** exemption for minority institutions, but went further to hold that the entire **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009** ( enacted under **Article 21A**, including **Section 12(1)(c)** mandating 25% admission of disadvantaged neighborhood children in Class I) is **inapplicable to both aided and unaided minority schools**, reasoning that compelling a minority school to admit 25% non-minority neighborhood students would abrogate its **Article 30(1)** minority character and violate the **Basic Structure**. (Note: In September 2025, a 2-Judge Bench in *Anjuman Ishaat-e-Taleem Trust v. State of Maharashtra* questioned whether *Pramati*'s blanket exemption of minority schools from the entire RTE Act—even basic learning and teacher-qualification norms—requires reconsideration by a larger Constitution Bench).

### 9. Constitutional Indicia of "Established and Administered": Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal (2024)

#### Overruling Azeez Basha (1967): Statutory Incorporation vs. Genesis of Establishment
For fifty-seven years, the jurisprudence of **Article 30(1)** was constrained by the 5-Judge Constitution Bench ruling in **S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India (1967)**. In **Azeez Basha**, the Court held that the **Aligarh Muslim University (AMU)** was not a minority educational institution under **Article 30(1)** because: (i) the words *"establish and administer"* in **Article 30(1)** must be read conjunctively; and (ii) although the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College** had been founded by **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** and the Muslim community in 1877, it was elevated into a University and given power to confer degrees by a Central Statute—the **Aligarh Muslim University Act, 1920**—meaning AMU was "established" by the Central Legislature and not by the Muslim minority. When Parliament enacted the **AMU (Amendment) Act, 1981** to explicitly restore AMU's historical definition as an institution established by the Muslims of India, the Allahabad High Court in **Naresh Agarwal (2006)** struck down the 1981 Amendment as a brazen legislative usurpation of the judicial factual finding in **Azeez Basha**.

On **November 8, 2024**, a **7-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Aligarh Muslim University Through its Registrar Faizan Mustafa v. Naresh Agarwal (2024)**—by a **4:3 majority** authored by Chief Justice **D.Y. Chandrachud** (joined by Sanjiv Khanna, J.B. Pardiwala, and Manoj Misra, JJ.; with Surya Kant, Dipankar Datta, and Satish Chandra Sharma, JJ. dissenting)—**overruled *S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India (1967)*** and fundamentally reconstructed the jurisprudence of **Article 30(1)**:

#### The Four-Pronged Holistic Test of Establishment and Administration
The 7-Judge majority in **Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal (2024)** laid down five transformative constitutional principles governing **Article 30(1)**:

*   **Distinction Between "Genesis of Establishment" and "Legal Incorporation":** The majority held that **Azeez Basha (1967)** committed a fatal formalist error by conflating the *historical genesis* of an institution's establishment with the *statutory mechanism* of its legal incorporation. Prior to the Constitution (and subsequently under **Section 22 of the University Grants Commission Act, 1956**), no educational institution could call itself a "University" or confer legally recognized degrees without being incorporated by or under a Central or State Act. If statutory incorporation automatically meant that the institution was "established" by the Legislature rather than the minority community, then no minority community could ever establish a full-fledged University without surrendering its Fundamental Right under **Article 30(1)**—a direct violation of the **Unconstitutional Conditions Doctrine** established in **In Re: The Kerala Education Bill (1958)**.
*   **Protection of Pre-Constitution Institutions:** Rejecting the argument that **Article 30(1)** applies only to institutions founded after January 26, 1950 (when the constitutional concept of "minority" came into legal force), the majority ruled that **Article 30(1)** is both retrospective and prospective in its protective sweep: educational institutions founded by religious or linguistic minorities during the colonial era before 1950 are fully entitled to **Article 30(1)** protection.
*   **The Four Holistic Indicia of "Establishment":** To determine whether an educational institution was **"established"** by a minority under **Article 30(1)**, courts must pierce the statutory veil and examine the historical genesis across four cumulative parameters:
    1.  **Ideation / Brainchild:** Who conceived the idea of establishing the institution, and did the originators belong to the minority community?
    2.  **Purpose:** Was the institution founded predominantly for the benefit and educational upliftment of that minority community?
    3.  **Implementation (Resources, Land, and Funds):** What steps were taken to translate the idea into reality—specifically, who raised the funds, collected public subscriptions, acquired the land, and built the physical assets? (Mere receipt of government grants or donations from non-minority philanthropists does not negate minority establishment if the primary impetus came from the minority).
    4.  **Initial Administrative Structure:** Did the charter or statute at the point of inception vest operational direction in a body reflecting the minority character of the institution?
*   **De-Coupling Current Administration from the Condition Precedent of Minority Status:** Crucially, while the majority affirmed that the words *"establish and administer"* in **Article 30(1)** are linked, it clarified that **"administer" is the consequence/right flowing from establishment, not a rigid condition precedent requiring 100% exclusive management by minority members at all times**. A minority institution may choose, in the exercise of its own choice under **Article 30(1)**, to appoint qualified non-minority administrators, Vice-Chancellors, or Governing Body members without forfeiting its minority character.
*   **The Three Dissents (Surya Kant, Dipankar Datta, and Satish Chandra Sharma, JJ.):** The three dissenting judges upheld **Azeez Basha (1967)** (or modified it narrowly), reasoning that in 1920 the Muslim founders of MAO College faced a clear choice—either remain a private affiliated minority college or surrender their private property and autonomy to the British Crown to create an Imperial Central University (**Entry 63 of the Union List** clearly designates AMU and Banaras Hindu University as institutions of national importance)—and having consciously chosen statutory surrender to the Crown in 1920, AMU could not reclaim private minority status under **Article 30(1)**. Having laid down the four-pronged legal test and overruled **Azeez Basha**, the 7-Judge Bench remitted the factual determination of AMU's historical charter and the validity of the 2006 Allahabad High Court judgment to a regular Bench.

## IV. The Heart and Soul of the Constitution: Prerogative Writs and Remedial Architecture (Article 32 vs. Article 226)

### 10. Structural Mechanics of Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

#### Basic Structure Entrenchment, Epistolary Locus Standi, and Parliamentary Empowerment (Article 32(1)–(4))
Speaking in the **Constituent Assembly** on December 9, 1948, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** famously declared of **Article 32** (then Draft Article 25): *"If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important—an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity—I could not refer to any other article except this one. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it."* Recognizing the maxim *ubi jus ibi remedium* (where there is a right, there is a remedy), the framers refused to leave the enforcement of **Part III** to ordinary civil suits or executive discretion; instead, they made the **remedy for enforcing Fundamental Rights a Fundamental Right in itself**.

The structural architecture of **Article 32** is divided across four interdependent clauses:

*   **Article 32(1) (The Guaranteed Right to Move the Supreme Court):** *"The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed."*
    1.  **Mandatory Duty vs. Discretionary Refusal (*Romesh Thappar*, 1950):** Unlike the discretionary certiorari or special leave jurisdiction, the Supreme Court in **Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950)** (6-Judge Bench) and **K.K. Kochunni v. State of Madras (1959)** held that because **Article 32(1)** is a guaranteed Fundamental Right, the Supreme Court has been constituted as the **protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights**, and it cannot consistently with the responsibility so laid upon it refuse to entertain applications seeking protection against infringements of such rights merely because the petitioner did not first approach the High Court under **Article 226** or because disputed questions of fact are involved (though as a rule of judicial self-restraint in **P.N. Kumar v. Municipal Corp. of Delhi, 1987**, the Court frequently encourages approaching the High Court first unless the issue is of pan-India constitutional significance).
    2.  **"Appropriate Proceedings" and Epistolary Jurisdiction:** In **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)** and **S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)**, the Court held that the deliberate choice of the phrase **"appropriate proceedings"** in **Article 32(1)** specifies that the appropriateness of the proceeding must be judged by reference to the *purpose* of enforcing Fundamental Rights, not rigid adversarial pleadings—thereby providing the textual anchor for **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**, relaxation of *locus standi*, and **Epistolary Jurisdiction** (converting letters/postcards from marginalized citizens into writ petitions).
    3.  **Compensatory Public Law Jurisprudence (*Rudul Sah*, 1983 & *Nilabati Behera*, 1993):** Under **Article 32(1)–(2)**, the Supreme Court's remedial power is **not merely injunctive or preventative, but also palliative and compensatory**. In **Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983)** (where a prisoner was illegally detained for 14 years after acquittal) and **Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993)** (custodial death), the Court established that in exercise of its public law jurisdiction under **Articles 32 and 226**, the Court can award **monetary compensation** against the State for gross violations of **Article 21**, wholly unrestricted by the archaic doctrine of **Sovereign Immunity** (which applies, if at all, only to private law tort suits).
*   **Article 32(2) (Plenary Writ-Issuing Power):** *"The Supreme Court shall have power to issue directions or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by this Part."* The use of the words **"directions or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of..."** frees the Indian Supreme Court from the archaic procedural technicalities that historically shackled English common-law prerogative writs in the King's Bench Division, empowering the Court to mould relief dynamically, including issuing **Continuing Mandamus** (**Vineet Narain v. Union of India, 1998**).
*   **Article 32(3) (Parliamentary Empowerment of Subordinate Courts):** Without prejudice to the powers conferred on the Supreme Court by clauses (1) and (2), **Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise within the local limits of its jurisdiction all or any of the powers exercisable by the Supreme Court under clause (2)**.
    *   *Critical Structural Caveat:* The phrase **"any other court"** in **Article 32(3)** refers to **Subordinate / District Courts** (or specialized statutory tribunals, as held in **Sampath Kumar, 1987** subject to **L. Chandra Kumar, 1997**), **NOT High Courts**, because High Courts already derive their plenary, independent writ jurisdiction directly from the Constitution under **Article 226** (which cannot be curtailed even by a Constitutional Amendment per the 7-Judge Bench in **L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India, 1997**, declaring judicial review under both **Article 32 and Article 226** an inviolable feature of the **Basic Structure**). To date, Parliament has not enacted a general law empowering District Courts to issue all five prerogative writs under **Article 32(3)**.
*   **Article 32(4) (Bar Against Unconstitutional Suspension):** *"The right guaranteed by this article shall not be suspended except as otherwise provided for by this Constitution."* The sole constitutional mechanism for suspending the right to move any court for the enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights is a Presidential Order issued under **Article 359** during the operation of a **National Emergency under Article 352**—and since the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978**, **Article 359(1)** explicitly bars the President from ever suspending the enforcement of **Article 20** and **Article 21** (consigning the infamous 4:1 majority ruling in **ADM Jabalpur v. Shivakant Shukla, 1976** to history, before it was formally overruled by the 9-Judge Bench in **K.S. Puttaswamy I, 2017**).

#### Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto

1.  **Habeas Corpus (*"To have the body of"*):** The quintessential bulwark of personal liberty against arbitrary detention. When a person is detained by the State or by a private individual without legal justification, the Court commands the detaining authority to produce the body of the detained person (though in **Kanu Sanyal v. District Magistrate, Darjeeling, 1974**, the Supreme Court clarified that physical production of the body in the courtroom is a procedural feature, not the core of the writ, which can be adjudicated by examining the legality of the detention record).
    *   *Unique Scope:* It is the **only writ** that can be issued against **both public authorities and private individuals** (e.g., illegal confinement by family members in inter-caste/inter-faith marriages as in **Shafin Jahan, 2018**, or child custody disputes).
    *   *Locus Standi & Res Judicata:* The rule of strict *locus standi* does not apply (since the detenu is physically incapacitated from approaching the Court), and as held in **Ghulam Sarwar v. Union of India (1967)**, the technical bar of **res judicata** does not prevent filing a subsequent **Habeas Corpus** petition in the Supreme Court under **Article 32** on fresh grounds after dismissal by a High Court under **Article 226**.
2.  **Mandamus (*"We Command"*):** A judicial command issued by a Constitutional Court to a public official, public corporation, tribunal, inferior court, or the Government, asking them to perform a **public duty forged by statute or the Constitution** which they have refused or failed to perform.
    *   *Threshold Requirements:* There must exist a **legal right** in the petitioner and a corresponding **mandatory public duty** (not a purely discretionary power) in the respondent, typically preceded by a demand for justice and its refusal.
    *   *Constitutional Immunities and Bars:* A writ of **Mandamus cannot be issued**: (i) against a private individual or body (unless entrusted with a statutory public duty); (ii) to enforce departmental instructions that lack statutory force; (iii) to enforce a purely private contractual obligation; (iv) **against the President of India or the Governor of a State** in respect of the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of their office (**Article 361(1)**, reaffirmed in **Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India, 2006**, though the *mala fide* action of the Government based on gubernatorial reports remains reviewable); (v) against the Chief Justice of a High Court acting in a judicial capacity; or (vi) **to command the Legislature to enact a particular law** or command the Executive to bring an enacted statute into force (**A.K. Roy v. Union of India, 1982**, where the Court held that a Mandamus could not be issued to compel the Central Government to notify the enforcement date of **Section 3 of the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978**).
3.  **Prohibition (*"To Forbid"*):** Issued by a higher court (Supreme Court or High Court) to an **inferior court or tribunal** to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction that it does not legally possess. While **Mandamus** commands positive activity, **Prohibition** commands **inactivity**. Crucially, **Prohibition is strictly preventative**: it lies only while the judicial or quasi-judicial proceeding is **still pending** before the inferior tribunal, and cannot be issued against legislative bodies, purely administrative authorities, or private individuals.
4.  **Certiorari (*"To be Certified"* or *"To be Informed"*):** Issued by a higher court to an inferior court, tribunal, or quasi-judicial authority either to transfer a pending case to itself or, primarily, to **quash an order already passed** by the inferior body. While **Prohibition** is preventative (operating before the final order), **Certiorari** is both preventative and **curative** (operating after the order is pronounced to quash it).
    *   *Four Jurisprudential Grounds (*Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Ahmad Ishaque, 1955* & *Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan, 1964*):* Certiorari lies on four strict grounds: (i) **want or excess of jurisdiction**; (ii) **violation of the Principles of Natural Justice** (*Nemo judex in causa sua*—rule against bias, and *Audi alteram partem*—right to a fair hearing); (iii) **fraud or collusion**; or (iv) **error of law apparent on the face of the record** (as distinguished from a mere error of fact, because a writ court exercising Certiorari does not sit as a regular court of appeal to re-appreciate evidence).
    *   *Expansion to Administrative Action (*A.K. Kraipak*, 1969):* Historically, in **Province of Bombay v. Khushaldas S. Advani (1950)**, Certiorari was confined strictly to judicial and quasi-judicial bodies. However, in the landmark 5-Judge Bench decision in **A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1969)**, Justice **K.S. Hegde** held that the dividing line between an administrative power and a quasi-judicial power has become wafer-thin and is being gradually obliterated; consequently, whenever an **administrative decision adversely affects the civil rights of an individual**, the duty to act fairly in accordance with natural justice applies, rendering such administrative orders amenable to **Certiorari**.
    *   *Immunity of High Court Judicial Orders from Article 32 Certiorari (*Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar*, 1967 & *Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002):* In the 9-Judge Bench ruling in **Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra (1967)**, affirmed by the 5-Judge Bench in **Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002)**, the Supreme Court held that a judicial order passed by a **High Court** (a superior Court of Record under **Article 215**, not an "inferior court" to the Supreme Court in the writ hierarchy) can never violate Fundamental Rights and **cannot be challenged or quashed by a writ of Certiorari under Article 32** (it can only be challenged via appellate channels under **Articles 132–136**). However, in **Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath (2015)** (3-Judge Bench overruling *Surya Dev Rai, 2003*), the Court clarified that judicial orders of **Civil Courts** similarly are not amenable to **Certiorari** under **Article 226**, though they remain subject to High Court supervisory jurisdiction under **Article 227**.
5.  **Quo Warranto (*"By What Authority or Warrant"*):** Issued by the Court to inquire into the legality of the claim of a person to a **public office**, preventing an illegal usurper from continuing to hold a public post.
    *   *Four Mandatory Conditions (*University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao*, 1963):* (i) The office must be a **public office** in which the public has a substantive interest; (ii) the office must be created by **a Statute or by the Constitution itself**; (iii) the office must be of a **substantive character** (not merely a ministerial employment or tenure at the pleasure of a private master); and (iv) there must be a **contravention of a binding statutory or constitutional provision** in appointing such person to that office.
    *   *Relaxed Locus Standi:* Like **Habeas Corpus**, **Quo Warranto** is an exception to the strict rule of *locus standi*: **any public-minded citizen** can file a petition for **Quo Warranto** to oust an unqualified usurper from a constitutional or statutory office, even if the petitioner has suffered no personal injury and is not a rival contender for the post.

#### Structural Divergence: Supreme Court (Article 32) vs. High Court (Article 226) Writ Jurisdiction
A classic paradox of Indian constitutional design is that while the **Supreme Court** sits at the apex of the unified judicial pyramid, the writ jurisdiction of a **High Court under Article 226** is **substantively wider** in scope while being **territorially narrower** in reach than that of the Supreme Court under **Article 32**:

*   **Substantive Scope ("For Any Other Purpose"):** Under **Article 32**, the Supreme Court can issue writs **only** for the enforcement of **Fundamental Rights** conferred by **Part III**. Under **Article 226(1)**, a High Court can issue writs not only for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by **Part III**, but also **"for any other purpose"**—empowering High Courts to issue writs to enforce non-fundamental constitutional rights (such as **Article 265** illegal taxation or **Article 300A** right to property), statutory rights, and common-law administrative legality.
*   **Constitutional Right vs. Discretionary Remedy:** Because **Article 32** is itself situated inside **Part III**, approaching the Supreme Court upon a proven violation of a Fundamental Right is a **guaranteed Fundamental Right** (**Article 32(1)**). By contrast, **Article 226** is situated in **Part VI**; therefore, the remedy under **Article 226** is discretionary, and a High Court may decline to exercise its writ jurisdiction if an equally efficacious alternative statutory remedy exists (unless there is a violation of Fundamental Rights, a breach of natural justice, or a challenge to the *vires* of a statute, as held in **Whirlpool Corporation v. Registrar of Trade Marks, 1998**).
*   **Territorial Jurisdiction and Cause of Action (Article 226(2)):** The Supreme Court's writ under **Article 32** runs across the entire territory of India. A High Court's writ under **Article 226** can be issued: (i) under **Article 226(1)**, to any person, authority, or government whose seat is located **within the territorial jurisdiction** of that High Court; OR (ii) under **Article 226(2)** (inserted by the **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963** to overcome *Election Commission v. Saka Venkata Subba Rao, 1953*), to any government, authority, or person **even if its seat is outside the territorial jurisdiction** of that High Court, provided the **cause of action, wholly or in part, arises within its territorial jurisdiction** (**Kusum Ingots & Alloys Ltd. v. Union of India, 2004**).
*   **Application of Res Judicata Between Article 226 and Article 32 (*Daryao v. State of UP*, 1961):** In the landmark 5-Judge Constitution Bench ruling in **Daryao v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1961)**, Justice **P.B. Gajendragadkar** resolved the tension between the guaranteed nature of **Article 32** and the finality of judicial judgments (**Res Judicata**): if a petitioner first approaches a High Court under **Article 226** alleging infringement of a Fundamental Right and the High Court **dismisses the petition on merits by a speaking order** after a contested hearing, that decision operates as **Res Judicata**, barring the petitioner from filing a fresh original writ petition on the same facts before the Supreme Court under **Article 32** (the petitioner's only remedy is to file an **Appeal / Special Leave Petition under Article 136** against the High Court's judgment). However, **Res Judicata does NOT apply** if: (i) the High Court dismissed the **Article 226** petition *in limine* (summarily without a speaking order on merits), or on technical grounds of **laches** (delay) or availability of an **alternative remedy**; or (ii) the petition is for a writ of **Habeas Corpus** (**Ghulam Sarwar, 1967**).

## V. Coercive State Exceptions: Armed Forces Discipline, Martial Law Indemnity, and Exclusive Parliamentary Competence (Articles 33–35)

### 12. Abrogation of Fundamental Rights for Disciplined Forces (Article 33)

#### Parliamentary Monopoly, Extended Scope of "Forces," and Court-Martial Immunity
The final three articles of **Part III** (**Articles 33, 34, and 35**) form an institutional triad that calibrates the exceptions to Fundamental Rights required for national security, military discipline, martial crisis management, and uniform national legislation. Across all three articles, the **Constituent Assembly** vested **exclusive legislative competence in Parliament**, explicitly excluding State Legislatures even where the underlying subject matter (such as **Police** under **Entry 2 of List II** or **Public Order** under **Entry 1 of List II**) falls within the State List.

Under **Article 33**, **Parliament may, by law**, determine to what extent any of the Fundamental Rights conferred by **Part III** shall, in their application to four specific classes of personnel, be **restricted or abrogated** so as to ensure the **proper discharge of their duties and the maintenance of discipline among them**:
*   **Expansion by the 50th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1984:** Originally, **Article 33** was a single clause covering only the Armed Forces and Forces charged with the maintenance of public order. Following intelligence and telecommunications strikes in the early 1980s, Parliament enacted the **50th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1984**, restructuring **Article 33** into four exhaustive sub-clauses:
    1.  **Clause (a):** The members of the **Armed Forces** (Army, Navy, Air Force, and paramilitary forces functioning under military statutes).
    2.  **Clause (b):** The members of the **Forces charged with the maintenance of public order** (this includes State Police Forces despite Police being a State List subject—meaning only **Parliament**, and never a State Legislature, can enact a law restricting the Fundamental Rights of State Police personnel, such as the **Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1966**).
    3.  **Clause (c):** Persons employed in **any bureau or other organisation established by the State for purposes of intelligence or counter-intelligence** (e.g., IB, R&AW).
    4.  **Clause (d):** Persons employed in, or in connection with, the **telecommunication systems** set up for the purposes of any Force, bureau, or organisation referred to in clauses (a) to (c).
*   **Inclusion of Non-Combatants (*Ous Kutilingal Achudan Nair*, 1976) and Scope of Judicial Review (*Prithi Pal Singh Bedi*, 1982):**
    *   In **Ous Kutilingal Achudan Nair v. Union of India (1976)**, the Supreme Court held that the expression *"members of the Armed Forces"* in **Article 33(a)** is not restricted to uniformed combatants; it squarely includes **civilian non-combatant employees** ("camp followers" such as mechanics, cooks, barbers, tailors, and bootmakers) attached to defence establishments who are subject to the **Army Act, 1950**, upholding the denial of their right to form trade unions under **Article 19(1)(c)**.
    *   Exercising its power under **Article 33**, Parliament enacted the **Army Act, 1950**, the **Navy Act, 1957**, the **Air Force Act, 1950**, and the **Border Security Force Act, 1968**, restricting **Article 19** freedoms (speech, assembly, union formation, press communication). In **Ram Sarup v. Union of India (1965)** (5-Judge Bench) and **Lt. Col. Prithi Pal Singh Bedi v. Union of India (1982)**, the Supreme Court held that **each and every provision** of the **Army Act, 1950** is a law made by Parliament under **Article 33**, and even if a court-martial procedure deviates from the procedural due process of **Article 21** or the equality mandate of **Article 14**, it is constitutionally immunized by **Article 33** insofar as it relates to military discipline (additionally, **Article 136(2)** and **Article 227(4)** explicitly exclude tribunals constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces from the Supreme Court's SLP jurisdiction and the High Court's superintendence, though **High Court and Supreme Court Writ Jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 32 over Armed Forces Tribunal / Court-Martial orders remains intact** as part of the **Basic Structure**, affirmed by the 5-Judge Bench in **Union of India v. Major General Shri Kant Sharma, 2023 modification / L. Chandra Kumar framework**).

### 13. Martial Law and Acts of Indemnity (Article 34)

#### Uncodified Martial Law, Retrospective Parliamentary Indemnity, and Structural Contrast with Article 352 National Emergency
Borrowed from English common law, **Article 34** provides for restrictions on Fundamental Rights while **Martial Law** is in force in any area within the territory of India: *"Notwithstanding anything in the foregoing provisions of this Part, Parliament may by law indemnify any person in the service of the Union or of a State or any other person in respect of any act done by him in connection with the maintenance or restoration of order in any area within the territory of India where martial law was in force or validate any sentence passed, punishment inflicted, forfeiture ordered or other act done under martial law in such area."*

The constitutional mechanics of **Article 34** rest on four critical legal propositions:

*   **Implicit Constitutional Recognition Without Express Authorization:** Notice that **Article 34** does **not** explicitly define "Martial Law," nor is there any separate article in the Constitution expressly authorizing the President to proclaim Martial Law. Following the British common-law doctrine (where Martial Law signifies the suspension of ordinary civil courts and the substitution of military rule when civil authority is paralyzed by insurrection or riot, governed by the maxim *Salus populi est suprema lex*), the **Constituent Assembly** assumed that the sovereign Executive inherently possesses the power to declare Martial Law in an extreme localized breakdown of civil order (additionally relatable to **Article 355**, which obligates the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance).
*   **Ex-Post Facto Parliamentary Act of Indemnity:** Because military officers acting under Martial Law step outside ordinary statutory procedures—detaining insurgents, imposing curfews, requisitions, and conducting summary military trials—they would automatically become liable to civil suits for damages and criminal prosecutions in ordinary courts the moment Martial Law is lifted. **Article 34** empowers **Parliament** (and **only Parliament**) to enact a retrospective **Act of Indemnity** shielding any public servant or private citizen who acted *"in connection with the maintenance or restoration of order"* during Martial Law, and validating any sentence, punishment, or forfeiture ordered by military tribunals, overriding the Fundamental Rights in **Part III** (including **Articles 14, 19, 20, 21, and 22**).
*   **Judicial Review Over Indemnity Acts:** Although **Article 34** begins with a *non-obstante* clause (*"Notwithstanding anything in the foregoing provisions of this Part"*), legal scholars and obiter observations in **ADM Jabalpur (1976)** (specifically Justice **H.R. Khanna's** celebrated dissent, later constitutionalized in **Puttaswamy I, 2017**) emphasize that an **Act of Indemnity** passed under **Article 34** remains subject to judicial review on two grounds: (i) whether Martial Law was *in fact* legitimately in force in that area out of genuine necessity; and (ii) whether the impugned act was done **bona fide** *"in connection with the maintenance or restoration of order"* (as opposed to gratuitous *mala fide* atrocities). Notably, the **Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 (AFSPA)** is **not** a declaration of Martial Law under **Article 34** because under AFSPA, civil government and ordinary courts continue to function while the Armed Forces operate *"in aid of the civil power"* under **Entry 2A of List I** (upheld by the 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Naga People's Movement of Human Rights v. Union of India, 1998**, and subjected to mandatory criminal investigation for excessive retaliatory force in **Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association v. Union of India, 2016**).

| Structural Parameter | Martial Law (Article 34) | National Emergency (Article 352) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Specific Grounds of Imposition** | Extreme breakdown of law and order, riot, insurrection, or violent resistance where ordinary civil administration collapses. | Strictly limited to three grounds: **War**, **External Aggression**, or **Armed Rebellion**. |
| **Status of Civil Administration & Ordinary Courts** | Ordinary civil administration and **ordinary courts of law are suspended** and replaced temporarily by military tribunals and military command. | Ordinary civil government and **ordinary courts of law continue to function** normally. |
| **Scope of Constitutional Impact** | Affects **only Fundamental Rights** in the specific area under Martial Law via subsequent Parliamentary Indemnity. | Affects **Fundamental Rights (Arts 358–359)**, **Centre-State Legislative and Financial Relations (Arts 250, 353, 354)**, and **Lok Sabha/Assembly Tenure (Art 83(2))**. |

| **Impact on Articles 20 and 21** | An Act of Indemnity under **Article 34** textually overrides *"the foregoing provisions of this Part"* to validate military sentences. | Under **Article 359(1)** (post-**44th Amendment, 1978**), the enforcement of **Articles 20 and 21 can NEVER be suspended**. |

### 14. Exclusive Parliamentary Competence to Effectuate Part III (Article 35)

#### Federal Pre-emption of Penal and Remedial Legislation under Part III
To prevent Fundamental Rights and their concomitant penal sanctions from varying from State to State across the Indian federation, **Article 35** overrides the normal federal distribution of legislative powers in the **Seventh Schedule** (**Article 246**) and vests **exclusive legislative competence in Parliament** (explicitly barring State Legislatures) over two distinct baskets of constitutional mandates:

*   **Article 35(a)(i) (Exclusive Non-Penal Empowering Laws):** Parliament alone shall have power, and the Legislature of a State shall not have power, to make laws with respect to any of the matters which under four specific provisions of **Part III** may be provided for by law made by Parliament:
    1.  **Article 16(3):** Prescribing residence within a State or Union Territory as a condition for certain classes of public employment under that State/UT (pursuant to which Parliament enacted the **Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957**, now expired except as superseded by **Article 371D** for Andhra Pradesh/Telangana).
    2.  **Article 32(3):** Empowering any court other than the Supreme Court and High Courts to issue prerogative writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights within its local limits.
    3.  **Article 33:** Restricting or abrogating Fundamental Rights in their application to the Armed Forces, Police, Intelligence, and associated Telecom personnel.
    4.  **Article 34:** Indemnifying any person for acts done during the operation of Martial Law.
*   **Article 35(a)(ii) (Exclusive Penal Laws for Part III Offences):** Parliament alone shall have power, and State Legislatures shall have zero power, to make laws **for prescribing punishment for those acts which are declared to be offences under this Part**—specifically:
    1.  **Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability):** Enforced via the **Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955** (renamed the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** in 1976) and the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**.
    2.  **Article 23(1) (Prohibition of Human Trafficking, *Begar*, and Forced Labor):** Enforced via the **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956**, the **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976**, and the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**.
*   **Article 35(b) (Continuance and Adaptation of Pre-Constitution Laws):** Any law in force immediately before the commencement of the Constitution in the territory of India with respect to any of the matters referred to in clause (a) (such as pre-1950 provincial temple-entry or anti-untouchability statutes, or colonial military codes) shall continue in force until altered, repealed, or amended **exclusively by Parliament** (read with **Article 372**).

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 6: From Justiciable Liberties and Remedial Writs to the Property-Socialism Dialectic and Directive Principles
With **Article 35**, the **Constituent Assembly** completed the edifice of **Part III**—securing individual bodily emancipation (**Articles 23–24**), spiritual conscience and denominational pluralism (**Articles 25–30**), guaranteed judicial enforcement via prerogative writs (**Article 32**), and calibrated national-security exceptions (**Articles 33–35**). Yet, as **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** warned on November 25, 1949, a regime of formal political and civil liberties (**Part III**) resting atop a deeply unequal social and economic structure would inevitably collapse unless paired with substantive socioeconomic democracy. This structural imperative propels our inquiry directly into **Part 6**, where we examine the **Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51)**, **Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)**, the **Uniform Civil Code (Article 44)**, and the 75-year constitutional war between **Part III** and **Part IV** over the **Right to Property (Articles 31, 31A, 31B, 31C, and 300A)**—culminating in the landmark November 2024 **9-Judge Constitution Bench** judgment in **Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra** redefining the boundaries of **Article 31C** and *"material resources of the community"* under **Article 39(b)**.
