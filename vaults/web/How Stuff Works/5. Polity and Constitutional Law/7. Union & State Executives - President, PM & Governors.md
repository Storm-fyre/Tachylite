# Part 7: The Union and State Executives: President, Vice-President, Governors, Prime Minister, and Council of Ministers

## I. The Union Executive Architecture: The President, Electoral Calculus, and Westminster Aid and Advice (Articles 52–78)

### 1. Constitutional Status of the President, Electoral College Mechanics, and Impeachment (Articles 52–62, 71)

#### Republican Head of State vs. Westminster Nominal Executive (Articles 52–53)
The constitutional design of the Union Executive in **Part V (Chapter I, Articles 52–78)** resolves a foundational tension between republican statehood and parliamentary democracy. During the Constituent Assembly Debates, K.T. Shah advocated an American-style presidential executive separated from the legislature, arguing that fixed-tenure executive independence would insulate administration from parliamentary factionalism. Conversely, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, moving the Draft Constitution on **November 4, 1948**, drew a sharp structural distinction between the daily assessment of executive responsibility (achieved through parliamentary Question Hour, adjournment motions, and no-confidence votes) and the periodic assessment of responsibility at fixed electoral intervals, deliberately choosing daily parliamentary accountability over fixed-term executive stability to prevent autocratic consolidation.

Consequently, while **Article 52** mandates that *"There shall be a President of India"* and **Article 53(1)** vests the executive power of the Union in the President (to be exercised either directly or through officers subordinate to him in accordance with the Constitution), the President occupies the position of the **Head of the State** rather than the **Head of the Executive**. As Dr. Ambedkar summarized, the President represents the Nation but does not rule the Nation; his constitutional role is analogous to the British monarch under the Westminster parliamentary cabinet model, stripped of hereditary status and anchored in indirect republican election.

*   **Vesting of Supreme Command (Article 53(2)):** Without prejudice to the generality of **Article 53(1)**, the supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union is vested in the President, but its exercise is explicitly made *"regulated by law."* Unlike the United States President, whose Commander-in-Chief power under Article II, Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution carries autonomous operational prerogatives, the Indian President’s military command is structurally subordinated to both parliamentary legislation and the aid and advice of the Union Council of Ministers under **Article 74(1)**.
*   **Preservation of Statutory Functions (Article 53(3)):** Nothing in **Article 53** transfers to the President any functions conferred by existing law on the Government of any State or other authority, nor does it prevent Parliament from conferring by law functions on authorities other than the President.
*   **Executive Power Co-extensiveness (Article 73):** Subject to constitutional provisions, the executive power of the Union extends under **Article 73(1)(a)** to all matters with respect to which Parliament has power to make laws (**List I — Union List**), and under **Article 73(1)(b)** to the exercise of rights, authority, and jurisdiction exercisable by the Government of India by virtue of any treaty or agreement. Under the **Proviso to Article 73(1)**, Union executive power does not extend to matters in the **Concurrent List (List III)** unless expressly provided in the Constitution or in any law made by Parliament, preserving the default executive administration of Concurrent subjects for the States under **Article 162**.

#### The Electoral College and Proportional Representation Calculus (Articles 54–55, 1971 Census Freeze)
Because the President is only the nominal constitutional head while real executive authority vests in a Council of Ministers accountable to the Lok Sabha, the Constituent Assembly rejected direct election by universal adult suffrage. Direct election of a nominal head by hundreds of millions of voters would have created an alternative pole of democratic legitimacy, inviting inevitable constitutional deadlock between a directly elected President and a Prime Minister commanding a parliamentary majority. Simultaneously, to prevent the President from becoming a mere nominee of the ruling party in Parliament, **Article 54** constructs a federal **Electoral College** comprising:
1.  The **elected members** of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha); and
2.  The **elected members** of the Legislative Assemblies of the States (including the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Puducherry, inserted via the **70th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992** effective from June 1, 1995).

**Nominated members** of the Rajya Sabha, the Lok Sabha (prior to the abolition of Anglo-Indian nomination via the **104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019**), State Legislative Assemblies, and **all members (elected and nominated) of State Legislative Councils (Vidhan Parishads)**, as well as members of dissolved Assemblies at the time of the election, are excluded from the Electoral College. Under **Article 71(4)** (inserted by the **11th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1961**, following the challenge in *Dr. N.B. Khare v. Election Commission of India*, 1957), the election of a person as President or Vice-President cannot be called in question on the ground of the existence of any vacancy for whatever reason among the members of the Electoral College, even if an entire State Legislative Assembly stands dissolved under **Article 356** or upon expiry of its term (reaffirmed by the 7-Judge Constitution Bench in ***In Re: Presidential Poll*, 1974**).

**Article 55** operationalizes two mathematical principles: **uniformity in the scale of representation of different States** (*inter se* state weighting based on demographic strength) and **parity between the States as a whole and the Union** (ensuring that the aggregate vote value of all elected MPs equals the aggregate vote value of all elected MLAs across the Union).

*   **Value of the Vote of an MLA (Article 55(2)(a) & (b)):** Determined by dividing the total population of the State by the total number of elected members in the State Legislative Assembly, and then dividing the quotient by $1,000$ (if the remainder is $500$ or more, the vote value is increased by $1$).
*   **The 1971 Census Population Freeze:** Under the **Explanation to Article 55**, originally the population meant the figure ascertained at the preceding census. However, to prevent Southern and progressive States that successfully implemented family planning programs from losing electoral weight in presidential elections relative to States with high fertility rates, the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** froze the population baseline at the **1971 Census** until the year 2000. Subsequently, the **84th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001** extended this **1971 Census freeze** until the relevant figures for the **first census taken after the year 2026** have been published.
*   **Value of the Vote of an MP (Article 55(2)(c)):** Determined by dividing the sum total of the vote values of all elected MLAs of all States (including Delhi and Puducherry) by the total number of elected members of both Houses of Parliament ($543 + 233 = 776$), disregarding fractions below one-half and counting fractions of one-half or more as one.
*   **Single Transferable Vote and Quota (Article 55(3)):** Unlike the First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) plurality system where a candidate can win with a minority of votes in a multi-cornered contest, presidential election uses **Proportional Representation by means of the Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV)** by secret ballot (governed by the **Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952**, under which a presidential candidate requires **50 electors as proposers and 50 electors as seconders**, and the Tenth Schedule anti-defection whip does not apply). To be declared elected, a candidate must secure an absolute majority of valid vote values, calculated as the **Electoral Quota**:
    $$\text{Electoral Quota} = \left\lfloor \frac{\text{Total Value of Valid Votes Polled}}{2} \right\rfloor + 1$$
    If no candidate reaches the quota in the first count of first-preference votes, the candidate with the lowest vote value is eliminated, and their ballots are transferred to the remaining candidates according to the second preferences marked thereon, repeating until a candidate crosses the quota.

#### Conditions of Office, Term, Electoral Disputes, and Impeachment for "Violation of the Constitution" (Articles 56–62, 71)
Under **Article 56(1)**, the President holds office for a term of **five years** from the date on which he enters upon his office, and continues to hold office notwithstanding the expiration of his term until his successor enters upon his office (**Article 56(1)(c)**; there is no situation in which the Vice-President acts as President merely because the successor's election is delayed beyond five years, though **Article 62(1)** mandates that an election to fill a vacancy caused by expiration of term *shall* be completed before the expiration of the term). A President is eligible for re-election any number of times (**Article 57**), unlike the two-term limit under the **22nd Amendment to the U.S. Constitution**.

*   **Qualifications and Conditions (Articles 58–60):** Under **Article 58**, a candidate must be a citizen of India, have completed **35 years of age**, be qualified for election as a member of the **Lok Sabha**, and hold no **Office of Profit** under the Union, State, or local authority (with an Explanation exempting the sitting President, Vice-President, Governor, and Union/State Ministers). Under **Article 60**, the President takes an oath before the **Chief Justice of India** (or in his absence, the senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court) to *" faithfully execute the office"* and to *"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law."*
*   **Exclusive Supreme Court Jurisdiction over Election Disputes (Article 71):** All doubts and disputes arising out of or in connection with the election of a President or Vice-President are inquired into and decided **exclusively by the Supreme Court** (sitting as a 5-Judge Bench under the Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952), whose decision is final (**Article 71(1)**). An election petition can only be filed by a candidate or by **20 or more electors** joined together as petitioners within 30 days of the declaration of results. Under **Article 71(2)**, if the Supreme Court declares the election of a person as President or Vice-President void, **acts done by him** in the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of his office on or before the date of the Supreme Court’s decision **are not invalidated**. (Notice that the **39th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975** had temporarily stripped the Supreme Court of this jurisdiction under Article 71, vesting it in a parliamentary body, before the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** restored the original Article 71).
*   **Quasi-Judicial Impeachment Architecture (Article 61):** The President may resign at any time by writing under his hand addressed to the **Vice-President** (**Article 56(1)(a)**), who must forthwith communicate it to the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha** (**Article 56(2)**). Otherwise, the President can only be removed before the expiry of his term by **impeachment for "violation of the Constitution"** under **Article 61**. Because the Constitution does not define the phrase *"violation of the Constitution,"* its determination is left to the politico-judicial judgment of Parliament under the **most stringent special majority in the entire Indian Constitution**:
    1.  **Initiation (Article 61(1)–(2)):** The charge may be preferred by **either House of Parliament** (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha). No such charge shall be preferred unless a proposal is contained in a resolution moved after at least **14 days' written notice** signed by not less than **one-fourth (25%) of the total number of members of that House**, and such resolution has been passed by a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the total membership of the House** (including both elected and nominated members, with no deduction for vacancies or absentees).
    2.  **Investigation and Right of Defence (Article 61(3)):** When a charge has been so preferred by either House, the **other House** investigates the charge or causes the charge to be investigated. The President has the constitutional right to appear and to be represented at such investigation.
    3.  **Removal Resolution (Article 61(4)):** If as a result of the investigation a resolution is passed by a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the total membership** of the investigating House declaring that the charge has been sustained, such resolution operates to **remove the President from his office as from the date on which the resolution is so passed** (requiring no separate formal order).
*   **Electoral vs. Impeachment Asymmetry:** While **nominated members** of Parliament are excluded from the Electoral College under **Article 54**, they **participate and vote** in the impeachment process under **Article 61**. Conversely, while **elected MLAs** of States and Union Territories (Delhi and Puducherry) participate in the election of the President under **Article 54**, they have **zero role** in the impeachment of the President under **Article 61**.

### 2. The Vice-President of India: Constitutional Position, Election, and Removal (Articles 63–71)

#### Dual Institutional Role: Ex-Officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha and Acting President (Articles 63–65)
Modeled on the American Vice-President, **Article 63** establishes the office of the Vice-President of India. Under **Article 64**, the Vice-President is the ***ex-officio* Chairman of the Council of States (Rajya Sabha)** and shall not hold any other office of profit. In his regular constitutional existence, the Vice-President has no executive functions attached to the office of the Vice-President *per se*; indeed, under **Article 97** and the **Second Schedule**, he draws his regular salary and emoluments not as Vice-President, but strictly in his capacity as the **Chairman of the Rajya Sabha**.

Under **Article 65**, however, when a vacancy occurs in the office of the President by reason of his death, resignation, or removal, the Vice-President acts as President until the date on which a new President is elected (which under **Article 62(2)** must occur as soon as possible, and in no case later than **six months** from the date of occurrence of the vacancy; the newly elected President holds office for a full five-year term, unlike the American Vice-President who serves out the remainder of the predecessor's term). Similarly, when the President is unable to discharge his functions owing to absence, illness, or any other cause, the Vice-President discharges his functions until the President resumes duties (**Article 65(2)**). During any period when the Vice-President acts as President, he ceases to perform the duties of Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and is not entitled to the salary of Chairman under **Article 97** (**Article 64 Proviso**), receiving instead all the powers, immunities, and emoluments of the President (**Article 65(3)**). Under the **President (Discharge of Functions) Act, 1969** (enacted when both President Dr. Zakir Husain died in office and Vice-President V.V. Giri resigned to contest the presidential election), if both the offices of President and Vice-President fall vacant simultaneously, the **Chief Justice of India** (or in his absence, the senior-most Judge of the Supreme Court available) acts as President (as **CJI M. Hidayatullah** did from July to August 1969).

#### Electoral College Divergence and Removal by Effective Majority (Articles 66–67)
Because the Vice-President’s primary regular function is to preside over the Rajya Sabha, and his acting tenure as President is only a temporary stopgap capped at six months, Dr. Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly that it was unnecessary to involve State Legislative Assemblies in his election.

*   **Electoral College and Qualifications (Article 66):** Under **Article 66(1)**, the Vice-President is elected by the members of an Electoral College consisting of **all the members of both Houses of Parliament (both elected and nominated)** in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote by secret ballot (requiring **20 proposers and 20 seconders** under the 1952 Act). Under **Article 66(3)**, a candidate must be a citizen of India, have completed **35 years of age**, and be qualified for election as a member of the **Rajya Sabha** (as opposed to the Lok Sabha for the President).
*   **Removal Mechanics — Effective Majority (Article 67(b)):** Unlike the President, the Vice-President does not require a formal two-thirds impeachment trial for "violation of the Constitution." Under **Article 67(b)**, the Vice-President may be removed from his office (without any constitutionally specified ground) by a **resolution of the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) passed by a majority of all the then members of the Council (Effective Majority = $>50\%$ of `[Total Strength minus Vacancies]`) and agreed to by the House of the People (Lok Sabha)** by a **Simple Majority** (majority of members present and voting), provided that at least **14 days' notice** has been given of the intention to move the resolution.

### 3. The Westminster Aid and Advice Dialectic: President, Prime Minister, and Council of Ministers (Articles 74–78)

#### Evolution of Article 74: From Constitutional Convention to Textual Compulsion (42nd and 44th Amendments)
The linchpin of the Westminster cabinet system in India is **Article 74(1)**. As originally enacted in 1950, **Article 74(1)** stated: *"There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President in the exercise of his functions."* Noticeably, the original text did not explicitly state that the President *shall* act in accordance with such advice, relying instead on British constitutional conventions. Early post-independence friction—most notably when **President Dr. Rajendra Prasad** in 1951 (over the Hindu Code Bill) and again in a November 1960 speech at the Indian Law Institute questioned whether the Indian President was strictly bound by ministerial advice to the same extent as the British monarch—prompted both judicial clarification and constitutional amendment.

*   **The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976:** Enacted during the Emergency on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, the **42nd Amendment** amended **Article 74(1)** to remove all textual ambiguity, substituting the mandatory directive that the Council of Ministers shall aid and advise the President who ***"shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice."***
*   **The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978:** While the post-Emergency Janata government retained the 42nd Amendment’s binding mandate that the President *"shall"* act in accordance with ministerial advice, it added a **Proviso to Article 74(1)** to equip the President with a limited suspensive check: *"Provided that the President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider such advice, either generally or otherwise, and the President shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration."* Thus, the President can return cabinet advice once for reconsideration, but is constitutionally bound if the Council of Ministers reiterates the same advice.
*   **Ouster of Judicial Inquiry into Advice (Article 74(2)):** Under **Article 74(2)**, the question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the President shall not be inquired into in any court. However, as the 9-Judge Constitution Bench held in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994)** (building on ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India*, 2006**), while **Article 74(2)** bars courts from inquiring into the *subjective advice* tendered by the Council of Ministers to the President, it **does not bar judicial scrutiny of the objective material or record** upon which the Council of Ministers formed its satisfaction (for instance, in imposing President's Rule under **Article 356** or appointing constitutional authorities). Furthermore, in ***U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971)**, the Supreme Court held that **Article 74(1) is mandatory**—the President cannot exercise the executive power of the Union without a Council of Ministers even after the Lok Sabha is dissolved; hence, a caretaker Prime Minister and Council of Ministers must continue in office until a new Lok Sabha is constituted.

#### Judicial Consolidation of Parliamentary Executive Primacy (Ram Jawaya Kapur 1955 & Shamsher Singh 1974)
Even prior to the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, the Supreme Court definitively settled the nature of the Indian executive through two foundational Constitution Bench judgments that rejected a literal reading of **Article 53(1)**:

1.  ***Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Writing for a unanimous Court, **Chief Justice B.K. Mukherjea** addressed two core constitutional questions: the nature of the executive in India, and whether the executive can act without prior legislative authorization. First, the Court held that although executive power is formally vested in the President under **Article 53(1)** and the Governor under **Article 154(1)**, the Indian Constitution has adopted the English system of a Parliamentary Executive: *"The President has thus been made a formal or constitutional head of the executive and the real executive powers are vested in the Ministers or the Cabinet."* Second, the Court defined the **Residuary Theory of Executive Power**: executive function is not confined to merely carrying out laws already enacted by the legislature; it comprises the determination of policy, the initiation of legislation, the maintenance of order, and the promotion of social and economic welfare. Provided the executive does not infringe upon any Fundamental Right (such as **Article 19(1)(g)**) or contravene any constitutional provision or existing statute, the Union or State Executive can formulate and execute policies (such as state textbook nationalization) under **Articles 73 and 162** even without a prior enabling legislative enactment (except where the Constitution specifically requires *"authority of law,"* such as taxation under **Article 265**, expenditure from the Consolidated Fund under **Article 266(3)**, or deprivation of property under **Article 300A**).
2.  ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Overruling its earlier contrary observations in ***Sardarilal v. Union of India* (1971)** (which had erroneously suggested that where the Constitution uses the phrase *"the President is satisfied,"* the President must exercise his *personal* satisfaction rather than acting on ministerial advice), a 7-Judge Bench led by **Chief Justice A.N. Ray** (with a celebrated concurring opinion by **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer**) held that wherever the Constitution requires the **"satisfaction" of the President or the Governor** (e.g., **Articles 123, 213, 311(2)(c), 356**), that satisfaction is **not the personal satisfaction of the President or Governor, but the constitutional satisfaction of the President or Governor acting on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**. Save in the narrow spheres where the Governor is expressly or by necessary implication required by the Constitution to act in his discretion under **Article 163**, neither the President nor the Governor exercises executive or legislative functions personally.

#### Collective vs. Individual Responsibility, the 91st Amendment (2003) Cap, and Cabinet Governance (Articles 75, 77–78)

*   **Appointment and Situational Discretion (Article 75(1)):** The Prime Minister is appointed by the President and the other Ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. While in normal circumstances the President has no choice but to appoint the leader of the majority party in the Lok Sabha as Prime Minister, the President exercises **situational constitutional discretion** when a general election yields a **hung Parliament** (where no single party or pre-poll coalition commands an absolute majority in the Lok Sabha, as observed in 1979, 1989, 1996, and 1998) or when an incumbent Prime Minister dies suddenly in office without an obvious successor. Furthermore, as held in ***Manoj Narula v. Union of India* (2014, 5-Judge Constitution Bench)**, while the Court cannot read a formal disqualification against persons facing criminal charges into **Article 75(1)** beyond those enacted by Parliament under **Article 102**, there is a **"constitutional expectation"** rooted in **constitutional morality** and institutional trust that the Prime Minister will avoid advising the appointment of individuals charged with heinous or moral turpitude offenses as Ministers.
*   **Individual Responsibility — Doctrine of Pleasure (Article 75(2)):** Ministers hold office *"during the pleasure of the President."* Because a Minister cannot be individually removed by a parliamentary motion of no-confidence, **Article 75(2)** operationalizes **individual responsibility** to the Executive head: in constitutional practice, the *"pleasure of the President"* is the pleasure of the **Prime Minister**. If a Minister disagrees with a Cabinet decision or commits an administrative impropriety and refuses to resign, the Prime Minister advises the President to dismiss that individual Minister.
*   **Collective Responsibility (Article 75(3)):** The Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha)** (and under **Article 164(2)**, the State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly), even if individual Ministers belong to the **Rajya Sabha**. Collective responsibility means that all Ministers stand and fall as a single unit (*"they swim and sink together"*): a **No-Confidence Motion** passed in the Lok Sabha under **Rule 198** (or the defeat of a Money Bill, the Budget, or a Motion of Thanks on the President's Address) obligates the entire Council of Ministers to resign, and the resignation or death of the Prime Minister automatically dissolves the entire Council of Ministers. Moreover, as clarified in ***Common Cause, A Registered Society v. Union of India* (1999)**, collective responsibility binds every Minister to publicly defend Cabinet decisions; if a Minister disagrees with a Cabinet policy, they must either accept the collective decision or resign.
*   **The 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003 (Size Cap and Defection Bar):** Prior to 2003, coalition instabilities routinely led to "jumbo cabinets" where dozens of legislators were inducted as Ministers to secure legislative majorities. Implementing the recommendations of the **Venkatachaliah Commission (NCRWC, 2002)**, the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003** inserted four critical structural clauses:
    1.  **Article 75(1A):** The total number of Ministers, **including the Prime Minister**, in the Union Council of Ministers **shall not exceed 15% of the total number of members of the House of the People (Lok Sabha)** (as upheld in *Aires Rodrigues v. State of Goa*, 2017).
    2.  **Article 164(1A):** The total number of Ministers, **including the Chief Minister**, in the Council of Ministers in a State **shall not exceed 15% of the total number of members of the Legislative Assembly** of that State, **Provided that the number of Ministers, including the Chief Minister, in a State shall not be less than 12** (applicable to smaller assemblies like Sikkim, Mizoram, and Goa; note that for the **NCT of Delhi**, the cap remains **10% of the Assembly strength = 7 Ministers** under **Article 239AA(4)**, unaffected by the 91st Amendment).
    3.  **Articles 75(1B), 164(1B), and 361B:** Any member of either House of Parliament or a State Legislature belonging to any political party who is **disqualified for being a member of that House under Paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law)** shall also be **disqualified to be appointed as a Minister** (under Articles 75(1B)/164(1B)) or to hold any **remunerative political post** (under **Article 361B**) from the date of their disqualification until the expiry of their term of office as a member, or until they contest and win a subsequent election to a Legislature, whichever is earlier.
*   **Six-Month Non-Member Window (Article 75(5) / Article 164(4)):** A Minister who for any period of **six consecutive months** is not a member of either House of Parliament (or of the State Legislature) shall at the expiration of that period cease to be a Minister. In ***S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda* (1996)**, the Supreme Court held that a person who is not a member of either House can be appointed even as **Prime Minister** for six months, but in ***S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab* (2001)**, the Court struck down the practice of repeatedly re-appointing a non-legislator as a Minister after the expiry of six months without getting elected during the term of the same legislature, declaring such repeated re-appointment a subversive fraud on **Article 164(4)** and parliamentary democracy.
*   **Council of Ministers vs. The Cabinet and Cabinet Committees (Articles 77–78, 352):** While **Articles 74 and 75** refer only to the broader **"Council of Ministers"** (comprising Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State with Independent Charge, and Ministers of State), the word **"Cabinet"** was entirely absent from the original 1950 Constitution and was inserted into the constitutional text **only once—in Article 352(3)—by the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978**, which defined it as *"the Council consisting of the Prime Minister and other Ministers of Cabinet rank appointed under Article 75."* Under **Article 77(3)** (and **Article 166(3)** in the States), the President makes rules for the more convenient transaction of the business of the Government of India and for the allocation of business among Ministers—operationalized through the **Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, 1961** and the **Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961**. Under these statutory rules, the Prime Minister constitutes standing **Cabinet Committees** (currently eight, including the **Cabinet Committee on Security [CCS]**, **Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs [CCEA]**, **Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs [CCPA]**, and **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet [ACC]**, all chaired by the Prime Minister except the **Cabinet Committee on Accommodation** and the **Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs**, which are typically chaired by senior Cabinet Ministers like the Home or Defence Minister) to achieve collective policy coordination and workload division.
*   **Constitutional Duties of the Prime Minister (Article 78):** Mirroring **Article 167** for the Chief Minister, **Article 78** casts three constitutional duties upon the Prime Minister as the sole channel of communication between the Council of Ministers and the President:
    1.  To communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation (**Article 78(a)**);
    2.  To furnish such information relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation as the President may call for (**Article 78(b)**); and
    3.  If the President so requires, to submit for the consideration of the Council of Ministers any matter on which a decision has been taken by a Minister but which has not been considered by the Council (**Article 78(c)**—reinforcing the doctrine of **collective responsibility** against unilateral ministerial action).

## II. Presidential and Gubernatorial Legislative, Ordinance, and Clemency Powers (Articles 72, 111, 123, 161, 213)

### 4. The Veto Architecture over Union Legislation (Article 111 and the 24th Amendment)

#### Absolute, Suspensive, and Pocket Vetoes under Article 111
Under **Article 79**, the President is an integral constituent part of the Parliament of India alongside the Council of States and the House of the People; no Bill passed by both Houses of Parliament can become an Act without Presidential assent. When a Bill has been passed by the Houses of Parliament and presented to the President under **Article 111**, the constitutional text mandates that the President *"shall declare either that he assents to the Bill, or that he withholds assent therefrom,"* accompanied by a **Proviso** governing non-Money Bills. From this textual architecture, three distinct veto powers emerge in Indian constitutional practice (notice that Indian Presidents do **not** possess a **Qualified Veto**—the American presidential veto under Article I, Section 7 of the U.S. Constitution, which can only be overridden by an extraordinary two-thirds majority of both Houses of Congress):

1.  **Absolute Veto ("Withholds Assent Therefrom"):** The power of the President to flatly refuse assent to a Bill passed by Parliament, terminating the Bill's legislative life. In Westminster convention, the President exercises the Absolute Veto on the advice of the Council of Ministers in two specific scenarios: first, with respect to a **Private Member’s Bill** passed by Parliament despite executive reservations (as **President Dr. Rajendra Prasad** did in **1954** by withholding assent to the **PEPSU Appropriation Bill**, which had been passed by Parliament when President's Rule in PEPSU had already been revoked prior to presentation for assent); and second, when a Cabinet resigns after a Bill has been passed by Parliament but before Presidential assent is granted, and the **newly inducted Cabinet advises the President to withhold assent** to the Bill passed by the outgoing government (as occurred in **1991** with the **Salary, Allowances and Pension of Members of Parliament (Amendment) Bill**, passed hurriedly on the last day of the Ninth Lok Sabha without prior Presidential recommendation).
2.  **Suspensive Veto (Proviso to Article 111):** Under the **Proviso to Article 111**, the President may, as soon as possible after the presentation to him of a Bill for assent (**if it is not a Money Bill**), return the Bill to the Houses with a message requesting that they reconsider the Bill or any specified provisions thereof, and in particular consider the desirability of introducing any amendments recommended in his message. When a Bill is so returned, the Houses shall reconsider the Bill accordingly, and **if the Bill is passed again by the Houses with or without amendment** (by an ordinary **Simple Majority**) and presented to the President for assent, ***"the President shall not withhold assent therefrom."*** (For example, in **2006**, **President Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam** exercised a Suspensive Veto by returning the **Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Amendment Bill, 2006** relating to the exemption of 56 Offices of Profit; when Parliament re-passed the Bill without amendments, President Kalam granted his mandatory assent).
3.  **Pocket Veto (Absence of Constitutional Time Limit):** Unlike Article I, Section 7 of the **U.S. Constitution**, which mandates that if the U.S. President does not return a Bill within **10 days (excluding Sundays)** while Congress is in session, the Bill automatically becomes law (and only suffers a "Pocket Veto" if Congress adjourns within those 10 days), **Article 111 of the Indian Constitution prescribes no fixed time limit** within which the President must declare his assent or withholding of assent, and uses the elastic phrase *"as soon as possible"* in the Proviso. Consequently, the Indian President can keep a Bill pending on his desk for an indefinite period without taking either positive or negative action—causing critics to remark that **the pocket of the Indian President is bigger than that of the American President**. Most famously, in **1986**, **President Giani Zail Singh** exercised a Pocket Veto over the **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986** (which sought to empower executive authorities to intercept private mail and curtailed freedom of the press); in **1989**, the successor National Front government led by V.P. Singh had the Bill formally withdrawn by President R. Venkataraman from the Rajya Sabha.

#### Asymmetric Veto Application Across Ordinary, Money, and Constitutional Amendment Bills
The availability of Presidential veto options under **Article 111** varies structurally across the three primary categories of parliamentary bills:

*   **Ordinary Bills (Article 107) & Financial Bills (Article 117):** Subject to all three vetoes—**Absolute Veto**, **Suspensive Veto** (returnable once under the Proviso to Article 111), and **Pocket Veto**.
*   **Money Bills (Article 110):** Expressly excluded from the **Proviso to Article 111** (*"if it is not a Money Bill"*). Therefore, the President **cannot exercise a Suspensive Veto** to return a Money Bill for reconsideration by Parliament. The President may either grant assent, withhold assent (though because a Money Bill is introduced in the Lok Sabha only with the prior recommendation of the President under **Article 117(1)**, assent is normally granted as a matter of course), or keep it pending.
*   **Constitutional Amendment Bills (Article 368):** Governed specifically by **Article 368(2)** rather than **Article 111**. Following the Supreme Court’s judgment in *Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967), Parliament enacted the **24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971**, replacing the words *"it shall be presented to the President for his assent and upon such assent being given to the Bill"* in **Article 368(2)** with the mandatory declaration: *"it shall be presented to the President **who shall give his assent to the Bill** and thereupon the Constitution shall stand amended."* Thus, the President possesses **zero veto power** (neither Absolute, nor Suspensive, nor Pocket) over a Constitutional Amendment Bill duly passed under **Article 368**.

### 5. Co-Extensive Legislative Power: The Ordinance-Making Regime and Judicial Safeguards (Articles 123 and 213)

#### Constitutional Preconditions, Temporal Ceiling, and Subject-Matter Limits of Articles 123 and 213
Inherited from **Section 42 and Section 88 of the Government of India Act, 1935** (and tracing back to the **Indian Councils Act, 1861**), **Article 123** empowers the President—and **Article 213** empowers the Governor of a State—to promulgate **Ordinances** during the recess of the Legislature. No other major democratic constitution in the Commonwealth or the Western world (neither the UK nor the US) vests such plenary legislative power in the Executive. Defending **Article 123** in the Constituent Assembly against fierce criticism from H.V. Kamath and Hriday Nath Kunzru, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** explained that the power is strictly an emergency stopgap to deal with unforeseen situations arising abruptly when Parliament is not in session and cannot wait for the summoning of the Houses.

1.  **Condition Precedent — Legislative Recess (Article 123(1) / 213(1)):** An Ordinance can be promulgated **only when both Houses of Parliament (or both Houses of a bicameral State Legislature) are not in session**. Because a Bill requires passage by both Houses to become law, an Ordinance **can be validly promulgated when only one House (e.g., the Rajya Sabha) is in session** and the other House (e.g., the Lok Sabha) has been prorogued or dissolved. An Ordinance promulgated when both Houses are in session is *ab initio* void.
2.  **Satisfaction on "Immediate Action":** The President or Governor must be satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action. Under ***Shamsher Singh* (1974)**, this satisfaction is the satisfaction of the Council of Ministers, not the personal discretion of the President or Governor.
3.  **Co-Extensive Legislative Competence and Constitutional Limits (Article 123(3)):** An Ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament (including the power to enact **retrospective laws** or amend/repeal an existing Act of Parliament), but is strictly **co-extensive** with Parliament’s legislative competence:
    *   Under **Article 123(3)**, if an Ordinance makes any provision which Parliament would not under the Constitution be competent to enact (e.g., an encroachment on the **State List** in the absence of an emergency, or a violation of **Part III Fundamental Rights**, since **Article 13(3)(a)** explicitly defines *"law"* to include an *"ordinance"*), it is **void** to that extent.
    *   An Ordinance **cannot be used to amend the Constitution**, because **Article 368** prescribes a special constituent procedure exclusively initiated by the introduction of a Bill in either House of Parliament.
    *   **Additional Proviso Restraints on a Governor's Ordinance (Article 213(1) Proviso):** Unlike the President under **Article 123**, the Governor of a State **shall not, without instructions from the President, promulgate any Ordinance** if: (a) a Bill containing the same provisions would under the Constitution have required the **previous sanction of the President** for its introduction into the Legislature (e.g., a Bill imposing restrictions on freedom of trade and commerce under the **Proviso to Article 304(b)**); or (b) he would have **deemed it necessary to reserve a Bill** containing the same provisions for the consideration of the President; or (c) an Act of the State Legislature containing the same provisions would under the Constitution have been **invalid unless, having been reserved for the consideration of the President, it had received the assent of the President** (e.g., a law on a **Concurrent List (List III)** subject that is repugnant to an existing Central law under **Article 254(2)**).
4.  **Temporal Ceiling — Six Weeks from Reassembly (Article 123(2)):** Every Ordinance must be laid before both Houses of Parliament and **ceases to operate at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament**, or if before the expiration of that period resolutions disapproving it are passed by both Houses, upon the passing of the second of those resolutions (**Article 123(2)(a)**; in a State Legislature, a disapproval resolution need only be passed by the **Legislative Assembly** and agreed to by the Legislative Council if one exists under **Article 213(2)(a)**). Where the Houses of Parliament are summoned to reassemble on different dates, the period of **six weeks is reckoned from the later of those dates** (**Explanation to Article 123(2)**). Because the maximum permissible gap between two sessions of Parliament under **Article 85(1)** is **six months**, the **maximum possible lifespan of an Ordinance without parliamentary approval is six months and six weeks (approximately 7.5 months)**. An Ordinance may also be withdrawn at any time by the President (**Article 123(2)(b)**).

#### Judicial Review of Satisfaction and the Re-Promulgation Bar (R.C. Cooper 1970, D.C. Wadhwa 1987, and 7-Judge Krishna Kumar Singh 2017)
The tension between executive urgency and legislative supremacy under **Articles 123 and 213** has been adjudicated across four landmark constitutional moments:

*   ***R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (1970, 11-Judge Bank Nationalization Case) & the 38th/44th Amendments:** When the Supreme Court in *R.C. Cooper* indicated that the President's satisfaction under **Article 123** could be challenged on the ground of *mala fides* or that no genuine urgency existed, Parliament enacted the **38th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975** (during the Emergency), inserting **Clause (4) into Article 123** and **Clause (4) into Article 213** to declare that the President’s or Governor’s satisfaction *"shall be final and conclusive and shall not be questioned in any court on any ground."* Subsequently, the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** **deleted Clause (4)** from both **Articles 123 and 213**, restoring judicial review over the satisfaction underlying an Ordinance (further affirmed in ***A.K. Roy v. Union of India*, 1982** and ***T. Venkata Reddy v. State of Andhra Pradesh*, 1985**, which held that while an Ordinance cannot be invalidated merely because of executive motives in the same way a statute cannot, the satisfaction of the existence of circumstances warranting immediate action is reviewable if wholly extraneous or a fraud on power).
*   ***Dr. D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Empirical research by Professor D.C. Wadhwa revealed that between 1967 and 1981, the State of Bihar had promulgated $256$ Ordinances that were systematically **re-promulgated** time and again (some surviving for up to 14 years) immediately after the Legislature was prorogued, without ever placing them before the Legislature for enactment into Acts. Writing for a unanimous 5-Judge Bench, **Chief Justice P.N. Bhagwati** struck down the practice as an unconstitutional usurpation of legislative power: *"The power to promulgate an Ordinance is essentially a power to be used to meet an extraordinary situation and it cannot be allowed to be 'perverted to serve political ends.' It is contrary to all democratic norms that the Executive should have the power to make a law;hence the power must be strictly circumscribed... **Re-promulgation of Ordinances without submitting them to the Legislature is a fraud on the Constitution** and a subversion of the democratic legislative process."*
*   ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2017, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Overruling earlier narrower interpretations in *State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose* (1962) and *T. Venkata Reddy* (1985) (which had equated an Ordinance with a temporary statute such that all acts and rights created under an Ordinance survived permanently even if the Ordinance lapsed without ever being laid before the Legislature), a **7-Judge Constitution Bench** (majority opinion authored by **Justice D.Y. Chandrachud**) established five seminal constitutional propositions governing **Articles 123 and 213**:
    1.  **Conditional Legislative Power:** The power to issue an Ordinance is **not an absolute or parallel source of legislation**; it is strictly conditional upon the satisfaction that circumstances exist rendering immediate action necessary when the legislature is not in session.
    2.  **Justiciability of Satisfaction:** The satisfaction of the President under **Article 123** and the Governor under **Article 213** is **not immune from judicial review**. While the Court will not evaluate the *sufficiency* or *adequacy* of the materials, it will examine whether there was at least *some* relevant material to arrive at the satisfaction and whether the exercise of power was actuated by an oblique motive or constituted a **fraud on constitutional power**.
    3.  **Mandatory Requirement to Lay Before the Legislature:** The requirement in **Article 123(2) / 213(2)** that every Ordinance *"shall be laid before"* the Legislature upon reassembly is **mandatory, not directory**. Failure to lay an Ordinance before the Legislature is a serious constitutional infraction and strong evidence of an abuse of the ordinance-making power.
    4.  **Re-Promulgation is Impermissible:** Re-promulgation of Ordinances is fundamentally destructive of parliamentary democracy and a **fraud on the Constitution**.
    5.  **No Automatic Survival of Enduring Rights After Lapse:** Unlike **Articles 358(1) and 359(1A)** (which explicitly contain a *"things done or omitted to be done"* saving clause for emergency actions), **Articles 123 and 213 contain no saving clause** for lapsed Ordinances. Therefore, rights, privileges, or obligations created by an Ordinance **do not survive automatically** after the Ordinance ceases to operate under **Article 123(2)(a) / 213(2)(a)** unless taking away what was done is physically irreversible, impracticable, or contrary to compelling public interest. (In *Krishna Kumar Singh*, teachers hired under seven successively re-promulgated Bihar Sanskrit School Ordinances that were never laid before the Assembly were held to have acquired no enduring statutory right to continue in state service after the Ordinances lapsed).

### 6. The Sovereign Prerogative of Clemency and Remission Jurisprudence (Articles 72 and 161)

#### Structural Taxonomy of Clemency: Pardon, Commutation, Remission, Respite, and Reprieve
Vested in the President under **Article 72** and in the Governor of a State under **Article 161**, the constitutional clemency power is not a personal act of grace or private charity, but a high constitutional responsibility exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers to correct possible judicial errors, mitigate harshness in sentencing, and advance public welfare. Both Articles authorize five distinct modalities of clemency:

1.  **Pardon:** Completely absolves the offender from the conviction, sentence, and all statutory punishments and disqualifications, placing the individual in the exact legal position as if they had never committed the offense.
2.  **Commutation:** Substitutes one form of punishment for a **lighter form or nature of punishment** (for example, commuting a sentence of death to rigorous imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment).
3.  **Remission:** Reduces the **quantum or duration of a sentence without changing its character** (for example, reducing a sentence of rigorous imprisonment for ten years to rigorous imprisonment for five years).
4.  **Respite:** Awards a **lesser sentence in place of the penalty originally prescribed** due to some **special fact or physical condition** personal to the convict (such as pregnancy of a woman offender, advanced disability, or terminal illness).
5.  **Reprieve:** A **temporary stay of the execution** of a sentence (especially a sentence of death) for a temporary period to enable the convict to apply for pardon or commutation from the President or Governor.

| Jurisdictional Dimension | President of India (Article 72) | Governor of a State (Article 161) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Territorial & Legislative Scope** | Extends to offenses against any law relating to a matter to which the **executive power of the Union extends** (**List I**, and **List III** where Union executive power is expressly vested) (**Article 72(1)(b)**). | Extends to offenses against any law relating to a matter to which the **executive power of the State extends** (**List II** and **List III**, subject to Union supremacy under Art. 73/162) (**Article 161**). |
| **2. Court-Martial Sentences** | Possesses **exclusive power** to grant pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, or commutation in **all cases where the punishment or sentence is by a Court-Martial** (Military Court) (**Article 72(1)(a)**). | Possesses **ZERO power** in respect of sentences or punishments inflicted by a **Court-Martial**. |
| **3. Sentences of Death** | Possesses power in **all cases where the sentence is a sentence of death** (whether under a Union law or a State law) (**Article 72(1)(c)**). Only the President can grant a complete **Pardon** of a death sentence. | Even where a death sentence is imposed under a law within the State's executive power (like the IPC/BNS in List III), the Governor **cannot grant a complete Pardon** of a death sentence, though under **Article 161** (and confirmed by the SC in ***State of Haryana v. Jagdish*, 2010** and ***Pyare Lal*, 2021**) the Governor can **suspend, remit, or commute** a death sentence. |

#### Constitutional Clemency vs. Statutory Remission: From Maru Ram (1980) and Kehar Singh (1989) to Epuru Sudhakar (2006) and Shatrughan Chauhan (2014)
Indian public law maintains a vital distinction between the **untouchable constitutional clemency power** under **Articles 72 and 161** and the **statutory remission/commutation power** exercised by the appropriate government under **Sections 432, 433, and 433A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (now Sections 473, 474, and 475 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita [BNSS], 2023)**.

*   ***Maru Ram v. Union of India* (1980, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Challenging **Section 433A of the CrPC** (inserted in 1978 to mandate that a person sentenced to life imprisonment for an offense for which death is one of the punishments, or whose death sentence was commuted to life, **shall not be released from prison unless he has served at least 14 years of actual imprisonment**), convicts argued that Section 433A unlawfully fettered the clemency power. Writing for the Constitution Bench, **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer** upheld **Section 433A**, holding that while statutory remission rules cannot override or fetter the **constitutional clemency power under Articles 72 and 161** (which remains untouchable by ordinary legislation), the President and Governor **never exercise Articles 72 and 161 on their own personal discretion**—they are strictly bound by the **aid and advice of the Union or State Council of Ministers**. Furthermore, the Court held that the exercise of **Articles 72 and 161** is subject to **Article 14 (non-arbitrariness)** and cannot be exercised for political, caste, or religious considerations.
*   ***Kehar Singh v. Union of India* (1989, 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Arising from the mercy petition of Kehar Singh (convicted in the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi), the President had rejected his request for an oral hearing and declined to examine the merits of the Supreme Court's evidentiary findings. Chief Justice R.S. Pathak held that: (1) While studying a mercy petition under **Article 72**, the **President is fully entitled to scrutinize the judicial record and come to a conclusion on guilt or sentence different from that of the Supreme Court**, doing so without modifying or superseding the judicial record itself (as the two operate on entirely distinct planes); (2) A convict has **no right to insist on an oral hearing** before the President; and (3) The Court need not lay down rigid guidelines for **Article 72**, though the power is amenable to limited judicial review within the *Maru Ram* bounds.
*   ***Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh* (2006):** When the Governor of Andhra Pradesh granted remission to a ruling-party Congress activist convicted of murder on the basis of irrelevant political representations and false reports of "good conduct," a 2-Judge Bench (**Justices Arijit Pasayat and S.H. Kapadia**) quashed the Governor's clemency order under **Article 161** and codified the **six grounds of judicial review over Articles 72 and 161**:
    1.  That the order has been passed **without application of mind**;
    2.  That the order is ***mala fide***;
    3.  That the order has been passed on **extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations** (such as political loyalty, caste, or religion);
    4.  That **relevant materials have been kept out of consideration**;
    5.  That the order suffers from **arbitrariness**; and
    6.  Noticeably, in ***Union of India v. V. Sriharan @ Murugan* (2015, 5-Judge Constitution Bench — Rajiv Gandhi Assassination Remission Case)**, the Supreme Court held that while a Sessions Court cannot award a special category sentence beyond 14 years, **the High Court or Supreme Court can award a "fixed-term life sentence" (e.g., 20 or 30 years or for the remainder of natural life) barring *statutory* remission under Section 432/433 CrPC**, though such a judicial bar **cannot touch the *constitutional* clemency power of the President under Article 72 or the Governor under Article 161**. Furthermore, the 5-Judge Bench held that where an offense was investigated by the **Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI)** or falls under a Central Act, the "appropriate government" for statutory remission is the **Central Government**, and under **Section 435 CrPC (Section 477 BNSS)**, *"consultation"* with the Central Government means **concurrence**. (Subsequently, in ***A.G. Perarivalan v. State of Tamil Nadu*, 2022**, when the Governor sat on the Tamil Nadu Cabinet’s **Article 161** remission advice for over two years and then referred it to the President, the Supreme Court held that the **Governor had zero constitutional authority to refer an Article 161 Cabinet recommendation to the President**, as the State Cabinet’s advice is binding under **Article 161**, and invoked **Article 142** to release Perarivalan).
*   ***Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014, 3-Judge Bench):** Synthesizing *Triveniben v. State of Gujarat* (1989) and overruling *Devender Pal Singh Bhullar v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (2013) (which had erroneously created an exception denying commutation for delay to convicts under terror statutes like TADA), **Chief Justice P. Sathasivam** held that **inordinate, unexplained, and unreasonable executive delay in disposing of a mercy petition under Article 72 or 161 inflicts dehumanizing psychological torture violating Article 21**, entitling the death-row convict to **commutation of the death sentence to life imprisonment** regardless of the gravity of the crime or whether the offense fell under IPC or terror statutes. The Court also recognized **supervening circumstances** (such as supervening mental illness/insanity or unlawful solitary confinement prior to the rejection of the mercy petition) as independent grounds for commuting death to life, and mandated a **minimum 14-day notice period** between the communication of the rejection of a mercy petition and the scheduled date of execution.

#### The Rule of Law in Statutory Remission: Bilkis Yakub Rasool (Bilkis Bano, 2024) and Procedural Mandates
In ***Bilkis Yakub Rasool v. Union of India* (January 2024 — the Bilkis Bano Remission Judgment)**, a 2-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court (**Justices B.V. Nagarathna and Ujjal Bhuyan**) delivered a watershed judgment reinforcing the **Rule of Law (Article 14)** over statutory remission under **Section 432 of the CrPC (Section 473 BNSS)**. Eleven convicts serving life imprisonment for gang-rape and multiple murders committed during the 2002 Gujarat riots—whose trial had been transferred by the Supreme Court from Gujarat to a Special CBI Court in Mumbai, Maharashtra to ensure a fair trial—had been granted premature remission by the **State of Gujarat** in August 2022 after one convict obtained a May 2022 order from a 2-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court (*Radheshyam Bhagwandas Shah*) directing Gujarat to consider remission under its 1992 Policy. Quashing the remission orders and directing all 11 convicts to surrender back to prison within two weeks, the Supreme Court laid down four binding constitutional and statutory principles:
1.  **Jurisdiction of the "Appropriate Government" (Section 432(7) CrPC / Section 473(7) BNSS):** Under **Section 432(7)(b)**, the *"appropriate Government"* competent to grant statutory remission is the Government of the State **within which the offender was sentenced** (i.e., the **State of Maharashtra**, where the Special Court tried and convicted the offenders), **not** the State where the crime was originally committed or where the convicts were imprisoned (Gujarat). The State of Gujarat had usurped jurisdiction that belonged exclusively to Maharashtra.
2.  **Nullity of Prior Judicial Order Obtained by Fraud (*Suppressio Veri*) and *Per Incuriam*:** Because the convict in the May 2022 proceeding (*Radheshyam Shah*) had suppressed material facts from the Supreme Court (including that the Bombay High Court and Gujarat High Court had previously ruled that Maharashtra, not Gujarat, was the appropriate government) and the May 2022 order ignored binding Constitution Bench precedents (*V. Sriharan*), the May 2022 Supreme Court order was declared ***per incuriam* and vitiated by fraud (*suppressio veri, suggestio falsi*)**, rendering it a legal nullity even without a formal Review Petition.
3.  **Mandatory Procedural Safeguards under Section 432(2) and Section 435 CrPC:** Granting remission requires strict compliance with: (a) obtaining the **opinion of the Presiding Judge of the Court which convicted the offender** with reasons under **Section 432(2)**; (b) **concurrence of the Central Government under Section 435** where the crime was investigated by the **CBI**; and (c) verification of whether court-imposed fines have been paid.
4.  **Rule of Law over Personal Liberty Upon Lawful Conviction:** Rejecting the plea that the convicts' liberty should not be disturbed since they had already been out of prison for 16 months, **Justice Nagarathna** held that **Article 21 protects personal liberty only in accordance with procedure established by law**; once liberty is obtained through an illegal and *ultra vires* executive order lacking jurisdiction, the **Rule of Law under Article 14** requires the restoration of the status quo ante, as compassion and sympathy cannot replace legality.

## III. The State Executive and the Gubernatorial Crucible: Federal Friction, Discretion, and Legislative Assent (Articles 153–167, 200–201)

### 7. The Office of the Governor: Dual Constitutional Role, Appointment, and Tenure Security (Articles 153–159)

#### Constitutional Link vs. Federal Agent: Constituent Assembly Debates and Hargovind Pant (1979)
Under **Part VI (Chapter II, Articles 153–167)**, the executive structure of the States mirrors the Westminster parliamentary framework of the Union, yet with a profound structural asymmetry rooted in the method of appointing the **Governor**. Under **Article 153**, there shall be a Governor for each State, and per the **Proviso to Article 153** (added by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**), nothing in the Article prevents the appointment of the **same person as Governor for two or more States** (in which case the emoluments and allowances payable to the Governor are allocated among the States in such proportion as the **President may by order determine** under **Article 158(3A)**).

In the original 1947 Provincial Constitution Committee draft, the Governor was proposed to be either directly elected by the people of the State or appointed by the President from a panel of four candidates elected by the State Legislative Assembly. However, on **May 30–31, 1949**, following the trauma of Partition and secessionist anxieties, the Constituent Assembly abandoned both models in favor of the **Canadian model** (**Article 155**): *"The Governor of a State shall be appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal."* Justifying this shift, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar**, and **B.R. Ambedkar** argued that an elected Governor would breed provincial separatism and clash with the Chief Minister, whereas a presidentially appointed nominee from outside the State would remain above local party politics and serve as an impartial constitutional lubricator.

This design invests the Governor with a **dual constitutional role**:
1.  First, as the **Constitutional Head of the State Polity** under **Article 154(1)**, discharging executive and legislative functions on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister (**Article 163(1)**); and
2.  Second, as a **Vital Constitutional Link between the Union and the State** (sending constitutional reports to the President under **Article 356(1)** and reserving specific State Bills for the consideration of the President under **Article 200**).

Crucially, in ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979, 5-Judge Constitution Bench)**, the Supreme Court had to decide whether the office of the Governor of a State is an *"employment under the Government of India"* within the meaning of **Article 319(d)** (which bars a retired member of a State Public Service Commission from taking up employment under the Government of India or a State Government). Holding that Dr. Raghukul Tilak (a former member of the Rajasthan PSC) was validly appointed Governor of Rajasthan, the 5-Judge Bench held that **the office of the Governor is an independent high constitutional office and is NOT an employment under, nor subordinate or subservient to, the Government of India**. Despite being appointed by the President under **Article 155** and holding office during the President's pleasure under **Article 156(1)**, the Governor is under no control of the Union Executive in discharging his constitutional functions and is not an agent or employee of the Central Government.

#### Doctrine of Pleasure and the 5-Judge Bench in B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010)
Under **Article 156**, while the normal term of a Governor is **five years** from the date on which he enters upon his office (**Article 156(3)**), this tenure is explicitly subject to **Article 156(1)**: ***"The Governor shall hold office during the pleasure of the President."*** Unlike the President (**Article 61**), Supreme Court and High Court Judges (**Articles 124(4), 217(1)(b)**), the Chief Election Commissioner (**Article 324(5)**), or the Comptroller and Auditor General (**Article 148(1)**), the Constitution provides **zero impeachment or formal inquiry procedure** and **no specified grounds** for the removal of a Governor.

For decades, whenever a new political coalition assumed office at the Centre, it routinely invoked **Article 156(1)** to summarily dismiss Governors appointed during the tenure of the previous Union Government. Following the abrupt dismissal of the Governors of Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, Haryana, and Goa in July 2004 after the change of government at the Centre, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by **Chief Justice K.G. Balakrishnan** (judgment authored by **Justice R.V. Raveendran**) adjudicated the constitutional contours of **Article 156(1)** in ***B.P. Singhal v. Union of India* (2010)**, establishing five binding principles:
1.  **Unfettered Textual Power vs. Rule of Law:** Under **Article 156(1)**, the President (acting on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers under **Article 74(1)**) has the power to remove a Governor at any time **without assigning any reason and without giving any opportunity to show cause** (principles of natural justice do not apply to the withdrawal of constitutional pleasure under Article 156(1)).
2.  **Non-Arbitrariness Bar:** However, even though no reasons need to be *communicated* to the Governor, the power under **Article 156(1) cannot be exercised in an arbitrary, capricious, or unreasonable manner**. The power is to be exercised only in rare and exceptional circumstances for valid and compelling reasons.
3.  **Ideological / Political Divergence is Invalid Ground:** A Governor **cannot be removed on the ground that he is out of sync with the policies and ideologies of the Union Government or the ruling party at the Centre**, nor can the Union Government remove Governors merely to make way for persons of its own political confidence. Because the Governor is the constitutional head of a State and not an employee or agent of the ruling party at the Centre (**reaffirming *Hargovind Pant*, 1979**), ideological variance is a wholly impermissible ground for removal.
4.  **Limited Judicial Review & Burden of Proof:** A Presidential order removing a Governor under **Article 156(1) is open to limited judicial review**. While the Court will presume that the President's action is valid, if the aggrieved Governor makes out a *prima facie* case of **arbitrariness, *mala fides*, or whimsicality** (for example, where the Union Government publicly admits that the removal was due to a change of regime or ideological difference), the Court will call upon the Union Government to produce the contemporary official records to verify whether any relevant material existed to show a valid ground for withdrawal of pleasure.

### 8. Constitutional Discretion vs. Ministerial Aid and Advice in the States (Articles 163–167)

#### Textual Asymmetry Between Article 74(1) and Article 163(1)–(2)
A textual comparison between **Article 74(1)** (governing the President) and **Article 163(1)–(2)** (governing the Governor) reveals a deliberate constitutional divergence:
*   **Article 163(1):** *"There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of his functions, **except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion**."*
*   **Article 163(2):** *"If any question arises whether any matter is or is not a matter as respects which the Governor is by or under this Constitution required to act in his discretion, **the decision of the Governor in his discretion shall be final, and the validity of anything done by the Governor shall not be called in question on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in his discretion**."*

Unlike **Article 74(1)**, **Article 163(1)** explicitly carves out a discretionary sphere for the Governor, and **Article 163(2)** appears on its face to make the Governor the sole judge of the boundaries of his own discretion. Furthermore, the **42nd and 44th Constitutional Amendment Acts** modified **Article 74(1)** (inserting *"shall act in accordance with such advice"* and the single-reconsideration Proviso for the President) **without making any corresponding textual amendment to Article 163(1)** for the Governor.

Khehar**, concurring opinions by **Justices Dipak Misra and Madan B. Lokur**) thoroughly deconstructed **Article 163** in ***Nabam Rebia & Bamang Felix v. 1.  **Historical Explanation of the Drafting Committee’s Deletion of Specific Discretionary Clauses:** In the 1948 Draft Constitution, Draft Articles 144(6) (appointment/dismissal of Ministers), 153(3) (summoning and dissolving the Assembly), 175 (returning Bills), 188 (Emergency in States), and 285(1)–(2) (Public Service Commission) had explicitly included the words *"in his discretion."* During the Constituent Assembly Debates in **June 1949**, the Drafting Committee deleted the phrase *"in his discretion"* from all those routine executive and legislative articles to ensure that the Governor would act solely on ministerial aid and advice, retaining **Article 163(1)–(2)** only because specific asymmetrical provisions (like the Sixth Schedule for Assam tribal areas, then in Paragraphs 9 and 18) required discretionary action.
2.  **Strict Narrowing of Article 163(1) and Judicial Review of Article 163(2):** The 5-Judge Bench held that **Article 163(1) does not confer any general discretionary power on the Governor**; his discretion is strictly confined to:
    *   **Express Discretion (*"by this Constitution"*):** Where the constitutional text explicitly uses the words *"in his discretion"* or *"individual judgment"*—such as **Articles 371A(1)(b), 371A(1)(d), 371A(2)(b), and 371A(2)(f)** (Nagaland), **Article 371F(g)** (Sikkim), **Article 371H(a)** (Arunachal Pradesh), **Sixth Schedule Paragraph 9(2)** (disputes over sharing of mineral royalties with Autonomous District Councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram), and **Article 239(2)** (where a Governor is appointed by the President as the Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory, he acts *independently* of his State Council of Ministers).
    *   **Implied Discretion (*"under this Constitution"*):** Where by necessary constitutional implication the Governor cannot possibly act on the advice of the Council of Ministers due to a **structural conflict of interest or constitutional peril**—such as: (i) sending a report to the President under **Article 356(1)** that the governance of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution; (ii) reserving a Bill for the consideration of the President under the **Second Proviso to Article 200** (where the Bill derogates from the powers of the High Court); (iii) granting **sanction for the prosecution of a Chief Minister or Minister** under the Prevention of Corruption Act where the Council of Ministers refuses sanction despite overwhelming *prima facie* evidence established by the Lokayukta (as held by the 5-Judge Constitution Bench in ***Madhya Pradesh Special Police Establishment v. State of M.P.*, 2004**); and (iv) situational trust-deficit discretion in appointing a Chief Minister in a hung Assembly or requiring a Chief Minister who has lost majority to face a floor test.
    *   **Rejecting Finality under Article 163(2):** If the Governor claims discretion in a field not backed *"by or under the Constitution,"* the finality clause in **Article 163(2) does not bar judicial review**, as allowing a Governor to expand his own discretionary jurisdiction at will would destroy Westminster parliamentary democracy and federalism (both part of the **Basic Structure**).
3.  **Summoning, Proroguing, and Dissolving the House (Article 174) & Floor Tests (*Shivraj Singh Chouhan*, 2020):** In *Nabam Rebia* (2016), the Court held that in ordinary circumstances, the Governor can summon, prorogue, or dissolve the House under **Article 174** **only on the aid and advice of the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers**, and the Governor cannot use **Article 175(2)** (sending messages to the House) to dictate the legislative agenda of the Speaker or intermeddle with **Tenth Schedule** disqualification proceedings. However, *Nabam Rebia* also confirmed that if the Governor has **objective material indicating that the incumbent Council of Ministers has lost the confidence of the Legislative Assembly** and is avoiding a session to evade accountability, the Governor is ceased of the obligation to follow that Cabinet's advice and **can exercise situational discretion to summon the House for a Floor Test**.
    *   Subsequently, in ***Shivraj Singh Chouhan v. Speaker, Madhya Pradesh Legislative Assembly* (2020)**, when 22 ruling Congress MLAs resigned in Madhya Pradesh, reducing the कमल Nath government to a minority, the Speaker adjourned the House citing COVID-19 and argued that under *Nabam Rebia*, the Governor can order a floor test only at the initial formation of a government and *not* during the session of an ongoing House. Rejecting this argument, a Bench led by **Justice D.Y. Chandrachud** held that **the Governor’s constitutional power under Articles 174 and 175(2) to direct a Floor Test is NOT restricted to the initial formation of a government; it is equally available during the life of an ongoing Assembly** whenever objective circumstances demonstrate that the incumbent government has prima facie lost its legislative majority, because a government lacking majority has no constitutional authority to tender binding aid and advice under **Article 163(1)**. (Note that in ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra*, May 2023**, a 5-Judge Bench held that **internal intra-party dissent or a split within a ruling party is NOT a valid objective ground for a Governor to call a Floor Test**, as the floor test cannot be used as a medium to resolve internal party disputes; *Subhash Desai* also referred certain aspects of *Nabam Rebia* concerning the Speaker's power under the Tenth Schedule during pending removal notices to a 7-Judge Bench).

### 9. The Article 200 & 201 Legislative Assent Jurisprudence (2023–2026 Baseline)

#### Textual Architecture of Articles 200 and 201: The Three Options and the First Proviso
Between 2022 and 2026, the sharpest constitutional battleground in Indian federalism shifted to **Articles 200 and 201**, triggered by Governors in non-ruling opposition-governed States (notably **Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Telangana**) sitting indefinitely on dozens of Bills duly passed by their State Legislative Assemblies without either granting assent or returning them for reconsideration.

When a Bill has been passed by the Legislative Assembly of a State (and by the Legislative Council in a bicameral State) and is presented to the Governor under **Article 200**, the **Substantive Clause** of **Article 200** states that the Governor ***"shall declare"***:
1.  **Option 1:** That he **assents to the Bill**; or
2.  **Option 2:** That he **withholds assent therefrom**; or
3.  **Option 3:** That he **reserves the Bill for the consideration of the President**.

Immediately following the Substantive Clause, **Article 200** attaches **two critical Provisos**:
*   **First Proviso to Article 200 (Suspensive Return & Reconsideration):** *"Provided that the Governor may, **as soon as possible** after the presentation to him of the Bill for assent, **return the Bill if it is not a Money Bill** together with a message requesting that the House or Houses will reconsider the Bill or any specified provisions thereof and, in particular, will consider the desirability of introducing any such amendments as he may recommend in his message and, when a Bill is so returned, the House or Houses shall reconsider the Bill accordingly, and **if the Bill is passed again by the House or Houses with or without amendment and presented to the Governor for assent, the Governor shall not withhold assent therefrom**."*
*   **Second Proviso to Article 200 (Mandatory Reservation for High Court Protection):** *"Provided further that the Governor **shall not assent to, but shall reserve for the consideration of the President**, any Bill which in the opinion of the Governor would, if it became law, **so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is by this Constitution designed to fill**."* (In addition to this mandatory reservation under the Second Proviso to Article 200, reservation for the President is constitutionally required for State Bills seeking immunity for acquisition of estates under the **First Proviso to Article 31A(1)**, State laws on the **Concurrent List** seeking to prevail over an earlier Central law under **Article 254(2)**, State tax laws on water or electricity stored/generated by an inter-state river authority under **Article 288(2)**, and post-enactment State Bills imposing reasonable restrictions on freedom of trade and commerce where prior Presidential sanction was not taken, under **Article 255** read with **Article 304(b)**).

Once a Bill is reserved by a Governor for the consideration of the President under **Article 200**, the Governor becomes ***functus officio*** (his role ends permanently; he cannot even assent to the Bill afterward), and the Bill enters the exclusive domain of **Article 201**:
*   Under **Article 201**, the President shall declare either that he **assents to the Bill** or that he **withholds assent therefrom**.
*   Under the **Proviso to Article 201**, the President may (if the Bill is not a Money Bill) **direct the Governor to return the Bill to the State Legislature** together with a message for reconsideration. When a Bill is so returned by the President, the State Legislature must reconsider it within a period of **six months** from the date of receipt of such message, and if it is passed again by the State Legislature with or without amendment, it shall be presented again to the President for his consideration. **Crucially, unlike Article 111 and the First Proviso to Article 200, Article 201 DOES NOT state that *"the President shall not withhold assent therefrom."*** Thus, even if the State Legislature re-passes a reserved Bill after reconsideration, the **President is NOT constitutionally bound to grant assent under Article 201** and exercises an overriding federal veto on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers.

#### Dismantling the Gubernatorial Pocket Veto: State of Punjab (2023), State of Kerala (2024), and State of Tamil Nadu (2025)
To halt the executive paralysis of elected State Legislatures, the Supreme Court delivered a trilogy of judgments between 2023 and 2025 interpreting **Articles 200 and 201**:

1.  ***State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor of Punjab* (November 2023, 3-Judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud):** When the Governor of Punjab withheld action on four Bills passed by the Punjab Vidhan Sabha and later argued that if a Governor declares under the Substantive Clause of **Article 200** that he *"withholds assent therefrom,"* the Bill falls dead permanently (an absolute veto) without needing to be returned to the Assembly under the First Proviso, the 3-Judge Bench unanimously rejected this interpretation:
    *   **Intrinsically Linked Proviso:** The First Proviso to **Article 200** is **not a separate fourth option** independent of the second option (*"withholds assent therefrom"*); rather, the First Proviso qualifies and attaches directly to the withholding of assent. Whenever the Governor decides to withhold assent to a non-Money Bill, **he is constitutionally mandated to follow the course of action in the First Proviso and return the Bill *"as soon as possible"* together with a message for reconsideration by the State Legislature**.
    *   **Democratic Supremacy Over Unelected Nominee:** As an unelected constitutional head, the Governor **cannot exercise a "Pocket Veto" (by sitting on Bills indefinitely) or an "Absolute Veto" (by declaring assent withheld without returning the Bill to the House)** to thwart the law-making power of the elected State Legislature. The phrase *"as soon as possible"* in the First Proviso carries significant constitutional content and prohibits indefinite inaction.
2.  ***State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (April 8, 2025, 2-Judge Bench — Justices J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan) & *State of Kerala v. Governor of Kerala* (2024–2025):** In Tamil Nadu, the Governor had kept $12$ Bills (including University Amendment Bills) passed between 2020 and 2023 pending without action for up to three years; when the Supreme Court issued notice in November 2023, the Governor abruptly declared that he was *"withholding assent"* to $10$ Bills, and when the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly convened a special session and **re-passed all $10$ Bills in their exact original form**, the Governor **reserved the $10$ re-passed Bills for the consideration of the President under Article 200/201**. Delivering a 415-page judgment, the Bench held:
    *   **Illegality of Reserving a Re-Passed Bill:** Under the scheme of **Article 200**, the option to reserve a Bill for the President must be exercised **at the initial stage** when the Bill is first presented to the Governor. Once the Governor elects to withhold assent/return the Bill for reconsideration and **the State Legislature re-passes the Bill in the same form, the First Proviso's command (*"the Governor shall not withhold assent therefrom"*) becomes absolute—the Governor CANNOT subsequently reserve the re-passed Bill for the President** (unless the Legislature amended the Bill during reconsideration in a manner that introduced a fresh constitutional ground for reservation). Allowing a Governor to reserve a re-passed Bill would render the legislative reconsideration mechanism in the First Proviso wholly illusory.
    *   **Prescribing Timelines and Invoking Article 142 "Deemed Assent":** Going a step further, the 2-Judge Bench in *State of Tamil Nadu* prescribed indicative timelines (one to three months for the Governor under **Article 200**, and three months for the President under **Article 201**) and invoked **Article 142** to declare that the $10$ re-passed Tamil Nadu Bills were **"deemed to have received assent"** on the date they were re-presented to the Governor.

#### The 16th Presidential Reference under Article 143(1) (Special Reference No. 1 of 2025): Constitutional Discretion, Timelines, and Deemed Assent
Immediately following the April 2025 judgment in *State of Tamil Nadu*, on **May 13, 2025**, **President Droupadi Murmu** invoked **Article 143(1)** to make the **16th Presidential Reference** since the inception of the Constitution (***In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India*, Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, 2025 INSC 1333**), referring **14 constitutional questions** concerning the powers of the Governor and the President under **Articles 200, 201, 142, and 361**. On **November 20, 2025**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** led by **Chief Justice B.R. Gavai** (with **Justices Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, and A.S. Chandurkar**) delivered a unanimous 111-page advisory opinion that synthesized and recalibrated the constitutional balance between federal legislative autonomy and separation of powers:

1.  **Maintainability of a "Functional Reference" under Article 143(1):** Rejecting the objection of opposition-ruled States that the Reference was an impermissible appeal in disguise against *State of Tamil Nadu (2025)*, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench held that where a two-judge ruling raises fundamental structural ambiguities concerning the coordinate powers of high constitutional functionaries (the President and Governors), a **functional reference under Article 143(1)** is fully maintainable to authoritatively declare the law.
2.  **Three Exhaustive Options Under Article 200 & Prohibition on Indefinite Pocket Vetoes:** The Constitution Bench reaffirmed that under **Article 200**, the Governor has **three constitutional options**: (i) grant assent; (ii) reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President; or (iii) withhold assent and return a non-Money Bill with a message comments to the Legislature for reconsideration under the First Proviso. Crucially, the Bench affirmed that **prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite inaction by a Governor (a Pocket Veto) is constitutionally impermissible, and in cases of such prolonged inaction, the Court may issue a limited judicial direction requiring the Governor to discharge his constitutional function within a reasonable period**.
3.  **No Judicially Prescribed Fixed Timelines for Articles 200 and 201:** However, the 5-Judge Bench held that the 2-Judge Bench in *State of Tamil Nadu (2025)* erred in prescribing fixed, statutory-style timelines (e.g., 1 month or 3 months) for the Governor under **Article 200** and the President under **Article 201**. Because the framers consciously omitted numerical deadlines from **Articles 200 and 201** (using *"as soon as possible"* instead of a fixed number of days), courts cannot judicially legislate rigid timelines to bind the President or Governor.
4.  **Constitutional Discretion Under Article 200:** Clarifying the interplay between **Article 163(1)** and **Article 200**, the Constitution Bench held that when a Bill is presented under **Article 200**, the Governor exercises **constitutional discretion** in deciding whether to assent, return for reconsideration, or reserve the Bill for the President (as obviously the Council of Ministers that piloted the Bill through the Assembly would always advise granting assent; if the Governor were mechanically bound by Cabinet advice under Article 200, the power to return a Bill or reserve a Bill contradicting the Constitution or Central laws under Article 200 would become redundant).
5.  **Impermissibility of "Deemed Assent" Under Article 142 and Pre-Enactment Judicial Adjudication:** Finally, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench held that **the Supreme Court cannot invoke Article 142 ("Complete Justice") to substitute executive constitutional power by declaring "deemed assent" to a Bill**. Under the doctrine of **Separation of Powers**, a Bill becomes law only upon receiving the actual constitutional assent of the Governor under **Article 200** or the President under **Article 201**, and courts cannot adjudicate the substantive contents of a Bill at a stage *anterior* to its enactment into law.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 8: From Executive Assent to Bicameral Legislative Architecture
The constitutional tension surrounding executive assent under **Articles 111, 200, and 201**, ordinance promulgation under **Articles 123 and 213**, and collective ministerial responsibility under **Articles 75(3) and 164(2)** demonstrates that in India’s Westminster parliamentary synthesis, the Union and State Executives do not exist in a vacuum separated from the Legislature; rather, they are organically fused into, and derive their democratic legitimacy and statutory authority from, the legislative chambers. How a Bill reaches the desk of the President under **Article 111** or the Governor under **Article 200**—and how the elected lower house enforces collective financial accountability over the Council of Ministers—depends entirely on the internal institutional mechanics of Parliament and the State Legislatures.
