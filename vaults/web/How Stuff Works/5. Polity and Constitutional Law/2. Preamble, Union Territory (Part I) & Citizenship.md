## I. The Preamble: Normative Teleology, Constituent Identity, and the Amendability Dialectic

### 1. Ideological Lineage and the Sovereign Matrix of the Preamble

The Preamble to the Constitution of India operates as the normative compass and foundational synthesis of the constituent compact. Rather than functioning as an independent source of substantive legislative power or a direct fetter upon parliamentary competence, the Preamble codifies the overarching teleology toward which all constituted authorities must orient their coercive and redistributive functions. Its structural architecture resolves the primary tension of modern constitutionalism: how to reconcile popular sovereignty and majority rule with entrenched individual liberties and transformative social egalitarianism.

#### From the Objectives Resolution to the Enacting Clause

The textual lineage of the Preamble traces directly to the **Objectives Resolution**, moved by **Jawaharlal Nehru** on **December 13, 1946**, and unanimously adopted by the **Constituent Assembly** on **January 22, 1947**. While the **Objectives Resolution** originally envisioned a confederation where residuary powers vested in autonomous units (reflecting the transient constraints of the **Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946**), the trauma of Partition in 1947 compelled the **Drafting Committee**, chaired by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, to recalibrate the Preamble toward a centripetal, unified nation-state. 

*   **The Locus of Popular Sovereignty ("We, the People of India")**: During the **Constituent Assembly Debates (CAD)** on **October 17, 1949**, amendments moved by **Maulana Hasrat Mohani** (seeking to establish a "Union of Indian Socialist Republics") and **H.V. Kamath** (seeking to invoke the name of God: *"In the name of God, We, the People of India"*) were decisively rejected. **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** defended the secular and popular phrasing to establish that constitutional legitimacy derives neither from divine sanction nor from a grant by the British Parliament under the **Indian Independence Act, 1947**, nor from a compact among federating princely or provincial units, but directly from the autochthonous constituent power of the Indian citizenry.
*   **The Enacting Formula and Temporal Anchor**: The terminal clause—*"in our Constituent Assembly this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION"*—demarcates the exact moment when constituent power crystallized into supreme law. Although the vast majority of the Constitution commenced operation on **January 26, 1950** (commemorating the **Purna Swaraj** declaration of 1930), **Article 394** explicitly brought **Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392, and 393** into force immediately on **November 26, 1949**.

#### The Nature of the Indian State: Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic

The Preamble's opening declaration establishes a five-fold ontological matrix defining the character of the Indian polity:

1.  **Sovereign**: Signifies absolute internal supremacy and external independence from any foreign dominion. Unlike the dominion status granted under the **Indian Independence Act, 1947**, the proclamation of the Republic terminated the suzerainty of the British Crown. In May 1949, when India agreed to remain within the **Commonwealth of Nations**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** clarified before the **Constituent Assembly** that this was a voluntary, non-treaty association of free nations that imposed zero legal or constitutional dilution upon India's external sovereignty. Internally, sovereignty is not vested in a single organ (unlike the British doctrine of Parliamentary Sovereignty), but is constitutionally partitioned across the Union and State legislatures, executives, and the judiciary, bounded by **Part III (Fundamental Rights)** and the **Basic Structure Doctrine**.
2.  **Socialist**: Inserted explicitly by **Section 2 of the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, though previously latent inside the **Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV)**—specifically **Article 38** and **Article 39(b) and (c)**. In **D.S. Nakara v. Union of India (1983)** and **Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997)**, the Supreme Court held that Indian socialism is not a doctrinaire Marxist-Leninist mandate for the total nationalization of the means of production, but a democratic-Gandhian synthesis aimed at eliminating radical inequalities in income, status, and standard of life while sustaining a mixed economy.
3.  **Secular**: Also inserted by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, making explicit what was structurally embedded in **Articles 25 to 28**. Unlike the French model of **Laïcité** (which enforces a rigid, hostile wall of separation banishing religion from the public sphere), Indian constitutional secularism embodies **"Principled Distance"** and **Sarva Dharma Sambhava** (equal respect for all religions). In **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)** (9-Judge Bench) and **Aruna Roy v. Union of India (2002)**, the Supreme Court declared secularism a foundational pillar of the **Basic Structure**, empowering the State to remain neutral across faiths while simultaneously intervening under **Article 25(2)** to reform regressive social and religious practices.
4.  **Democratic**: Encompasses not merely procedural representative democracy anchored in universal adult suffrage (**Article 326**), collective parliamentary responsibility (**Article 75(3)**), and periodic free and fair elections, but also substantive socio-economic democracy. As **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** warned in his seminal closing address to the **Constituent Assembly** on **November 25, 1949**, political democracy cannot survive unless it stands upon the base of social democracy—a way of life that recognizes liberty, equality, and fraternity as the principles of collective existence.
5.  **Republic**: Contrasts with a hereditary monarchy (such as the United Kingdom, where the Crown serves as the titular Head of State). In a constitutional republic, the Head of State—the **President of India** (**Article 52**)—is elected indirectly for a fixed five-year term (**Articles 54–56**), and all public offices are open to every citizen without privileged caste or aristocratic monopolies.

#### The Teleological Triad: Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity

The second half of the Preamble specifies the substantive objectives that the sovereign polity is obligated to secure for its citizens:

*   **Justice (Social, Economic, and Political)**: Inspired by the **Russian Revolution of 1917**, the deliberate sequencing places **Social and Economic Justice** ahead of **Political Justice**. Social justice mandates the abolition of structural hierarchies based on caste, sex, or birth (**Articles 15, 17**); economic justice mandates the eradication of exploitative wealth concentration (**Article 39**); and political justice guarantees equal participation in the electoral and governance apparatus (**Articles 16, 325, 326**). The synthesis of Part III and Part IV represents the operationalization of this triad.
*   **Liberty (of Thought, Expression, Belief, Faith, and Worship)**: Drawn from the **French Revolution (1789)**, preambular liberty is not negative licence (the mere absence of state restraint), but positive constitutional empowerment operationalized through **Article 19(1)(a)** and **Articles 25–28**, subject only to proportionate restrictions tailored to preserve public order, morality, and constitutional integrity.
*   **Equality (of Status and of Opportunity)**: Combines formal civic equality before the law (**Article 14**) and the prohibition of state-sanctioned titles or untouchability (**Articles 17–18**) with substantive redistributive equality (**Articles 15(4)–(6) and 16(4)–(6)**), ensuring that historical deprivation is neutralized to create a level playing field.
*   **Fraternity (assuring the Dignity of the Individual and the Unity and Integrity of the Nation)**: **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** insisted on elevating **Fraternity** to a core constitutional pillar to counteract the graded inequality and social fragmentation of the caste system. Without fraternity, liberty and equality would require permanent state coercion to survive. At the intervention of **K.M. Munshi**, the phrase *"dignity of the individual"* was woven into Fraternity to signify that the State exists for the moral and material development of the human personality—a textual anchor later invoked by the Supreme Court in **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)** and **K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)** (9-Judge Bench) to read the **Right to Privacy** and bodily autonomy into **Article 21**. The word **"Integrity"** was added alongside "Unity" by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, reinforcing the anti-secessionist mandate of the **16th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963** and **Article 51A(c)**.

### 2. The 42nd Amendment (1976) and the 2024 Constitutional Adjudication on "Socialist" and "Secular"

#### Constituent Authority vs. The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

Enacted during the **Internal Emergency (1975–1977)** based on the recommendations of the **Swaran Singh Committee**, **Section 2 of the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** (with effect from **January 3, 1977**) amended the Preamble for the first and only time in Indian constitutional history. It performed two textual modifications:
1.  Substituted the words *"SOVEREIGN DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC"* with *"SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC"*.
2.  Substituted the words *"unity of the Nation"* with *"unity and integrity of the Nation"*.

For nearly five decades, a persistent doctrinal challenge questioned whether Parliament, exercising constituted amending power under **Article 368**, could validly alter a text that carries a fixed historical date stamp (**November 26, 1949**) without falsifying historical reality, and whether the emergency-era insertion of **"Socialist"** and **"Secular"** unlawfully bound future generations to a fixed ideological dogma that **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** had consciously declined to insert in 1949.

#### Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India (2024): Retrospective Legitimacy and Evolutionary Semantics

In **Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India (November 2024)**, the Supreme Court (comprising **Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna** and **Justice Sanjay Kumar**) decisively dismissed a batch of writ petitions challenging the constitutional validity of **Section 2 of the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**. The Court's ratio established four foundational propositions of public law:

*   **Plenary Reach of Article 368 Over the Preamble**: Reaffirming **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**, the Court held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and is therefore fully amenable to the amending power under **Article 368**, provided the amendment does not abrogate or destroy the **Basic Structure**.
*   **Resolution of the Temporal Date-Stamp Paradox**: Rejecting the argument that a document dated **November 26, 1949** cannot be retrospectively altered in 1976, the Bench held that the Constitution is a living organic instrument. The date of adoption in the enacting clause records the historical birth of the Republic; it does not create an immutable textual freeze that immunizes the Preamble from subsequent constitutional evolution under **Article 368**.
*   **Evolutionary Interpretation of "Socialist"**: In 1949, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** had opposed **Prof. K.T. Shah's** amendment to insert "Socialist" because he feared it would lock the Constitution into a narrow, state-monopolistic economic straightjacket and destroy parliamentary democracy's ability to choose economic policy. In **Dr. Balram Singh (2024)**, the Supreme Court held that over the past seven decades, Indian jurisprudence has stripped the word **"Socialist"** of any rigid, totalitarian Marxist connotation. In the Indian constitutional lexicon, "Socialist" denotes a commitment to a **Welfare State**, equality of opportunity, and the eradication of poverty (**Articles 38–39**)—a principle that seamlessly accommodates vibrant private enterprise, market liberalization, and mixed-economy reforms since 1991.
*   **Constitutional Grounding of "Secular" and the Doctrine of Laches**: The Court emphasized that **Secularism** was always embedded in the original 1950 text through **Articles 14, 15, 16, 25, 26, 27, 28, and 44**. The **42nd Amendment** merely foregrounded an existing **Basic Structure** feature (**S.R. Bommai, 1994**). Furthermore, even when the post-Emergency Parliament enacted the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978** to dismantle the authoritarian excesses of the **42nd Amendment**, it consciously chose to retain **"Socialist"**, **"Secular"**, and **"Integrity"** in the Preamble, signifying an enduring multi-party constituent consensus that could not be unsettled after 48 years.

#### From Non-Component (Berubari 1960) to Basic Structure Anchor (Kesavananda Bharati 1973 & LIC 1995)

1.  **In Re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960) (8-Judge Bench)**: When the Union Government argued that the Preamble did not restrict its sovereign power to cede national territory to Pakistan, Justice P.B. Gajendragadkar, writing for an 8-Judge Bench, quoted Joseph Story’s commentary on the U.S. Constitution to hold that while the Preamble is *"a key to open the mind of the makers"* which may show the general purposes for which they made the several provisions, **the Preamble is NOT a part of the Constitution**. Furthermore, the Court held that the Preamble had never been regarded as the source of any substantive power conferred on the Government of the Indian Union or of any of its departments.
2.  **I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967) (11-Judge Bench)**: Justice M. Hidayatullah began shifting the judicial lens, observing that the Preamble is not a mere platitude but sets out the *"Soul of the Constitution—eternal and unaltered"*, encapsulating the original objectives in a nutshell.
3.  **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) (13-Judge Bench)**: The 13-Judge Bench squarely overruled **In Re: Berubari Union (1960)** on the structural status of the Preamble. Chief Justice S.M. Sikri and Justices Shelat and Grover demonstrated that **Berubari** had ignored the empirical record of the **Constituent Assembly Debates** of **October 17, 1949**. Unlike ordinary statutes where a preamble is inserted casually by draftsmen, the **Constituent Assembly** debated the Preamble clause-by-clause *after* finalizing the rest of the Constitution to ensure perfect structural conformity, and the President of the Assembly (**Dr. Rajendra Prasad**) formally put the question: *"The question is that the Preamble stands part of the Constitution"*—which was adopted by the House. The Court established three binding principles:
    *   The Preamble is a formal, integral **part of the Constitution**.
    *   Because it is part of the Constitution, it falls within the ambit of **"this Constitution"** under **Article 368** and can be amended by Parliament.
    *   However, the edifice of the Constitution is built upon the basic elements formulated in the Preamble; therefore, the amending power under **Article 368** cannot be used to destroy or damage the fundamental features (**Basic Structure**) enshrined in the Preamble. It can only be amended to enrich, amplify, or strengthen those foundational values.
4.  **L.I.C. of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre (1995)**: The Supreme Court reiterated that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and that socio-economic justice guaranteed in the Preamble, read with **Articles 14, 21, and 38**, informs the judicial scrutiny of state instrumentalities (such as public sector insurance contracts). Nevertheless, the Court maintained two enduring structural boundaries:
    *   **Non-Source of Legislative Competence**: Neither Parliament nor a State Legislature can claim a standalone legislative power from the Preamble outside the **Seventh Schedule** (**Articles 245–248**).
    *   **Non-Justiciability in Isolation**: A citizen cannot file a writ petition under **Article 32** or **Article 226** alleging a violation of the Preamble alone, untethered to a specific enforceable right in **Part III** or another operative constitutional article.

## II. The Union and Its Territory (Part I, Articles 1–4): Federal Asymmetry and Territorial Sovereignty

### 4. Article 1: "India, that is Bharat" and the Indestructible Union of Destructible States

**Part I of the Constitution (Articles 1 to 4)** establishes the territorial framework of the Indian Republic and regulates the constitutional mechanics for reconfiguring its internal and external geography. Unlike classical federations where pre-existing sovereign states bargain to surrender limited powers to a central authority while retaining territorial inviolability, India's constitutional design privileges national territorial integrity and central parliamentary adaptability over provincial permanence.

#### Constituent Assembly Debates on Dual Nomenclature and Federal Permanence

**Article 1(1)** declares: *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."* Both the nomenclature of the nation and the description of its federal polity emerged from intense ideological contestation in the **Constituent Assembly**:

*   **The Dual Nomenclature Compromise ("India, that is Bharat")**: On **September 18, 1949**, the Assembly debated the name of the nation. Members such as **H.V. Kamath**, **Seth Govind Das**, **Kallur Subba Rao**, and **Hargovind Pant** advocated foregrounding the ancient civilizational name **"Bharat"** or **"Bharatvarsha"** (calling for formulations like *"Bharat, or in the English language, India"*), arguing that "India" was a colonial exonym. Conversely, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and **Jawaharlal Nehru** emphasized the necessity of retaining **"India"** for international continuity—given that India was already a founding signatory to the **United Nations Charter** and numerous international treaties under that name. The resulting text—*"India, that is Bharat"*—synthesizes indigenous civilizational continuity with modern international legal personality.
*   **"Union of States" vs. "Federation of States"**: In presenting the Draft Constitution on **November 4, 1948**, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** explained why the **Drafting Committee** deliberately substituted the term *"Union of States"* (borrowed from the preamble to the **British North America Act, 1867** of Canada) for the term *"Federation of States"* that had appeared in the **Cabinet Mission Plan**:
    1.  **Non-Compact Origin**: The Indian Federation is **not** the result of an agreement or treaty by sovereign federating units (unlike the 13 American colonies at Philadelphia in 1787).
    2.  **Zero Right to Secede**: Because the Union was not formed by a voluntary compact, no State possesses the legal or constitutional right to secede from the Union. The Union is permanent and indissoluble.
*   **An Indestructible Union of Destructible States**: Whereas the United States Supreme Court in **Texas v. White (1869)** famously characterized the American polity under **Article IV, Section 3 of the U.S. Constitution** as *"an indestructible Union, composed of indestructible States"* (meaning the U.S. Congress cannot alter a state's borders or merge it without that state legislature's explicit consent), **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** described the Indian polity as **an indestructible Union of destructible States**. While the country is an integral whole, its division into different States is solely for administrative convenience and federal governance; Parliament can unilaterally redraw, bifurcate, merge, or extinguish the territorial existence of any State under **Article 3**. Nonetheless, in **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)** (9-Judge Bench) and **State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)** (6-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court clarified that although individual State boundaries are malleable under **Article 3**, the overarching principle of **Federalism**—the existence of autonomous States with independent legislative and executive spheres—is an indestructible element of the **Basic Structure**.

#### Taxonomy of Indian Territory under Article 1(3) and the First Schedule

**Article 1(2)** stipulates that *"The States and the territories thereof shall be as specified in the First Schedule."* Originally, the **First Schedule** classified units into four categories (**Part A** former British Governor's provinces, **Part B** princely states/unions with legislatures, **Part C** centrally administered Chief Commissioner's provinces, and **Part D** Andaman and Nicobar Islands). The **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956** abolished this four-tier hierarchy, replacing it with a binary classification of **States** and **Union Territories** (currently comprising **28 States and 8 Union Territories**).

**Article 1(3)** establishes a crucial legal distinction between the **"Union of India"** and the **"Territory of India"** by defining the **Territory of India** as comprising three distinct sub-categories:
*   **Article 1(3)(a)**: The territories of the **States** (Presently 28 States).
*   **Article 1(3)(b)**: The **Union Territories** specified in the **First Schedule** (Presently 8 Union Territories).
*   **Article 1(3)(c)**: Such other territories as may be **acquired**.

**Analytical Deduction on "Union of India" vs. "Territory of India"**:
*   **The "Union of India" (Article 1(1))** is the narrower constitutional category: it includes **only the States** that share in the federal distribution of legislative and executive powers under **Parts VI, XI, and XII** with the Central Government.
*   **The "Territory of India" (Article 1(3))** is the wider geographical and sovereign category: it encompasses not only the federal **States**, but also the centrally administered **Union Territories** (**Part VIII**) and any future territories **acquired** by the Indian State under international law (via cession, treaty, plebiscite, purchase, gift, or conquest/occupation), such as Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Goa, Daman and Diu, and Puducherry prior to their formal incorporation into the First Schedule.
*   Under **Article 245(1)**, Parliament's legislative competence extends to the whole or any part of the **"Territory of India"**, whereas a State Legislature's competence is confined strictly to the territory of that State.

### 5. Articles 2, 3, and 4: External Admission vs. Internal Reconfiguration and Unicameral Federalism

#### Structural Demarcation: Article 2 vs. Article 3

The Constitution separates external territorial incorporation from internal cartographic reorganization across **Article 2** and **Article 3**:

*   **Article 2 (External Admission or Establishment of New States)**: Empowers Parliament by law to *"admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit."* **Article 2** governs territories that are **not** currently part of the **Union of India**. It confers two distinct powers on Parliament:
    1.  *The power to admit into the Union new States*: Refers to the admission of duly organized, pre-existing political entities or states outside the Union.
    2.  *The power to establish new States*: Refers to the creation and admission of a new State out of foreign or newly acquired territory that did not previously exist as a state.
    *   *The Sikkim Constitutional Precedent (35th and 36th Amendments, 1974–1975)*: In **R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India (1993)** (5-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court examined the scope of the phrase *"on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit"* in **Article 2**. When Sikkim (previously a hereditary protectorate ruled by the Chogyal) sought integration with India, Parliament first enacted the **35th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974**, inserting **Article 2A** and the **Tenth Schedule** to make Sikkim an **"Associate State"**—a status unknown to **Article 1(3)**. Following a 1975 referendum abolishing the monarchy, Parliament enacted the **36th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1975**, repealing **Article 2A** and the Tenth Schedule, admitting Sikkim as a full State in the **First Schedule**, and inserting **Article 371F** (providing asymmetric seat reservations in the Sikkim Legislative Assembly for ethnic Bhutia-Lepchas and the *Sangha* Buddhist monasteries). In **R.C. Poudyal (1993)**, the Supreme Court held that while *"terms and conditions"* under **Article 2** grant Parliament wide latitude to accommodate the historical and ethnic peculiarities of a newly admitted territory, those conditions **cannot violate the Basic Structure** of the Constitution.
*   **Article 3 (Internal Formation of New States and Alteration of Areas, Boundaries, or Names of Existing States)**: Governs the internal readjustment *inter se* of the territories of the existing **States** and **Union Territories**. Supported by **Explanations I and II** (inserted by the **18th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1966**), **Article 3** empowers Parliament by law to:
    *   **(a)** Form a new State by separation of territory from any State, or by uniting two or more States or parts of States, or by uniting any territory to a part of any State;
    *   **(b)** Increase the area of any State;
    *   **(c)** Diminish the area of any State;
    *   **(d)** Alter the boundaries of any State;
    *   **(e)** Alter the name of any State.
    *   *Impact of the 18th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1966*: Conducted during the trifurcation of Punjab into Punjab, Haryana, and the Union Territory of Chandigarh (under the **Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966**), the **18th Amendment** added **Explanations I and II** to **Article 3**. **Explanation I** clarified that the word **"State"** in clauses (a) to (e) of **Article 3** (though **not** in the Proviso) includes a **"Union Territory"**. **Explanation II** clarified that the power to form a new State under clause (a) includes the power to form a new **Union Territory** by uniting a part of any State or Union Territory to any other State or Union Territory.

#### The Article 3 Proviso and Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1960)

To provide a procedural channel for federal consultation without granting States a veto over national map-making, the **Proviso to Article 3** (as amended by the **5th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1955**) imposes two mandatory procedural conditions before any Bill under **Article 3** can be introduced in either House of Parliament:

1.  **Mandatory Prior Presidential Recommendation**: No Bill for the purpose shall be introduced in either House of Parliament except on the recommendation of the **President**.
2.  **Mandatory Reference to the Affected State Legislature within a President-Specified Timeframe**: Where the proposal contained in the Bill affects the area, boundaries, or name of any of the **States**, the Bill **must** be referred by the President to the Legislature of that State for expressing its views thereon within such period as may be specified in the reference (or within such further period as the President may allow), and the period so specified or allowed has expired.

The constitutional limits of this consultative safeguard were tested before a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1960)**:
*   *Factual Matrix*: The **States Reorganisation Bill, 1956** was originally referred by the President to the Bombay State Legislature proposing a three-unit formula (Maharashtra, Gujarat, and a separate centrally administered Union Territory of Bombay City). After the Bombay Legislature expressed its views, Parliament drastically amended the Bill during its passage to create a single composite bilingual State of Bombay instead. The petitioner argued that this radical alteration required a fresh Presidential reference to the Bombay State Legislature under the **Proviso to Article 3**.
*   *Ratio Decidendi*: The 5-Judge Bench unanimously rejected the challenge, establishing four enduring rules of **Article 3** jurisprudence:
    *   **Non-Binding Nature of State Views**: The President and Parliament are **not bound** to accept or act upon the views expressed by the State Legislature. Even if a State Legislature unanimously rejects a bifurcation Bill (as the Andhra Pradesh Legislative Assembly did prior to the enactment of the **Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014** creating Telangana), Parliament retains plenary power to enact the law.
    *   **No Fresh Reference for Parliamentary Amendments**: Once the original Bill has been validly referred to the State Legislature, Parliament's plenary power to move and pass germane amendments to the Bill is unfettered; no fresh reference to the State Legislature is required for subsequent parliamentary modifications.
    *   **Lapse of Timeframe**: If the State Legislature fails to express its views within the time limit fixed by the President, the constitutional condition stands fully discharged upon the expiry of that deadline.
    *   **Exemption for Union Territories**: Because **Explanation I to Article 3** explicitly excludes the **Proviso**, a Bill affecting the area, boundaries, or name of a **Union Territory** (even one with a legislative assembly under **Article 239A** or **Article 239AA**) does **not** require any reference to that Union Territory's legislature; Parliament can alter it unilaterally.

#### Article 4: Simple Parliamentary Majority Outside the Article 368 Crucible

**Article 4** provides the constitutional mechanism that insulates laws enacted under **Articles 2 and 3** from the rigid procedural requirements of **Article 368**:
*   **Article 4(1)** mandates that any law referred to in **Article 2** or **Article 3** shall contain such provisions for the amendment of the **First Schedule** (names and territorial extent of States/UTs) and the **Fourth Schedule** (allocation of seats in the Rajya Sabha) as may be necessary to give effect to the provisions of the law, and may also contain such **supplemental, incidental, and consequential provisions** (including provisions as to representation in Parliament and in the Legislature or Legislatures of the State or States affected, High Court jurisdiction, apportionment of assets, liabilities, and public service cadres) as Parliament may deem necessary. In **Mangal Singh v. Union of India (1967)**, the Supreme Court held that under **Article 4(1)**, Parliament can even temporarily create a Legislative Assembly with fewer than the minimum 60 members prescribed under **Article 170(1)** during a transitional reorganisation phase.
*   **Article 4(2)** explicitly declares: *"No such law as aforesaid shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368."* Consequently, a State Reorganisation Act requires only an **ordinary legislative process** passed by a **Simple Majority** (a majority of members present and voting) in both Houses of Parliament.

#### Asymmetric Reorganisation and the J&K Constitutional Threshold (In Re: Article 370, 2023)

Historically, the only exception to Parliament's unilateral power under **Article 3** applied to the former State of Jammu and Kashmir. Under the **Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order, 1954**, a second proviso had been appended to **Article 3** stipulating that no Bill providing for increasing or diminishing the area of Jammu and Kashmir or altering its name or boundary could be introduced in Parliament **without the consent of the Legislature of that State**.

In August 2019, following the issuance of **Constitutional Order 272** and **Constitutional Order 273** superseding the 1954 Order, while Jammu and Kashmir was under **President's Rule (Article 356)**, Parliament exercised the powers of the State Legislature under **Article 356(1)(b)** to furnish its own views under the **Proviso to Article 3** and enacted the **Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019**, bifurcating the State into two Union Territories (**Jammu and Kashmir** with a legislature, and **Ladakh** without a legislature).

In **In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution (December 2023)** (5-Judge Constitution Bench), the petitioners argued that **Article 3** does not permit Parliament to downgrade an entire existing State into two Union Territories (claiming it violates federal Basic Structure), and that Parliament cannot substitute its own views for those of an elected State Legislature during **President's Rule** to effect an irreversible cartographic alteration.
*   *The 5-Judge Bench Ruling*:
    1.  The Court upheld the reorganization of **Ladakh** as a separate Union Territory, holding that **Article 3(a)** read with **Explanation I and II** explicitly empowers Parliament to carve a Union Territory out of a State.
    2.  Regarding the larger constitutional question of whether **Article 3** permits the wholesale extinguishment of a State's statehood by converting an entire State into Union Territories, the Bench kept the abstract doctrinal question open because the Solicitor General gave a solemn constitutional undertaking before the Supreme Court that Jammu and Kashmir's status as a Union Territory is temporary and that its full **Statehood** will be restored, directing the **Election Commission of India** to conduct Legislative Assembly elections (subsequently completed in late 2024).

### 6. Sovereign Cession vs. Boundary Settlement and the Historical Evolution of States

#### In Re: Berubari Union (1960), the 9th Amendment, and the 100th Amendment Act (2015)

A critical question of constitutional law arose when Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Pakistani Prime Minister Feroz Khan Noon signed the **Nehru-Noon Agreement (1958)** to divide the **Berubari Union No. 12** (a 9-square-mile territory in Jalpaiguri district, West Bengal) equally between India and East Pakistan and to exchange Cooch Behar enclaves. The Union Government contended that it could implement the transfer either via executive treaty-making power or by enacting an ordinary law under **Article 3(c)** (*"diminish the area of any State"*) read with **Article 4(2)**. The President referred the question to the Supreme Court under **Article 143(1)**.

In **In Re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960)** (8-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court drew a sharp constitutional boundary between internal federal adjustment and external cession of national sovereignty:

*   **Scope of Article 3(c)**: The Court held that **Article 3** deals exclusively with the **internal readjustment** *inter se* of the territories of the constituent States of India. When Parliament diminishes the area of a State under **Article 3(c)**, the excised territory continues to remain part of the **"Territory of India"** under **Article 1(3)** (being absorbed into another State or Union Territory). **Article 3(c)** does **not** empower Parliament to cede Indian sovereign territory to a foreign nation.
*   **Mandatory Article 368 Constitutional Amendment for Cession**: Because ceding sovereign territory to a foreign power permanently reduces the total territorial footprint recorded in the **First Schedule** under **Article 1(3)**, it can only be accomplished by exercising constituent power to amend the **First Schedule** through a formal **Constitutional Amendment Act under Article 368(2)**. Consequently, Parliament had to enact the **9th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1960** to validate the Berubari transfer.
*   **The Boundary Dispute Exception (Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India, 1969)**: In **Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel (1969)** (5-Judge Bench), concerning the **Rann of Kutch International Tribunal Award**, the Supreme Court carved out a crucial corollary to **Berubari**. Where a border between India and a foreign country is genuinely **disputed, uncertain, and undemarcated**, the settlement and demarcation of that boundary does **not** amount to a "cession" of sovereign Indian territory. Instead, boundary settlement can be executed solely by **Executive Action** under **Article 73** without requiring either an **Article 368** amendment or an **Article 3** statute.
*   **The 100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015 (Indo-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement)**: To resolve the complex post-Partition crisis of 111 Indian enclaves inside Bangladesh (17,160.63 acres) and 51 Bangladeshi enclaves inside India (7,110.02 acres), along with adverse possessions across Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya, and Tripura, India and Bangladesh operationalized the **1974 Land Boundary Agreement (LBA)** and its **2011 Protocol**. Because transferring the 111 enclaves involved the physical cession of de jure Indian territory to a foreign state, Parliament strictly adhered to the **Berubari (1960)** doctrine by enacting the **100th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2015**, which amended the **First Schedule** under **Article 368** to formalize both the cession and the acquisition of enclaves.

#### Linguistic and Administrative Reorganization: Dhar, JVP, Fazl Ali Commissions, and the 1956 Act

At Independence, India's internal map was an arbitrary product of British military conquests and the swift integration of 552 Princely States (spearheaded by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** and **V.P. Menon**, with Hyderabad integrated via **Operation Polo**, Junagadh via plebiscite, and Jammu & Kashmir via the **Instrument of Accession**). The transition from colonial administrative provinces to rationalized federal states unfolded through four major phases:

1.  **The Linguistic Provinces Commission (Dhar Commission, June–December 1948)**: Appointed by the President of the Constituent Assembly under the chairmanship of **S.K. Dhar** to examine the demand for linguistic states in South India. The Commission **rejected** linguistic reorganization, warning that sub-national linguistic chauvinism would threaten national integration, and recommended that provinces be organized strictly on the basis of **administrative convenience, geographical contiguity, financial self-sufficiency, and developmental potential**.
2.  **The JVP Committee (December 1948–April 1949)**: Comprising **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Vallabhbhai Patel**, and **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** (appointed by the Indian National Congress to review the public backlash against the Dhar report). The **JVP Committee** formally reiterated that national security, unity, and economic consolidation must override linguistic considerations, though it conceded that if public sentiment became overwhelming and insistent, the demand could be re-examined interactively.
3.  **Creation of Andhra State (1953) and the Fazl Ali Commission (1953–1955)**: Following the death of Gandhian leader **Potti Sreeramulu** in December 1952 after a 56-day fast-unto-death, the Union Government carved out the first linguistic state—**Andhra State**—by separating the Telugu-speaking areas from Madras State in October 1953. This triggered a nationwide cascade of linguistic demands, compelling the Government in December 1953 to appoint the three-member **States Reorganisation Commission (SRC)** chaired by **Justice Fazl Ali**, alongside **K.M. Panikkar** and **H.N. Kunzru**.
    *   *Core Recommendations of the Fazl Ali Commission (September 1955)*: The Commission broadly **accepted language and cultural homogeneity** as the primary basis for state reorganization, while emphatically **rejecting the theory of "One Language, One State"** (recognizing that a single language could span multiple states, and every state would contain linguistic minorities). It established four governing principles for any **Article 3** reorganization:
        1.  Preservation and strengthening of the **unity and security of the country**;
        2.  **Linguistic and cultural homogeneity**;
        3.  **Financial, economic, and administrative considerations**;
        4.  Planning and promotion of the **welfare of the people** in each state as well as the nation as a whole.
4.  **The States Reorganisation Act, 1956 and the 7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**: Enacted to implement the Fazl Ali report (which came into force on **November 1, 1956**), abolishing the Part A, B, C, and D distinction and reorganizing the country into **14 States and 6 Union Territories**. The **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** (Section 15) also established five statutory **Zonal Councils** (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, and Southern) chaired by the **Union Home Minister** to foster inter-state cooperative federalism and mitigate linguistic parochialism.

## III. Citizenship Mechanics (Part II, Articles 5–11) and the Statutory Regime of the Citizenship Act, 1955

### 7. Constitutional Citizenship at Commencement and Plenary Parliamentary Competence (Articles 5–11)

While **Part I** demarcates the physical territory of the State, **Part II (Articles 5 to 11)** defines the human polity—the legal bond of political membership that transforms an individual from a mere resident subject into a right-bearing citizen. In the **Constituent Assembly**, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** noted that **"Part II drafted the most intense headache"** for the **Drafting Committee** because Partition had triggered the largest cross-border demographic displacement in modern history, requiring an urgent constitutional ledger to determine who belonged to the new Republic on **January 26, 1950**, while delegating all future citizenship regulation to Parliament.

#### The Four-Tier Commencement Matrix (Articles 5, 6, 7, and 8)

**Articles 5 to 8** do **not** lay down a permanent code of citizenship; they solely determine four distinct classes of persons who became citizens of India **at the commencement of the Constitution (January 26, 1950)**, though **Articles 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9** were brought into operational force prematurely on **November 26, 1949** under **Article 394**:

1.  **Article 5 (Citizenship by Domicile at Commencement)**: A person became a citizen of India on January 26, 1950 if they satisfied **two cumulative conditions**:
    *   *Condition 1*: They had their **domicile** in the territory of India; **AND**
    *   *Condition 2*: They fulfilled **any one** of three connecting factors: **(a)** they were born in the territory of India; OR **(b)** either of their parents was born in the territory of India; OR **(c)** they had been **ordinarily resident** in the territory of India for not less than **five years** immediately preceding such commencement.
    *   *Jurisprudence on Single Domicile (Pradeep Jain v. Union of India, 1984)*: In **Pradeep Jain (1984)**, the Supreme Court emphasized that "domicile" (physical residence coupled with *animus manendi*—the intention to reside permanently or indefinitely) under **Article 5** is strictly a **Domicile of India**. India knows only **one domicile**, just as it knows only one citizenship; the concept of a separate "State domicile" (as distinct from residential preference requirements under **Articles 15 and 16(3)**) is legally alien to the Indian Constitution.
2.  **Article 6 (Rights of Citizenship of Certain Persons Who Have Migrated to India from Pakistan)**: Because millions of Hindu and Sikh refugees fleeing Pakistan lacked five years' prior residence in truncated India under **Article 5(c)**, **Article 6** created a dual-track mechanism for any person who migrated to India from territory now included in Pakistan, provided they or either of their parents or any of their grandparents was born in **undivided India** (as defined in the **Government of India Act, 1935**):
    *   *Track 1 (Migration BEFORE July 19, 1948)*: Deemed a citizen if they had been ordinarily resident in the territory of India since the date of their migration.
    *   *Track 2 (Migration ON OR AFTER July 19, 1948)*: The cut-off date of **July 19, 1948** marks the exact day the **Indo-Pakistan Influx from Pakistan (Control) Ordinance** introduced the **Permit System** between the two dominions. Persons migrating on or after this date had to reside in India for at least **six months** preceding their application and be formally **registered** as a citizen before commencement by an officer appointed by the Government of the Dominion of India.
3.  **Article 7 (Rights of Citizenship of Certain Migrants to Pakistan)**: Operates as a *non-obstante* overriding clause (*"Notwithstanding anything in articles 5 and 6"*):
    *   **The Disqualification Rule**: Any person who, after **March 1, 1947** (the date when widespread communal disturbances and partition migrations began in Punjab and Bengal), migrated from the territory of India to the territory now included in Pakistan **shall NOT be deemed to be a citizen of India**. In **State of Bihar v. Kumar Amar Singh (1955)** (5-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court held that **Article 7** overrides **Article 5**; even if a wife's domicile technically followed her husband's domicile in India under private international law, her physical migration to Pakistan after March 1, 1947 stripped her of citizenship under **Article 7**.
    *   **The Proviso for Reverse Migrants (Returnees)**: Carves out an exception for persons who, after migrating to Pakistan, **returned to the territory of India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return** issued by or under the authority of any law; such returnees are treated on par with post-July 19, 1948 migrants under **Article 6(b)**.
4.  **Article 8 (Rights of Citizenship of Certain Persons of Indian Origin Residing Outside India)**: Designed for the overseas Indian diaspora residing outside undivided India (e.g., in Malaya, Burma, East Africa, Fiji, or the UK). Any person who, or either of whose parents or any of whose grandparents, was born in undivided India (as defined in the **Government of India Act, 1935**), and who is ordinarily residing in any country outside India, shall be deemed a citizen of India if they have been **registered as a citizen of India by the diplomatic or consular representative of India** in that country, whether before or after the commencement of the Constitution. Unlike **Articles 5–7**, registration under **Article 8** operated both before and after January 26, 1950.

#### Single Citizenship, Automatic Termination (Article 9), and Parliamentary Plenary Power (Article 11)

The remaining three articles of **Part II** establish the structural rules of single citizenship, continuities, and complete parliamentary supremacy:

*   **Single Citizenship vs. Dual Federal Citizenship**: Unlike the **United States** (under the **14th Amendment**) or **Switzerland**, where citizens hold dual citizenship (national citizenship plus state/cantonal citizenship, which historically allowed states to discriminate in voting rights, public office, or judicial access), the Indian Constitution establishes a **Uniform Single Citizenship**. All citizens enjoy identical political and civil rights across the entire territory of India, subject only to narrow constitutional exceptions:
    1.  **Article 16(3)**: Empowering **Parliament alone** (and not State Legislatures) to prescribe residence within a State or Union Territory as a condition for certain classes of public employment (operationalized via the **Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act, 1957**, and preserved asymmetrically for Telangana and Andhra Pradesh under **Article 371D**).
    2.  **Article 15**: Permitting States to offer domicile-based fee concessions or seat quotas in state-funded educational institutions (**Pradeep Jain, 1984**; **Saurabh Chaudri, 2003**), though **Article 15(1)** strictly prohibits discrimination based *solely* on place of birth.
    3.  **Article 19(5)** and **Fifth/Sixth Schedule & Article 371A/371G Protections**: Permitting reasonable restrictions on the right to move freely and reside/settle (**Article 19(1)(d) and (e)**) to protect the interests of **Scheduled Tribes** (such as the **Inner Line Permit** regime under the **Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873** and restrictions on non-tribal land alienation).
*   **Article 9 (Persons Voluntarily Acquiring Citizenship of a Foreign State Not to be Citizens)**: Provides that no person shall be a citizen of India by virtue of **Article 5**, or be deemed to be a citizen of India by virtue of **Article 6 or Article 8**, if they have **voluntarily acquired the citizenship of any foreign State** prior to the commencement of the Constitution. (For voluntary foreign acquisition *after* January 26, 1950, the identical rule of automatic termination is enforced statutorily under **Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955**). In **Izhar Ahmad Khan v. Union of India (1962)** (5-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court held that obtaining a foreign passport creates a powerful, conclusive statutory presumption of voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship unless proven to have been obtained under duress or compulsion.
*   **Article 10 (Continuance of the Rights of Citizenship)**: Guarantees that every person who is or is deemed to be a citizen under **Articles 5 to 8** shall continue to be such citizen, *"subject to the provisions of any law that may be made by Parliament"*—protecting citizens from arbitrary executive stripping of citizenship without legislative backing (**Ebrahim Vazir Mavat v. State of Bombay, 1954**).
*   **Article 11 (Parliament to Regulate the Right of Citizenship by Law)**: The supreme enabling anchor of **Part II**: *"Nothing in the foregoing provisions of this Part shall derogate from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship."* Supported by **Entry 17 of List I (Union List)** of the **Seventh Schedule** (*"Citizenship, naturalisation and aliens"*), **Article 11** vests exclusive, plenary legislative competence in **Parliament**—even permitting Parliament to override or modify the commencement criteria of **Articles 5 to 8** (**In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, 2024**). State Legislatures possess **zero competence** to legislate on citizenship.

### 8. The Citizenship Act, 1955: The Shift from Jus Soli to Jus Sanguinis and Modes of Loss

Exercising its plenary power under **Article 11**, Parliament enacted **The Citizenship Act, 1955**, which has been substantially amended in **1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, and 2019**. The evolutionary arc of the Act reflects a profound philosophical shift from the inclusive, territory-based doctrine of **jus soli** (right of the soil—citizenship by birth within the territory) toward the bloodline and descent-based doctrine of **jus sanguinis** (right of blood), driven by security concerns over undocumented cross-border migration along India's eastern borders.

#### Five Statutory Pathways of Acquisition (Sections 3 to 7): The Contraction of Birthright Citizenship

The **Citizenship Act, 1955** prescribes five distinct statutory mechanisms for acquiring Indian citizenship after January 26, 1950:

1.  **Citizenship by Birth (Section 3)**: As illustrated in the schematic above, the original 1955 Act adopted the Anglo-American **jus soli** model. However, the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1986** and the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003** progressively dismantled **jus soli** by introducing the statutory category of **"Illegal Migrant"** under **Section 2(1)(b)** (defined as a foreigner who enters India without a valid passport/travel documents, or enters with valid documents but stays beyond the permitted time). Under **Section 3(1)**, citizenship by birth operates across three strict temporal tiers:
    *   *Tier 1 (Born between Jan 26, 1950 and June 30, 1987)*: Citizen by birth regardless of parents' nationality.
    *   *Tier 2 (Born between July 1, 1987 and Dec 2, 2004)*: Citizen by birth only if **either parent** is an Indian citizen at the time of birth.
    *   *Tier 3 (Born on or after Dec 3, 2004)*: Citizen by birth only if **both parents** are Indian citizens, OR if **one parent** is an Indian citizen and the **other parent is NOT an illegal migrant** at the time of birth. *(In all three tiers, children of foreign diplomats enjoying immunity and children of enemy aliens born in enemy-occupied territory are excluded under **Section 3(2)**).*
2.  **Citizenship by Descent (Section 4)**: Governs persons born **outside India** on or after January 26, 1950:
    *   *Jan 26, 1950 to Dec 9, 1992 (Patrilineal Bias)*: Citizen by descent only if the **father** was a citizen of India by birth at the time of the child's birth (if the father was a citizen by descent only, the birth had to be registered at an Indian consulate within 1 year).
    *   *Dec 10, 1992 to Dec 2, 2004 (Gender Parity via 1992 Amendment)*: Eliminated patriarchal discrimination following India's ratification of **CEDAW**, granting citizenship by descent if **either parent** (father or mother) was a citizen of India at the time of birth.
    *   *On or after Dec 3, 2004 (2003 Amendment Mandatory Consular Registration)*: A person born outside India shall **not** be a citizen by descent unless their birth is **registered at an Indian Consulate within one year** of birth (or with Central Government permission afterward), accompanied by a mandatory parents' declaration that the minor child **does not hold the passport of any other country**.
3.  **Citizenship by Registration (Section 5)**: The Central Government may, on an application, register as a citizen of India any person (**not being an illegal migrant**) who falls into specified categories holding a close nexus to India, including:
    *   **(a)** A person of Indian origin (PIO) who is ordinarily resident in India for **seven years** before making an application;
    *   **(c)** A person who is married to a citizen of India and is ordinarily resident in India for **seven years** before applying;
    *   **(d)** Minor children of persons who are citizens of India;
    *   **(f) & (g)** A person of full age and capacity who (or either of his parents) was earlier a citizen of independent India and is ordinarily resident in India for **twelve months** immediately before applying, OR an **Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) Cardholder** registered for **five years** and ordinarily resident in India for **twelve months** before applying. Every registered adult must take an **Oath of Allegiance** in the **Second Schedule**.
4.  **Citizenship by Naturalisation (Section 6 and the Third Schedule)**: The Central Government may grant a certificate of naturalisation to any foreigner of full age and capacity (**not being an illegal migrant**) who is not a citizen of a country that prevents Indian citizens from becoming its naturalized citizens, and who fulfills the qualifications in the **Third Schedule**:
    *   They must renounce their existing foreign nationality upon grant of the certificate;
    *   They must be of **good character** and possess an **adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule** (22 languages);
    *   **Residency Requirement**: They must have resided in India or been in the service of a Government in India continuously for the **12 months** immediately preceding the application, AND during the **14 years** preceding the said 12 months, have resided in India for an aggregate period of **not less than 11 years** (effectively **12 out of 15 years**). *(Note: The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019** reduced this 11-year aggregate threshold to **5 years** for six specified religious minority communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan).*
    *   *Special Waiver Proviso to Section 6(1)*: The Government of India may **waive all or any** of the **Third Schedule** conditions if, in its opinion, the applicant has rendered **distinguished service to the cause of science, philosophy, art, literature, world peace, or human progress generally** (e.g., Mother Teresa, Adnan Sami).
5.  **Citizenship by Incorporation of Territory (Section 7)**: If any foreign territory becomes a part of India, the Central Government may, by Order notified in the Official Gazette, specify the persons who shall be citizens of India by reason of their connection with that territory from the date specified in the Order (exercised via the **Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962** and the **Goa, Daman and Diu (Citizenship) Order, 1962**).

#### Three Statutory Modes of Loss (Sections 8, 9, and 10): Renunciation, Termination, and Deprivation

1.  **By Renunciation (Section 8 — Voluntary Act)**: Any citizen of India of full age and capacity can make a declaration in the prescribed manner renouncing their Indian citizenship. Upon registration of that declaration by the authority, the person ceases to be a citizen of India.
    *   *Wartime Exception*: If a declaration is made during any war in which India may be engaged, registration shall be withheld until the Central Government directs otherwise.
    *   *Derivative Loss and Resumption by Minor Children*: When a person renounces their Indian citizenship under **Section 8(1)**, **every minor child** of that person also ceases to be a citizen of India. However, any such child may, **within one year of attaining full age (18 years)**, make a declaration in the prescribed form that they wish to resume Indian citizenship, and shall thereupon again become a citizen of India (**Section 8(2)**).
2.  **By Termination (Section 9 — Automatic Operation of Law)**: Any citizen of India who, by naturalisation, registration, or otherwise, **voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country** at any time between January 26, 1950 and the present **shall, upon such acquisition, automatically cease to be a citizen of India**.
    *   *Wartime Exception*: Does not apply to a citizen who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country during a war in which India is engaged (preventing citizens from evading treason laws or wartime obligations by defecting to an enemy nationality).
    *   *Exclusive Central Adjudication (Section 9(2))*: Under **Section 9(2)** read with **Rule 40 and Schedule III of the Citizenship Rules, 2009**, if any question arises as to whether, when, or how any citizen of India has acquired the citizenship of another country, it shall be determined **exclusively by the Central Government** (Ministry of Home Affairs)—and in **State of U.P. v. Rehmatullah (1971)** and **Bhagwati Prasad Dixit v. Rajeev Gandhi (1986)**, the Supreme Court held that Civil Courts and High Courts lack original jurisdiction to decide whether an Indian citizen has acquired foreign citizenship until the Central Government makes a determination under **Section 9(2)**.
3.  **By Deprivation (Section 10 — Compulsory State Stripping)**: The Central Government may, by Order, compulsory deprive a citizen of their Indian citizenship.
    *   *Immunity for Natural-Born Citizens*: Crucially, **Section 10(1)** applies **ONLY** to citizens who acquired citizenship by **Naturalisation (Section 6)**, by **Registration (Section 5)**, or by **Domicile/Residence under Article 5(c)** or **Article 6(b)**. A person who is a citizen of India **by birth (Section 3)** or **by descent (Section 4)** can **NEVER** be stripped of their citizenship under **Section 10**.
    *   *Five Statutory Grounds for Deprivation (Section 10(2))*:
        1.  The registration or certificate of naturalisation was obtained by means of **fraud, false representation, or the concealment of any material fact**;
        2.  The citizen has shown themselves by act or speech to be **disloyal or disaffected towards the Constitution of India** as by law established;
        3.  The citizen has, during any war in which India may be engaged, **unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy**;
        4.  The citizen has, **within five years** after registration or naturalisation, been **sentenced in any country to imprisonment for a term of not less than two years**;
        5.  The citizen has been **ordinarily resident out of India for a continuous period of seven years** (unless they are a student abroad, in Government of India service, an employee of an international organization of which India is a member, or have registered annually at an Indian Consulate their intention to retain Indian citizenship).
    *   *Procedural Safeguard*: Before issuing a deprivation order, the Central Government must be satisfied that it is not conducive to the public good that the person should continue to be a citizen, and must refer the case to a **Committee of Inquiry** (chaired by a person who has held judicial office for at least 10 years) if the person requests a reference (**Section 10(4)–(5)**).

### 9. Asymmetric Citizenship Jurisprudence: Section 6A, the Assam Accord, NRC, and the CAA 2019

#### Section 6A and the 5-Judge Constitution Bench in In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act (October 2024)

Following the six-year **Assam Agitation (1979–1985)** led by the All Assam Students' Union (AASU) and All Assam Gana Sangram Parishad (AAGSP) against the massive electoral and demographic influx of migrants from erstwhile East Pakistan (Bangladesh), the Rajiv Gandhi Government signed the **Assam Accord on August 15, 1985**. To implement Clause 5 of the Accord, Parliament enacted the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1985**, inserting **Section 6A** into the **Citizenship Act, 1955** as a special, asymmetric statutory regime applicable exclusively to the State of **Assam**.

**Section 6A** divides persons of Indian origin who migrated to Assam from the **"specified territory"** (territories included in Bangladesh immediately before the commencement of the 1985 Amendment) into three distinct temporal cohorts:
*   **Cohort 1 (Entered Assam BEFORE January 1, 1966)**: All persons of Indian origin who came to Assam from the specified territory before **January 1, 1966**, and who have been ordinarily resident in Assam since the date of their entry, **shall be deemed to be citizens of India as from January 1, 1966** (**Section 6A(2)**).
*   **Cohort 2 (Entered Assam ON OR AFTER January 1, 1966, AND BEFORE March 25, 1971)**: Persons of Indian origin who came to Assam during this 5-year window, have been ordinarily resident in Assam since entry, and have **been detected to be a foreigner** in accordance with the **Foreigners Act, 1946** and the **Foreigners (Tribunals) Order, 1964**, must register themselves. From the date of detection, they enjoy all rights and obligations of a citizen of India **EXCEPT the right to vote**; their names are deleted from the electoral rolls for a **10-year disenfranchisement period**, upon the expiry of which they become full voting citizens (**Section 6A(3)–(5)**).
*   **Cohort 3 (Entered Assam ON OR AFTER March 25, 1971)**: The date **March 25, 1971** marks the evening the Pakistani military launched **Operation Searchlight** in East Pakistan, triggering the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War. Any person entering Assam on or after **March 25, 1971** is conferred **zero protection** under **Section 6A** and is subject to detection, detention, and deportation as an illegal migrant.

In **In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act, 1955 (October 17, 2024)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** of the Supreme Court, by a **4:1 majority** (**CJI D.Y. Chandrachud**, **Justice Surya Kant** authoring for himself, **Justices M.M. Sundresh** and **Manoj Misra**; with **Justice J.B. Pardiwala** dissenting), upheld the constitutional validity of **Section 6A**:

*   **Plenary Override of Articles 6 and 7 via Article 11**: The petitioners argued that **Articles 6 and 7** permanently fixed **July 19, 1948** as the constitutional cut-off date for migration from Pakistan, rendering **Section 6A's** **March 25, 1971** cut-off for Assam unconstitutional. Both CJI Chandrachud and Justice Surya Kant rejected this contention, holding that **Articles 5 to 8** only determined citizenship at the snapshot moment of **January 26, 1950**. The non-obstante opening of **Article 11** (*"Nothing in the foregoing provisions of this Part shall derogate from the power of Parliament..."*) empowers Parliament to legislate new cut-off dates and conditions for post-commencement citizenship acquisition.
*   **Article 14 Classification and the Doctrine of Fraternity**: Rejecting the plea that singling out Assam violated **Article 14** (under-inclusiveness compared to West Bengal, Tripura, and Meghalaya), the majority held that the percentage of migrant inflow relative to Assam's smaller local population, coupled with the necessity of ending the violent 6-year Assam Agitation via the **1985 Assam Accord**, constituted an **intelligible differentia** bearing a rational nexus to peace and humanitarian relief. CJI Chandrachud invoked preambular **Fraternity** to hold that **Section 6A** represents a balanced statutory compromise between humanitarian refuge for victims of pre-1971 Pakistani atrocities and the protection of Assamese economic and political resources.
*   **Article 29(1) Cultural Rights Scrutiny**: The petitioners contended that legalizing pre-1971 Bengali-speaking migrants violated the fundamental right of indigenous Assamese people to *"conserve their distinct language, script or culture"* under **Article 29(1)**. The majority held that **Article 29(1)** protects the right to take active steps to conserve culture; the mere presence of diverse linguistic or ethnic communities in a state does not, by itself, establish an unconstitutional erosion of **Article 29(1)**, especially given existing constitutional and statutory safeguards for Assamese language and Sixth Schedule areas.
*   **Prospective Dissent of Justice J.B. Pardiwala**: In a sharp dissent, Justice Pardiwala applied the doctrine of **unconstitutionality by efflux of time** (temporal obsolescence). He pointed out that under **Section 6A(3)**, a 1966–1971 migrant's 10-year disenfranchisement clock only starts ticking *after* the State detects them before a **Foreigners Tribunal**, and the statute provides zero mechanism for a migrant to voluntarily self-declare and register. Consequently, a migrant who evades detection continues to enjoy full voting rights on the electoral rolls indefinitely, whereas a detected migrant is disenfranchised for 10 years—creating a perverse, manifestly arbitrary statutory design that defeated the very purpose of the Assam Accord. He therefore voted to strike down **Section 6A** prospectively.
*   **Continuing Mandamus on Post-1971 Enforcement**: While upholding **Section 6A**, the 4-Judge majority issued binding administrative directions placing the ongoing identification and deportation of **post-March 25, 1971** illegal migrants in Assam under the continuing judicial monitoring of the Supreme Court.

#### Sarbananda Sonowal (2005), Foreigners Tribunals, and the National Register of Citizens (NRC)

The enforcement machinery of **Section 6A** is inextricably linked with the jurisprudence of **Foreigners Tribunals** and the **National Register of Citizens (NRC)**:

*   **Sarbananda Sonowal v. Union of India (2005) (3-Judge Bench)**: While the rest of India was governed by the **Foreigners Act, 1946** (under **Section 9** of which the **burden of proof** lies strictly upon the **accused person** to prove that they are *not* a foreigner), Parliament had enacted the **Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) Act (IMDT Act), 1983** exclusively for Assam, which reversed the burden of proof onto the State and complaint-filers, resulting in fewer than 1,500 deportations over two decades. In **Sarbananda Sonowal (2005)**, the Supreme Court **struck down the IMDT Act, 1983** as unconstitutional, holding that the massive, unchecked influx of illegal migrants into Assam constituted **"External Aggression"** within the meaning of **Article 355** (which imposes a constitutional duty on the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance), and that the IMDT Act violated **Article 14** by making deportation tougher in Assam than in the rest of India. In **Sarbananda Sonowal II (2006)**, the Court also struck down the **Foreigners (Tribunals for Assam) Order, 2006** for attempting to bypass the 2005 judgment.
*   **Foreigners Tribunals and Section 9 Burden of Proof**: Post-**Sonowal**, adjudication in Assam reverted to quasi-judicial **Foreigners Tribunals** constituted under the **Foreigners (Tribunals) Order, 1964** issued under **Section 3 of the Foreigners Act, 1946**, where the **Section 9** reverse burden of proof applies on the proceedee (subject to the state establishing initial prime facie grounds for suspicion before referring a **"D-Voter" / Doubtful Voter** or border police reference, as clarified in **Md. Rahim Ali v. State of Assam, July 2024**).
*   **National Register of Citizens (NRC) (Section 14A)**: Inserted by the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003**, **Section 14A** empowers the Central Government to compulsorily register every citizen of India, issue a national identity card, and maintain a **National Register of Indian Citizens (NRIC)**, operationalized via the **Citizenship (Registration of Citizens and Issue of National Identity Cards) Rules, 2003** (where **Rule 4A** creates a special exception for Assam based on legacy data linked to the **1951 NRC** and electoral rolls up to midnight of **March 24, 1971**, supervised by a 2-Judge Bench in **Assam Sanmilita Mahasangha v. Union of India, 2014**, culminating in the August 2019 final Assam NRC list that excluded ~19.06 lakh applicants).

#### The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 and 2024 Rules: Article 14 Classification vs. Sixth Schedule/ILP Exemptions

Enacted in December 2019 and operationalized through the notification of the **Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024** on **March 11, 2024**, the **Citizenship (Amendment) Act (CAA), 2019** introduced a targeted statutory carve-out to the definition of **"Illegal Migrant"** under **Section 2(1)(b)** and added **Section 6B** to the **Citizenship Act, 1955**:

*   **The Three-Filter Eligibility Matrix (Proviso to Section 2(1)(b) & Section 6B)**: Any person belonging to **six specified religious communities**—**Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, or Christian**—originating from **three specified neighbouring countries**—**Afghanistan, Bangladesh, or Pakistan**—who entered into India **on or before December 31, 2014**, and who has been exempted by the Central Government under the **Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920** and the **Foreigners Act, 1946**:
    1.  Shall **NOT** be treated as an **"Illegal Migrant"** under the Act;
    2.  Shall be eligible for fast-tracked **Naturalisation** under the **Proviso to Clause (d) of the Third Schedule**, reducing the mandatory aggregate residency requirement from **11 years to 5 years**;
    3.  Upon grant of citizenship under **Section 6B(2)**, such person shall be **deemed to be a citizen of India from the date of their entry into India**, and all pending legal proceedings against them in respect of illegal migration or citizenship shall stand **abated** (**Section 6B(3)**).
*   **Asymmetric Geographical Exemptions (Section 6B(4))**: To insulate the indigenous demographic and land-ownership protections of the North-East, **Section 6B(4)** explicitly stipulates that the provisions of the **CAA, 2019** **shall NOT apply** to two categories of protected territories:
    1.  **Sixth Schedule Tribal Areas**: The tribal areas of **Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, or Tripura** as included in the **Sixth Schedule** to the Constitution (comprising the 10 Autonomous District Councils under **Article 244(2)**); AND
    2.  **Inner Line Permit (ILP) Areas**: The areas covered under **"The Inner Line"** notified under the **Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation (BEFR), 1873** (which fully encompasses the states of **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Manipur**—with Manipur brought under the ILP regime via Presidential Order in December 2019).
*   **The Pending Constitutional Challenge under Article 14 and Secularism (Indian Union Muslim League v. Union of India)**: Over 200 writ petitions pending before the Supreme Court challenge the **CAA, 2019** across two competing doctrinal axes:
    *   *The Petitioners' Contention (Article 14 Manifest Arbitrariness & Secularism)*: Argues that introducing a religious filter into Indian citizenship law for the first time violates the **Basic Structure** feature of **Secularism** (**S.R. Bommai, 1994**) and fails both the **Article 14 Reasonable Classification test** and the **Manifest Arbitrariness test** (**Shayara Bano, 2017**). The classification is alleged to be **under-inclusive** across three dimensions: **(a)** it excludes persecuted Muslim sects within those three countries (such as **Ahmadiyyas** and **Hazaras/Shias** in Pakistan and Afghanistan) as well as atheists/rationalists; **(b)** it excludes neighbouring states without state religions where religious or linguistic persecution occurred (such as **Sri Lankan Tamils** and **Rohingyas** from Myanmar, or Tibetan Buddhists from China); and **(c)** the **2019 Act** textually requires zero individualized proof of actual religious persecution, relying on a blanket statutory presumption.
    *   *The Union of India's Defense (Constitutional Recognition of Partition & Theocratic States)*: Contends that **Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Bangladesh** constitute a distinct, well-defined constitutional class because their respective constitutions explicitly establish **Islam as the State Religion**, resulting in systemic, structural vulnerability for non-Muslim minorities since the 1947 Partition and the failure of the **Nehru-Liaquat Pact (1950)**. The Union relies on **Clarence Pais v. Union of India (2001)** and **State of Madhya Pradesh v. Amit Shrivas (2020)** to argue that under **Article 14**, the legislature is not obligated to formulate an all-embracing global refugee law curing every evil in the world at once; addressing the historical unfinished responsibility of Partition in theocratic neighbouring states constitutes an **intelligible differentia** with a direct nexus to the legislative object, while still leaving the standard **11-year naturalisation window under Section 6** open to all foreign nationals (including Muslims from those three countries).

### 10. Overseas Citizens of India (OCI), Refugees, Statelessness, and the Bridge to Fundamental Rights

#### Statutory Architecture of OCI (Sections 7A–7D): Rights, Restrictions, and Cancellation Mechanics

While **Article 9** of the Constitution and **Section 9** of the **Citizenship Act, 1955** strictly prohibit true **Dual Citizenship** (where an individual holds two sovereign passports, voting rights, and political allegiances simultaneously), the high-level **L.M. Singhvi Committee on the Indian Diaspora (2000–2002)** recommended creating a statutory framework to reconnect the global Indian diaspora with India's economic and cultural life.

1.  **Evolution from PIO to OCI (2003, 2005, and 2015 Amendments)**:
    *   The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003** initially inserted **Sections 7A to 7D** granting **Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)** to Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) in **16 specified Western countries** (excluding Pakistan and Bangladesh).
    *   The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2005** expanded the **OCI** scheme to **all countries globally** whose laws permit dual nationality in some form, **except Pakistan and Bangladesh**.
    *   The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2015** formally abolished the separate executive **Person of Indian Origin (PIO) Card** scheme and merged it into a unified **"Overseas Citizen of India Cardholder" (OCI Cardholder)** regime (**Section 7A**), while extending eligibility to great-grandchildren (up to the fourth generation) and foreign-origin spouses of Indian citizens or OCI cardholders whose marriage has subsisted for at least **two continuous years** prior to the application (subject to automatic cancellation upon divorce under the **Proviso to Section 7A(1)(d)**).
2.  **Absolute Bar on Pakistani and Bangladeshi Lineage (Proviso to Section 7A(1))**: No person shall be eligible for registration as an **OCI Cardholder** if **they, or either of their parents, or any of their grandparents, or any of their great-grandparents** is or had ever been a citizen of **Pakistan or Bangladesh**.
3.  **Conferred Rights vs. Explicit Constitutional Exclusions (Section 7B)**:
    *   *Affirmative Benefits*: Under **Section 7B(1)** and notifications issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs (including the consolidated **March 4, 2021 MHA Notification**), an **OCI Cardholder** enjoys a **multiple-entry, multi-purpose lifelong visa** to visit India, **exemption from registration** with the Foreigners Regional Registration Officer (FRRO) for any length of stay, and general parity with **Non-Resident Indians (NRIs)** in financial, economic, and educational fields (though per the **March 2021 Notification**, narrowed by the Supreme Court prospectively in **Anushka Rengunthwar v. Union of India, 2023**, OCI cardholders are eligible only for NRI quota seats in NEET/JEE/all-India entrance exams unless born before the 2021 notification, and require special permission from the FRRO/Mission for missionary, mountaineering, **journalistic**, or **research** activities, or visiting Protected/Restricted Areas).
    *   *Nine Explicit Constitutional Bars (Section 7B(2))*: An **OCI Cardholder** is **NOT** a citizen of India and is statutorily barred from:
        1.  Equality of opportunity in matters of public employment (**Article 16**);
        2.  Election as **President** (**Article 58**) or **Vice-President** (**Article 66**);
        3.  Appointment as a **Judge of the Supreme Court** (**Article 124**) or of a **High Court** (**Article 217**);
        4.  Registration as a **voter** in electoral rolls (**Section 16 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950**);
        5.  Qualification for membership of the **Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha** (**Sections 3–4 of the RPA, 1951**) or of a **State Legislative Assembly or Council** (**Sections 5–5A of the RPA, 1951**);
        6.  Appointment to Public Services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or of any State (except by special Central Government appointment);
        7.  Acquisition of **agricultural or plantation properties** in India (unlike NRIs, who are full Indian citizens holding Indian passports residing abroad for $>182$ days and can purchase agricultural land).
4.  **Cancellation of OCI Registration (Section 7D)**: The Central Government may, by Order, cancel an OCI registration after providing a **reasonable opportunity of being heard** across six grounds:
    *   **(a)** Obtained by fraud, false representation, or concealment of material fact;
    *   **(b)** Shown disaffection towards the Constitution of India;
    *   **(c)** Unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy during a war;
    *   **(d)** Sentenced to imprisonment for **not less than two years** within **five years** of OCI registration;
    *   **(da)** *(Inserted by the **Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019**)*: The OCI cardholder has **violated any of the provisions of the Citizenship Act, 1955 or any other law in force as may be specified by the Central Government** by notification;
    *   **(e)** It is necessary to do so in the **interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations of India with any foreign country**, or in the interests of the general public;
    *   **(f)** The marriage of a foreign-origin spouse OCI cardholder has been dissolved by a competent court or they have solemnized marriage with another person while the first marriage subsists.

#### Constitutional Status of Refugees and Stateless Persons: Non-Refoulement and Article 21 Protections

India is **neither a signatory** to the **1951 United Nations Refugee Convention** nor to its **1967 Protocol**, nor has it ratified the **1954 or 1961 UN Conventions on Statelessness**, and it lacks a dedicated domestic refugee statute. Statutorily, all non-citizens entering without valid travel documents—whether economic migrants, asylum seekers, or stateless persons—are governed indistinctly by the **Foreigners Act, 1946**, the **Registration of Foreigners Act, 1939**, and the **Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920** (which are currently slated for consolidation and modernization under the **Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025** regime).

However, the constitutional distinction between **"Citizens"** and **"Persons"** within **Part III of the Constitution** creates a vital judicial shield for refugees and stateless persons:

*   **The Citizen-Exclusive vs. Universal Rights Dichotomy**:
    *   **Five Fundamental Rights Exclusive to Citizens ONLY**: **Article 15** (Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth), **Article 16** (Equality of opportunity in public employment), **Article 19** (The six democratic freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession), **Article 29**, and **Article 30** (Cultural and educational rights of minorities).
    *   **Universal Fundamental Rights Available to ALL Persons (Citizens, Aliens, Refugees, and Stateless Persons)**: **Article 14** (Equality before the law and equal protection of the laws), **Article 20** (Protection in respect of conviction for offences), **Article 21** (Protection of life and personal liberty), **Article 21A** (Right to elementary education), **Article 22** (Protection against arbitrary arrest and detention, *except for enemy aliens under Article 22(3)(a)*), **Articles 23–24** (Right against exploitation), and **Articles 25–28** (Freedom of religion).
*   **Jurisprudence on Article 21, Non-Refoulement, and Deportation**:
    *   **National Human Rights Commission v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996)** (*Chakma Refugees Case*): The Supreme Court held that the State is bound under **Article 21** to protect the life and personal liberty of every human being within its borders, whether a citizen or a non-citizen, restraining the forcible eviction of **Chakma refugees** (who had fled the Chittagong Hill Tracts in 1964) while their citizenship applications under **Section 5** were pending.
    *   **Louis De Raedt v. Union of India (1991)**: The Supreme Court clarified the precise limit of a foreigner's **Article 21** right: while a foreigner in India enjoys the full protection of **Article 21** for their life and personal liberty, a foreigner's fundamental right is **confined to Article 21** and does **NOT include the right to reside and settle in India**, which is guaranteed under **Article 19(1)(e)** exclusively to **citizens**. Consequently, the Union Executive retains the unrestricted sovereign power under **Section 3(2)(c) of the Foreigners Act, 1946** to expel and deport foreigners.
    *   **Mohammad Salimullah v. Union of India (2021)** (*Rohingya Deportation Case*): When petitioners invoked the customary international law principle of **Non-Refoulement** (which prohibits a state from returning refugees to a country where they face persecution or torture) under **Article 21** read with **Article 51(c)** to halt the deportation of detained Rohingya migrants to Myanmar, a 3-Judge Bench headed by CJI S.A. Bobde held that while the right against deportation is **ancillary to the right to reside or settle under Article 19(1)(e)** (available only to citizens) and India is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention, any deportation by the Union must strictly follow **"procedure established by law"** under **Article 21** (meaning diplomatic nationality verification with the receiving state and due process under the Foreigners Act).

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 3: From Civic Membership to Enforceable Rights against "The State"

Having established the normative teleology of the Republic in the **Preamble**, demarcated the sovereign and federal physical boundaries of the **"Territory of India"** under **Part I (Articles 1–4)**, and codified the constitutional and statutory rules that differentiate **"Citizens"** from **"Persons"** under **Part II (Articles 5–11)** and the **Citizenship Act, 1955**, the constitutional architecture transitions directly from *who constitutes the polity* to *how the coercive power of the sovereign is bounded by Fundamental Rights*. This distinction between **Citizens** (who alone command the equality guarantees of **Articles 15 and 16**, the democratic freedoms of **Article 19**, and the cultural protections of **Articles 29 and 30**) and **All Persons** (who command the universal rule-of-law shield of **Article 14** and life/liberty under **Article 21**) forms the immediate jurisdictional threshold of **Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12–35)**. In **Part 3**, we examine who bears the constitutional obligation to respect these rights by deconstructing the definition of **"The State" under Article 12** (and horizontal enforceability under **Kaushal Kishor, 2023**), the mechanics of **Judicial Review under Article 13**, the jurisprudential evolution of **Article 14** from formal classification to manifest arbitrariness, and the substantive affirmative action battles across **Articles 15 to 18** culminating in **Janhit Abhiyan (2022)** and **State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024)**.
