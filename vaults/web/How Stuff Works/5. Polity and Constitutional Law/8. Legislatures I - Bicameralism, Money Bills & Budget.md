# Part 8: The Union and State Legislatures I: Bicameralism, Legislative Procedures, Money Bills, and Budgetary Architecture

## I. The Constitutional Architecture of Bicameralism and Representational Asymmetry (Articles 79–84, 168–173, 330–334A)

### 1. Federal Bicameralism and the Structural Tension Between Popular Mandate and Federal Revision

The institutional design of the Union Legislature under **Part V, Chapter II (Articles 79–122)** and the State Legislatures under **Part VI, Chapter III (Articles 168–212)** embodies a deliberate constitutional equilibrium between two competing structural forces: the majoritarian democratic impulse of a directly elected popular chamber and the deliberative, federal, and revisionary counter-weight of a second chamber. Under **Article 79**, the Parliament of the Union consists of the **President** and two Houses known respectively as the **Council of States (Rajya Sabha)** and the **House of the People (Lok Sabha)**. The inclusion of the **President** as an integral constituent of Parliament—despite not sitting or voting in either chamber—mirrors the Westminster doctrine of the "King-in-Parliament," anchoring the fusion of executive and legislative powers whereby executive assent (**Article 111**), summoning and prorogation (**Article 85**), and ordinance promulgation (**Article 123**) remain structurally inseparable from the legislative process.

In designing the **Rajya Sabha** under **Article 80**, the Constituent Assembly (guided by **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar's** Union Constitution Committee calculations) rejected the classical American federal model of symmetrical territorial representation—where the US Senate grants two seats to every state regardless of demographic size—in favor of **asymmetric weighted representation** calibrated to population under the **Fourth Schedule**. Because the Indian Union was not forged through a compact among pre-existing sovereign states (*"an indestructible Union of destructible states"* under **Article 1**), the **Fourth Schedule** allocates Rajya Sabha seats ranging from **31 for Uttar Pradesh** down to **1 each for Nagaland, Mizoram, Sikkim, Manipur, Meghalaya, Tripura, Goa, Arunachal Pradesh, and Puducherry**. While this demographic weighting prevents tiny federal units from vetoing the national majority, it dilutes the capacity of peripheral states to use the Upper House as a shield against heartland majoritarianism.

#### Structural Mechanics, Judicial Doctrine, and Exclusive Powers of the Rajya Sabha
*   **Composition and Indirect Electoral Mechanics (Article 80):** Under **Article 80(1)**, the maximum strength of the Rajya Sabha is fixed at **250 members**, comprising not more than **238 representatives of the States and Union Territories** and **12 members nominated by the President** under **Article 80(3)** for special knowledge or practical experience in **literature, science, art, and social service** (note the absence of the "cooperative movement," which exists only for State Legislative Councils under **Article 171(5)**). Under **Article 80(4)**, State representatives are elected by the **elected members of the Legislative Assembly of the State** in accordance with the system of **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (PR-STV)**. Among Union Territories, only those with legislative assemblies—currently **NCT of Delhi, Puducherry, and Jammu & Kashmir**—have representation in the Fourth Schedule under **Article 80(5)**.
*   **Continuous Chamber Status (Article 83(1)):** Unlike the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha is a permanent, continuing institution not subject to dissolution. Under **Article 83(1)**, nearly **one-third of its members retire on the expiration of every second year**, and the **Representation of the People Act, 1951 (Section 154)** fixes the statutory term of an individual Rajya Sabha MP at **six years**.
*   **The Domicile and Open-Ballot Transformation (*Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India*, 2006):** In 2003, Parliament amended **Section 3** and **Sections 59, 94, and 128 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951**, abolishing the requirement that a Rajya Sabha candidate be an "elector" (ordinarily resident/domiciled) in the specific State from which they seek election, and replacing the secret ballot in Rajya Sabha elections with an **open ballot** system (requiring party MLAs to show their marked ballot to their authorized party agent to curb cross-voting and money power). In **Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2006)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously upheld both amendments:
    1.  **Non-Domicile Validity:** Writing for the Bench, **Chief Justice Y.K. Sabharwal** held that **Article 84** exhaustively prescribes constitutional qualifications for membership of Parliament (citizenship, oath under the Third Schedule, minimum age of **30 years for Rajya Sabha** and **25 years for Lok Sabha**, and statutory qualifications prescribed by Parliament). Because residency within a State is neither mentioned in **Article 80(4)** nor entrenched as a prerequisite of federal representation, removing the domicile condition does not violate the **Basic Structure** of federalism; the federal character of the Rajya Sabha flows from *who elects* the representative (the State Legislative Assembly), not where the candidate resides.
    2.  **Open Ballot Validity:** The Court ruled that the principle of secret ballot under the **Representation of the People Act, 1951** is vital for direct elections based on universal adult suffrage (**Article 326**) to shield individual voters from coercion, but in an **indirect election** under **Article 80(4)** where MLAs act as party delegates, open voting legitimately prevents political corruption and aligns with the **Tenth Schedule** anti-defection framework without infringing free speech under **Article 19(1)(a)**.
*   **Exclusive Federal and Institutional Prerogatives of the Rajya Sabha:** Despite being subordinate to the Lok Sabha in money matters (**Article 109**) and executive confidence (**Article 75(3)**), the Rajya Sabha wields four exclusive constitutional powers where the Lok Sabha cannot act unilaterally:
    1.  **Temporary Transfer of State List Competence in the National Interest (Article 249):** If the Rajya Sabha declares by a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest that Parliament should make laws with respect to any matter enumerated in the **State List (List II)** (or impose a Goods and Services Tax under **Article 246A**), Parliament becomes competent to legislate on that subject for the whole or any part of India. Such a resolution remains in force for **up to one year** at a time (renewable indefinitely by one-year increments), and laws enacted thereunder cease to have effect **six months** after the resolution expires.
    2.  **Creation of New All-India Services (Article 312):** If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** declaring it necessary or expedient in the national interest, Parliament may by law create one or more **All-India Services** (including an **All-India Judicial Service** under **Article 312(3)**) common to the Union and the States, regulating their recruitment and conditions of service.
    3.  **Initiation of Vice-Presidential Removal (Article 67(b)):** A resolution for the removal of the **Vice-President of India** (who serves as the *ex-officio* Chairman of the Rajya Sabha under **Article 64**) can originate *exclusively* in the Rajya Sabha, passed by a **majority of all the then members of the Council (Effective Majority)** and subsequently agreed to by the Lok Sabha by a simple majority.
    4.  **Emergency Custodianship During Lok Sabha Dissolution (Articles 352(4), 356(3), and 360(2)):** If a Proclamation of National Emergency, President's Rule, or Financial Emergency is issued at a time when the Lok Sabha is dissolved—or if the dissolution of the Lok Sabha takes place during the statutory one-month or two-month window without the Lok Sabha having approved the Proclamation—the Proclamation survives only if approved by the **Rajya Sabha alone** within the stipulated constitutional timeframe, remaining valid until **30 days** from the first sitting of the newly reconstituted Lok Sabha.

### 2. Popular Chamber Composition, Delimitation Freezes, and Affirmative Representation (Articles 81–82, 104th and 106th Amendments)

The **Lok Sabha** represents the popular sovereignty of the Indian electorate as a whole. Under **Article 81(1)**, the House is composed of not more than **530 members** chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies in the States, and not more than **20 members** to represent the Union Territories. To ensure the formal equality of the franchise (*"one person, one vote, one value"*), **Article 81(2)** lays down two demographic mandates: first, under **Article 81(2)(a)**, there shall be allotted to each State a number of seats in the Lok Sabha in such manner that the ratio between that number and the population of the State is, so far as practicable, the same for all States (an inter-state proportionality rule exempted under the Proviso to **Article 81(2)** for States having a population not exceeding **six million**); and second, under **Article 81(2)(b)**, each State shall be divided into territorial constituencies such that the ratio between the population of each constituency and the number of seats allotted to it is, so far as practicable, the same throughout the State.

However, the constitutional mandate of **Article 82**—which originally required the readjustment (delimitation) of both inter-state seat allocations and intra-state constituency boundaries upon the completion of *each decennial Census*—triggered a profound federal conflict between demographic equity and developmental incentives. Because Southern and Western States successfully implemented family planning and fertility-reduction programs while Northern heartland States experienced rapid population growth, strict enforcement of **Article 81(2)(a)** and **Article 82** after the 1971 Census threatened to penalize Southern States by shrinking their share of seats in the Lok Sabha.

#### The Delimitation Freezes and the 104th & 106th Constitutional Amendments
*   **The Inter-State vs. Intra-State Delimitation Split (42nd, 84th, and 87th Amendments):**
    1.  **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976:** Froze the total number of Lok Sabha seats allocated to each State and the division of States into territorial constituencies at the **1971 Census** level until the year 2000.
    2.  **84th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001:** Amended the Provisos to **Articles 81, 82, 170, 330, and 332** to extend the freeze on the *total number of seats* allocated to each State in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies (based on the **1971 Census**) until the **first Census taken after the year 2026** has been published. Concurrently, it permitted the rationalization and readjustment of *intra-state* territorial constituencies (without altering any State's total seat count) and the refixing of SC/ST reserved seats based on the **1991 Census**.
    3.  **87th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003:** Further amended **Articles 81, 82, 170, and 330** to substitute the **2001 Census** for the 1991 Census as the demographic basis for *intra-state* constituency boundary readjustment and SC/ST seat reservation (implemented by the **4th Delimitation Commission** chaired by **Justice Kuldip Singh** under the **Delimitation Act, 2002**), while preserving the **1971 Census** freeze on the *inter-state* allocation of total seats until post-2026.
*   **The 104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019 (Abolition of Anglo-Indian Nomination):** Under **Part XVI**, **Article 330** (Lok Sabha) and **Article 332** (State Legislative Assemblies) mandate the reservation of seats for **Scheduled Castes (SCs)** and **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** in proportion to their population, while **Article 331** empowered the President to nominate up to **2 members of the Anglo-Indian community** to the Lok Sabha and **Article 333** empowered the Governor to nominate **1 Anglo-Indian member** to the State Legislative Assembly if the community was not adequately represented. Originally subject to a 10-year sunset under **Article 334**, both reservations were extended every decade until the **104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019** amended **Article 334** to extend the political reservation for **SCs and STs** by another ten years (up to **80 years** from the commencement of the Constitution, i.e., **January 25, 2030**), while deliberately allowing the nomination of **Anglo-Indians** under **Articles 331 and 333** to expire on January 25, 2020, reducing the maximum effective strength of the Lok Sabha from 552 to **550**.
*   **The 106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023 (*Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*):** Enacted to rectify the chronic structural under-representation of women in Indian legislatures, the **106th Amendment** inserts three new substantive articles (**Articles 330A, 332A, and 334A**) and amends **Article 239AA**:
    1.  **Horizontal and Vertical Quota Intersection (Articles 330A, 332A, and 239AA(2)(b)):** **Article 330A** (Lok Sabha), **Article 332A** (State Legislative Assemblies), and amended **Article 239AA(2)(b)** (Legislative Assembly of the NCT of Delhi) mandate that, as nearly as may be, **one-third (33%) of the total number of seats** filled by direct election in these chambers shall be reserved for women. Crucially, this operates as a **horizontal interlocking quota**: under **Articles 330A(2) and 332A(2)**, **one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** under **Articles 330 and 332** must be reserved for SC and ST women respectively, and under **Articles 330A(3) and 332A(3)**, one-third of the total seats (including the seats reserved for SC/ST women) shall be reserved for women. The Act provides **zero separate sub-quota for Other Backward Classes (OBC) women** and does **not** apply to the **Rajya Sabha** or **State Legislative Councils** (where elections are indirect via PR-STV without territorial constituencies).
    2.  **Contingent Commencement, Rotation, and Sunset (Article 334A):** Under **Article 334A(1)**, the reservation of seats for women shall *not* take effect immediately; it shall come into effect only **after an exercise of delimitation is undertaken** for this purpose after the relevant figures for the **first Census taken after the commencement of the 106th Amendment Act** have been published. Under **Article 334A(2)**, the reservation shall cease to have effect on the expiration of a period of **15 years** from such commencement (unless extended by Parliamentary law under **Article 334A(4)**), and under **Article 334A(3)**, seats reserved for women shall be **rotated** after each subsequent delimitation exercise as determined by Parliament by law.

### 3. State Bicameralism and the Subordinate Status of Vidhan Parishads (Articles 168–171, 197)

At the sub-national level, the Constitution rejects mandatory bicameralism, leaving the existence of a second chamber—the **State Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad)**—entirely contingent upon political convenience and parliamentary discretion. Under **Article 168(1)**, six States currently maintain a bicameral legislature (**Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh**), while the remaining 22 States operate unicamerally through a **State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)** alone.

#### Creation, Corporatist Composition, and Legislative Subordination of the Vidhan Parishad
*   **Asymmetric Creation and Abolition Mechanics (Article 169):** Under **Article 169(1)**, Parliament may by law provide for the **abolition** of the Legislative Council of a State having such a Council, or for the **creation** of such a Council in a State having no such Council, if the **Legislative Assembly of the State passes a resolution** to that effect by a **Special Majority**—namely, a majority of the **total membership of the Assembly** AND a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members of the Assembly present and voting**. This two-stage mechanism exhibits stark federal asymmetry: while Parliament *cannot* initiate the creation or abolition of a Vidhan Parishad without a prior special-majority resolution from the State Assembly, the passage of such a resolution by a State Assembly (as seen in pending resolutions from Assam, Odisha, Rajasthan, and West Bengal) binds Parliament to nothing—Parliament may delay or refuse to enact the law indefinitely. Furthermore, under **Article 169(3)**, although the parliamentary statute amends **Article 168(1)(a)**, it is enacted by a **simple majority** in the ordinary legislative process and is expressly declared **not to be deemed an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368**.
*   **Functional and Corporatist Composition (Article 171):** Under **Article 171(1)–(2)**, the total strength of a Vidhan Parishad cannot exceed **one-third of the total membership of the State Legislative Assembly** and cannot be less than **40 members**, subject to Parliament altering the composition formula by law under **Article 171(2)**. Until Parliament provides otherwise, **Article 171(3)** distributes seats across five distinct functional and electoral streams (the **5/6th indirectly elected via PR-STV + 1/6th nominated** matrix):
    1.  **One-third (1/3)** elected by electorates consisting of members of **municipalities, district boards, and other local authorities** in the State;
    2.  **One-twelfth (1/12)** elected by **graduates of three years' standing** residing in the State;
    3.  **One-twelfth (1/12)** elected by **teachers of three years' standing** in educational institutions within the State not lower in standard than **secondary schools**;
    4.  **One-third (1/3)** elected by the members of the **Legislative Assembly of the State** from amongst persons who are not members of the Assembly;
    5.  **One-sixth (1/6)** **nominated by the Governor** under **Article 171(5)** from amongst persons having special knowledge or practical experience in **literature, science, art, co-operative movement, and social service**.
*   **Absolute Legislative Subordination (Article 197):** Whereas the Rajya Sabha enjoys co-equal legislative authority with the Lok Sabha on all Ordinary Bills (resolvable only via an **Article 108** Joint Sitting), the Vidhan Parishad is reduced under **Article 197** to a purely dilatory revisionary body with **zero veto power** and **zero joint sitting provision**:
    1.  **First Transmission (Maximum 3 Months):** When an Ordinary Bill passed by the Vidhan Sabha is transmitted to the Vidhan Parishad, and the Council (a) rejects the Bill, (b) passes it with amendments to which the Assembly does not agree, or (c) allows **more than three months** to elapse from the date on which the Bill is laid before the Council without passing it, the Vidhan Sabha may pass the Bill *again* in the same or in any subsequent session (with or without the Council's amendments) and transmit it to the Council a second time (**Article 197(1)**).
    2.  **Second Transmission (Maximum 1 Month):** Upon this second transmission, if the Council rejects the Bill, makes unacceptable amendments, or allows **more than one month** to elapse without passing it, the Bill is **deemed to have been passed by both Houses** in the form in which it was passed by the Legislative Assembly for the second time (**Article 197(2)**). Thus, the absolute maximum delaying capacity of a Vidhan Parishad on an Ordinary Bill is **four months** (3 months + 1 month). Conversely, if a Bill originates in the Vidhan Parishad and is rejected by the Vidhan Sabha, the Bill ends immediately and is **dead**. On Money Bills (**Article 198**), the Council's power is identical to the Rajya Sabha's: a **14-day** window for non-binding recommendations.

## II. Parliamentary Sessions, Legislative Lifecycles, Lapsing Mechanics, and Joint Sittings (Articles 85–88, 107–108, 174–177, 196–197)

### 4. Summoning, Adjournment, Prorogation, and Dissolution Dynamics (Articles 85 and 174)

The temporal operation of the legislature is governed by a precise constitutional division of authority between the **Executive** (acting through the President under **Article 85** or the Governor under **Article 174**, bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers under **Articles 74 and 163**) and the **Presiding Officer** of the House (the Speaker/Chairman acting under the Rules of Procedure framed pursuant to **Articles 118 and 208**). Understanding this boundary is foundational to preventing executive manipulation of parliamentary accountability.

#### The Four Constitutional Terminations of Parliamentary Sittings
*   **Summoning and the Six-Month Mandate (Article 85(1) / Article 174(1)):** The President (or Governor) from time to time summons each House of Parliament to meet at such time and place as they think fit, subject to the mandatory constitutional ceiling that **six months shall not intervene between its last sitting in one session and the date appointed for its first sitting in the next session**. While convention establishes three annual sessions—the **Budget Session** (February–May), the **Monsoon Session** (July–August), and the **Winter Session** (November–December)—the Constitution strictly enforces only the maximum six-month inter-session gap. As settled by the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh Legislative Assembly (2016)**, the Governor possesses **zero discretionary power** under **Article 174** to summon, advance, or prorogue the House unless the Chief Minister has prima facie lost the confidence of the House and is evading a floor test.
*   **Adjournment and Adjournment *Sine Die* (Presiding Officer's Domain):** A **session** is the period spanning from the first sitting of a House upon summoning until its **prorogation** (or dissolution in the case of the Lok Sabha). Within a session, the House meets in daily **sittings** (typically divided into a morning sitting and a post-lunch sitting). An **Adjournment** suspends the work in a sitting for a specified time (hours, days, or weeks) and is ordered exclusively by the **Presiding Officer** (Speaker or Chairman). An **Adjournment *sine die*** ("without setting a day") terminates a sitting indefinitely without naming a day for reassembly. Crucially, even after the Presiding Officer adjourns the House *sine die*, he retains the power to call a sitting of the House *before* the date or time to which it has been adjourned, or at any time after an adjournment *sine die*, until the President formally issues a notification of **Prorogation**. Neither adjournment nor adjournment *sine die* affects pending Bills, motions, resolutions, or notices before the House.
*   **Prorogation (Article 85(2)(a) / Article 174(2)(a)):** Issued by the **President** (or Governor) on Cabinet advice—ordinarily within a few days after the Presiding Officer adjourns the House *sine die*, though constitutionally the President can prorogue the House even while it is in session—**Prorogation** terminates not merely a sitting, but the **entire session** of the House. In departure from the historical British Westminster rule (where prorogation quashed all pending parliamentary business), **Article 107(3)** explicitly insulates legislation from executive prorogation: **a Bill pending in Parliament shall NOT lapse by reason of the prorogation of the Houses**. However, all pending **notices** (other than notices of intention to move for leave to introduce a Bill) lapse on prorogation and fresh notices must be given for the next session, whereas pending Bills, motions, resolutions, and business pending before **Parliamentary Committees** survive intact.
*   **Dissolution (Article 85(2)(b) / Article 174(2)(b)):** Applicable exclusively to the **Lok Sabha** (and **Vidhan Sabha**), **Dissolution** terminates the very life of the existing House, necessitating a general election for the constitution of a new House. Dissolution occurs in two ways: (1) **Automatic Dissolution** on the expiry of its **five-year tenure** under **Article 83(2)** (or expiration of one-year extensions granted during a National Emergency under the Proviso to **Article 83(2)**); or (2) **Presidential Dissolution** under **Article 85(2)(b)** prior to the completion of five years, which is irrevocable once ordered.

### 5. The Constitutional Calculus of Lapsing of Bills (Articles 107 and 196)

When the **Lok Sabha** is dissolved, it extinguishes the democratic mandate of the representatives who initiated or approved pending legislative measures. Consequently, **Article 107(4) and (5)** (and **Article 196(4) and (5)** in the States) construct a rigorous constitutional filter: **any Bill that has been touched by—or remains in the custody of—the dissolved Lok Sabha lapses, whereas any Bill that is wholly independent of the dissolved Lok Sabha (or has already completed its journey through *both* Houses) survives**.

#### The Six-Scenario Constitutional Lapsing Matrix and *Purushothaman Nambudiri*
*   **Textual Rules of Article 107(4) and (5):**
    1.  **Rule of Survival in the Upper Chamber (Article 107(4)):** A Bill pending in the **Rajya Sabha** which has **not been passed by the Lok Sabha** shall **NOT lapse** on a dissolution of the Lok Sabha. Because the dissolved Lok Sabha never voted on or received this Bill, the continuous nature of the Rajya Sabha preserves it intact for transmission to the newly elected Lok Sabha.
    2.  **Rule of Extinguishment via Lower Chamber Custody or Origin (Article 107(5)):** A Bill which is **pending in the Lok Sabha**, OR which **having been passed by the Lok Sabha is pending in the Rajya Sabha**, shall—subject to the provisions of **Article 108**—**LAPSE** on a dissolution of the Lok Sabha. Even if the Bill is sitting in the permanent Rajya Sabha at the moment of dissolution, because its legal existence derived from the mandate of the now-dissolved Lok Sabha, its democratic foundation vanishes and it dies.
*   **Post-Passage Survival and Presidential Custody (*Purushothaman Nambudiri v. State of Kerala*, 1962):** What happens when a Bill has been passed by *both* Houses (or by a State Legislature) and is awaiting the **Assent of the President (Article 111)** or the **Governor/President (Articles 200–201)** when the Lower House is dissolved—or when the President returns the Bill under the Proviso to **Article 111** for reconsideration? In **Purushothaman Nambudiri v. State of Kerala (1962)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** interpreted **Articles 196 and 200–201** (pari materia with **Articles 107 and 111**) and ruled that because **clauses (4) and (5) of Article 107/196** exhaustively specify every contingency in which a Bill lapses upon dissolution, no implied rule of lapsing can be read into **Articles 111 or 200–201**. Consequently:
    *   A Bill passed by both Houses and **pending for the assent of the President** (or reserved by a Governor for the consideration of the President under **Article 200**) **does NOT lapse** on the dissolution of the Lok Sabha (or State Assembly).
    *   Furthermore, if the President (or Governor) returns such a Bill for reconsideration with recommended amendments, the **newly constituted Lok Sabha (or State Assembly)** is fully competent to reconsider and re-pass the Bill under **Article 111** (or **Articles 200–201**).

### 6. Ordinary Legislative Procedure and the Deadlock-Breaking Joint Sitting (Article 108)

Every **Ordinary Bill** (any Bill other than a Money Bill under **Article 110**, a Financial Bill under **Article 117**, or a Constitutional Amendment Bill under **Article 368**) may originate in either House of Parliament under **Article 107(1)**, introduced either by a Minister (**Government Bill**, requiring 7 days' notice) or by any other Member (**Private Member's Bill**, requiring 1 month's notice). To become an Act, it must traverse **Three Readings** in both Houses:
1.  **First Reading:** Motion for leave to introduce the Bill; upon leave being granted, the Bill is introduced by reading its title and objectives (no debate on merits occurs unless the Bill is opposed at the introduction stage on grounds of **legislative competence**), followed by **publication in the Gazette of India** (if published in the Gazette prior to introduction with the Presiding Officer's permission, no motion for leave is needed).
2.  **Second Reading (The Substantive Crucible):** Comprises two sub-stages:
    *   *Stage of General Discussion:* The House may (i) take the Bill into consideration immediately or on a fixed date, (ii) refer it to a **Select Committee** of the House, (iii) refer it to a **Joint Committee** of both Houses with the other House's concurrence, or (iv) circulate it to elicit public opinion (in practice, Presiding Officers also refer bills to the **24 Department-Related Standing Committees** after introduction).
    *   *Committee & Consideration Stage:* After the committee reports back, the House conducts a **clause-by-clause consideration** where individual clauses, schedules, and amendments are debated and voted upon separately, becoming part of the Bill if adopted by a simple majority.
3.  **Third Reading:** Confined strictly to acceptance or rejection of the Bill as a whole; **no substantive amendments** are permitted (only formal, verbal, or consequential amendments). Once authenticated by the Presiding Officer, the Bill is transmitted to the second House, which has four options: (a) pass the Bill as sent, (b) pass it with amendments and return it to the originating House, (c) reject the Bill altogether, or (d) take no action and keep it pending.

#### Constitutional Mechanics and Scope of the Joint Sitting (Article 108)
*   **The Three Constitutional Triggers (Article 108(1)):** Borrowed from **Section 57 of the Australian Constitution**, **Article 108(1)** empowers the **President** (acting on Cabinet advice) to notify to the Houses by message (if they are sitting) or by public notification (if they are not sitting) his intention to summon them to meet in a **Joint Sitting** if, after a Bill has been passed by one House and transmitted to the other House:
    1.  The Bill is **rejected** by the other House; OR
    2.  The Houses have **finally disagreed** as to the amendments to be made in the Bill; OR
    3.  **More than six months** elapse from the date of the reception of the Bill by the other House without the Bill being passed by it. Under **Article 108(2)**, in reckoning this six-month period, **no account shall be taken of any period during which the House is prorogued or adjourned for more than four consecutive days**.
*   **Strict Constitutional Exclusions:** Under the express text of **Article 108(1)**, the Joint Sitting mechanism applies **only to Ordinary Bills and Financial Bills (Category I & II under Article 117)**. It is **strictly inapplicable** to:
    1.  **Money Bills (Article 109):** Where the Lok Sabha enjoys overriding authority after 14 days; and
    2.  **Constitutional Amendment Bills (Article 368):** Which must be passed by *each House separately* by the special majority prescribed under **Article 368(2)** to safeguard the federal compact against Lower House numerical majoritarianism.
*   **Presiding Hierarchy, Rules, and Amendment Restrictions (Articles 108(4) and 118(3)–(4)):**
    *   **Presiding Officer (Article 118(4)):** The **Speaker of the Lok Sabha** presides over a Joint Sitting. In the Speaker's absence, the **Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha** presides; if he is also absent, the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** presides; and if he is also absent, such other person as may be determined by the members present at the sitting presides. The **Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Vice-President of India) can NEVER preside** over a Joint Sitting because he is not a member of either House of Parliament.
    *   **Governing Rules and Numerical Dominance:** Under rules framed by the President in consultation with the Chairman and Speaker pursuant to **Article 118(3)**, a Joint Sitting is governed by the **Rules of Procedure of the Lok Sabha** (not the Rajya Sabha), with a **quorum of one-tenth of the total number of members of both Houses**. Under **Article 108(4)**, if the Bill is passed by a **majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting** (a **Simple Majority**), it is deemed for the purposes of the Constitution to have been passed by both Houses. Because the Lok Sabha's strength (**543**) is more than double that of the Rajya Sabha (**245**), the Joint Sitting mathematically subordinates the Upper House whenever the ruling government commands a solid majority in the Lok Sabha.
    *   **Strict Cap on New Amendments (Proviso to Article 108(4)):** At a Joint Sitting, no new amendments may be proposed to the Bill, except (a) if the Bill has *not* been passed by the other House with amendments and returned, only such amendments as are made **necessary by the delay in the passage of the Bill**; and (b) if the Bill *has* been so passed and returned, only such amendments as aforesaid AND such other amendments as are **relevant to the matters with respect to which the Houses have not agreed**; and the decision of the person presiding as to the admissibility of any amendment is **final**.
*   **The Three Historical Invocations Since 1950:** Across more than seven decades of constitutional history, the **Article 108** Joint Sitting has been invoked to pass only **three Bills**:
    1.  **Dowry Prohibition Bill, 1960** (Joint Sitting held on **May 6 & 9, 1961**);
    2.  **Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill, 1977** (Joint Sitting held on **May 16, 1978**); and
    3.  **Prevention of Terrorism Bill (POTA), 2002** (Joint Sitting held on **March 26, 2002**).

## III. The Fiscal-Legislative Divide: Money Bills, Financial Bills, and the Jurisprudence of Bicameral Bypass (Articles 109–110, 117, 198–199, 207)

### 7. Textual Architecture of Money Bills and Financial Bills Category I & II (Articles 109, 110, and 117)

Rooted in the historic English constitutional principle of *"no taxation without representation"* and the **Parliament Act, 1911** (which stripped the un-elected House of Lords of its veto over the 1909 "People's Budget"), the Indian Constitution vests the power of the purse exclusively in the directly elected **Lok Sabha** (**Article 109**) and **Vidhan Sabha** (**Article 198**). However, because classifying a measure as a **Money Bill** disenfranchises the **Rajya Sabha**—stripping the federal chamber of its power to amend or reject the legislation and barring an **Article 108** Joint Sitting—the framers constructed a three-tier constitutional taxonomy distinguishing **Money Bills (Article 110)** from **Financial Bills Category I (Article 117(1))** and **Financial Bills Category II (Article 117(3))**. Every Money Bill is a Financial Bill, but **not every Financial Bill is a Money Bill**.

#### Exhaustive Textual Deconstruction of Articles 109, 110, and 117
*   **The Exhaustive Seven Clauses and the Word *"Only"* (Article 110(1)):** Under **Article 110(1)** (and State **Article 199(1)**), a Bill shall be deemed to be a **Money Bill** if it contains **ONLY** provisions dealing with all or any of the following seven matters:
    1.  **Clause (a):** The imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of any **tax**;
    2.  **Clause (b):** The regulation of the **borrowing of money or the giving of any guarantee** by the Government of India, or the amendment of the law with respect to any financial obligations undertaken or to be undertaken by the Government of India;
    3.  **Clause (c):** The custody of the **Consolidated Fund or the Contingency Fund of India**, the payment of moneys into or the withdrawal of moneys from any such Fund;
    4.  **Clause (d):** The **appropriation of moneys** out of the **Consolidated Fund of India**;
    5.  **Clause (e):** The declaring of any expenditure to be **expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** or the increasing of the amount of any such expenditure;
    6.  **Clause (f):** The receipt of money on account of the **Consolidated Fund of India or the Public Account of India** or the custody or issue of such money or the **audit of the accounts** of the Union or of a State; or
    7.  **Clause (g):** Any matter **incidental to any of the matters specified in sub-clauses (a) to (f)**.
*   **Explicit Exclusions (Article 110(2)):** Under **Article 110(2)**, a Bill shall **NOT** be deemed to be a Money Bill by reason only that it provides for:
    *   The imposition of **fines or other pecuniary penalties**; or
    *   The demand or payment of **fees for licences or fees for services rendered**; or
    *   The imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of any tax by any **local authority or body for local purposes**.
*   **Asymmetric Bicameral Procedure for Money Bills (Article 109):**
    1.  **Exclusive Lok Sabha Origin & Presidential Recommendation:** Under **Article 109(1)** and **Article 117(1)**, a Money Bill **shall not be introduced in the Rajya Sabha**, and can be introduced in the Lok Sabha **only by a Minister** on the prior recommendation of the **President**.
    2.  **The 14-Day Rajya Sabha Window (Article 109(2)–(5)):** After a Money Bill is passed by the Lok Sabha and endorsed with the **Speaker's Certificate** under **Article 110(4)**, it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha for its *recommendations*. The Rajya Sabha cannot reject or amend a Money Bill; it must within a period of **14 days** from the date of its receipt return the Bill to the Lok Sabha with its recommendations:
        *   If the Lok Sabha **accepts** any of the recommendations of the Rajya Sabha, the Money Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses with those amendments (**Article 109(3)**).
        *   If the Lok Sabha **does not accept** any of the recommendations of the Rajya Sabha, the Money Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the exact form in which it was originally passed by the Lok Sabha (**Article 109(4)**).
        *   If the Money Bill is **not returned by the Rajya Sabha within the said period of 14 days**, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses at the expiration of the 14 days in the form in which it was passed by the Lok Sabha (**Article 109(5)**).
    3.  **Presidential Assent Restriction (Article 111):** When a Money Bill is presented to the President under **Article 111** (accompanied by the Speaker's endorsed certificate under **Article 110(4)**), the President may either give his assent or withhold his assent, but under the explicit text of the **Proviso to Article 111**, the President **cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration** of the Houses (and because it was introduced with his prior recommendation on Cabinet advice, assent is conventionally granted).
*   **Financial Bills Category I (Article 117(1)) vs. Category II (Article 117(3)):**
    *   **Financial Bill Category I (Article 117(1)):** This is a hybrid Bill that contains one or more of the matters specified in **sub-clauses (a) to (f) of Article 110(1)**, **PLUS** other matters of general legislation (thereby failing the *"only"* test of **Article 110(1)**—for example, a Bill containing a taxation clause alongside substantive regulatory or structural provisions). Like a Money Bill, a **Category I Financial Bill** shares **two initial restrictions** under **Article 117(1)**: (1) it can be **introduced ONLY in the Lok Sabha**, and (2) it cannot be introduced **except on the recommendation of the President** (though under the **Proviso to Article 117(1)**, no recommendation is required for moving an amendment making provision for the *reduction or abolition of any tax*). However, in **all other respects**, a Category I Financial Bill is governed by the exact same procedure as an **Ordinary Bill**: the **Rajya Sabha has full power to reject or amend it** (subject to the President's recommendation for any amendment other than tax reduction/abolition), in case of a deadlock the President can summon a **Joint Sitting under Article 108**, and when presented to the President under **Article 111**, he **can return it for reconsideration**.
    *   **Financial Bill Category II (Article 117(3)):** This is a Bill that contains **NONE of the matters specified in Article 110**, but which, if enacted and brought into operation, would **involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India**. A **Category II Financial Bill** is treated identically to an **Ordinary Bill** at every stage—it can be **introduced in EITHER House of Parliament**, and **NO recommendation of the President is required for its introduction**. Its sole special feature under **Article 117(3)** is that it **shall not be passed by either House** unless the **President has recommended to that House the consideration of the Bill**. The Rajya Sabha enjoys full power to reject or amend it, an **Article 108 Joint Sitting** is fully applicable, and the President may return it for reconsideration under **Article 111**.

### 8. Speaker's Certification and the Constitutional Crisis of Bicameral Bypass (Article 110(3) and 122)

The most acute institutional battleground in modern Indian legislative jurisprudence centers on the interplay between **Article 110(3)**—which provides that *"If any question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill or not, the decision of the Speaker of the House of the People thereon shall be final"*—and **Article 122(1)**, which states that *"The validity of any proceedings in Parliament shall not be called in question on the ground of any alleged irregularity of procedure."* When a ruling executive commands a majority in the Lok Sabha but lacks a majority in the Rajya Sabha, certifying complex regulatory, surveillance, or institutional legislation as a **Money Bill** under **Article 110(3)** enables the executive to bypass the deliberative veto of the Upper House altogether—precipitating a direct collision between **Speaker finality** and **bicameralism as a pillar of the Basic Structure**.

#### The Evolution from Procedural Immunity to Basic Structure Scrutiny
*   **Phase I: The Procedural Irregularity Shield (*Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui*, 2014 & *Yogendra Kumar Jaiswal*, 2015):** In early rulings such as **Mangalore Ganesh Beedi Works v. State of Mysore (1963)**, and later in **Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2014)** (3-Judge Bench) and **Yogendra Kumar Jaiswal v. State of Bihar (2015)** (2-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court held that the decision of the Speaker under **Article 199(3)** (or **Article 110(3)**) is final and that any error in certifying an Ordinary or Financial Bill as a Money Bill amounts merely to an *"irregularity of procedure"* fully insulated from judicial scrutiny under **Article 212(1)** (or **Article 122(1)**).
*   **Phase II: The Aadhaar Judgment Clash (*Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India [Puttaswamy II]*, 2018):** When the Union Government introduced and passed the **Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act, 2016** as a **Money Bill** (and subsequently rejected substantive amendments recommended by the Rajya Sabha), the certification was challenged before a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Puttaswamy II (2018)**:
    1.  **The Majority Ratio (A.K. Sikri, J. for himself, Dipak Misra, CJI, and A.M. Khanwilkar, J.; Ashok Bhushan, J. concurring):** While the majority retreated from absolute immunity and accepted that the Speaker's certification under **Article 110(3)** is subject to judicial review in cases of patent unconstitutionality (following the **Tenth Schedule** precedent in *Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu*, 1992 and the **Article 356** precedent in *S.R. Bommai*, 1994), it **upheld the Aadhaar Act as a valid Money Bill**. Reasoning through the **Doctrine of Pith and Substance**, the majority held that the heart of the Aadhaar Act lay in **Section 7**, which mandated biometric authentication for the receipt of subsidies, benefits, and services whose expenditure was drawn from the **Consolidated Fund of India** (**Article 110(1)(c) and (e)**), while the remaining provisions creating the UIDAI, regulating private enrollment, and penalizing data breaches were merely *"incidental"* under **Article 110(1)(g)**.
    2.  **The Dissent of Dr. D.Y. Chandrachud, J.:** In a landmark dissent, **Justice Chandrachud** struck down the entire Aadhaar Act as unconstitutional on the ground that its passage as a Money Bill constituted a **"fraud on the Constitution"** and a **"subterfuge"** designed to evade the Rajya Sabha. He articulated three foundational constitutional propositions:
        *   First, **bicameralism and the existence of the Rajya Sabha** as a check against majoritarian haste form an integral part of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**;
        *   Second, the Speaker's decision under **Article 110(3)** is not a mere internal *"irregularity of procedure"* protected by **Article 122(1)**, but a **substantive constitutional prerequisite** determining the jurisdiction of a co-equal House of Parliament; and
        *   Third, the word **"only"** in **Article 110(1)** is an express textual restriction borrowed from **Section 1(2) of the UK Parliament Act, 1911**; the Doctrine of Pith and Substance (designed for Seventh Schedule federal competence disputes) *cannot* be imported into **Article 110(1)** to dilute the word *"only"*, and a Bill that creates a pan-India biometric identity architecture cannot be reduced to a Money Bill merely because one section references the Consolidated Fund.
*   **Phase III: Doubting *Puttaswamy II* and the Reference to a 7-Judge Constitution Bench (*Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd.*, 2019):** The constitutional crisis escalated with the **Finance Act, 2017**, an omnibus Money Bill through which the Union Government amended 26 statutes, abolished or merged eight autonomous Tribunals, and via **Part XIV (Section 184)** conferred sweeping powers on the central executive to frame rules governing the appointment, qualification, tenure, and removal of chairpersons and members across **19 Tribunals**—as well as successive Finance Acts (2015–2019) that amended the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA)** and the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010**. In **Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd. (2019)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** led by **Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi**:
    1.  **Explicitly overruled** the 3-Judge and 2-Judge decisions in *Mohd. Saeed Siddiqui (2014)* and *Yogendra Kumar Jaiswal (2015)*, holding unequivocally that the Speaker's certification under **Article 110(3)** is **subject to judicial review** and is **not** immunized as a procedural irregularity under **Article 122(1)**;
    2.  Held that in **Puttaswamy II (2018)**, the majority did not substantially examine or explain the effect of the restrictive word **"only"** in **Article 110(1)** or define the legal boundaries distinguishing a **Money Bill (Article 110)** from a **Financial Bill Category I (Article 117(1))**; and
    3.  **Referred the authoritative interpretation of Article 110(1) and (3)**—alongside the validity of **Part XIV of the Finance Act, 2017** (and subsequently tagged challenges to the **PMLA** and **Electoral Bonds** Finance Act amendments)—to a **7-Judge Constitution Bench** of the Supreme Court.

## IV. Parliamentary Budgetary Architecture and the Three Constitutional Funds (Articles 112–116, 202–206, 266–267)

### 9. The Annual Financial Statement, Charged vs. Voted Expenditure, and the Six-Stage Budgetary Cycle (Articles 112–114)

The Constitution of India never uses the word "Budget." Instead, **Article 112(1)** (and **Article 202(1)** for the States) designates the annual budget as the **"Annual Financial Statement"**—requiring the **President** in respect of every financial year (running from **April 1 to March 31**) to cause to be laid before **both the Houses of Parliament** a statement of the estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for that year. Under **Article 112(2)**, the estimates of expenditure embodied in the Annual Financial Statement must satisfy two mandatory structural classifications: first, they must show **separately** the sums required to meet **expenditure described by this Constitution as expenditure charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India** (**Article 112(2)(a)**) and the sums required to meet **other expenditure proposed to be made from the Consolidated Fund of India** (**Article 112(2)(b)**); and second, they must **distinguish expenditure on revenue account from other expenditure (capital account)**.

#### Structural Reforms (2017) and the Charged vs. Voted Expenditure Firewall
*   **The 2017 Budgetary Architecture Overhaul:** For 92 years (from **1924 to 2016**), based on the recommendations of the **10-member Acworth Committee (1920–21)**, the **Railway Budget** was separated from the General Budget to grant financial flexibility to the railways while paying a fixed annual dividend to general revenues. In **September 2016** (taking effect from the **2017–18 Budget**), acting on the recommendation of the **NITI Aayog Committee chaired by Bibek Debroy**, the Union Government executed **three structural reforms** without requiring a constitutional amendment:
    1.  **Merger of the Railway Budget into the General Budget:** Terminated the separate Railway Budget presentation and abolished the statutory dividend paid by the Railways to General Revenues, presenting a single unified **Annual Financial Statement** and a single **Appropriation Bill** including Railway demands;
    2.  **Advancement of Budget Presentation to February 1:** Shifted the presentation date from the last working day of February to **February 1** so that the entire Six-Stage Parliamentary Budgetary Cycle (**Appropriation Bill** and **Finance Bill**) is completed *before* the new financial year begins on **April 1**, eliminating the routine annual dependence on a two-month **Vote on Account** (**Article 116(1)(a)**) except during Lok Sabha election years; and
    3.  **Abolition of Plan vs. Non-Plan Expenditure Classification:** Replaced the artificial Planning Commission-era dichotomy of "Plan" and "Non-Plan" expenditure with the constitutional classification mandated by **Article 112(2)**: **Revenue Expenditure vs. Capital Expenditure**.
*   **Charged Expenditure (Article 112(3)) vs. Voted Expenditure (Article 113(2)):** To insulate high constitutional authorities and sovereign financial commitments from partisan legislative coercion, **Article 113(1)** stipulates that so much of the estimates as relates to **expenditure charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India shall NOT be submitted to the vote of Parliament**, although nothing in the clause prevents the **discussion** in either House of Parliament of any of those estimates. Under **Article 112(3)(a)–(g)**, the following seven categories of expenditure are **charged** on the Consolidated Fund of India:
    1.  The emoluments and allowances of the **President** and other expenditure relating to his office (**Art 112(3)(a)**);
    2.  The salaries and allowances of the **Chairman and the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** and the **Speaker and the Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha** (**Art 112(3)(b)**);
    3.  **Debt charges** for which the Government of India is liable, including interest, sinking fund charges, and redemption charges, and other expenditure relating to the raising of loans and the service and redemption of debt (**Art 112(3)(c)**);
    4.  (i) The salaries, allowances, and pensions payable to or in respect of **Judges of the Supreme Court**, AND (ii) the **pensions** payable to or in respect of **Judges of the Federal Court** and **Judges of any High Court** (**Art 112(3)(d)**—*note carefully the federal distinction*: the **salaries and allowances** of sitting **High Court Judges** are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State** under **Article 202(3)(d)**, whereas the **pensions** of retired High Court Judges are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** under **Article 112(3)(d)(iii)**);
    5.  The salary, allowances, and pension payable to or in respect of the **Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (CAG)** (**Art 112(3)(e)**);
    6.  Any sums required to satisfy any **judgment, decree, or award of any court or arbitral tribunal** (**Art 112(3)(f)**); and
    7.  Any other expenditure declared by the Constitution (such as the administrative expenses and salaries/pensions of the **Supreme Court** under **Article 146(3)** and the **Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)** under **Article 322**) or by **Parliament by law** to be so charged (**Art 112(3)(g)**).
*   **The Exclusive Lok Sabha Prerogative over Demands for Grants (Article 113(2)–(3)):** All other expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India is submitted in the form of **Demands for Grants** exclusively to the **Lok Sabha** under **Article 113(2)** (the **Rajya Sabha has zero power to vote on Demands for Grants**). The Lok Sabha has the power to **assent to, or to refuse to assent to, any demand, or to assent to any demand subject to a reduction of the amount** specified therein—meaning the Lok Sabha **cannot increase a Demand for Grant** or alter its destination, and under **Article 113(3)**, no Demand for a Grant can be made except on the **recommendation of the President**.
*   **The Constitutional Lock of the Appropriation Bill (Article 114):** Even after the Lok Sabha has voted and passed all Demands for Grants under **Article 113(2)**, not a single rupee can be legally withdrawn from the **Consolidated Fund of India** until Parliament enacts the **Appropriation Bill** under **Article 114** (as reinforced by **Article 114(3)**: *"Subject to the provisions of articles 115 and 116, no money shall be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund of India except under appropriation made by law passed in accordance with the provisions of this article"*). The Appropriation Bill aggregates both **all Grants voted by the Lok Sabha** (**Art 114(1)(a)**) and **all Expenditure Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (**Art 114(1)(b)**). To prevent the House from reopening settled grants, **Article 114(2)** imposes an absolute procedural bar: **no amendment shall be proposed to any such Appropriation Bill in either House of Parliament which will have the effect of varying the amount or altering the destination of any grant so made or of varying the amount of any expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**, and the decision of the person presiding as to whether an amendment is inadmissible under this clause is **final**.
*   **The Finance Bill and the 75-Day Statutory Window:** Unlike the Appropriation Bill (where amendments to amounts are barred by **Article 114(2)**), when the **Finance Bill** (which gives legal effect to the government's taxation proposals for the new financial year) is debated, members of the Lok Sabha **can move amendments to reject or reduce a tax** (though increasing a tax or introducing a new tax requires the President's recommendation under **Article 117(1)**). Under the **Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 2023** (which replaced the **Provisional Collection of Taxes Act, 1931**), declared customs and excise/indirect tax impositions or increases take immediate provisional effect from the midnight of the day the Finance Bill is introduced to prevent speculative hoarding, but the **Finance Bill must be enacted into law (assented to by the President) within 75 days** of its introduction, failing which the provisionally collected tax must be refunded.

### 10. Parliamentary Fiscal Control Instruments: Cut Motions, Guillotine, and Exceptional Grants (Articles 113, 115–116)

During **Stage 4** of the budgetary cycle (Voting on Demands for Grants under **Article 113(2)**), members of the **Lok Sabha** (and never the Rajya Sabha) possess a potent legislative instrument to challenge the executive's policies, enforce fiscal economy, or ventilate specific citizen grievances: the **Cut Motion**. Because the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under **Article 75(3)**, the **passage of any Cut Motion in the Lok Sabha amounts to an expression of want of parliamentary confidence**, constitutionally obligating the government to resign. Conversely, because the ruling party or coalition commands a majority in the Lok Sabha, Cut Motions rarely pass and serve primarily as instruments of structured parliamentary debate.

#### The Three Cut Motions, the Guillotine, and Extraordinary Grants (Articles 115–116)
*   **The Three Constitutional Species of Cut Motions (Lok Sabha Rules 209–211):**
    1.  **Disapproval of Policy Cut Motion:** Moved in the exact formula *"That the amount of the demand be reduced to **Re. 1**."* This is the most severe Cut Motion, representing **total disapproval of the policy underlying the Demand for Grant**. A member giving notice of a Policy Cut Motion must indicate in precise terms the particulars of the policy which he proposes to discuss, keeping the discussion confined to the specific points mentioned in the notice, and it is open to the member to **advocate an alternative policy**.
    2.  **Economy Cut Motion:** Moved in the formula *"That the amount of the demand be reduced by a **specified amount**."* This motion asserts that the proposed expenditure can be reduced through administrative efficiency and fiscal economy without impairing the underlying policy. The specified amount may represent either a **lump-sum reduction** in the demand or the **omission or reduction of a specific item** in the demand, and notice must briefly state the particular matter on which discussion is sought.
    3.  **Token Cut Motion:** Moved in the exact formula *"That the amount of the demand be reduced by **Rs. 100**."* The objective is neither to reject the policy nor to enforce substantial financial economy, but to **ventilate a specific grievance** which is within the sphere of responsibility of the Government of India, concentrating debate strictly on the single grievance specified in the notice.
*   **Strict Admissibility Conditions for Cut Motions (Lok Sabha Rule 210):** For any Cut Motion to be admitted by the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha**, it must relate to **one demand only**, must be clearly expressed without arguments or defamatory statements, must be confined to **one specific matter**, must **not make suggestions for the amendment or repeal of existing laws**, must **not refer to a matter that is *sub judice*** or not primarily the concern of the Government of India, and must **not relate to Expenditure Charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (**Article 112(3)**).
*   **The Guillotine and the Deficit of Parliamentary Scrutiny:** Because the Lok Sabha allots a fixed number of days (ordinarily around 26 days) for the discussion and voting of all Demands for Grants across more than 100 Ministries and Departments, only 4 to 6 Ministries' demands are typically debated on the floor. On the **last day of the period allotted** by the Speaker for voting on Demands for Grants, at the appointed hour, the Speaker applies the **"Guillotine"**—putting **every remaining Demand for Grant** (whether discussed by the House or not) to vote simultaneously and disposing of them without any further debate. In recent decades, between **75% and 100% of total Union expenditure** has frequently been passed via the Guillotine without floor debate, shifting the real burden of line-item scrutiny to the **24 Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)** during the recess between Stage 2 and Stage 4.
*   **Extraordinary and Supplementary Parliamentary Grants (Articles 115 and 116):** To address timing gaps, emergencies, and mid-year fiscal overruns without violating the lock of **Article 114(3)**, the Constitution establishes six specialized fiscal grants across **Articles 115 and 116** (governed by the exact same procedure as the Annual Financial Statement and Appropriation Bill):
    1.  **Supplementary Grant (Article 115(1)(a) First Limb):** Granted when the amount authorized by the current year's **Appropriation Act** for a particular service is found to be **insufficient** for the purposes of that year.
    2.  **Additional Grant (Article 115(1)(a) Second Limb):** Granted when a need has arisen during the current financial year for **supplementary or additional expenditure upon some "new service"** not contemplated in the Annual Financial Statement for that year.
    3.  **Excess Grant (Article 115(1)(b)):** Granted when any money has *already been spent* on any service during a financial year **in excess of the amount granted** for that service and for that year. Unlike Supplementary or Additional Grants (which are passed *during* the financial year), an **Excess Grant** is voted by the Lok Sabha **after the financial year has ended**, only *after* the excess expenditure has been audited by the **Comptroller and Auditor-General (CAG)** and examined and recommended for regularization by the **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**.
    4.  **Vote on Account (Article 116(1)(a)):** Empowered the Lok Sabha to make any grant **in advance** in respect of the estimated expenditure for a **part of any financial year**, pending the completion of the voting of the Demands for Grants (**Article 113**) and the enactment of the **Appropriation Bill** (**Article 114**). Prior to the 2017 advancement of the Budget to February 1, Parliament routinely passed a **Vote on Account** for two months (equivalent to **one-sixth (1/6th)** of total estimated expenditure) every March so the government could fund salaries and operations through April and May; post-2017, a Vote on Account is used primarily during **Lok Sabha general election years** via an **Interim Budget**. Crucially, whereas an Interim Budget covers **both receipts (taxation) and expenditure**, a **Vote on Account** deals **exclusively with the expenditure side** of the budget and involves no policy alterations.
    5.  **Vote of Credit (Article 116(1)(b)):** Empowers the Lok Sabha to make a grant for meeting an **unexpected demand upon the resources of India** when, on account of the **magnitude or the indefinite character of the service** (such as a sudden war or national security crisis), the demand **cannot be stated with the details ordinarily given** in an Annual Financial Statement. It functions as an executive **"blank cheque"** granted by the Lok Sabha during extraordinary national exigencies.
    6.  **Exceptional Grant (Article 116(1)(c)):** Empowers the Lok Sabha to make an **exceptional grant which forms no part of the current service of any financial year**—granted for a special, non-recurring standalone purpose.

#### Comparative Legal Mechanics of the Three Funds (Articles 266(1), 266(2), and 267)
*   **The Consolidated Fund of India (Article 266(1)):** Under **Article 266(1)**, subject to the provisions of **Article 267** and to the provisions of Chapter I of Part XII with respect to the assignment of the whole or part of the net proceeds of certain taxes and duties to States, three streams of money **shall form one consolidated fund to be entitled "the Consolidated Fund of India"** (and a corresponding **"Consolidated Fund of the State"** for each State):
    1.  **All revenues** received by the Government of India (tax and non-tax receipts);
    2.  **All loans raised** by that Government by the issue of **treasury bills, loans, or ways and means advances**; and
    3.  **All moneys received** by that Government in **repayment of loans**.
    Under the double constitutional lock of **Article 266(3)** and **Article 114(3)**, **no moneys out of the Consolidated Fund of India or the Consolidated Fund of a State shall be appropriated except in accordance with law** and for the purposes and in the manner provided in the Constitution. Every single rupee of charged or voted expenditure from this Fund is subject to **Parliamentary authorization** and **CAG audit (Article 149)**.
*   **The Public Account of India (Article 266(2)):** Under **Article 266(2)**, **all other public moneys** received by or on behalf of the Government of India or the Government of a State shall be credited to the **"Public Account of India"** or the **"Public Account of the State,"** as the case may be. This account holds transactions where the government acts merely in a **fiduciary capacity as a banker or custodian**—including **Provident Fund deposits, National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) collections, judicial deposits, savings bank deposits, departmental deposits, and remittances**, as well as dedicated statutory reserve funds into which proceeds of specific cesses are transferred after initial credit to the Consolidated Fund. Because these moneys do not belong to the sovereign revenue of the government and must be returned to the rightful depositors or disbursed per statutory trust obligations, **payments from the Public Account of India are made solely by executive action without requiring any Parliamentary Appropriation Act** (though they remain subject to audit by the **CAG**).
*   **The Contingency Fund of India (Article 267):** Whereas **Article 266** *directly creates* the Consolidated Fund and the Public Account by the text of the Constitution itself, **Article 267(1)** is an **enabling provision** empowering **Parliament by law to establish a Contingency Fund** in the nature of an **imprest** to be entitled *"the Contingency Fund of India,"* into which shall be paid from time to time such sums as may be determined by such law. Pursuant to **Article 267(1)**, Parliament enacted the **Contingency Fund of India Act, 1950** (with an initial corpus of Rs. 50 crore, increased to Rs. 500 crore in 2005, and enhanced to **Rs. 30,000 crore by the Finance Act, 2021**, where 40% of the corpus is placed at the disposal of the Expenditure Secretary).
    *   **Custody and Operation:** The Fund is placed at the disposal of the **President of India** (and held on the President's behalf by the **Finance Secretary, Department of Economic Affairs / Department of Expenditure**) to enable **advances to be made by the President out of such Fund for the purposes of meeting unforeseen expenditure pending authorisation of such expenditure by Parliament by law under Article 115 or Article 116**.
    *   **Ex-Post Parliamentary Recoupment Loop:** While the initial advance out of the Contingency Fund is made via **executive action** without prior parliamentary voting, the executive *must* subsequently present a **Supplementary or Additional Demand for Grant** to Parliament under **Article 115** or **Article 116**; once Parliament passes the corresponding Appropriation Act, an equivalent sum is drawn from the **Consolidated Fund of India** and paid back into the **Contingency Fund of India** to restore its statutory imprest corpus. Under **Article 267(2)**, the Legislature of a State may similarly by law establish a **Contingency Fund of the State** placed at the disposal of the **Governor**.
