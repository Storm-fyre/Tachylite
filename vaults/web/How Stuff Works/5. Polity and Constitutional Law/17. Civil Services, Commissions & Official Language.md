# Part 17: Constitutional Public Services, Public Service Commissions, National Commissions for SCs/STs/BCs, and Official Language Jurisprudence

## I. Constitutional Public Services: The Doctrine of Pleasure, Article 311 Due Process, and All-India Federal Integration (Part XIV, Articles 308–314)

### 1. Legislative Regulation of Public Services and the Doctrine of Pleasure (Articles 308–310)

The constitutional architecture governing the permanent executive in **Part XIV** (**Articles 308–323**) resolves a foundational tension in modern public law: the clash between **sovereign executive prerogative**—which demands a disciplined, responsive bureaucracy subordinate to the elected ministry—and **administrative constitutionalism**, which requires insulating civil servants from partisan vendetta so they can render fearless, objective advice and uphold the rule of law. Unlike the American spoils system or pure Westminster common law—where Crown servants lacked justiciable constitutional tenure—the framers of the Indian Constitution constitutionalized the terms of public employment. Following the reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir in 2019, **Article 308** defines the expression "State" in **Part XIV** as uniformly encompassing all States within the Union, establishing a pan-Indian baseline for civil service governance.

#### The Statutory and Executive Architecture of Article 309

**Article 309** vests plenary legislative competence in the appropriate Legislature (Parliament for Union services and State Legislatures for State services, aligned with **Entry 70 of List I** and **Entry 41 of List II** of the **Seventh Schedule**) to regulate the recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or of any State. Recognizing that comprehensive statutory enactments for thousands of cadres could not be enacted instantaneously by legislatures, the **Proviso to Article 309** creates a transitional yet enduring executive rule-making power.

*   **Co-Extensive Legislative Character of Proviso Rules:** Under the **Proviso to Article 309**, the **President** (for Union services) or the **Governor** (for State services), or any person directed by them, is empowered to make rules regulating recruitment and service conditions until provision in that behalf is made by or under an Act of the appropriate Legislature. In **B.S. Yadav v. State of Haryana (1980)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** established that the rule-making power exercised by the President or Governor under the **Proviso to Article 309** is strictly **legislative in character**, not executive. Consequently, these rules can be given **retrospective effect** just like an Act of Parliament, provided they do not violate **Part III** (**Articles 14, 16, and 21**) or take away vested constitutional rights arbitrarily (**State of Gujarat v. Raman Lal Keshav Lal Soni, 1983**).
*   **Subordination to Part III and Constitutional Clauses:** Both statutory enactments and **Proviso to Article 309** rules operate expressly *"Subject to the provisions of this Constitution."* Thus, service rules cannot override the equality guarantees of **Articles 14 and 16**, the procedural safeguards of **Article 311**, or the consultative and supervisory mandates of **Article 320**.
*   **Interplay with Executive Instructions (Articles 73 and 162):** Where neither a statute under the main clause of **Article 309** nor a rule under its **Proviso** occupies the field, the Union or State Executive can issue administrative instructions under **Article 73** or **Article 162** to fill gaps in service conditions, though executive instructions can never supplant or amend statutory **Article 309** rules (**Sant Ram Sharma v. State of Rajasthan, 1967**).

#### The Common Law Lineage and Constitutional Adaptation of the Doctrine of Pleasure (Article 310)

**Article 310(1)** constitutionalizes the common law **Doctrine of Pleasure** (*durante bene placito*), stipulating that every person who is a member of a defence service or of a civil service of the Union or of an All-India Service, or holds any post connected with defence or any civil post under the Union, holds office during the **pleasure of the President**, and every person who is a member of a civil service of a State or holds any civil post under a State holds office during the **pleasure of the Governor of the State**.

*   **Departure from English Crown Prerogative:** Under British constitutional theory, a servant of the Crown held office strictly at the Crown's pleasure, could be dismissed at will without assigning reasons, and could not even sue the Crown for arrears of salary because the engagement was deemed unilateral rather than contractual. In India, however, **Article 310(1)** opens with the decisive constitutional fetter: *"Except as expressly provided by this Constitution."* In **State of Bihar v. Abdul Majid (1954)**, the Supreme Court buried the feudal incidents of the English prerogative, holding that the **Doctrine of Pleasure** in India is not an untrammeled royal privilege but a qualified constitutional doctrine subject to **Article 311** and statutory law, thereby recognizing a civil servant's right to sue the State for arrears of salary.
*   **Constitutional Offices Exempt from Pleasure (*Dum Bene Se Gesserit*):** By virtue of the opening exception in **Article 310(1)**, high constitutional functionaries whose independence is structurally indispensable hold office during **good behaviour** (*dum bene se gesserit*) rather than pleasure. These include **Judges of the Supreme Court** (**Article 124**), **Judges of the High Courts** (**Article 217**), the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India** (**Article 148**), the **Chief Election Commissioner** (**Article 324**), and the **Chairman and Members of the UPSC and SPSCs** (**Article 317**).
*   **Non-Delegability of Sovereign Pleasure vs. Ministerial Exercise:** In **State of Uttar Pradesh v. Babu Ram Upadhya (1961)** and the **7-Judge Constitution Bench** decision in **Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)**, the Supreme Court clarified a vital structural distinction: while the **Doctrine of Pleasure** under **Article 310** cannot be fettered or abrogated by an ordinary statute or **Article 309** rule, the pleasure of the President or the Governor is **not a personal discretionary prerogative**. In a parliamentary system governed by **Articles 74 and 163**, the pleasure of the President or Governor is exercised through the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**, acting through authorized officers according to the **Rules of Business** (**Articles 77(3) and 166(3)**).
*   **Contractual Compensation for Special Non-Cadre Specialists (Article 310(2)):** To enable the State to recruit external domain specialists outside established civil service cadres, **Article 310(2)** provides that where a person not being a member of a defence service, an All-India Service, or a civil service is appointed under a special contract requiring special qualifications, the contract may stipulate payment of **compensation** if the post is abolished before the expiration of the contractual period, or if the person is required to vacate the post for reasons **not connected with any misconduct** on their part.

### 2. Substantive and Procedural Due Process for Civil Servants (Article 311)

**Article 311** operates as the direct constitutional counterweight to **Article 310**, erecting a two-tiered procedural shield around civil servants. Crucially, the text of **Article 311(1)** restricts this shield to persons who are members of a **civil service of the Union**, an **All-India Service**, or a **civil service of a State**, or who hold a **civil post under the Union or a State**.

#### The Dual Substantive Shields: Subordinate Authority Bar (Article 311(1)) and Reasonable Opportunity (Article 311(2))

The threshold jurisdictional requirement of **Article 311** is that the employee must hold a "civil post"—defined in **State of Assam v. Kanak Chandra Dutta (1967)** as an office on the civil side of administration where a **master-and-servant relationship** exists with the Union or State, evidenced by state control over appointment, suspension, dismissal, and payment out of state revenues.

*   **Categorical Exclusions from Article 311:**
    1.  **Defence Personnel and Civilian Defence Employees:** Members of the Armed Forces and even **civilians employed in defence establishments** (who hold posts "connected with defence" under **Article 310(1)** rather than "civil posts") are entirely excluded from **Article 311** (**Union of India v. K.S. Subramanian, 1976**).
    2.  **Statutory Corporations, PSUs, and Autonomous Bodies:** Employees of statutory corporations (such as LIC, ONGC, or nationalized banks), government companies, and registered societies do not hold "civil posts" under the Union or a State because these corporations possess a separate juristic personality (**Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram Sardar Singh Raghuvanshi, 1975**). While they cannot invoke **Article 311**, they remain protected against arbitrary dismissal under **Articles 14 and 21** because such corporations constitute **"The State" under Article 12**.
*   **The Three Triggering Major Penalties (Dismissal, Removal, Reduction in Rank):** **Article 311** does not apply to every adverse administrative action (such as transfer, censure, withholding of increments, or suspension pending inquiry). It is triggered **exclusively** by three major penal consequences: **Dismissal**, **Removal** (where removal, unlike dismissal, generally does not disqualify for future employment, though both are penal terminations under **Article 311**), and **Reduction in Rank**.
*   **The Parshotam Lal Dhingra (1958) Test for Punitive Termination:** In the landmark **5-Judge Constitution Bench** ruling in **Parshotam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India (1958)**, the Supreme Court held that **Article 311** protects **both permanent and temporary/probationary** civil servants. However, because a probationer or temporary servant has no substantive right to the post, a simple termination of service or reversion to a lower substantive post in accordance with contract or service rules (*termination simpliciter*) does not attract **Article 311**. **Article 311(2)** is triggered only when the termination or reversion operates by way of **punishment**—tested by two criteria: (1) whether the servant had a **right to hold the post/rank** (such as a permanent appointee), or (2) whether the termination/reversion visits the servant with **evil consequences**, such as forfeiture of earned pay/pension or casting an indelible **stigma** on their character.
*   **The "Motive vs. Foundation" Doctrine and Lifting the Veil:** Building on *Dhingra* and **Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)**, the Supreme Court in **Anoop Jaiswal v. Government of India (1984)** and **Dipti Prakash Banerjee v. Satyendra Nath Bose National Centre (1999)** established that the Court will **lift the veil** of an innocuously worded order of *termination simpliciter*. If prior allegations of misconduct merely acted as the **motive** (assessing general unsuitability during probation), **Article 311(2)** is not attracted; but if a factual finding of specific misconduct arrived at behind the employee's back forms the very **foundation** of the termination order, the order is punitive and void for violating **Article 311(2)**. Conversely, **compulsory retirement in the public interest** after attaining a prescribed age or length of service is **not** a dismissal or removal under **Article 311**, because it entails no stigma and forfeits no accrued terminal benefits (**Shyam Lal v. State of U.P., 1954**; **Baikuntha Nath Das v. Chief District Medical Officer, 1992**).
*   **First Shield—The Appointing Authority Rank Floor (Article 311(1)):** No civil servant shall be dismissed or removed by an authority **subordinate to that by which they were appointed**. The constitutional benchmark is the **actual appointing authority** in fact, not an authority onto whom appointing powers were subsequently delegated (**Krishna Kumar v. Divisional Assistant Electrical Engineer, 1979**). While a subordinate authority may initiate disciplinary proceedings and conduct the factual inquiry (**State of Madhya Pradesh v. Shardul Singh, 1970**), the final order of dismissal or removal must be passed by the original appointing authority or an authority of **coordinate or superior rank**.
*   **Second Shield—Mandatory Inquiry and Reasonable Opportunity (Article 311(2)):** No civil servant shall be dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank except after an **inquiry** in which they have been **informed of the charges** against them and given a **reasonable opportunity of being heard** in respect of those charges (**Khem Chand v. Union of India, 1958**).
*   **Constitutional Amendments to Article 311(2) (15th and 42nd Amendments):** Originally, **Article 311(2)** was interpreted to grant the civil servant **two distinct opportunities** to make representations: first at the factual inquiry stage, and second at the stage of the proposed quantum of penalty. The **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963** clarified that the second representation on penalty must be based solely on the evidence already adduced during the inquiry without adducing fresh evidence. Subsequently, the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** amended the **First Proviso to Article 311(2)** to **completely abolish the right to make a representation at the second stage on the proposed penalty**. However, in **Union of India v. Mohd. Ramzan Khan (1990)** and the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** ruling in **Managing Director, ECIL v. B. Karunakar (1993)**, the Supreme Court held that where the **Inquiry Officer** is a person other than the **Disciplinary Authority**, the principles of natural justice embedded in the **first stage of the inquiry** under **Article 311(2)** still mandate furnishing a copy of the **Inquiry Officer's Report** to the delinquent employee so they can contest the adverse factual findings *before* the Disciplinary Authority records its final guilt determination.

#### The Second Proviso Exceptions and the Tulsiram Patel (1985) Constitution Bench Synthesis

1.  **Clause (a)—Conviction on a Criminal Charge:** Where a person is dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank on the ground of **conduct which has led to their conviction on a criminal charge**. In **Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985)** and **Deputy Director of Collegiate Education v. S. Nagoor Meera (1995)**, the Supreme Court held that the Disciplinary Authority need not wait for the exhaustion of all criminal appeals; even if an appellate court suspends the execution of the *sentence* under criminal procedure, **Clause (a)** remains fully operative unless the appellate court stays the **conviction itself** (**Rama Narang v. Ramesh Narang, 1995**). However, dismissal upon conviction is not mechanical: the authority must still examine the **conduct** that led to the conviction (e.g., whether a minor traffic offence vs. moral turpitude warrants dismissal) without giving a fresh hearing (**Shankar Dass v. Union of India, 1985**).
2.  **Clause (b)—Impracticability of Holding Inquiry:** Where the authority empowered to dismiss, remove, or reduce a person in rank is satisfied that for some reason, **to be recorded by that authority in writing**, it is **not reasonably practicable to hold such inquiry** (such as where delinquent employees resort to collective violence, terrorize witnesses, or abduct inquiry officers). Under **Article 311(3)**, if a question arises whether it is reasonably practicable to hold such an inquiry, the decision of the empowered authority shall be **final**.
3.  **Clause (c)—Interest of the Security of the State:** Where the **President** or the **Governor**, as the case may be, is satisfied that in the **interest of the security of the State** it is not expedient to hold such inquiry. As clarified in **Sardari Lal v. Union of India (1971)** as overruled by **Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)**, the "satisfaction" of the President or Governor under **Clause (c)** is the constitutional satisfaction exercised on the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**, not personal satisfaction. Furthermore, "security of the State" is a narrower, higher threshold than mere "law and order" or "public order" (**Ramaswami v. Union of India**).

In the watershed **5-Judge Constitution Bench** judgment in **Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel (1985)** (4:1 majority led by Justice D.P. Madon), the Supreme Court settled the constitutional interplay between **Articles 14, 310, and 311**:

*   **Exclusion of *Audi Alteram Partem* via Express Constitutional Mandate:** The challengers argued that even if the **Second Proviso to Article 311(2)** dispensed with a formal inquiry, **Article 14** (non-arbitrariness under *E.P. Royappa* and *Maneka Gandhi*) still required giving a minimal opportunity of hearing before dismissal. The Constitution Bench rejected this argument, relies on the maxim *expressum facit cessare tacitum* (what is expressed makes what is implied cease). Because the **Second Proviso to Article 311(2)** is itself a constitutional provision of co-equal rank that expressly excludes an inquiry in the public interest, the principles of natural justice under **Article 14** cannot be invoked to re-introduce through the backdoor the very inquiry that **Article 311(2)** expressly bars.
*   **Judicial Review over Clause (b) and Article 311(3) Finality:** Simultaneously, *Tulsiram Patel* (reinforced in **Satyavir Singh v. Union of India, 1985** and **Jaswant Singh v. State of Punjab, 1991**) held that the finality clause in **Article 311(3)** only binds intra-departmental appellate authorities and **cannot oust the judicial review of the Supreme Court (Article 32) or High Courts (Article 226)**. Recording reasons in writing under **Clause (b)** is a **mandatory condition precedent**; if the recorded reasons are *mala fide*, perverse, based on wholly extraneous or irrelevant materials, or serve as a cloak to avoid an inconvenient inquiry where witnesses are readily available, the Court will strike down the summary dismissal. Moreover, a civil servant summarily dismissed under the **Second Proviso** retains the right to file a **post-decisional departmental appeal or revision** under the service rules, where they can challenge both the factual basis and the quantum of punishment.

### 3. The All-India Services Architecture and Federal Dual Control (Articles 312–314)

While classical federations like the United States maintain a strict vertical separation between federal civil servants (who execute solely federal laws) and state civil servants (who execute solely state laws), the Indian Constitution engineered a unique federal instrument under **Article 312**: the **All-India Services (AIS)**. Spearheaded in the **Constituent Assembly** by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**—who famously described an independent, pan-Indian civil service as the **"Steel Frame"** essential to prevent administrative Balkanization ("Remove them and I see nothing but a picture of chaos all over the country")—the All-India Services institutionalize administrative integration alongside political federalism.

#### Rajya Sabha Constituent Gateway and the Dual-Control Mechanics of Article 312

Because the creation of an All-India Service directly trenches upon State executive autonomy by staffing key State secretariat and district posts with officers recruited by the Union, **Article 312(1)** conditions its creation on an extraordinary **federal constituent gateway vested exclusively in the Council of States (Rajya Sabha)**.

*   **The Three Extant All-India Services:** Under **Article 312(2)**, the **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)** and the **Indian Police Service (IPS)**—which replaced the colonial Indian Civil Service (ICS) and Indian Police (IP)—were constitutionally deemed to be services created by Parliament under **Article 312**. Acting under this mandate, Parliament enacted the **All-India Services Act, 1951**, authorizing the Union Government, after consulting State Governments, to frame rules regulating recruitment and service conditions. In **1966**, following a **Rajya Sabha** resolution and the **All-India Services (Amendment) Act, 1963**, the **Indian Forest Service (IFoS)** was constituted as the third All-India Service (note that although the 1963 Amendment Act also authorized an Indian Medical and Health Service and an Indian Service of Engineers, they were never formally operationalized).
*   **Asymmetrical Dual Control (Immediate vs. Ultimate Control):** Members of the **IAS**, **IPS**, and **IFoS** are recruited centrally through the **UPSC** and appointed by the **President**, after which they are allocated to specific **State Cadres** (or Joint Cadres) by the respective Union cadre-controlling authorities—the **Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT)** for the IAS, the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)** for the IPS, and the **Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC)** for the IFoS. This creates a calibrated regime of **dual control**:
    1.  **Immediate Control by the State Government:** While serving in the State cadre, the officer works under the direct administrative control of the State Government, which exercises full authority over postings, transfers, work allocation, **Performance Appraisal Reports (PAR)**, and the power to **suspend** an officer or initiate disciplinary inquiries under the **All-India Services (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969**.
    2.  **Ultimate Control by the Union Government:** Because the **President** is the appointing authority for all AIS officers, **Article 311(1)** and the **AIS (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969** vest the power to impose the ultimate penalties of **dismissal, removal, or compulsory retirement** exclusively in the **Central Government** (acting in consultation with the **UPSC**). Furthermore, under the proviso to **Rule 6(1) of the IAS (Cadre) Rules, 1954**, in the event of any disagreement between the Central Government and a State Government regarding the deputation of a cadre officer to the Centre, the matter shall be decided by the **Central Government**, creating recurring federal friction over central deputations and suspensions.

#### The All-India Judicial Service (Article 312(3)) Federal Standoff and Transitional Repeals (Articles 312A–314)

The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** amended **Article 312(1)** and inserted **Clauses (3) and (4)** to expressly include the creation of an **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** within the ambit of **Article 312**.

*   **Constitutional Parameters of the AIJS (Article 312(3)–(4)):**
    1.  **Rank Floor:** **Article 312(3)** explicitly stipulates that the **All-India Judicial Service** shall **not include any post inferior to that of a District Judge** as defined in **Article 236** (which encompasses Judges of a City Civil Court, Additional/Joint/Assistant District Judges, Chief Judges of Small Cause Courts, Chief Presidency Magistrates, and Sessions Judges).
    2.  **Deemed Non-Amendment Clause (Article 312(4)):** Any Parliamentary law providing for the creation of the **AIJS** may contain incidental and consequential provisions as may be necessary for amending **Chapter VI of Part VI** (**Articles 233–237** governing Subordinate Courts), and no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of **Article 368**.
*   **The Federal and Institutional Deadlock over AIJS:** Despite endorsements from the **1st, 8th, and 116th Law Commission Reports** and the Supreme Court in **All India Judges' Association v. Union of India (1992)** as a vehicle to attract top legal talent at the entry level of the Higher Judicial Service, the **AIJS** remains unimplemented due to intense opposition from major **High Courts** and **State Governments**. The competing structural forces center on: (a) **Erosion of High Court Administrative Control under Articles 233 and 235**, which vest District Judge appointments and disciplinary control in the Governor acting in consultation with the High Court; (b) **Linguistic Plurilingualism**, as trial and appellate district proceedings require deep mastery of local vernaculars; and (c) promotional stagnation for lower subordinate judicial officers (**Article 234**).
*   **Transitional Continuation and the 28th Amendment Repeal of ICS Privileges (Articles 312A–314):** **Article 313** provides for the transitional continuation of pre-constitutional service laws until other provision is made under the Constitution. Originally, **Article 314** guaranteed that members of the former **Indian Civil Service (ICS)** appointed by the Secretary of State for India would continue to enjoy their colonial-era conditions of service regarding remuneration, leave, pension, and disciplinary privileges. Viewing these entrenched colonial privileges as incompatible with egalitarian constitutionalism, Parliament enacted the **28th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972**, which **repealed Article 314** and inserted **Article 312A**, empowering Parliament by law to vary or revoke, prospectively or retrospectively, the conditions of service of former Secretary of State officers while barring court jurisdiction over disputes arising out of pre-1947 service covenants.

## II. Constitutional Recruitment Watchdogs: The Public Service Commissions (Part XIV, Chapter II, Articles 315–323)

### 4. Institutional Architecture, Appointment, and Federal Asymmetry in Removal (Articles 315–323)

To prevent the permanent civil service from degenerating into an instrument of political patronage or nepotism, **Chapter II of Part XIV** (**Articles 315–323**) establishes independent constitutional recruitment watchdogs—the **Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)** and the **State Public Service Commissions (SPSCs)**. Operating as the institutional guardian of the merit principle enshrined in **Article 16(1)**, the Public Service Commission architecture balances executive appointment power against ironclad security of tenure, judicialized removal filters, and strict post-retirement disqualifications.

#### Structural Typology: UPSC, SPSCs, and Joint State Public Service Commissions (Articles 315–316)

**Article 315(1)** mandates that there shall be a **Public Service Commission for the Union** and a **Public Service Commission for each State**. Alongside these two permanent constitutional bodies, **Article 315(2)** provides for a third, flexible federal mechanism: if the Legislatures of **two or more States** pass resolutions to that effect, **Parliament may by law** provide for the appointment of a **Joint State Public Service Commission (JSPSC)** to serve the needs of those States (as historically enacted for Punjab and Haryana for a brief period in 1966). Thus, while the **UPSC** and **SPSCs** are direct creations of the Constitution, a **JSPSC** is a **statutory body** created by an Act of Parliament pursuant to state legislative resolutions, though once created it is governed by the constitutional safeguards of **Chapter II of Part XIV**. Additionally, under **Article 315(4)**, the **UPSC** may, with the approval of the **President**, agree to serve all or any of the needs of a State upon the request of the **Governor** of that State.

*   **Appointment and Composition (Article 316(1) & Article 318):** The Chairman and other members of the **UPSC** and of a **JSPSC** are appointed by the **President**, whereas the Chairman and other members of an **SPSC** are appointed by the **Governor** of the State. The Constitution does not fix the numerical strength of the Commissions, leaving the number of members and their conditions of service to be determined by the **President** (for UPSC/JSPSC) or the **Governor** (for SPSC) via regulations under **Article 318** (traditionally 9 to 11 members for the UPSC). Crucially, the **Proviso to Article 318** stipulates that the conditions of service of a member of a Public Service Commission **shall not be varied to their disadvantage after their appointment**.
*   **The Official-Experience Quota (Proviso to Article 316(1)):** While no specific educational qualifications are prescribed in the Constitution, the **Proviso to Article 316(1)** mandates that **as nearly as may be one-half** of the members of every Public Service Commission shall be persons who at the dates of their respective appointments have **held office for at least ten years** either under the Government of India or under the Government of a State. In **Jai Shankar Prasad v. State of Bihar (1993)**, the Supreme Court held that the phrase *"as nearly as may be one-half"* is directory rather than an arithmetic straitjacket, designed to blend seasoned administrative expertise with independent non-official perspectives. Furthermore, in **Mehar Singh Saini, In Re (2010)** and **State of Punjab v. Salil Sabhlok (2013)**, the Supreme Court ruled that although **Article 316** does not prescribe statutory qualifications for the non-official half, the institutional integrity of the Commission is an implied constitutional requirement, empowering High Courts under **Article 226** to review the appointment process if a State Government appoints a person lacking impeccable integrity or basic competence without a fair, deliberative selection mechanism.
*   **Tenure, Age Ceilings, and Acting Chairmen (Article 316(1A)–(3)):** A member of the **UPSC** holds office for a term of **six years** or until they attain the age of **65 years**, whichever is earlier. A member of an **SPSC** or a **JSPSC** holds office for a term of **six years** or until they attain the age of **62 years** (raised from 60 to 62 years by the **41st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**), whichever is earlier. Under **Article 316(1A)** (inserted by the **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963**), if the office of the Chairman becomes vacant or the Chairman is unable to perform their duties, the President (for UPSC/JSPSC) or the Governor (for SPSC) may appoint one of the other members to act as **Acting Chairman**. Under **Article 316(3)**, a person who holds office as a member of a Public Service Commission shall, on the expiration of their term of office, be **ineligible for re-appointment to that office** (i.e., cannot have a second term in the exact same post).

#### The Federal Asymmetry of Removal and the Article 317 Supreme Court Reference Filter

To shield **State Public Service Commissions** from coercion by State political executives, **Article 317** establishes a remarkable **federal asymmetry**: while the **Governor** appoints the Chairman and members of an **SPSC** under **Article 316(1)**, the Governor has **zero power to remove them**. Only the **President of India** can remove the Chairman or any member of the **UPSC**, a **JSPSC**, **OR an SPSC**, and strictly in the manner prescribed in **Article 317(1) and 317(3)**.

    1.  **Binding Nature of Supreme Court Advice:** Unlike a general Presidential Reference under **Article 143** (where the Supreme Court's advisory opinion is not strictly binding), the report of the Supreme Court following a disciplinary inquiry under **Article 317(1)** is **constitutionally binding on the President** (**In Re: Reference under Article 317(1), 1983**; **In Re: Mehar Singh Saini, Chairman, HPSC, 2010**).
    2.  **Deemed Misbehaviour (Article 317(4)):** A Chairman or member is deemed guilty of misbehaviour under **Article 317(4)** if they are or become in any way concerned or interested in any **contract or agreement** made by or on behalf of the Government of India or the Government of a State, or participate in any way in the profit thereof or in any benefit or emolument arising therefrom otherwise than as a member and in common with the other members of an incorporated company.
    3.  **Interim Suspension Pending Supreme Court Reference (Article 317(2)):** Neither the President nor the Governor possesses a standalone power to suspend a PSC member at will. *   **Pathway B—Direct Presidential Removal without Supreme Court Reference (Article 317(3)):** Notwithstanding **Article 317(1)**, the **President** may by order remove from office the Chairman or any other member of a Public Service Commission **without** a Supreme Court reference under three specific objective conditions in **Article 317(3)**: (a) if they are adjudged an **insolvent**; or (b) if they engage during their term of office in any **paid employment outside the duties of their office**; or (c) if they are, in the opinion of the President, unfit to continue in office by reason of **infirmity of mind or body**.

#### The Strict Post-Retirement Employment Bar (Article 319) and Constitutional Office Jurisprudence

To prevent sitting members of Public Service Commissions from currying favour with the Union or State Executive in anticipation of lucrative post-retirement sinecures, **Article 319** enforces an uncompromising matrix of post-retirement disqualifications, allowing upward or lateral movement **only** within the Public Service Commission architecture itself:

*   **The Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak (1979) 5-Judge Constitution Bench Ratio:** When Dr. Raghukul Tilak, a former member of the Rajasthan SPSC, was appointed as the **Governor of Rajasthan**, his appointment was challenged as violating **Article 319(d)**. A **5-Judge Constitution Bench** unanimously rejected the challenge, establishing a foundational distinction in Indian public law: the word *"employment"* in **Article 319** denotes a relationship of **master and servant** involving administrative subordination to the Government of India or the Government of a State. The office of the **Governor of a State** (and similarly high constitutional offices like Supreme Court or High Court Judges or the President/Vice-President) is an **independent constitutional office** that is not subordinate or subservient to the Government of India; hence, appointing a former SPSC member as a Governor does not violate **Article 319**.

#### The Advisory Mandate of Article 320, Directory Jurisprudence, and Parliamentary Accountability (Articles 320–323)

**Article 320** delineates the operational domain of the **UPSC** and **SPSCs** across two distinct vectors: **executory examination functions** (**Article 320(1)–(2)**) and **advisory consultation functions** (**Article 320(3)**).

*   **Examinations and Joint Recruitment (Article 320(1)–(2)):** Under **Article 320(1)**, it is the duty of the Union and State Public Service Commissions to conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the Union and the services of the State respectively. Under **Article 320(2)**, it is also the duty of the **UPSC**, if requested by any **two or more States**, to assist those States in framing and operating schemes of joint recruitment for any services for which candidates possessing special qualifications are required.
*   **The Five Mandatory Consultation Heads (Article 320(3)):** The **UPSC** or the **SPSC**, as the case may be, **shall be consulted** on:
    1.  All matters relating to **methods of recruitment** to civil services and for civil posts (**Clause (a)**);
    2.  The **principles to be followed** in making appointments to civil services and posts and in making **promotions and transfers** from one service to another, and on the **suitability of candidates** for such appointments, promotions, or transfers (**Clause (b)**);
    3.  All **disciplinary matters** affecting a person serving under the Government of India or the Government of a State in a civil capacity, including memorials or petitions relating to such matters (**Clause (c)**);
    4.  Any claim by or in respect of a current or former civil servant that any **costs incurred by them in defending legal proceedings** instituted against them in respect of acts done or purporting to be done in the execution of their duty should be paid out of the **Consolidated Fund of India** or of the **State** (**Clause (d)**); and
    5.  Any claim for the award of a **pension in respect of injuries** sustained by a person while serving under the Government of India or a State in a civil capacity, and any question as to the amount of any such award (**Clause (e)**).
*   **The Directory Nature of Article 320(3) Consultation (*State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava, 1957*):** What is the legal consequence if the Union or a State Government dismisses a civil servant without consulting the Public Service Commission under **Article 320(3)(c)**, or rejects the Commission's recommendation? In the landmark **5-Judge Constitution Bench** decision in **State of U.P. v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava (1957)** (reaffirmed in **Ram Gopal Chaturvedi v. State of M.P., 1969**), the Supreme Court held that the provisions of **Article 320(3)** are **directory and not mandatory**. Unlike **Article 311**, which is couched in negative, prohibitive terms conferring an enforceable right on the civil servant, **Article 320(3)** neither confers any justiciable right on a public servant nor invalidates a disciplinary order merely for want of consultation with the Commission. Furthermore, because the Public Service Commission is strictly an **advisory body**, its recommendations on recruitment or disciplinary matters are **not binding** on the Government (**Jatinder Kumar v. State of Punjab, 1985**), though the Government cannot act arbitrarily or *mala fide* in rejecting the merit list.
*   **Constitutional Limitations and Statutory Extensions of Jurisdiction (Article 320(3) Proviso, Article 320(4), and Article 321):**
    1.  **Affirmative Action Exclusion (Article 320(4)):** Nothing in **Article 320(3)** shall require a Public Service Commission to be consulted as respects the manner in which any provision referred to in **Article 16(4)** (reservations for backward classes) may be made or the manner in which effect may be given to the provisions of **Article 335** (claims of SCs and STs to services and posts).
    2.  **Executive Exclusion Regulations (Proviso to Article 320(3)):** The **President** (for All-India and Central Services) and the **Governor** (for State Services) may make regulations specifying the matters in which, either generally or in any particular class of case, it **shall not be necessary for a Public Service Commission to be consulted** (such as Group C and Group D posts, or temporary appointments lasting less than one year). However, under **Article 320(5)**, all such exclusion regulations must be laid for not less than **14 days** before each House of Parliament or the State Legislature, which may amend or repeal them. Notably, following the establishment of the **Central Vigilance Commission (CVC)** in **1964**, overlapping jurisdiction arose in disciplinary vigilance cases, where the CVC exercises investigative oversight over corruption while the **UPSC** retains its constitutional consultative role under **Article 320(3)(c)** prior to the imposition of formal penalties on Group A officers.
    3.  **Extension of Functions (Article 321):** An Act made by **Parliament** or the **Legislature of a State** may provide for the exercise of **additional functions** by the **UPSC** or the **SPSC** as respects the services of the Union or the State, and also as respects the services of any **local authority or other body corporate constituted by law or of any public institution**.
*   **Fiscal Autonomy (Article 322) and the Parliamentary Accountability Shield (Article 323):** Under **Article 322**, the expenses of the **UPSC** or an **SPSC**—including any salaries, allowances, and pensions payable to or in respect of the members or staff of the Commission—shall be **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** or the **Consolidated Fund of the State** (making them non-votable in the legislature under **Articles 113(1) and 203(1)**). Finally, because **Article 320(3)** is directory in courts of law, the framers created a powerful **political accountability check in Article 323**: the **UPSC** presents an annual report to the **President**, and an **SPSC/JSPSC** presents an annual report to the **Governor**, who **shall cause a copy of the report to be laid before each House of Parliament or the State Legislature**, accompanied by a **memorandum explaining, as respects the cases, if any, where the advice of the Commission was not accepted, the reasons for such non-acceptance** (processed in the Union Government through the **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet - ACC**).

## III. Constitutional Commissions for Marginalized Sections and the Federal Identification Battle (Part XVI, Articles 338–342A)

### 6. The Tripartite Constitutional Watchdogs: NCSC, NCST, and NCBC (Articles 338, 338A, 338B)

**Part XVI** of the Constitution (**Articles 330–342A**, titled *"Special Provisions Relating to Certain Classes"*) institutionalizes substantive redistributive justice for historically oppressed and socially and educationally backward communities. Recognizing that constitutional guarantees of non-discrimination (**Articles 15–17**) and political representation (**Articles 330–334**) remain hollow without dedicated institutional oversight, the Constitution evolved from a single individual commissioner in 1950 into a tripartite architecture of specialized multi-member constitutional commissions endowed with civil court powers: the **National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC — Article 338)**, the **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST — Article 338A)**, and the **National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC — Article 338B)**.

#### Institutional Evolution from a Single Special Officer to the 65th, 89th, and 102nd Amendments

*   **Phase I (1950–1990)—The Single Special Officer:** Under the original text of **Article 338**, the President appointed a single **Special Officer for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** (designated as the Commissioner for SCs and STs) to investigate all matters relating to the constitutional safeguards for SCs and STs (and such other backward classes as the President might specify on the report of the **Article 340** Commission, as well as the **Anglo-Indian community** under **Article 338(3)**).
*   **Phase II (1990–2003)—The 65th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1990:** Finding a single officer structurally inadequate to monitor atrocities and socio-economic deprivation across the subcontinent, Parliament enacted the **65th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1990**, replacing the single Special Officer with a multi-member **National Commission for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** under **Article 338**.
*   **Phase III (2003–Present)—The 89th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003:** Recognizing that the challenges confronting **Scheduled Castes** (rooted in ritual untouchability and social segregation across mainland villages) are structurally distinct from those confronting **Scheduled Tribes** (rooted in geographical isolation, forest displacement, customary land alienation, and **Fifth/Sixth Schedule** governance), the **89th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003** (effective February 19, 2004) bifurcated the combined Commission into:
    1.  The **National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC)** under **Article 338** (which also retained residual oversight over the **Anglo-Indian community** under **Article 338(10)**); and
    2.  The **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST)** under the newly inserted **Article 338A**. Furthermore, under **Article 339(1)**, the President may at any time (and was required at the expiration of 10 years from commencement—the **Dhebar Commission, 1960**, followed by the **Bhuria Commission, 2002**) appoint a Commission to report on the administration of the **Scheduled Areas** and the welfare of the **Scheduled Tribes** in the States, while **Article 339(2)** empowers the Union Executive to give mandatory directions to a State regarding schemes essential for ST welfare.
*   **Phase IV (1992–2018)—From Article 340 and Indra Sawhney to the 102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018 (NCBC):** Originally, **Article 340** empowered the President to appoint an *ad hoc* Commission to investigate the conditions of **socially and educationally backward classes** (exercised twice: the **First Backward Classes Commission / Kaka Kalelkar Commission, 1953**, and the **Second Backward Classes Commission / B.P. Mandal Commission, 1979**). In the landmark **9-Judge Constitution Bench** judgment in **Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992)**, the Supreme Court directed the Union and State Governments to establish a permanent statutory body to examine complaints of over-inclusion and under-inclusion in the OBC lists. Parliament responded by enacting the **National Commission for Backward Classes Act, 1993**. However, because the 1993 statutory NCBC lacked the constitutional mandate of the **NCSC** to investigate individual complaints of atrocities or rights deprivation, Parliament enacted the **102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018**, repealing the 1993 Act and inserting **Article 338B** to constitute a constitutional **National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)**, while simultaneously stripping the **NCSC** (by amending **Article 338(10)**) of its former residual jurisdiction over Other Backward Classes.

#### Quasi-Judicial Civil Court Powers and Jurisdictional Boundaries

The internal architecture of **Articles 338, 338A, and 338B** is virtually identical across clauses **(1) to (9)**:

*   **Composition and Presidential Warrant (Clauses (2)–(3)):** Each Commission consists of a **Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, and three other Members** appointed by the **President by warrant under his hand and seal** (Elevating the appointment to a sovereign presidential warrant akin to Supreme Court Judges and the CAG, though their tenure—typically **three years**—and conditions of service are determined by Presidential rules under **Clause (2)**).
*   **Core Constitutional Duties (Clause (5)):** Each Commission is mandated to: (a) **investigate and monitor** all matters relating to the safeguards provided for the respective class (SCs / STs / SEBCs) under the Constitution or under any other law or order of the Government; (b) **inquire into specific complaints** with respect to the deprivation of rights and safeguards; (c) **participate and advise** on the planning process of socio-economic development and evaluate their progress under the Union and any State; (d) present an **annual report** to the **President** (which the President causes to be laid before Parliament under **Clause (6)**, or forwards to the **Governor** of a State under **Clause (7)** if it relates to a State Government, in both cases accompanied by an **Action Taken Memorandum** explaining the reasons for non-acceptance of any recommendation); and (e) discharge such other functions in relation to their protection, welfare, development, and advancement as the President may, subject to any Parliamentary law, by rule specify (under which the **NCST** was entrusted in 2005 with special functions regarding **minor forest produce**, mineral and water rights, livelihood of tribal communities, and relief against displacement).
*   **Limits of Civil Court Powers (*All India Indian Overseas Bank SC and ST Employees' Welfare Association v. Union of India, 1996*):** While **Clause (8)** of **Articles 338, 338A, and 338B** vests each Commission with all the powers of a **civil court trying a suit** (summoning witnesses, discovery of documents, receiving affidavits, requisitioning public records), the Supreme Court in **All India Indian Overseas Bank SC and ST Employees' Welfare Association v. Union of India (1996)** and **Collector v. Ajit Jogi (2011)** drew a sharp constitutional boundary. The phrase *"have all the powers of a civil court trying a suit"* is explicitly qualified by the words *"in respect of the following matters"* (**sub-clauses (a) to (f)**—purely procedural powers to gather evidence). The Commissions are **not** substantive civil courts or tribunals on the merits: they **cannot issue interim injunctions, stay promotions, direct the registration of FIRs as an executory court decree, or adjudicate service disputes** with binding coercive force. Their findings remain **recommendatory**, enforceable politically through the **Clauses (6)–(7)** parliamentary/legislative reporting mechanism.

### 7. The Federal Crisis of Backward Class Identification: From the 102nd to the 105th Constitutional Amendment (Article 342A)

While the designation of **Scheduled Castes (Article 341)** and **Scheduled Tribes (Article 342)** had always followed a centralized two-step constitutional procedure since 1950, the extension of that exact template to **Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs)** via **Article 342A** in the **102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018** ignited a major federal constitutional crisis between the Union and the States.

#### Presidential Notification Mechanics (Articles 341, 342, 342A) and the Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil (2021) Split

Under **Articles 341(1) and 342(1)**, the **President** may with respect to any State or Union Territory, and where it is a State, **after consultation with the Governor thereof**, by public notification, specify the castes, races, or tribes (or tribal communities) which shall for the purposes of the Constitution be deemed to be **Scheduled Castes** or **Scheduled Tribes** in relation to that State or Union Territory. Once the initial Presidential Notification is issued (such as the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950), **Articles 341(2) and 342(2)** impose an **absolute bar on executive modification**: *only* **Parliament may by law** include in or exclude from the list of SCs or STs any caste, race, or tribe, and a notification issued under Clause (1) *"shall not be varied by any subsequent notification."*

*   **Impermissibility of Executive or Judicial Tinkering with SC/ST Lists (*State of Maharashtra v. Milind, 2001*):** In the **5-Judge Constitution Bench** decision in **State of Maharashtra v. Milind (2001)** (reaffirmed in **Bir Singh v. Delhi Jal Board, 2018**), the Supreme Court held that Presidential SC/ST Orders under **Articles 341 and 342** must be read **strictly as written**. Neither a State Government (by executive circular or legislation) nor any Court or Tribunal possesses jurisdiction to hold an inquiry or let in evidence to declare that a particular sub-caste or synonym is included within an entry in the Presidential Order; only an **Act of Parliament under Articles 341(2)/342(2)** can alter the list. (Note: As established by the **7-Judge Constitution Bench** in **State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh, August 2024**, while States cannot add or delete castes from the **Article 341** list, States *can* sub-classify existing castes already within the **Article 341** list for equitable distribution of reservations under **Articles 15(4) and 16(4)** based on empirical data of inter-se backwardness, because sub-classification does not exclude any caste from the Presidential List).
*   **The 102nd Amendment and the Maratha Quota Constitutional Crisis (*Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, May 2021*):** The **102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018** inserted **Article 342A** (mirroring the language of **Articles 341 and 342**) and **Article 366(26C)** (defining "socially and educationally backward classes" as *"such backward classes as are so deemed under Article 342A for the purposes of this Constitution"*). When Maharashtra enacted the Socially and Educationally Backward Classes Act, 2018 granting reservations to the Maratha community, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** in **Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister (2021)** not only unanimously struck down the Maratha reservation for breaching the **50% ceiling of Indra Sawhney (1992)** without exceptional circumstances, but also split **3:2** on the federal meaning of **Article 342A**:
    1.  **The 3-Judge Majority (Justices S. Ravindra Bhat, L. Nageswara Rao, and Hemant Gupta):** Relying on the literal text of **Article 342A(1)–(2)** and **Article 366(26C)** (*"for the purposes of this Constitution"*), the majority held that just as **Articles 341 and 342** create a **single unified Presidential List** of SCs and STs for each State, the **102nd Amendment** abolished the dual central-and-state list system for OBCs and **stripped State Governments and State Legislatures of their power to identify and notify SEBCs**, vesting that power exclusively in the **President** (initially) and **Parliament** (subsequently).
    2.  **The 2-Judge Dissent (Justices Ashok Bhushan and S. Abdul Nazeer):** Relying on Parliamentary Select Committee reports and the Union Home Minister's assurance on the floor of Parliament during the passage of the 102nd Amendment, the minority argued that **Article 342A** was intended solely to govern the **Central List** of OBCs for Union employment/institutions, leaving State powers over State OBC lists untouched.

#### The 105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021 and the Restoration of Federal Sub-National Autonomy

Because the 3:2 majority ruling in *Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil (2021)* threatened to invalidate decades of State OBC lists—where roughly 671 caste communities existed in State OBC lists without appearing in the Central OBC List—Parliament acted with bipartisan urgency to enact the **105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021**, overriding the majority's textual construction and restoring cooperative federalism in backward class identification.

## IV. Official Language Jurisprudence, the Eighth Schedule, and Classical Languages (Part XVII, Articles 343–351)

### 8. The Munshi-Ayyangar Compromise and the Union-State Language Architecture (Articles 343–347)

No issue in the **Constituent Assembly** generated sharper existential polarization than the question of language—pitting proponents of **linguistic nationalism** (who demanded that Hindi in the Devanagari script immediately replace English as the sole "National Language" of a decolonized republic) against leaders from non-Hindi-speaking southern and eastern provinces (who warned that linguistic majoritarianism would disenfranchise non-Hindi citizens in public employment, legislation, and adjudication). Resolved in September 1949 through the historic **Munshi-Ayyangar Formula** (drafted by **K.M. Munshi** and **N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar**), **Part XVII** of the Constitution (**Articles 343–351**) deliberately **rejects the concept of a single "National Language"**, establishing instead a pragmatic architecture of an **Official Language of the Union** paired with plurilingual regional autonomy and judicial English supremacy.

#### Union Official Language Mechanics (Articles 343–344) and the Official Languages Act, 1963/1967

**Chapter I of Part XVII** (**Articles 343–344**) regulates the language of the Union through a phased, revisable transition mechanism:

*   **Script and Numeral Compromise (Article 343(1)):** Under **Article 343(1)**, the **official language of the Union** shall be **Hindi in Devanagari script**, while the form of numerals to be used for the official purposes of the Union shall be the **international form of Indian numerals** (a crucial Munshi-Ayyangar concession to southern states over Devanagari numerals).
*   **The 15-Year Sunset and Parliamentary Extension Power (Article 343(2)–(3)):** **Article 343(2)** stipulated that for a period of **fifteen years** from the commencement of the Constitution (i.e., until January 26, 1965), the **English language** shall continue to be used for all the official purposes of the Union for which it was being used immediately before such commencement. However, anticipating that a hard cutoff in 1965 could fracture national unity, **Article 343(3)** empowered **Parliament by law** to provide for the continued use of the **English language**, or the Devanagari form of numerals, for such purposes as may be specified in the law even **after the said period of fifteen years**.
*   **The Article 344 Commission and Parliamentary Committee:** Under **Article 344(1)**, the President was required at the expiration of five years from commencement (and ten years) to constitute a **Commission on Official Language** consisting of a Chairman and members representing the different languages specified in the **Eighth Schedule**. The first Commission was appointed in **1955** under the chairmanship of **B.G. Kher**, and its report was examined under **Article 344(4)** by a **30-member Joint Parliamentary Committee** (**20 from Lok Sabha and 10 from Rajya Sabha** elected by proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote) chaired by **Govind Ballabh Pant** in **1957** (note that the second Commission scheduled for 1960 was never appointed). Crucially, **Article 344(3)** mandates that in making its recommendations, the Commission **shall have due regard to the industrial, cultural and scientific advancement of India, and the just claims and the interests of persons belonging to the non-Hindi speaking areas in regard to the public services**.
*   **The Official Languages Act, 1963 and the 1967 Amendment Guarantee:** Acting under **Article 343(3)** and Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's assurance to non-Hindi speakers, Parliament enacted the **Official Languages Act, 1963**, providing that the English language **may** continue to be used, in addition to Hindi, for all the official purposes of the Union and for the transaction of business in Parliament even after January 26, 1965. Following intense linguistic agitations in Madras (Tamil Nadu) in 1965 over whether "may" allowed executive phasing out of English, Parliament enacted the **Official Languages (Amendment) Act, 1967**, which:
    1.  Mandated that **English shall be used** for purposes of communication between the Union and a State which has **not** adopted Hindi as its official language;
    2.  Mandated bilingualism (both Hindi and English) for specified central administrative instruments (resolutions, general orders, rules, notifications, administrative reports, parliamentary papers, contracts, and licenses); and
    3.  Enacted a **statutory non-Hindi State veto**: the provisions mandating the continued use of English shall remain in force **until resolutions for the discontinuance of the use of the English language have been passed by the Legislatures of all the States which have not adopted Hindi as their official language**, and after considering those resolutions, a resolution for such discontinuance has been passed by each House of Parliament. Under this Act, the **Official Languages Rules, 1976** classify States and UTs into **Region A** (Hindi-speaking states), **Region B** (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Punjab, and Chandigarh/DNHDD), and **Region C** (all other non-Hindi states, where correspondence from the Union is conducted in English), while explicitly **exempting the State of Tamil Nadu** from the 1976 Rules.

#### Regional Plurilingualism and Presidential Recognition of Minority State Languages (Articles 345–347)

**Chapter II of Part XVII** (**Articles 345–347**) grants wide linguistic autonomy to the constituent States:

*   **State Official Language Choice Not Restricted to the Eighth Schedule (Article 345):** Subject to **Articles 346 and 347**, the **Legislature of a State may by law** adopt **any one or more of the languages in use in the State** or **Hindi** as the language or languages to be used for all or any of the official purposes of that State (until the State Legislature so provides by law, English continues to be used). Crucially, a State Legislature is **not** confined to the languages listed in the **Eighth Schedule** when adopting an official language under **Article 345** (for instance, **Mizoram** has adopted Mizo, **Meghalaya** has adopted Khasi and Garo as associate official languages, **Nagaland** and **Arunachal Pradesh** use English, and **Sikkim** recognizes multiple local tribal languages not in the Eighth Schedule). In **Uttar Pradesh Hindi Sahitya Sammelan v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2014)**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** upheld the constitutional competence of a State Legislature under **Article 345** to adopt a **second official language** (such as Urdu in Uttar Pradesh) for specified official purposes across the State.
*   **Inter-State and Union-State Communication (Article 346):** The language for the time being authorized for use in the Union for official purposes shall be the official language for communication between one State and another State and between a State and the Union, provided that if **two or more States agree** that the **Hindi language** should be the official language for communication between such States, that language may be used for such communication.
*   **Presidential Protection for Intra-State Linguistic Minorities (Article 347):** To prevent a State majority from suppressing a substantial linguistic minority within its borders, **Article 347** provides that on a demand being made in that behalf, the **President** may, if he is satisfied that a **substantial proportion of the population of a State** desire the use of any language spoken by them to be recognised by that State, **direct that such language shall also be officially recognised throughout that State or any part thereof** for such purpose as he may specify.

### 9. Judicial Language Supremacy, Linguistic Minority Safeguards, and Cultural Directives (Articles 348–351)

Ved Murti (1970)**, when an intervenor insisted on arguing a Habeas Corpus petition in Hindi before the Supreme Court despite opposing counsel and several judges on the Bench not knowing Hindi, the Constitution Bench cancelled his intervention, holding that **Article 348(1)(a)** makes **English the sole constitutional language of the Supreme Court** until Parliament enacts a law to the contrary. (Note: Recent Supreme Court digital initiatives providing AI-assisted translations of Supreme Court judgments into scheduled languages—**e-SCR**—are purely informational accessibility aids and do not alter the constitutional requirement of **Article 348(1)(a)**).
*   **High Court Proceedings vs. Judgments, Decrees, and Orders (Article 348(2) & Section 7 of the 1963 Act):** Under **Article 348(2)**, the Governors of **Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and Bihar** (with the previous consent of the President) have authorized the use of **Hindi** in proceedings of the Rajasthan, Allahabad, Madhya Pradesh, and Patna High Courts respectively (whereas similar requests by Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, and Chhattisgarh for Tamil, Gujarati, and Hindi were declined by the Union Executive on the advice of the **Full Court of the Supreme Court** to preserve the pan-Indian transferability of High Court Judges under **Article 222**). Crucially, the **Proviso to Article 348(2)** bars this exception from applying to any **judgment, decree, or order** passed by such High Court, though **Section 7 of the Official Languages Act, 1963** permits the Governor (with Presidential consent) to authorize the optional use of Hindi or the State official language in High Court judgments, decrees, or orders **provided they are accompanied by an English translation** issued under the authority of the High Court.
*   **Resolution of Statutory Conflicts (Article 348(3)):** Where a State Legislature enacts a statute in Hindi or a regional language and the Governor publishes its authoritative English translation under **Article 348(3)**, the Supreme Court has consistently ruled (**J.K. Jute Mills Co. Ltd. v. State of U.P., 1961**; **Nityanand Sharma v. State of Bihar, 1996**; **Prabhat Kumar Sharma v. UPSC, 2006**) that if there is an irreconcilable ambiguity or conflict between the regional/Hindi version and the English version published under **Article 348(3)**, the **English authoritative text prevails** by command of **Article 348(1)(b)** and **(3)** (whereas for Central Acts enacted originally in English, a Hindi translation published by the President under the **Authoritative Texts (Central Laws) Act, 1973** yields to the original English enactment under **Article 348(1)(b)**). Finally, **Article 349** provided a 15-year procedural safeguard requiring the **previous sanction of the President** (after considering the **Article 344** Commission and Committee reports) before any Bill or amendment altering the language regime of **Article 348(1)** could be introduced in Parliament.

#### Linguistic Minority Protections (Articles 350–350B) and the Directive for Hindi Development (Article 351)

Following the linguistic reorganization of States recommended by the **Fazl Ali States Reorganisation Commission (1955)**, Parliament enacted the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956** to insert **Articles 350A and 350B** into **Chapter IV of Part XVII** (*"Special Directives"*, **Articles 350–351**), creating an institutional safety net for linguistic minorities residing within linguistically reorganized States:

*   **Right to Submit Grievance Representations in Any Language (Article 350):** Every person is entitled to submit a representation for the redress of any grievance to any officer or authority of the Union or a State **in any of the languages used in the Union or in the State**, as the case may be. Unlike **Article 343** or **Article 345**, **Article 350** is not restricted to the official language of the Union or the State or even to the **Eighth Schedule**: if a language is actually spoken and "used" by inhabitants within that State, a citizen cannot be denied the right to submit a grievance petition in that language.
*   **Instruction in Mother-Tongue at Primary Stage (Article 350A):** Inserted by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**, **Article 350A** directs that it shall be the endeavour of every State and of every local authority within the State to provide **adequate facilities for instruction in the mother-tongue at the primary stage of education to children belonging to linguistic minority groups**; and the **President** may issue such directions to any State as he considers necessary or proper for securing the provision of such facilities. However, in the landmark **5-Judge Constitution Bench** decision in **State of Karnataka v. Associated Management of (Government Recognised – Unaided – English Medium) Primary and Secondary Schools (2014)**, the Supreme Court held that while **Article 350A** obligates the State to *provide facilities* for mother-tongue instruction, a State Government **cannot compel** parents and students to study only in their mother tongue or regional language at the primary stage, as the choice of medium of instruction by a child/parent is protected as part of the **freedom of expression under Article 19(1)(a)** (and for minority institutions under **Article 30(1)**).
*   **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Article 350B):** Also inserted by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**, **Article 350B(1)** mandates that there shall be a **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities** to be appointed by the **President** (designated as the **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities**, established in **1957** with headquarters at Prayagraj/Allahabad and operating under the **Ministry of Minority Affairs**). Under **Article 350B(2)**, it is the duty of the Special Officer to investigate all matters relating to the safeguards provided for linguistic minorities under the Constitution and report to the **President** at such intervals as the President may direct, and the President shall cause all such reports to be laid before each House of **Parliament** and sent to the Governments of the **States** concerned. Notice that the Constitution itself **does not prescribe the qualifications, tenure, salaries, or removal procedure** for the Special Officer under **Article 350B**.
*   **Directive for Development of the Hindi Language (Article 351):** Concluding **Part XVII**, **Article 351** issues a cultural directive to the Union: it shall be the duty of the Union to promote the spread of the **Hindi language**, to develop it so that it may serve as a **medium of expression for all the elements of the composite culture of India**, and to secure its enrichment by assimilating without interfering with its genius, the forms, style and expressions used in **Hindustani and in the other languages of India specified in the Eighth Schedule**, and by drawing, wherever necessary or desirable, for its vocabulary, **primarily on Sanskrit and secondarily on other languages**.

### 10. The Eighth Schedule Evolution and the October 2024 Classical Languages Architecture

Beyond the operational framework of **Articles 343–351**, India's linguistic constitutionalism rests on two distinct tiers of symbolic and material recognition: the **Eighth Schedule** of the Constitution (comprising **22 Scheduled Languages**) and the executive classification of **Classical Languages of India** (expanded by the Union Cabinet in **October 2024** from 6 to **11 Classical Languages**).

#### Constitutional Politics and Statutory Effects of the Eighth Schedule (22 Languages)

Explicitly cross-referenced in **Article 344(1)** (representation on the Official Language Commission) and **Article 351** (sources for enriching the vocabulary of Hindi), the **Eighth Schedule** originally contained **14 languages** in 1950, and was subsequently expanded to **22 languages** through four Constitutional Amendment Acts under **Article 368**:

*   **Constitutional and Administrative Consequences of Eighth Schedule Inclusion:**
    1.  **Constitutional Textual Links:** Representation on the **Official Language Commission under Article 344(1)** and mandatory inclusion among the linguistic sources used by the Union to enrich the vocabulary and forms of **Hindi under Article 351**.
    2.  **Civil Services Examination Parity:** Pursuant to the **Official Language Resolution of 1968** adopted by both Houses of Parliament, candidates appearing in the **All-India and Higher Central Services Examinations** conducted by the **UPSC** are entitled to opt for any of the **22 languages in the Eighth Schedule** (in addition to English) as an alternative medium for writing the examination.
    3.  **Institutional & Currency Recognition:** Recognition by the **Sahitya Akademi** (which recognizes all 22 Eighth Schedule languages plus **English** and **Rajasthani**—totaling 24), translation facilities in Parliamentary debates, and inclusion on the language panel of **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** currency notes (which feature 15 languages on the reverse panel alongside Hindi and English on the face).
    4.  **Absence of Objective Constitutional Criteria:** Neither **Articles 344(1) and 351** nor the **Eighth Schedule** prescribes objective linguistic or demographic criteria for including a language in the Eighth Schedule. Although the Union Government appointed the **Pahwa Committee (1996)** and the **Sitakant Mohapatra Committee (2003)** to evolve objective criteria for examining pending demands from 38 more languages (such as Bhojpuri, Rajasthani, Tulu, Gondi, Garhwali, Kumaoni, and Ho), no fixed statutory criteria have been enacted to date.

#### The October 2024 Expansion of Classical Languages to 11 and the Revised Linguistic Criteria

Unlike the **Eighth Schedule**, the category of **"Classical Languages of India"** is not mentioned in the text of the Constitution; it was created by an executive notification of the **Ministry of Culture** (in consultation with the **Ministry of Home Affairs** and the **Linguistic Experts Committee (LEC)** constituted under the **Sahitya Akademi**) in **October 2004** to preserve, document, and institutionalize India's ancient linguistic heritage.

*   **The October 3, 2024 Watershed Expansion (From 6 to 11 Classical Languages):** Between 2004 and 2014, six languages—**Tamil (2004)**, **Sanskrit (2005)**, **Kannada (2008)**, **Telugu (2008)**, **Malayalam (2013)**, and **Odia (2014)**—were accorded Classical Language status. On **October 3, 2024**, the **Union Cabinet** approved the conferment of **Classical Language** status on **five additional languages**: **Marathi**, **Pali**, **Prakrit**, **Assamese**, and **Bengali**, bringing the total number of **Classical Languages of India to 11**.
*   **Crucial Non-Overlap with the Eighth Schedule (Pali and Prakrit):** Whereas the first nine Classical Languages (**Tamil, Sanskrit, Kannada, Telugu, Malayalam, Odia, Marathi, Assamese, and Bengali**) are all listed in the **Eighth Schedule**, **Pali** (the canonical liturgical language of Theravada Buddhism and Ashokan-era Magadhan discourse) and **Prakrit** (the vernacular literary and epigraphical language of Jainism, Satavahana inscriptions, and classical Sanskrit drama) are **NOT listed in the Eighth Schedule**. Thus, the **October 2024 notification** proves that inclusion in the **Eighth Schedule** is **not a condition precedent** for designation as a **Classical Language of India**.
*   **The Revised 2024 Linguistic Experts Committee (LEC) Criteria:** In **July 2024**, the **Linguistic Experts Committee (LEC)** under the **Sahitya Akademi** revised the 2005 criteria for Classical Language designation, which formed the basis for the **October 2024 Cabinet approval**:
    1.  **High Antiquity:** High antiquity of its early texts / recorded history over a period of **1500–2000 years**;
    2.  **Ancient Literary Heritage:** A body of **ancient literature/texts**, which is considered a **heritage by generations of speakers**;
    3.  **Knowledge Texts (Prose + Poetry) and Epigraphical Evidence (New 2024 Addition):** Presence of **knowledge texts, especially prose texts in addition to poetry, epigraphical and inscriptional evidence** (replacing the much-debated 2005 criterion that the literary tradition *"be original and not borrowed from another speech community"*, which had previously stalled Indo-Aryan daughter languages like Marathi, Bengali, and Assamese); and
    4.  **Distinctness from Modern Form:** The Classical Languages and literature could be **distinct from its current form** or could be **discontinuous with later forms of its offshoots**.
*   **Institutional Benefits of Classical Language Designation:** Executed primarily by the **Ministry of Education**, designation as a Classical Language triggers: (1) **two major annual international awards** for scholars of eminence in the concerned classical language; (2) establishment of a **Centre of Excellence for Studies in the Classical Language** (such as the Central Institute of Classical Tamil in Chennai); and (3) a request to the **University Grants Commission (UGC)** to create **Professional Chairs** for the Classical Language, at least in Central Universities.
