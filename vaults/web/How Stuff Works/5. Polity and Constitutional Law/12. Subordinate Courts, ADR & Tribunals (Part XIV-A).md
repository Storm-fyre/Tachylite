# Part 12: The Subordinate Judiciary, Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR), and the Tribunalization Regime (Part XIV-A)

## I. Constitutional Architecture of the Subordinate Judiciary (Part VI, Chapter VI: Articles 233–237)

### 1. The Dual-Control Federal Equilibrium: Executive Appointment vs. High Court Administrative Supremacy (Articles 233–237)

The constitutional design of the subordinate judiciary in **Part VI, Chapter VI (Articles 233 to 237)** resolves a foundational institutional tension between **formal executive sovereignty in state-level appointments** and **substantive judicial independence at the grassroots trial tier**. Unlike the American federal model, which bifurcates federal and state court hierarchies, the Indian Constitution establishes a single, integrated judicial pyramid where the subordinate judiciary processes the vast majority of civil, criminal, and statutory litigation. Recognizing that the rule of law at the trial level is acutely vulnerable to local political patronage and executive coercion, the Constituent Assembly engineered a bifurcated governance mechanism: while formal orders of initial appointment and cadre rule-making are issued in the name of the **Governor** (**Articles 233 and 234**), the substantive power of recommendation, administrative superintendence, disciplinary inquiry, posting, and promotion is vested exclusively in the **High Court** (**Article 235**), giving operational tooth to the Directive Principle of separation of the judiciary from the executive under **Article 50**.

#### Diagnostic Unpacking of Constitutional Provisions and Judicial Ratios

*   **Article 233 (Appointment of District Judges — The Dual Entry Channel):** **Article 233(1)** stipulates that appointments, postings, and promotions of District Judges in any State shall be made by the Governor of the State *in consultation with the High Court* exercising jurisdiction in relation to such State. Through the landmark rulings in **Chandra Mohan v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1966)** and **MM Gupta v. State of Jammu & Kashmir (1982)**, the Supreme Court established that "consultation" under **Article 233(1)** is not a formal executive courtesy; the High Court is the sole constitutional repository capable of assessing judicial suitability, and consultation must be effective, meaningful, and accorded institutional primacy. Furthermore, in **All India Judges Association v. Union of India (2002)**, the Court bifurcated recruitment to the Higher Judicial Service (District Judge cadre) into three distinct streams: **50% by promotion from Civil Judges (Senior Division)** on the basis of merit-cum-seniority, **25% by accelerated promotion (Limited Competitive Examination)** from Civil Judges (Senior Division) with at least five years of qualifying service, and **25% by direct recruitment from the Bar**.
*   **Article 233(2) and the *Dheeraj Mor (2020)* Continuous Practice Rule:** **Article 233(2)** governs direct recruitment from the Bar, providing that a person not already in the service of the Union or of the State shall only be eligible to be appointed a District Judge if he has been for not less than **seven years an advocate or a pleader** and is recommended by the High Court for appointment. For decades, in-service Civil Judges who had practiced as advocates for seven years prior to joining the subordinate judicial service attempted to compete under the 25% Bar quota for direct elevation as District Judges. In **Dheeraj Mor v. High Court of Delhi (2020)**, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court settled the interpretation of **Article 233(2)** by establishing a strict exclusionary barrier between the two channels:
    *   **The Bar vs. Service Dichotomy:** **Article 233** creates two mutually exclusive sources of recruitment—in-service judicial officers under **Article 233(1)** and practicing advocates under **Article 233(2)**.
    *   **The Continuity Requirement:** To be eligible under **Article 233(2)**, a candidate must not merely possess an historical aggregate of seven years of past legal practice; the candidate must be a **continuing practicing advocate on the date of application as well as on the date of appointment**. Once an advocate enters the judicial service of the Union or State, they cease to be eligible for the 25% Bar quota and can only ascend to the District Judge cadre via the 50% regular promotion or 25% Limited Departmental Competitive Examination channels (a constitutional threshold subsequently referred for re-examination before a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in *Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa, 2025*).
*   **Article 233A (Validation of Certain Appointments):** Inserted by the **20th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1966** in the wake of the Supreme Court's invalidation of several Uttar Pradesh District Judge appointments in *Chandra Mohan (1966)*, **Article 233A** retrospectively validated past appointments, postings, promotions, and transfers of District Judges made prior to 1966 that had failed to strictly comply with the procedural mandates of **Articles 233 or 235**.
*   **Article 234 (Recruitment of Persons Other Than District Judges):** Appointments of persons other than District Judges to the judicial service of a State (i.e., **Civil Judges of the Junior and Senior Divisions**) are made by the Governor in accordance with rules made by him in that behalf after consultation with the **State Public Service Commission (SPSC)** and with the **High Court** of the State. While the SPSC or the High Court itself conducts the competitive examination (popularly termed the PCS-J examination), the Supreme Court held in **Bihar Public Service Commission v. Sanjay Kumar Singh (2003)** and **State of Bihar v. Bal Mukund Sah (2000)** that the High Court's constitutional consultation is paramount regarding recruitment standards, and general state reservation statutes cannot override High Court rules without its concurrence.
*   **Article 235 (Plenary High Court Control Over Subordinate Courts):** **Article 235** is the constitutional linchpin of trial-court independence. It vests the "control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto"—including the posting and promotion of, and the grant of leave to, persons belonging to the judicial service of a State and holding any post inferior to the post of District Judge—exclusively in the **High Court**.
    *   **Exclusivity of Disciplinary Jurisdiction:** In **State of West Bengal v. Nripendra Nath Bagchi (1966)** and the 7-Judge Constitution Bench in **Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)**, the Supreme Court held that the word "control" in **Article 235** encompasses complete disciplinary jurisdiction over both District Judges and subordinate judges.
    *   **Bar on Executive Investigation:** The State Executive cannot initiate a disciplinary inquiry or ask the State Vigilance Department/CID to investigate a judicial officer without the explicit permission and direction of the High Court. While formal orders of dismissal, removal, reduction in rank, or compulsory retirement of a District Judge must be issued by the Governor (as the appointing authority under **Article 233**, satisfying **Article 311(1)**), the Governor is constitutionally bound by the recommendation of the High Court (**Registrar, High Court of Madras v. R. Rajiah, 1988**).
*   **Articles 236 and 237 (Interpretative Definitions and Magisterial Integration):** **Article 236(a)** defines the expression "District Judge" expansively to include judges of a city civil court, additional district judge, joint district judge, assistant district judge, chief judge of a small cause court, chief presidency magistrate, additional chief presidency magistrate, sessions judge, additional sessions judge, and assistant sessions judge. **Article 236(b)** defines "judicial service" as a service consisting exclusively of persons intended to fill the post of District Judge and other civil judicial posts inferior to the post of District Judge. Finally, **Article 237** empowers the Governor, by public notification, to direct that the provisions of Chapter VI and rules made thereunder shall apply to any class or classes of **Magistrates** in the State, thereby serving as the constitutional bridge that enabled the complete separation of the criminal trial magistracy from the executive revenue administration across India.

### 2. The All-India Judicial Service (AIJS) under Article 312(3): Centralization vs. Federal-Linguistic Autonomy

The constitutional debate over the creation of an **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** exposes a deep structural fault line between **centralized meritocratic standardization** and **federal-linguistic judicial autonomy**. Modeled on the All-India Services (IAS and IPS), the proposal seeks to establish a pan-India recruitment framework for the entry-level Higher Judicial Service (District Judges) to attract premier legal talent and rectify persistent judicial vacancies across states. However, because the subordinate judiciary functions in vernacular languages and operates under the exclusive constitutional guardianship of state High Courts (**Articles 233 and 235**), the AIJS has triggered enduring federal resistance from both State Governments and multiple High Courts.

#### Constitutional Mechanics and the Federal Impasse

*   **The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 Architecture:** Originally, **Article 312** contemplated only administrative and police All-India Services. Following the recommendations of the **14th Law Commission Report (1958, chaired by M.C. Setalvad)** and the **Chief Justices' Conferences (1961, 1963, 1965)**, the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** amended **Article 312(1)** to empower Parliament to create an **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** common to the Union and the States, provided the **Rajya Sabha** passes a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest to do so.
*   **Constitutional Floor and Non-Obstante Override (Articles 312(3) and 312(4)):** To prevent central encroachment into the lowest rungs of the state trial judiciary, **Article 312(3)** imposes an explicit constitutional floor: *the All-India Judicial Service shall not include any post inferior to that of a District Judge as defined in Article 236*. Simultaneously, **Article 312(4)** provides a powerful structural bypass: the parliamentary statute creating the AIJS may contain any provisions necessary for amending **Chapter VI of Part VI (Articles 233–237)**, and no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of **Article 368**. Thus, Parliament can alter the recruitment mechanics of **Article 233** by a simple legislative majority once armed with a **Rajya Sabha resolution under Article 312(1)**.
*   **Judicial Endorsement vs. Federal-Linguistic Ground Realities:** While the **116th Law Commission Report (1986)** and the Supreme Court in **All India Judges Association v. Union of India (1992 and 1993 review)** strongly directed the Union to establish the AIJS to attract brilliant young law graduates and eliminate state-level recruitment delays, the proposal remains deadlocked due to four structural counter-arguments:
    1.  **Erosion of High Court Primacy (Articles 233 and 235):** Shifting recruitment of District Judges to a central agency (such as the UPSC or a National Judicial Commission) severs the organic link between High Court supervision and entry into the Higher Judicial Service, undermining the federal balance of **Part VI**.
    2.  **The Vernacular Evidentiary Imperative:** Unlike the High Courts and Supreme Court, which operate in English under **Article 348**, District and Sessions Courts are courts of first-instance trial where witness cross-examinations, dying declarations, police case diaries, and centuries-old vernacular land/revenue records are examined in the local language of the State (**Section 307 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023**). An outsider officer with only conversational training in a regional language faces severe handicap in appreciating nuances of oral testimony.
    3.  **Demoralization of the State Subordinate Judiciary:** Because **Article 312(3)** bars AIJS below the rank of District Judge, all AIJS recruits would enter directly as District Judges, severely shrinking the promotional quota (currently 75% combined under *All India Judges Association, 2002*) available to state Civil Judges recruited under **Article 234**.
    4.  **Divergence in State Affirmative Action Rosters:** State judicial services implement state-specific reservation rosters for SCs, STs, OBCs, women, and domicile categories, which clash with a uniform central reservation roster.

### 3. The Post-July 2024 Criminal and Civil Trial Hierarchy: BNSS, BNS, and BSA (2023) Procedural Overhaul

On **July 1, 2024**, the statutory architecture governing the subordinate criminal trial judiciary underwent its most radical structural transformation since colonial codification, as the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023**, the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023**, and the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023** replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure (1973), the Indian Penal Code (1860), and the Indian Evidence Act (1872). This legislative overhaul re-engineers the balance between **state investigative efficiency** and **procedural due process under Article 21**, abolishing colonial urban-rural judicial distinctions while imposing mandatory technological, forensic, and temporal constraints on police investigation and magisterial adjudication.

#### Structural Transformations in Trial Hierarchy and Procedure

*   **Abolition of Metropolitan Magistrates and Assistant Sessions Judges (BNSS Section 6):** Under the erstwhile CrPC 1973, cities with a population exceeding one million were designated as "Metropolitan Areas" with separate cadres of Chief Metropolitan Magistrates (CMM) and Metropolitan Magistrates. **Section 6 of the BNSS, 2023** completely abolishes the colonial category of Metropolitan Areas and Metropolitan Magistrates, creating a uniform, pan-India four-tier criminal court hierarchy below the High Court: **(i) Courts of Session, (ii) Judicial Magistrates of the First Class (JMFC, including the Chief Judicial Magistrate - CJM), (iii) Judicial Magistrates of the Second Class (JMSC), and (iv) Executive Magistrates**. Furthermore, the BNSS abolishes the post of **Assistant Sessions Judge** (who previously had sentencing power up to 10 years), streamlining trial jurisdiction between the Sessions Judge/Additional Sessions Judge and the CJM/JMFC, while enhancing the fine limits of JMFCs (from ₹10,000 to **₹50,000**) and JMSCs (from ₹5,000 to **₹10,000**) and empowering Magistrates to impose **Community Service** (**Section 4 of BNS, 2023** read with **Section 23 of BNSS**) as a restorative punishment for petty offences.
*   **Zero FIR and e-FIR Statutory Mandate (BNSS Section 173):** Codifying the jurisprudence of *Lalita Kumari v. Government of UP (2014)*, **Section 173(1) of the BNSS** mandates that every information relating to the commission of a cognizable offence may be given orally or by **electronic communication (e-FIR)**, **irrespective of the area where the offence is committed (Zero FIR)**. An e-FIR must be taken on record by the police officer upon being signed by the informant within **three days**. Furthermore, under **Section 173(3)**, for cognizable offences punishable with **3 years or more but less than 7 years**, the officer-in-charge may, with prior permission of a Deputy Superintendent of Police (DSP), conduct a **preliminary inquiry within 14 days** to ascertain whether a *prima facie* case exists before registering the FIR.
*   **Mandatory Forensic Investigation (BNSS Section 176(3)):** To shift Indian criminal trials from ocular and confession-heavy evidence toward scientific forensics, **Section 176(3) of the BNSS** mandates that upon receipt of information regarding an offence punishable with **seven years of imprisonment or more**, the officer-in-charge of the police station shall cause a **forensic expert to visit the crime scene** to collect forensic evidence and also record the process on a mobile phone or any other electronic device (subject to a maximum 5-year phased notification window for states to build forensic capacity).
*   **Mandatory Audio-Video Recording of Search and Seizure (BNSS Sections 105 and 185):** As an institutional check against police planting of evidence and custodial fabrication, **Section 105** and **Section 185(2) of the BNSS** mandate that the entire process of conducting any search of a place or seizure of property—including the preparation of the seizure list—**shall be recorded through any audio-video electronic means** (preferably a mobile phone), and the police officer shall **without delay forward such recording to the District Magistrate, Sub-Divisional Magistrate, or Judicial Magistrate of the First Class**.
*   **Statutory Trial Timelines, Trial in Absentia, and Undertrial Bail:** To combat chronic trial delays that violate the **Article 21** right to a speedy trial (*Hussainara Khatoon, 1979*):
    *   **60-Day Charge Framing (Section 251(1)(b) BNSS):** In Sessions trials, the Judge must frame charges within **60 days** from the date of the first hearing on charge, and the accused may apply for discharge within **60 days** from the date of committal (**Section 250**).
    *   **90-Day Supply of Police Papers and Committal (Sections 230 and 232 BNSS):** Copies of the police report, FIR, and statements must be supplied within **14 days** of production/appearance of the accused, and committal proceedings must be completed within **90 days** (extendable to 180 days).
    *   **Cap on Adjournments (Section 346 BNSS):** A trial court cannot grant more than **two adjournments** to a party even when circumstances are beyond their control.
    *   **30-to-45-Day Judgment Delivery (Section 258 BNSS):** After completion of arguments, the trial judge must give a judgment within **30 days**, extendable only up to **45 days** for specific reasons recorded in writing, and must upload the judgment on the court portal within **7 days** of pronouncement.
    *   **Trial in Absentia for Proclaimed Offenders (Section 356 BNSS):** If a proclaimed offender absconds to evade trial and there is no immediate prospect of arrest, the court may, after waiting **90 days** from the framing of charges, proceed with a **trial in absentia** and pronounce judgment as if the accused were present.
    *   **Relaxed Undertrial Bail Threshold (Section 479 BNSS):** Expanding upon old Section 436A CrPC, a **first-time offender** (never previously convicted of any offence) who has undergone detention for a period extending up to **one-third of the maximum period of imprisonment** specified for that offence (not punishable with death or life imprisonment) shall be released on bail by the Court, and the Jail Superintendent is placed under a statutory duty (**Section 479(3)**) to make a written application to the Court once the undertrial completes one-third or one-half of the sentence. However, **Section 479(2)** restricts bail where investigation, inquiry, or trial in more than one offence or in multiple cases are pending against a person.
    *   **Digital Evidence Parity (Section 63 BSA, 2023):** Replacing Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, **Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** integrates semiconductor memories, cloud servers, communication devices, and digital logs directly into primary/admissible electronic evidence, subject to a standardized two-part certification (Part A by the party and Part B by a digital expert).

### 4. Systemic Pathologies, Grassroots Access, and Institutional Infrastructure (NJDG, Gram Nyayalayas, e-Courts Phase III, and NJIAI)

Despite its robust constitutional insulation under **Article 235** and the procedural modernization of the 2023 criminal Sanhitas, the Indian subordinate judiciary remains gripped by a structural crisis of institutional capacity. Because trial courts serve as the primary interface between the citizen and the rule of law, chronic case pendency, severe deficits in judicial strength, underfunded physical infrastructure, and the failure of rural mobile courts directly erode the fundamental right to a speedy trial under **Article 21** and equal access to justice under **Article 39A**.

*   **The >5 Crore Pendency Crisis on the National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG):** Tracked in real time by the **National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG)**—an open-data transparency repository developed under the **e-Courts Project** by the Supreme Court’s e-Committee in collaboration with the National Informatics Centre (NIC)—total judicial pendency across India has crossed **5.1 crore cases**. Of this total, the District and Subordinate Judiciary accounts for approximately **4.5 crore pending cases (nearly 87% of total national litigation)**, with over 75% comprising criminal matters (heavily skewed by Negotiable Instruments Act Section 138 cheque bounce cases, motor vehicle challans, and petty summons trials) and over one-fourth pending for more than five years.
*   **Judge-to-Population Deficit and Vacancy Bottlenecks:** While the **120th Law Commission Report (1987)** on *Manpower Planning in Judiciary* recommended raising India’s judicial strength from 10.5 to **50 judges per million population** (a benchmark reiterated by the Supreme Court in *All India Judges Association v. Union of India, 2002* and *Imtiyaz Ahmad v. State of UP, 2012*), India’s sanctioned judge-to-population ratio remains trapped at approximately **21 judges per million population**. Worse still, out of a sanctioned strength of roughly 25,000+ subordinate judges across states, more than **20% of posts remain chronically vacant** due to irregular recruitment cycles despite the mandatory time-schedule laid down in **Malik Mazhar Sultan v. U.P. Public Service Commission (2006)**. To address systemic docket mismanagement without awaiting massive cadre expansion, the Supreme Court in **High Court Bar Association, Allahabad v. State of Uttar Pradesh (February 2024)**—a 5-Judge Constitution Bench overruling *Asian Resurfacing of Road Agency (2018)*—held that while constitutional courts should not fix automatic 6-month vacation of interim stay orders (as automatic vacation penalizes litigants for systemic judicial delay), High Courts and District Courts must institutionalize scientific case-flow management and prioritize old trials.
*   **The Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008:** Enacted on the recommendation of the **114th Law Commission Report (1986)** to actualize **Article 39A**, the **Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008** (effective October 2, 2009) provides for the establishment of **Gram Nyayalayas** at the intermediate Panchayat level. Presided over by a **Nyayadhikari** holding the rank and qualifications of a **Judicial Magistrate of the First Class (JMFC)** appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court, Gram Nyayalayas are designed as **mobile rural courts** that hold periodic camp sittings directly inside villages.
    *   **Procedural Elasticity:** Under **Sections 11 to 30**, Gram Nyayalayas exercise both criminal (summary trials for offences punishable up to 2 years) and civil jurisdiction, are **not bound by the strict technical rules of the Indian Evidence Act / Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam**, and are guided by the **principles of natural justice** with a mandatory statutory duty to attempt conciliation first (**Section 26**).
    *   **Operational Paralysis:** Despite targeting 5,000+ courts nationally, fewer than 500 Gram Nyayalayas have been notified and fewer than 350 are operational across states (prompting repeated Supreme Court mandamus interventions in *National Federation of Societies for Fast Justice v. Union of India, 2020–2024*). Their failure stems from the **non-mandatory wording of Section 3(1)** ("The State Government *may*... establish one or more Gram Nyayalayas"), lack of dedicated central funding, absence of exclusive jurisdiction (overlapping with regular Taluka JMFC courts), and resistance from local Bar associations due to summary procedures.
*   **e-Courts Mission Mode Project Phase III (2023–2027):** Approved by the Union Cabinet with a financial outlay of **₹7,210 crore** (more than quadrupling Phase II’s allocation), **e-Courts Phase III** transitions the subordinate judiciary from mere digitization of case statuses (Phase I and II) to an **end-to-end digital and paperless judicial ecosystem**. Key pillars include scanning and digitization of over 3,100 crore pages of legacy and live court records, cloud-native data architecture, expansion of **24x7 Virtual Courts** for traffic and petty offences, **e-Sewa Kendras** in every court complex to bridge the digital divide for rural litigants, AI-assisted live voice-to-text transcription of witness depositions, and seamless integration with police, prisons, and forensic labs via the **Inter-Operable Criminal Justice System (ICJS) 2.0**.
*   **The Proposed National Judicial Infrastructure Authority of India (NJIAI):** Currently, physical infrastructure for subordinate courts (court halls, residential quarters, women’s washrooms, digital hardware) is funded through a **Centrally Sponsored Scheme (CSS)** on a 60:40 cost-sharing ratio between the Centre and States (90:10 for North-Eastern and Himalayan States). Because State Governments routinely divert or delay their 40% matching share, central funds lapse while over 25% of district courts lack basic amenities. To resolve this fiscal bottleneck, the proposal for a **National Judicial Infrastructure Authority of India (NJIAI)**—championed by the Supreme Court and the 2021 Chief Justices’ Conference—envisions an autonomous statutory body modeled on NALSA, with the Chief Justice of India as Patron-in-Chief and State Judicial Infrastructure Authorities (SJIAs) under High Court Chief Justices, where pooled funds do not lapse at the end of the financial year and infrastructure planning is removed from state executive bureaucracy.

## II. The Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) Ecosystem and Commercial Justice Architecture

### 5. Statutory Foundations of Non-Adversarial Justice: Section 89 CPC, Article 39A, and the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (NALSA & Lok Adalats)

The constitutional mandate of **Article 39A** (inserted by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**), which obligates the State to secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity, has catalyzed a statutory shift from adversarial courtroom combat toward **multi-door non-adversarial dispute resolution**. In the Indian legal order, this shift operates across two statutory axes: first, the **court-annexed referral gateway under Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure (CPC), 1908**; and second, the **access-to-justice architecture of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**, which institutionalizes both conciliatory **Regular Lok Adalats** and hybrid concilio-adjudicatory **Permanent Lok Adalats**.

#### Statutory Mechanics and Jurisprudential Thresholds

*   **Section 89 CPC and the *Afcons Infrastructure (2010)* Referral Matrix:** Re-inserted into the CPC by the **Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1999** (effective July 1, 2002) following the **Malimath Committee Report (1990)** and the **129th Law Commission Report**, **Section 89 CPC** (read with **Order X Rules 1A, 1B, and 1C**) empowers the civil court, where it appears that there exist elements of a settlement which may be acceptable to the parties, to refer the dispute to **(a) Arbitration, (b) Conciliation, (c) Judicial Settlement including settlement through Lok Adalat, or (d) Mediation**. After upholding its constitutional validity in **Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India (I & II, 2003 and 2005)**, the Supreme Court in **Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (P) Ltd. (2010)** corrected blatant legislative drafting errors in **Section 89** (where the definitions of "mediation" and "judicial settlement" had been accidentally swapped by the legislature and where the requirement of formulating full terms of settlement prior to referral threatened to turn the trial judge into a pre-mediator) and laid down binding operational rules:
    1.  **Timing of Referral:** The court should consider **Section 89** referral *after* the completion of pleadings (complaint and written statement) and *before* the framing of issues under **Order XIV**.
    2.  **Consent Thresholds:** A civil court **cannot refer a suit to Arbitration or Conciliation without the mutual consent of all parties** (because arbitration permanently removes the dispute from the civil court stream into the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**). Conversely, the court **does not require party consent** to refer a matter to **Lok Adalat, Mediation, or Judicial Settlement**; if those non-binding processes fail, the suit simply returns to the originating civil court for regular trial.
    3.  **Excluded Categories:** Representative suits under Order I Rule 8, election disputes, probate/administration grants, serious allegations of fraud/forgery, and criminal prosecutions are inherently unsuitable for **Section 89** ADR referral.
*   **The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (NALSA Hierarchy):** Enacted to operationalize **Article 39A**, the Act establishes a four-tier statutory network: the **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)** at the apex (with the Chief Justice of India as Patron-in-Chief and the senior-most Supreme Court Judge as Executive Chairman), **State Legal Services Authorities (SLSA)** headed by High Court Chief Justices, **District Legal Services Authorities (DLSA)** chaired by District Judges, and **Taluk Legal Services Committees (TLSC)**, alongside Supreme Court and High Court Legal Services Committees. Under **Section 12**, free legal aid is a statutory right for SCs, STs, victims of human trafficking/beggar (**Article 23**), women, children, persons with disabilities, victims of mass disaster/ethnic violence, industrial workmen, persons in custody, and individuals falling below specified annual income thresholds.
*   **Regular Lok Adalats (Sections 19–21) and the Bar on Adjudication:** Organized by SLSAs, DLSAs, or TLSCs, a Regular Lok Adalat exercises jurisdiction over any case pending before a court or any pre-litigation matter falling within the jurisdiction of that court, **provided it does not relate to a non-compoundable offence** (**Section 19(5)**).
    *   **Purely Conciliatory Nature:** In **State of Punjab v. Jalour Singh (2008)** (3-Judge Bench), the Supreme Court held that Regular Lok Adalats have **zero adjudicatory or judicial functions**; their role is strictly confined to acting as conciliators guiding the parties toward a voluntary compromise. If no compromise is reached, the Lok Adalat cannot pass an order on merits and must return the record to the regular court (**Section 20(5)**).
    *   **Deemed Decree and Absolute Finality:** Under **Section 21(1)–(2)**, every award of a Lok Adalat is deemed to be a **decree of a civil court**, the full court fee paid by the plaintiff is refunded under the **Court-Fees Act, 1870**, and the award is **final and binding on all parties, with no appeal lying to any court against the award**. In **Bhargavi Constructions v. Kothakapu Muthyam Reddy (2017)**, the Supreme Court held that a regular civil suit to set aside a Lok Adalat award is barred; the *sole* remedy available to an aggrieved party alleging fraud or misrepresentation in recording the settlement is an extraordinary writ petition before the High Court under **Articles 226 and 227**.
*   **Permanent Lok Adalats (Sections 22A–22E) and Hybrid Adjudicatory Power:** Inserted by the **Legal Services Authorities (Amendment) Act, 2002**, **Chapter VI-A (Sections 22A to 22E)** created **Permanent Lok Adalats (PLAs)** specifically for pre-litigation disputes relating to **Public Utility Services** (transport, postal/telegraph/telephone, power/light/water supply, public conservancy/sanitation, hospitals/dispensaries, insurance services, plus housing/real estate, education, and banking added via notifications) up to a pecuniary limit of **₹1 crore** (enhanced from ₹10 lakh in 2015).
    *   **Compositional Structure:** A PLA consists of a Chairman (who is or has been a District Judge or Additional District Judge or has held judicial office higher in rank) and two other persons having adequate experience in public utility services (**Section 22B**).
    *   **The Two-Stage Concilio-Adjudicatory Mandate:** Unlike a Regular Lok Adalat, if the parties before a Permanent Lok Adalat fail to reach an agreement during mandatory conciliation proceedings under **Section 22C(4)–(7)**, the Permanent Lok Adalat **shall, if the dispute does not relate to any offence, decide the dispute on merits under Section 22C(8)**.
    *   **Constitutional Validity and Mandatory Step-by-Step Procedure:** In **Bar Council of India v. Union of India (2012)**, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of **Section 22C(8)** and **Section 22E** (which makes PLA merits awards final and non-appealable), holding that vesting summary merits-adjudication in a specialized statutory forum chaired by a senior judicial officer does not violate the Rule of Law. Subsequently, in **Canara Bank v. G.S. Jayarama (2022)**, the Supreme Court clarified that the conciliation procedure under **Section 22C(4)–(7)** is **mandatory**; a Permanent Lok Adalat cannot jump directly to adjudicating a dispute on merits under **Section 22C(8)** without first conducting genuine conciliation and formulating settlement terms for the parties' consideration.

### 6. The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (2015–2021 Amendments) and Constitution Bench Jurisprudence (Stamp Act Interplay, Cox and Kings, and CORE II)

The **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**—enacted on the blueprint of the **UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration (1985)** to consolidate and replace the fragmented colonial regime of the Arbitration Act, 1940—embodies a fierce jurisprudential dialectic between **party autonomy (minimal judicial intervention under Section 5 and arbitral tribunal competence under Section 16)** and **public law constitutional accountability (public policy scrutiny under Section 34 and equality under Article 14)**. Over the last decade, four major statutory interventions (**the 2015, 2019, and 2021 Amendment Acts**) and three watershed **Constitution Bench verdicts delivered between December 2023 and November 2024** (**In Re: Stamp Act Interplay (7-Judge)**, **Cox and Kings (5-Judge)**, and **CORE II (5-Judge)**) have fundamentally reconstructed Indian arbitration law.

#### The Statutory Amendment Trajectory (2015, 2019, and 2021 Amendments)

*   **The Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2015:** Enacted following the **246th Law Commission Report** to cure the pathologies of *Bhatia International (2002)* and *ONGC v. Saw Pipes (2003)* (which had turned Indian courts into de facto appellate forums over arbitral awards), the **2015 Amendment** introduced five structural reforms:
    1.  **Strict 12-Month Timeline (Section 29A):** Mandated that domestic arbitral tribunals must render an award within **12 months** (extendable by 6 months by mutual party consent, after which the mandate terminates unless extended by the Court).
    2.  **Narrowing of "Public Policy" and "Patent Illegality" (Section 34):** Restricted the ground of "conflict with the public policy of India" to three narrow parameters: (i) fraud or corruption, (ii) contravention with the fundamental policy of Indian law, or (iii) conflict with the most basic notions of morality or justice (barring any review on the merits of the dispute). It also inserted **Section 34(2A)**, clarifying that **"Patent Illegality"** appearing on the face of the award is a ground available *only* in purely domestic arbitrations (excluding International Commercial Arbitrations) and that an award cannot be set aside merely on the ground of an erroneous application of law or by re-appreciation of evidence (**Ssangyong Engineering & Construction Co. v. NHAI, 2019**).
    3.  **Abolition of Automatic Stay on Enforcement (Section 36):** Overturning *National Aluminum Co. v. Pressteel & Fabrications (2004)*, the 2015 Amendment provided that the mere filing of a **Section 34** setting-aside petition **does not operate as an automatic stay** on the enforcement of the arbitral award; the judgment-debtor must obtain a specific stay order subject to deposit conditions akin to a money decree under **Order XLI Rule 5 CPC**.
    4.  **Arbitrator Neutrality and the Fifth & Seventh Schedules (Section 12(5)):** Modeled on the IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest, the **Fifth Schedule** lists grounds giving rise to justifiable doubts as to an arbitrator's independence or impartiality, while the **Seventh Schedule** (read with **Section 12(5)**) renders any person having an employee, consultant, manager, or direct/indirect business relationship with a party *de jure* ineligible to be appointed as an arbitrator (unless waived by an express post-dispute agreement in writing).
    5.  **Court-Ordered vs. Tribunal-Ordered Interim Measures (Sections 9 and 17):** Armed the arbitral tribunal under **Section 17** with the exact same interim-relief powers as a civil court under **Section 9**, and barred courts from entertaining **Section 9** applications once the arbitral tribunal is constituted unless the **Section 17** remedy is rendered inefficacious (**ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel India Ltd. v. Essar Bulk Terminal Ltd., 2021**).
*   **The Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2019:** Acting on the **Justice B.N. Srikrishna High-Level Committee Report (2017)**, the **2019 Amendment** sought to transition India from *ad hoc* to institutional arbitration. It provided for the establishment of an **Arbitration Council of India (ACI)** (**Part IA, Sections 43A–43M**) to grade arbitral institutions and accredit arbitrators (though heavily criticized by international arbitration bodies because the ACI is appointed and controlled by the Central Government, frequently a major litigant). The 2019 Amendment also inserted **Section 23(4)**, requiring statements of claim and defence to be completed within **6 months**, and modified **Section 29A** so that the 12-month timeline for making an award begins *after* the completion of pleadings under **Section 23(4)** (while completely exempting International Commercial Arbitrations from the statutory 12-month deadline). Additionally, it narrowed **Section 45** (judicial referral in New York Convention international arbitrations) by replacing the word "satisfied" with *"prima facie* finds" that the agreement is null and void, and introduced **Section 42A** (confidentiality of arbitral proceedings) and **Section 87** (which attempted to bar the retrospective application of the 2015 Amendment to pre-2015 arbitrations, but **Section 87 was struck down as manifestly arbitrary under Article 14** by a 3-Judge Bench in **Hindustan Construction Company Ltd. v. Union of India, 2019**, reaffirming **BCCI v. Kochi Cricket Pvt. Ltd., 2018**).
*   **The Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2021:** This amendment made two key modifications: first, it deleted the controversial **Eighth Schedule** (inserted in 2019, which had effectively barred foreign lawyers from sitting as arbitrators in India); second, it added a proviso to **Section 36(3)** mandating that the Court **shall grant an unconditional stay** on the enforcement of an arbitral award where the Court is *prima facie* satisfied that the arbitration agreement/contract or the making of the award was induced or effected by **fraud or corruption** (a provision criticized for reviving dilatory boilerplate allegations of fraud by losing PSUs to stall award execution).

#### The Three Landmark Constitution Bench Verdicts (2023–2024)

*   **1. The 7-Judge Constitution Bench in *In Re: Interplay between Arbitration Agreements under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 and the Indian Stamp Act 1899* (December 2023):**
    *   **The Doctrinal Conflict:** In April 2023, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **N.N. Global Mercantile Pvt. Ltd. v. Indo Unique Flame Ltd. (N.N. Global II)** had ruled (3:2) that an arbitration agreement contained within an unstamped or insufficiently stamped commercial contract is non-existent in law, void, and unenforceable under **Sections 33 and 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899** read with **Section 2(g) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872**, requiring courts to impound the document at the threshold stage of appointing an arbitrator under **Section 11**. Because this ruling threatened to paralyze summary arbitrator appointments across India, a 7-Judge Constitution Bench (headed by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud) was convened under curative/reference jurisdiction.
    *   **The 7-Judge Ratio Overruling *N.N. Global II* (2023) and *SMS Tea Estates* (2011):** Unanimously overruling *N.N. Global II*, the 7-Judge Bench established a foundational distinction between **voidness (unenforceability)** and **inadmissibility in evidence**:
        1.  **Inadmissibility vs. Voidness:** **Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act** renders an unstamped or insufficiently stamped instrument *inadmissible in evidence*, NOT *void* or *invalid*. Non-stamping or inadequate stamping is a **curable fiscal defect** (upon payment of duty and penalty under Sections 35/42 of the Stamp Act), whereas a void agreement under **Section 2(g) of the Contract Act** is incurable *ab initio*.
        2.  **Doctrine of Separability and *Kompetenz-Kompetenz* (Section 16):** An arbitration clause is a distinct, autonomous agreement separable from the underlying commercial contract (**Section 16(1)**). Under the principle of **negative *Kompetenz-Kompetenz*** (**Section 16** read with the non-obstante judicial non-interference mandate of **Section 5**), the Arbitral Tribunal has primary jurisdiction to rule on its own jurisdiction, including objections regarding the stamping of the underlying contract.
        3.  **Strict Restriction on Section 8 and Section 11 Courts:** When a referral court under **Section 8** or an appointing court under **Section 11(6A)** examines an application, its scrutiny is strictly confined to a **prima facie examination of the existence of an arbitration agreement**. The Court **cannot examine or impound** the agreement for stamp duty compliance at the Section 8 or Section 11 stage; once the Arbitral Tribunal is constituted, the Tribunal itself acts as a person authorized by law to receive evidence and will impound the unstamped instrument under **Section 33 of the Stamp Act** if required.
*   **2. The 5-Judge Constitution Bench in *Cox and Kings Ltd. v. SAP India Pvt. Ltd.* (December 2023):**
    *   **The Doctrinal Conflict:** Under **Section 7** of the 1996 Act, an arbitration agreement must be in writing between parties. In **Chloro Controls India Pvt. Ltd. v. Severn Trent Water Purification Inc. (2013)**, the Supreme Court had imported the **"Group of Companies" Doctrine** into Indian law to bind non-signatory corporate affiliates of a conglomerate to an arbitration agreement, but had controversially anchored this power in the statutory phrase *"any person claiming through or under"* a party in **Section 8 / Section 45**.
    *   **The 5-Judge Ratio Validating the Group of Companies Doctrine:** The 5-Judge Constitution Bench definitively retained and upheld the **Group of Companies Doctrine** in Indian arbitration jurisprudence to reflect the economic reality of modern multi-layered corporate structures, while placing it on a rigorous doctrinal footing:
        1.  **Consent and Mutual Intention (Sections 2(1)(h) and 7):** A "party" to an arbitration agreement under **Section 2(1)(h)** read with **Section 7** includes both signatory and non-signatory parties. Although **Section 7** requires a written arbitration agreement, the intention of a non-signatory to be bound by that written agreement can be inferred from **conduct and surrounding commercial circumstances**.
        2.  **The Cumulative Fact-Intensive Test:** Mere membership in the same corporate group or common shareholding is insufficient to pierce corporate separateness. To bind a non-signatory under the Group of Companies Doctrine, the Court or Tribunal must apply the cumulative factors laid down in **Oil and Natural Gas Corporation Ltd. v. Discovery Enterprises Pvt. Ltd. (2022)**: (i) the mutual intent of the parties; (ii) the relationship of the non-signatory to the signatory; (iii) the commonality of the subject-matter; (iv) the composite nature of the transaction; and (v) the **direct involvement of the non-signatory in the negotiation, performance, or termination of the underlying contract**.
        3.  **Partial Overruling of *Chloro Controls*:** The Bench overruled *Chloro Controls (2013)* strictly to the extent that it had conflated the Group of Companies Doctrine with the phrase *"claiming through or under"* in **Sections 8 and 45**. A person "claiming through or under" (such as an assignee, successor-in-interest, or subrogated insurer) acts only in a derivative capacity, whereas a non-signatory bound under the **Group of Companies Doctrine** is bound as a **veritable "party" in its own right** under **Section 2(1)(h) and Section 7**, and can therefore even apply for interim measures under **Section 9**.
*   **3. The 5-Judge Constitution Bench in *Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v. M/s ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV) [CORE II]* (November 2024):**
    *   **The Doctrinal Conflict:** Standard-form government and Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) procurement contracts routinely contained asymmetrical arbitration clauses where the Managing Director or General Manager of the PSU either unilaterally appointed the sole arbitrator or furnished a narrow, curated panel of four retired PSU/Railway officers from which the private contractor was forced to select names. While 2- and 3-Judge Benches in **TRF Ltd. v. Energo Engineering Projects Ltd. (2017)** and **Perkins Eastman Architects DPC v. HSCC (India) Ltd. (2019)** held that a person ineligible under **Section 12(5) / Seventh Schedule** cannot unilaterally nominate an sole arbitrator ("what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly"), a 3-Judge Bench in **CORE I (2019)** had upheld the Railways' curated panel system on the ground that the contractor's right to choose two names out of four "counterbalanced" the Railways' power to appoint from them.
    *   **The 5-Judge Ratio Overruling *CORE I* and Constitutionalizing Arbitral Equality:** By a majority judgment (authored by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, with partial concurrences/dissents by Justices Hrishikesh Roy and P.S. Narasimha on total prohibition in purely private vs. public contracts), the 5-Judge Constitution Bench overruled *CORE I (2019)* and established a landmark fusion of **Part III Fundamental Rights** and **arbitral procedural justice**:
        1.  **Horizontal Infusion of Article 14 into Public-Private Arbitration:** When the State or an instrumentality of the State under **Article 12** (such as Railways, NHAI, or PSUs) enters into commercial contracts containing arbitration clauses, it cannot shed its constitutional obligation of **fairness, non-arbitrariness, and equality under Article 14**.
        2.  **Section 18 Mandatory Equal Treatment at the Appointment Stage:** **Section 18 of the Arbitration Act** ("The parties shall be treated with equality and each party shall be given a full opportunity to present his case") is a mandatory, non-derogable statutory command that applies not merely *after* the tribunal is constituted, but **at the very stage of constituting the arbitral tribunal**.
        3.  **Invalidity of Unilateral Appointments and Unilateral Curated Panels:** First, a clause allowing one party to **unilaterally appoint a sole arbitrator** gives rise to justifiable doubts regarding independence and impartiality and violates **Section 18 and Article 14**. Second, in a three-member tribunal, **mandating the private contractor to select its nominee arbitrator exclusively from a curated panel unilaterally maintained by the PSU** violates the principle of equal treatment because the PSU exercises asymmetrical control over the composition of the entire tribunal (while the PSU is free to maintain a broad panel for its *own* nominee, it cannot compel the private party to choose its nominee from the PSU's curated list). Any unilateral waiver under the proviso to **Section 12(5)** must occur *after* the dispute has arisen through an express agreement in writing.

### 7. The Mediation Act, 2023 and the Commercial Courts Act, 2015: Institutionalizing Pre-Litigation and Commercial Dispute Resolution

#### The Commercial Courts Act, 2015 (and 2018 Amendment) Architecture

*   **Institutional Specialization and ₹3 Lakh Threshold:** Enacted following the **188th and 253rd Law Commission Reports**, the **Commercial Courts Act, 2015** establishes specialized **Commercial Courts** at the District level, **Commercial Divisions** in High Courts possessing ordinary original civil jurisdiction (Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, and Himachal Pradesh), and **Commercial Appellate Divisions** in High Courts to adjudicate a wide definition of "Commercial Disputes" (**Section 2(1)(c)**, encompassing mercantile documents, infrastructure contracts, intellectual property, joint ventures, insurance, and maritime claims). Through the **Commercial Courts, Commercial Division and Commercial Appellate Division of High Courts (Amendment) Act, 2018**, Parliament drastically lowered the minimum pecuniary **"Specified Value"** of a commercial dispute from ₹1 crore down to **₹3 lakh**, bringing district-level MSME and mid-sized business litigation into the specialized commercial track.
*   **Procedural Fast-Tracking (Summary Judgment and Case Management):** The Act amends the CPC in its application to commercial disputes by introducing: (i) **forfeiture of the right to file a Written Statement** beyond an absolute outer ceiling of **120 days** from service of summons (**Order VIII Rule 1**, held strictly mandatory by the Supreme Court in **SCG Contracts India Pvt. Ltd. v. K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd., 2019**); (ii) **Summary Judgment under Order XIII-A**, empowering the Commercial Court to decide a claim without recording oral evidence if the plaintiff or defendant has "no real prospect of succeeding"; and (iii) mandatory **Case Management Hearings under Order XV-A** to fix binding calendars for witness cross-examination and written arguments, with appeals disposed of by the Commercial Appellate Division within **6 months** (**Section 14**).
*   **Section 12A Mandatory Pre-Institution Mediation and *Patil Automation (2022)*:** Inserted by the **2018 Amendment**, **Section 12A(1)** stipulates that a suit which *does not contemplate any urgent interim relief* under the Act shall not be instituted unless the plaintiff exhausts the remedy of **pre-institution mediation** conducted by Authorities constituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (within a period of **3 months**, extendable by **2 months** by party consent, with the time spent excluded from limitation under the Limitation Act, 1963). Any settlement reached has the status and effect of an arbitral award on agreed terms under **Section 30(4) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996**. In **M/s Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022)**, the Supreme Court held that **Section 12A is mandatory, not directory**; any commercial suit instituted without exhausting pre-institution mediation (where no genuine urgent interim relief is sought) must be **rejected *suo motu* by the Court under Order VII Rule 11 of the CPC**, and the plaintiff cannot bypass **Section 12A** through clever drafting or sham interim-relief prayers (**Yamini Manohar v. T.K.D. Keerthi, 2023**).

#### The Mediation Act, 2023: Comprehensive Statutory Codification

*   **Transition from Mandatory to Voluntary Pre-Litigation Mediation (Section 5):** Whereas the initial 2021 Draft Mediation Bill had proposed making pre-litigation mediation compulsory prior to filing *any* civil or commercial suit in India—a proposal criticized by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice because India lacked the mediator capacity and forcing reluctant litigants into mediation without infrastructures would become a dilatory hurdle—**Section 5(1) of the enacted Mediation Act, 2023** makes pre-litigation mediation **voluntary** (Whether any mediation agreement exists or not, the parties *may voluntarily and with mutual consent* take steps to settle the disputes by pre-litigation mediation). However, **Section 5(2)** expressly preserves the mandatory pre-institution mediation mandate of **Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015**, while **Section 7** empowers any court or tribunal to refer parties to mediation at any stage of a proceeding.
*   **Territorial Scope and Exclusion of Non-Commercial Foreign Settlements (Section 2):** The Act applies to mediations conducted in India where: (i) all or both parties habitually reside or are incorporated in India; (ii) the mediation agreement provides that mediation shall be conducted in accordance with the Act; (iii) there is an **international mediation** (defined as a mediation relating to a **commercial dispute** where at least one party is a foreign individual, foreign corporate body, or foreign government); or (iv) the Central or State Government or public body is a party to a commercial dispute (or other notified dispute under **Section 49**, requiring prior written approval of the competent government authority before signing a settlement). Because the Act applies only to mediations *conducted in India*, it does not yet provide a direct enforcement mechanism for cross-border mediated settlements conducted *outside* India (explaining why India has signed but not yet ratified the **Singapore Convention**).
*   **The First Schedule Negative List of Non-Mediable Disputes (Section 6):** **Section 6** read with the **First Schedule** enumerates disputes that are not fit for mediation: (i) claims against minors, deities, and persons with intellectual disabilities or unsound mind; (ii) **prosecution for criminal offences** (though the proviso to **Section 6** allows a criminal court to refer **compoundable offences and matrimonial disputes** connected with civil proceedings to mediation, without deeming the outcome a civil decree); (iii) proceedings before statutory bodies concerning professional misconduct (e.g., Bar Council, NMC, ICAI); (iv) disputes affecting the rights of **third parties** ( proceedings *in rem*, except matrimonial disputes where child custody is affected); (v) any levy, collection, penalty, or offence relating to **direct or indirect taxation**; (vi) proceedings before the **Competition Commission of India (CCI)**, Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT), SEBI/SAT, Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board, Electricity Commissions, and the **National Green Tribunal (NGT)**; and (vii) land acquisition and compensation determinations.
*   **Strict 120+60 Day Timeline, Confidentiality, and Online/Community Mediation:** Under **Section 18**, mediation proceedings must be completed within **120 days** from the date fixed for the first appearance before the mediator, extendable by mutual consent of the parties by a maximum of **60 days** (imposing an absolute statutory ceiling of **180 days**). **Sections 22 and 23** impose strict statutory **confidentiality and evidentiary privilege**, barring any party or mediator from relying on admissions, proposals, or views expressed during mediation in any subsequent court or arbitral proceeding. **Section 30** statutorily validates **Online Mediation** using encrypted electronic communications, while **Sections 43 and 44** create a framework for **Community Mediation** (conducted by a three-member panel notified by the DLSA or District Magistrate to preserve local peace and harmony in neighborhood/community disputes, though community settlements do not carry the force of a civil court decree).
*   **Direct Enforceability (Section 27), Narrow Challenge Grounds (Section 28), and the Mediation Council of India (Sections 31–39):** Under **Section 27**, a signed and authenticated **Mediated Settlement Agreement (MSA)** is final and binding on the parties and is **enforceable directly in accordance with the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in the same manner as if it were a judgment or decree passed by a court**. Under **Section 28**, an MSA can be challenged before the court of competent jurisdiction within **90 days** (extendable by a further 90 days on sufficient cause) **strictly on four exhaustive grounds**: **(a) fraud, (b) corruption, (c) impersonation, or (d) where the mediation was conducted in disputes or matters not fit for mediation under Section 6**. Finally, **Chapter VIII (Sections 31–39)** establishes the **Mediation Council of India (MCI)** as a corporate regulator (consisting of a Chairperson, two full-time members, one academic/practitioner part-time member, and three *ex-officio* Union Secretaries) tasked with registering mediators and recognizing **Mediation Service Providers** and **Mediation Institutes**.

## III. Administrative Adjudication and the Tribunalization Regime (Part XIV-A: Articles 323A & 323B)

### 8. Constitutional Dichotomy of Part XIV-A: Article 323A vs. Article 323B and the Proliferation of Specialized Tribunals (CAT, NGT, NCLT/NCLAT, AFT, ITAT, SAT)

The insertion of **Part XIV-A (Articles 323A and 323B)** into the Constitution by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**—acting on the blueprint of the **Swaran Singh Committee** during the National Emergency—marked a profound structural reorganization of the sovereign judicial power of the State. Ostensibly justified by the need for domain expertise, procedural informality, and the decongestion of High Court dockets, **Part XIV-A** authorized the creation of parallel administrative and specialized tribunals while empowering the legislature to **completely strip the High Courts of their constitutional writ and supervisory jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 227** (leaving only discretionary Special Leave Petitions to the Supreme Court under **Article 136**). Consequently, the tribunalization regime has functioned as the primary battleground between **executive centralization of regulatory adjudication** and the **constitutional monopoly of independent judicial review**.

#### Structural Contrast Between Article 323A and Article 323B and Extra-Part XIV-A Competence

*   **The Three Constitutional Divergences (Article 323A vs. Article 323B):**
    1.  **Subject-Matter Exclusivity vs. Multi-Sectoral Enumeration:** **Article 323A(1)** is strictly confined to disputes and complaints with respect to **recruitment and conditions of service** of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, of any State, or of any local or other authority or government-controlled corporation. Conversely, **Article 323B(2)** spans eight broad socio-economic and regulatory domains: **(a) taxation, (b) foreign exchange and customs, (c) industrial and labour disputes, (d) land reforms under Article 31A, (e) ceiling on urban property, (f) parliamentary and state legislative elections, (g) production, procurement, supply, and distribution of foodstuffs/essential goods, and (h) rent and tenancy rights** (inserted by the **75th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1994**), along with incidental offences and fees (**clauses (i) and (j)**).
    2.  **Parliamentary Monopoly vs. Federal Legislative Sharing:** Under **Article 323A(1)**, **Parliament alone** has the exclusive competence to enact a law establishing administrative tribunals (both for the Centre and for the States). State Legislatures have zero legislative competence under **Article 323A**. In contrast, under **Article 323B(1)**, the "appropriate Legislature"—meaning **both Parliament and the State Legislatures**, within their respective Seventh Schedule legislative spheres—may by law provide for the adjudication or trial by tribunals of any of the matters specified in **Article 323B(2)**.
    3.  **Single-Tier Restriction vs. Hierarchical Tiering:** **Article 323A(2)(a)** permits the establishment of only **one tribunal for the Union** and **one tribunal for each State or for two or more States**; there is no constitutional provision for an appellate administrative tribunal tier above the initial tribunal. Conversely, **Article 323B(3)(b)** explicitly authorizes the appropriate legislature to **establish a hierarchy of tribunals** (e.g., an original adjudicating tribunal and an appellate tribunal).
*   **Plenary Legislative Power Outside Article 323B (*Union of India v. R. Gandhi, 2010*):** When Parliament enacted laws creating the **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)** or the **Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRT)**, petitioners argued that because "company law" and "banking debt recovery" are not enumerated in **Article 323B(2)**, Parliament lacked the constitutional competence to establish such tribunals. In **Union of India v. Delhi High Court Bar Association (2002)** (upholding DRTs) and the 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010)** (upholding NCLT/NCLAT), the Supreme Court rejected this contention. The Court held that **Part XIV-A (Articles 323A and 323B) is merely an enabling framework (originally crafted to authorize the ouster of High Court jurisdiction) and does NOT curtail Parliament’s plenary legislative competence under Articles 245 and 246 read with the Seventh Schedule**—specifically **Entry 11A of List III (Concurrent List)** (*"Administration of justice; constitution and organisation of all courts, except the Supreme Court and the High Courts"*, transferred from List II to List III by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**) and **Entries 43, 44, 45, and 95 of List I**.

#### Diagnostic Profile of India’s Principal Specialized Tribunals

*   **Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT — Article 323A):** Established on November 1, 1985 under the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**, CAT exercises original jurisdiction in relation to recruitment and all service matters of **All-India Services (IAS, IPS, IFoS)**, Central Civil Services, civilians in defence services, and notified autonomous bodies (such as CSIR, EPFO, ICAR, and AIIMS). **Section 2** expressly **excludes** members of the naval, military, or air forces, officers and servants of the **Supreme Court or any High Court**, and secretarial staff of **Parliament or State Legislatures**. Guided by the principles of natural justice under **Section 22** rather than the strict CPC, CAT sits in mixed benches of one Judicial Member and one Administrative Member across 19 regular benches (Principal Bench at New Delhi). Under the Act, the Central Government may also establish **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)** at the request of a State Government (though several states such as Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, and Andhra Pradesh subsequently requested the abolition of their SATs, returning state service litigation to their respective High Courts).
*   **National Green Tribunal (NGT — Article 21 & Entry 11A List III / Entry 97 List I):** Established under the **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010** (fulfilling India's obligations under the **1972 Stockholm Declaration**, the **1992 Rio Earth Summit**, the **186th Law Commission Report**, and the right to a healthy environment under **Article 21**), the NGT exercises original and appellate civil jurisdiction over all cases where a substantial question relating to the environment is involved arising out of the implementation of **seven scheduled enactments listed in Schedule I** (Water Act 1974, Water Cess Act 1977, Forest Conservation Act 1980, Air Act 1981, Environment Protection Act 1986, Public Liability Insurance Act 1991, and Biological Diversity Act 2002; **explicitly excluding the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 and the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006**).
    *   **Jurisprudential Mandates (Section 20):** The NGT is statutorily mandated under **Section 20** to apply the principles of **Sustainable Development**, the **Precautionary Principle**, and the **Polluter Pays Principle**, with a 6-month disposal target (**Section 18(3)**) and a 5-year limitation period for compensation claims (**Section 15(3)**).
    *   **Suo Motu Power and High Court Scrutiny:** In **Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. Ankita Sinha (2021)**, the Supreme Court held that the NGT is a *sui generis* environmental institution vested with **suo motu jurisdiction** to take cognizance of environmental degradation based on media reports or letters. In **Madhya Pradesh High Court Advocates Bar Association v. Union of India (2022)**, the Supreme Court held that notwithstanding **Section 22 of the NGT Act** (which provides for direct statutory appeal from NGT to the Supreme Court), the writ jurisdiction of **High Courts under Articles 226 and 227** over NGT orders remains completely intact as part of the Basic Structure under *L. Chandra Kumar (1997)*.
*   **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT):** Constituted on June 1, 2016 under **Sections 408 and 410 of the Companies Act, 2013** (replacing the Company Law Board, the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction - BIFR, and the Appellate Authority for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction - AAIFR), the **NCLT** serves as the single-window adjudicating authority for corporate mergers, oppression and mismanagement, class action suits, and corporate insolvency resolution and liquidation under the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016** (while the **NCLAT** additionally functions as the appellate tribunal over orders of the **Competition Commission of India (CCI)** and the **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)**). In **Embassy Property Developments Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2019)**, the Supreme Court clarified that while NCLT/NCLAT possess comprehensive jurisdiction over insolvency matters, they cannot usurp the public law judicial review jurisdiction of High Courts under **Article 226** over sovereign government decisions (such as refusal to extend mining leases under the MMDR Act).
*   **Armed Forces Tribunal (AFT):** Established under the **Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007** (pursuant to the Supreme Court’s recommendation in **Prithi Pal Singh Bedi v. Union of India, 1982** and the **169th Law Commission Report**), the AFT adjudicates service disputes (commission, appointments, enrolments, conditions of service) and appeals from **Court Martial** convictions of personnel subject to the Army Act (1950), Navy Act (1957), and Air Force Act (1950) (excluding paramilitary/CAPF forces like BSF, CRPF, and ITBP, and limiting jurisdiction over summary punishments and transfers). Crucially, in **Union of India v. Parashotam Dass (March 2023)**, a 3-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court overruled its earlier decision in *Union of India v. Major General Shri Kant Sharma (2015)* and held that **High Courts possess full writ jurisdiction under Article 226 to scrutinize orders passed by the Armed Forces Tribunal**; **Article 227(4)** of the Constitution only excludes the High Court’s administrative *superintendence* over tribunals relating to the Armed Forces, NOT the High Court’s judicial review jurisdiction under **Article 226**.
*   **Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) and Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT):** Established in January 1941 under the Income Tax Act (currently governed by **Section 252 of the Income Tax Act, 1961**), the **ITAT** is India's oldest functioning tribunal ("Mother of Tribunals"), functioning under the administrative aegis of the **Ministry of Law and Justice** with benches of one Judicial Member and one Accountant Member; its findings of fact are final, and appeals lie to the High Court under **Section 260A** only on a "substantial question of law." The **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)**, established under **Section 15K of the SEBI Act, 1992**, consists of a Presiding Officer (retired Supreme Court Judge or High Court Chief Justice/Judge) and Judicial/Technical Members, exercising appellate jurisdiction over orders passed by **SEBI**, the **Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI)**, and the **Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA)**.

### 9. Separation of Powers and the Basic Structure Counter-Revolution: From Sampath Kumar to the 7-Judge Bench in L. Chandra Kumar (1997)

The enactment of the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** pursuant to **Article 323A** triggered a profound constitutional crisis over whether Parliament could invoke its constituent power under **Article 368** (via the **42nd Amendment**) and its legislative power under **Part XIV-A** to extinguish the judicial review jurisdiction of the **High Courts under Articles 226 and 227** and the **Supreme Court under Article 32**. #### The Two-Stage Jurisprudential Arc: *Sampath Kumar (1987)* vs. *L. Chandra Kumar (1997)*

*   **Stage 1: The "Alternative Institutional Mechanism" Doctrine in *S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987)*:** When the validity of **Article 323A** and **Section 28 of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** was first challenged before a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in **S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987)**, the Supreme Court sought to save the statute from invalidation by crafting the **"Alternative Institutional Mechanism" (Effective Substitute) Theory**. Relying on an observation by Justice Bhagwati in *Minerva Mills (1980)*, the 5-Judge Bench held that while **judicial review** is an integral part of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**, Parliament can constitutionally divest High Courts of their jurisdiction under **Articles 226 and 227** over a specific subject matter *provided* it vests that power in an alternative institutional mechanism (the Tribunal) that is **no less effective, independent, and efficacious a substitute than the High Court itself** (while leaving the Supreme Court's jurisdiction under **Articles 32 and 136** untouched). To ensure that CAT functioned as a true "substitute" for High Courts, the Bench directed amendments to the 1985 Act disqualifying pure bureaucrats from serving as Chairman without judicial experience and mandating consultation with the Chief Justice of India in appointments.
*   **The Empirical Failure of the Substitute Theory (1987–1997):** Within a decade of *Sampath Kumar*, the "effective substitute" paradigm collapsed in practice. In **J.B. Chopra v. Union of India (1987)**, the Supreme Court held that because CAT was a "substitute" for the High Court, it possessed the authority to strike down even parliamentary statutes and presidential rules under **Article 309** as unconstitutional. Yet, in **M.B. Majumdar v. Union of India (1990)**, the Court held that CAT members could *not* claim parity of status, pay, or conditions of service with High Court judges. Subsequently, in **R.K. Jain v. Union of India (1993)**, a 3-Judge Bench lamented the acute dysfunctionalities of tribunals (manned by retired bureaucrats lacking legal training and dependent on parent executive ministries) and called for a comprehensive reconsideration of *Sampath Kumar* by a larger Constitution Bench.
*   **Stage 2: The 7-Judge Constitution Bench Counter-Revolution in *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)*:** Convened to settle the constitutional limits of **Part XIV-A**, a unanimous **7-Judge Constitution Bench** (authored by Chief Justice A.M. Ahmadi) in **L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)** fundamentally dismantled the "substitutional" premise of *Sampath Kumar* and laid down six enduring axioms of Indian public law:
    1.  **Basic Structure Inviolability of Articles 226, 227, and 32:** The power of judicial review over legislative action vested in the **High Courts under Articles 226 and 227** and in the **Supreme Court under Article 32** is an integral, essential, and unamendable feature of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**. Because constitutional courts are protected by entrenched tenure, appointment, and removal safeguards that statutory tribunals lack, the jurisdiction of High Courts under **Articles 226/227** cannot be ousted even by a constitutional amendment under **Article 368**.
    2.  **Invalidation of the Ouster Clauses:** **Clause 2(d) of Article 323A** and **Clause 3(d) of Article 323B**, to the extent they empower Parliament or State Legislatures to exclude the jurisdiction of the High Courts under **Articles 226 and 227** and the Supreme Court under **Article 32**, are **unconstitutional and struck down**. For identical reasons, **Section 28 of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985** and all "exclusion of jurisdiction" clauses in other legislations enacted under the aegis of **Articles 323A and 323B** are declared unconstitutional.
    3.  **Supplemental, Not Substitutional Role ("Courts of First Instance"):** While High Court judicial review cannot be excluded, there is no constitutional prohibition against Tribunals performing a **supplemental—as opposed to a substitutional—role** in discharging the powers conferred by **Articles 226/227 and 32**. Accordingly, Tribunals continue to function as the **only courts of first instance** in respect of the areas of law for which they have been constituted. Litigants **cannot bypass the Tribunal** and approach the High Court directly under **Article 226** at the initial stage.
    4.  **Power of Tribunals to Test Statutory Constitutionality (With the Parent-Act Carve-Out):** So long as the jurisdiction of High Courts under **Articles 226/227** is retained as an appellate/supervisory check, Tribunals are fully competent to test the constitutional validity of **any statutory legislation, subordinate rule, or regulation**, subject to two strict caveats: (i) any question regarding the vires of a statute or rule must be heard by a **Bench of at least two Members, one of whom MUST be a Judicial Member** (never by a single member or a purely administrative bench); and (ii) being creatures of statute, **a Tribunal CANNOT entertain a challenge to the constitutional validity of the Parent Act under which it was created** (in which solitary situation, the litigant may approach the High Court directly).
    5.  **Mandatory Division Bench High Court Scrutiny Before Article 136:** Every decision of a Tribunal (including decisions rendered prior to the 1997 judgment that have not yet attained finality) is subject to scrutiny before a **Division Bench (two judges) of the High Court** within whose territorial jurisdiction the concerned Tribunal falls under **Articles 226 and 227**. No party is permitted to file a direct **Special Leave Petition (SLP) under Article 136** to the Supreme Court bypassing the jurisdictional High Court Division Bench (because direct SLPs from tribunals were found to be too costly, geographically inaccessible for ordinary litigants, and clogging the Supreme Court's constitutional docket).
    6.  **Administrative Independence from Line Ministries:** Until a single independent nodal agency is established, all Tribunals should be placed under the administrative supervision of a single nodal ministry—preferably the **Ministry of Law and Justice**—rather than remaining dependent on the very administrative line ministries that appear as primary litigants before them.

### 10. Executive Capture vs. Judicial Independence: R. Gandhi (2010), Rojer Mathew (2019), Madras Bar Association I–IV (2014–2021), and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 Deadlock

Over the past fifteen years, the constitutional conflict over tribunalization has shifted from the **external question of High Court jurisdiction** (settled in *L. Chandra Kumar*) to the **internal question of institutional design, appointment mechanics, tenure security, and executive capture**. Whenever the legislature transfers core judicial functions that were historically adjudicated by High Courts or Civil Courts to a newly created statutory tribunal, the **Doctrine of Separation of Powers** and the **Independence of the Judiciary** (**Article 50** and the Basic Structure) mandate that the tribunal's members must enjoy qualifications, selection insulation, and security of tenure commensurate with the judicial forum they replace. Between 2010 and 2026, this principle has produced an extraordinary cycle of **judicial invalidation followed by legislative re-enactment** across five major judgments and the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021**.

#### The Jurisprudential Milestones of Tribunal Independence (2010–2021)

*   **1. *Union of India v. R. Gandhi, President, Madras Bar Association* (2010 — 5-Judge Bench):** Challenging the creation of the **NCLT and NCLAT** under the Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2002, the Madras Bar Association argued that wholesale transfer of the High Court's company jurisdiction to a tribunal packed with civil servants destroyed judicial independence. While upholding Parliament's legislative competence to create NCLT/NCLAT, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench struck down Parts IB and IC as structured and laid down binding constitutional preconditions for any tribunal replacing a High Court:
    *   **Primacy of Judicial Members over Technical Members:** Only Judges and experienced Advocates can be considered for appointment as **Judicial Members**; persons who have held civil service posts cannot be treated as possessing "judicial experience." **Technical Members** are permissible *only* where specialized domain expertise (science, accountancy, engineering) is indispensable, and the **number of Technical Members can never exceed the number of Judicial Members** (Benches must always have a Judicial Member, and in two-member benches, the senior member must be a Judicial Member).
    *   **Disqualification of Career Bureaucrats as Technical Members:** Seniority in the civil service (such as holding the post of Joint Secretary for 5 years) does not automatically qualify a bureaucrat as an "expert" Technical Member; a Technical Member must possess proven domain specialization, and serving civil servants cannot retain a **lien on their parent executive ministry** while serving on a tribunal.
    *   **Balanced Selection Committee and Tenure:** The Selection Committee must consist of 4 members (Chief Justice of India or nominee as Chairperson with a **casting vote** in case of a tie, a senior Judge, and two Union Secretaries) to prevent executive packing, and members must have a minimum tenure of **5 to 7 years** (rather than short 3-year renewable tenures that breed subservience to the government).
*   **2. *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India [MBA I]* (2014 — 5-Judge Bench):** When Parliament enacted the **National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005** (under **Article 323B**) to create a **National Tax Tribunal (NTT)** that completely divested High Courts of their jurisdiction to decide appeals involving **"substantial questions of law"** under the Income Tax Act, Customs Act, and Central Excise Act, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench **struck down the entire National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005 as unconstitutional**. The Court held that while Parliament can create tribunals to adjudicate factual and mixed questions of law and fact, a tribunal exercising the core appellate judicial power of High Courts over substantial questions of law must fully mirror the conventions, customs, and independence of High Courts. Specifically, **Sections 5, 6, 7, 8, and 13** of the NTT Act violated the **Basic Structure** because: (i) they empowered the Central Government to decide the location and transfer of benches/members; (ii) they permitted **Chartered Accountants and Company Secretaries** to sit as members deciding substantial questions of constitutional and statutory interpretation; and (iii) the Selection Committee was dominated by executive secretaries.
*   **3. *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India [MBA II]* (2015 — 5-Judge Bench):** When Parliament enacted the **Companies Act, 2013**, it re-introduced provisions for NCLT and NCLAT that diluted the binding directives of *R. Gandhi (2010)* (making Joint Secretaries eligible as Technical Members and creating a 5-member Selection Committee with 3 executive bureaucrats). The 5-Judge Bench struck down **Sections 409(3)(a), 409(3)(c), 411(3), and 412(2) of the Companies Act, 2013**, mandating strict compliance with *R. Gandhi* before NCLT and NCLAT could finally be operationalized in 2016.
*   **4. *Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd.* (2019 — 5-Judge Bench):** Through **Part XIV of the Finance Act, 2017 (Sections 158 to 184)**—introduced and passed purely as a **Money Bill under Article 110**, bypassing the Rajya Sabha—the Union Government abolished/merged 8 tribunals and enacted **Section 184**, conferring sweeping delegated power on the Central Government to frame rules governing the qualifications, appointment, term of office, salaries, and removal of Chairpersons and Members across **19 Central Tribunals**. The resulting **Tribunal, Appellate Tribunal and other Authorities Rules, 2017** diluted judicial qualifications, gave executive nominees a majority in Search-cum-Selection Committees, empowered the Central Government to remove members, and reduced tenures to **3 years**. In **Rojer Mathew (2019)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench:
    *   **Struck down the 2017 Tribunal Rules in their entirety** as unconstitutional for contravening *L. Chandra Kumar*, *R. Gandhi*, and *MBA I & II* by destroying judicial independence and conferring uncanalized executive control over appointments, short tenures, and removals.
    *   **Referred the Money Bill Question to a 7-Judge Bench:** Questioning the correctness of the 5-Judge majority in *K.S. Puttaswamy II (Aadhaar, 2018)*, the Bench held that because the salaries of several tribunals are *not* charged on the Consolidated Fund of India and service conditions are substantive structural policy rather than incidental fiscal matters under **Article 110(1)**, the validity of passing **Part XIV of the Finance Act, 2017 as a Money Bill** must be decided by a **7-Judge Constitution Bench**.
    *   **Mandated a Judicial Impact Assessment:** Directed the Union Government to conduct a scientific Judicial Impact Assessment before merging tribunals or transferring their workloads.
*   **5. *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India [MBA III]* (November 2020) and *[MBA IV]* (July 2021):** Following *Rojer Mathew*, the Central Government notified new **2020 Tribunal Rules** in February 2020. In **MBA III (November 2020)**, a 3-Judge Bench led by Justice L. Nageswara Rao mandated modifications to the 2020 Rules to make them constitutional: (i) Search-cum-Selection Committees must be chaired by the **Chief Justice of India or his nominee** with a casting vote (consisting of 2 judicial seats and 2 executive seats, with only one non-voting Member-Secretary); (ii) the Committee shall recommend **only one name per post** (with a waiting list) to be appointed by the Government **within 3 months**; (iii) the tenure must be **5 years** for Chairpersons and Members; (iv) **Advocates with 10 years of standing** must be eligible for appointment as Judicial Members; and (v) the Union must constitute an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** as an umbrella administrative body.
    *   **The April 2021 Ordinance and *MBA IV (July 2021)*:** Instead of implementing *MBA III*, the Executive promulgated the **Tribunals Reforms (Rationalisation and Conditions of Service) Ordinance, 2021** in April 2021. Amending Section 184 of the Finance Act, 2017, the Ordinance fixed a **minimum age requirement of 50 years** for any appointment as Chairperson or Member (thereby completely neutralizing the Supreme Court's mandate that advocates with 10 years' standing be eligible, since advocates typically complete 10 years at age 32–35) and fixed a **maximum tenure of 4 years** instead of 5 years, while reviving the requirement that the Search-cum-Selection Committee recommend a **panel of two names per vacancy** for the Executive to choose from.
    *   **The *MBA IV* Strike-Down:** On **July 14, 2021**, a 3-Judge Bench (2:1, Justices L. Nageswara Rao and S. Ravindra Bhat; Justice Hemant Gupta dissenting) in **MBA IV (2021)** **struck down the first and second provisos to Section 184(1) (the 50-year minimum age bar) and Section 184(11) (the 4-year tenure cap)** of the 2021 Ordinance. The Court held that while the legislature can cure the legal basis of a judgment, it **cannot pass a law that directly nullifies a binding writ of mandamus issued to fortify the Basic Structure (judicial independence under Articles 14, 21, and 50)** without removing the underlying constitutional defect (the **Doctrine of Unconstitutional Legislative Override**).

#### The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 and the Continuing Constitutional Deadlock

*   **Rationalization and Abolition of Nine Appellate Tribunals:** Within three weeks of the Supreme Court's judgment in *MBA IV (July 2021)*, Parliament enacted the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** in August 2021 (replacing the 2021 Ordinance and repealing Section 184 of the Finance Act, 2017 to create a standalone parent statute). First, the Act **abolished nine existing statutory appellate tribunals/boards** whose low case-disposal volumes and overlap failed to justify separate institutional existence—most notably the **Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB)** (under the Trade Marks, Patents, Copyright, and Geographical Indications Acts), the **Film Certification Appellate Tribunal (FCAT)** (under the Cinematograph Act, 1952), the **Airport Appellate Tribunal**, the **Authority for Advance Rulings** (under the Income Tax Act, replaced by a Board for Advance Rulings), the **Plant Varieties Protection Appellate Tribunal**, and appellate bodies under the Control of National Highways Act, Customs Act, and Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators Act—transferring their pending appellate dockets directly back to the **High Courts** (leading the Delhi, Madras, and Calcutta High Courts to establish specialized **Intellectual Property Divisions - IPDs**) or Commercial Civil Courts.
*   **Verbatim Re-Enactment of the Struck-Down Provisions (Sections 3 and 5):** Simultaneously, in an unprecedented institutional confrontation, **Chapter II of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** verbatim re-enacted the very provisions that the Supreme Court had struck down as unconstitutional just days earlier in *MBA IV (July 2021)*:
    1.  **The 50-Year Minimum Age Bar (Proviso to Section 3(1)):** Stipulates that *"a person who has not completed the age of fifty years shall not be eligible for appointment as a Chairperson or Member"*—once again excluding younger advocates with 10 years of litigation experience from the tribunal bench.
    2.  **The 4-Year Tenure Cap (Section 5):** Fixes the term of office of the Chairperson and every Member at **four years** (with an upper retirement age of 70 for Chairpersons and 67 for Members), directly defying the 5-year constitutional minimum mandated in *R. Gandhi (2010)*, *MBA III (2020)*, and *MBA IV (2021)*.
    3.  **The Two-Name Panel Discretion (Section 3(7)):** Mandates that the Search-cum-Selection Committee **shall recommend a panel of two names for appointment to each post of Chairperson or Member**, and the Central Government shall take a decision **"preferably within three months"** (replacing the mandatory 3-month timeline with directory language and empowering the Executive to cherry-pick between two candidates or delay appointments indefinitely through selective security/IB clearance vetoes).
*   **The Pending Constitutional Reckoning (2021–2026):** Challenged immediately in *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (W.P.(C) No. 1018/2021)* alongside contempt petitions against the Union Executive for leaving hundreds of Judicial and Technical posts vacant across NCLT, NCLAT, DRT, ITAT, and CAT or selectively appointing second-placed candidates from Search-cum-Selection Committee lists, the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** crystallizes the ultimate constitutional question of Part XIV-A: whether Parliament can deploy ordinary legislative majorities to repeatedly re-enact statutory provisions held violative of **Article 50 and the Basic Structure**, thereby reducing specialized judicial tribunals to short-tenure extensions of the executive bureaucracy.
