# Part 9: The Union and State Legislatures II: Presiding Officers, Parliamentary Privileges, Committee Oversight, and the Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule)

## I. Constitutional Architecture of Presiding Officers and Secretarial Autonomy (Articles 89–98, 178–187)

### 1. Institutional Design of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker: Westminster Neutrality vs. Partisan Continuity (Articles 93–97, 178–181)

The office of the Speaker in a parliamentary democracy sits at the structural fault line between **majoritarian executive control** and **autonomous legislative oversight**. Although the Speaker is elected by a simple majority of the House from amongst its sitting members—reflecting the numerical dominance of the ruling coalition—the constitutional functions vested in the Chair require strict adjudicatory impartiality. In India, the Speaker does not merely regulate floor debates under **Article 118** (or **Article 208** in the States); the Speaker exercises quasi-judicial authority as a statutory **Tribunal** under the **Tenth Schedule**, possesses final certification authority over **Money Bills** under **Article 110(3)** (and **Article 199(3)**) that can bypass the Upper House, and determines the admissibility of executive censure mechanisms. The central pathology of Indian legislative constitutionalism arises from importing the vast, uncodified discretionary powers of the Westminster Speaker without importing the institutional conventions that insulate the British Chair from partisan allegiance.

#### Constitutional Mechanics of Election, Tenure Survival, and Removal (Articles 93–96, 178–181)
*   **Mandate of Election (Article 93 and Article 178):** Both the **Lok Sabha** (**Article 93**) and every **State Legislative Assembly** (**Article 178**) "shall, as soon as may be, choose two members of the House to be respectively Speaker and Deputy Speaker." The election is conducted by a simple majority of members present and voting on a motion moved inside the House, without requiring any formal oath separate from the ordinary member's oath under the **Third Schedule**. Whenever the office of Speaker or Deputy Speaker falls vacant, the House is constitutionally obligated to choose another member to fill the vacancy.
*   **Institutional Continuity Post-Dissolution (Article 94 Second Proviso and Article 179 Second Proviso):** To prevent an institutional vacuum in the leadership of the Lower House between the dissolution of one House and the constitution of the next, the **Second Proviso to Article 94** (and **Article 179** for States) mandates that whenever the House of the People is dissolved, the Speaker *shall not vacate his office until immediately before the first meeting of the House of the People after the dissolution*. At that inaugural sitting of the newly elected House, the President (or Governor under **Article 180(1)**) appoints a **Speaker Pro-Tem**—by convention the senior-most member of the House in terms of continuous legislative service—solely to administer oaths to new members and preside over the election of the regular Speaker.
*   **Three Modes of Vacation and Removal by Effective Majority (Article 94 and Article 179):** A member holding office as Speaker or Deputy Speaker vacates office in three exhaustive scenarios:
    1.  **Loss of Membership (Article 94(a) / Article 179(a)):** If they cease to be a member of the House of the People or Legislative Assembly.
    2.  **Mutual Resignation (Article 94(b) / Article 179(b)):** By writing under their hand addressed, if such member is the Speaker, to the **Deputy Speaker**, and if such member is the Deputy Speaker, to the **Speaker** (never to the President or Governor).
    3.  **Removal Resolution by Effective Majority (Article 94(c) / Article 179(c)):** By a resolution of the House passed by a **majority of all the then members of the House** (an **Effective Majority**, calculated as more than 50% of the total strength minus existing vacancies). Under the **First Proviso to Article 94**, no such resolution shall be moved unless at least **14 days' notice** has been given of the intention to move the resolution, and in the Lok Sabha, the motion requires the support of at least **50 members** to be admitted for discussion.
*   **Procedural Disentitlement and First-Instance Voting During Removal (Article 96 and Article 181):** Under **Article 96(1)** (and **Article 181(1)**), at any sitting of the House while any resolution for the removal of the Speaker or Deputy Speaker from office is under consideration, the targeted officer *shall not, though present, preside*. However, under **Article 96(2)** (and **Article 181(2)**), the Speaker retains the constitutional right to speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of, the House during the consideration of the removal resolution, and **shall be entitled to vote only in the first instance** on such resolution or on any other matter during such proceedings, *but not in the case of an equality of votes*.
*   **The Casting Vote Principle (Article 100(1) and Article 189(1)):** In all ordinary legislative proceedings outside a removal resolution, **Article 100(1)** (and **Article 189(1)**) bars the Speaker or person acting as such from voting in the first instance, directing that they **shall have and exercise a casting vote in the case of an equality of votes**. This mechanism preserves the Speaker's appearance of floor neutrality unless the House is deadlocked, whereupon the casting vote is traditionally exercised to maintain the *status quo* or allow further deliberation rather than create new law by a single vote.
*   **Fiscal Insulation (Article 97 and Article 112(3)(b)):** The salaries and allowances of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker (as well as the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha) are fixed by Parliament by law (under the **Salaries and Allowances of Officers of Parliament Act, 1953**), are specified in the **Second Schedule**, and are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** under **Article 112(3)(b)** (and on the Consolidated Fund of the State under **Article 202(3)(b)**), insulating their emoluments from the annual vote of the legislature.

#### Partisan Convention vs. Westminster Neutrality
In the United Kingdom, the convention *"Once a Speaker, always a Speaker"* is enforced through four structural safeguards: (1) upon election, the British Speaker formally resigns from their political party; (2) the Speaker does not campaign on a party platform in general elections and stands for re-election solely as *"The Speaker seeking re-election,"* uncontested by major political parties; (3) the Speaker abstains from policy debates; and (4) upon retirement, the Speaker does not return to partisan politics, being elevated instead to the House of Lords as a crossbencher.

In contrast, while **Paragraph 5 of the Tenth Schedule** offers an optional constitutional safe harbor permitting the Speaker, Deputy Speaker, or Deputy Chairman to voluntarily sever their political party membership upon election without incurring disqualification for defection—and to rejoin a party upon ceasing to hold office—Indian Presiding Officers routinely retain active party affiliation, contest subsequent elections on party tickets, and frequently transition back into Union or State Cabinet Ministries or Gubernatorial offices. Although early icons like **G.V. Mavalankar** (whom Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru famously described as the *"linchpin of the parliamentary chariot"*) and **Neelam Sanjiva Reddy** (who formally resigned from the Congress Party upon assuming the Chair in 1967) attempted to cultivate non-partisan conventions, modern practice has entrenched executive dominance over the Chair.

#### The Deputy Speaker Vacancy Crisis (Article 93 and Article 178)
The Deputy Speaker is not a subordinate assistant to the Speaker; when the office of Speaker is vacant or the Speaker is absent from a sitting, the Deputy Speaker exercises the full plenary constitutional powers of the Chair under **Article 95(1)** (and **Article 180(1)**) and is directly accountable solely to the House. Furthermore, whenever the Deputy Speaker is appointed as a member of a Parliamentary Committee, they automatically become its Chairperson. By post-1990 parliamentary convention, the office of Speaker goes to the ruling alliance while the **Deputy Speaker** is offered to the Opposition to ensure bipartisan stewardship of the House.

However, because **Article 93** and **Article 178** employ the phrase *"shall, as soon as may be, choose"* without specifying a hard statutory deadline or vesting the power to fix the election date of the Deputy Speaker in an independent authority (under **Rule 8 of the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha**, the **Speaker** fixes the date of the Deputy Speaker's election), ruling majorities have increasingly left the office vacant. Most notably, the **17th Lok Sabha (2019–2024)** functioned for its entire five-year term without electing a Deputy Speaker—the first such instance in independent India's history—alongside similar prolonged vacancies across multiple State Legislative Assemblies (prompting the Supreme Court in 2023 PIL proceedings to issue notices questioning how a mandatory *"shall"* in **Article 93/178** can be rendered otiose by executive-legislative inertia).

### 2. The Bicameral Asymmetry: Rajya Sabha Chairman, Deputy Chairman, and Legislative Secretariats (Articles 89–92, 98, 182–187)

The presiding architecture of the Upper House (**Rajya Sabha** under **Articles 89–92** and **State Legislative Councils** under **Articles 182–185**) exhibits a profound structural divergence from the Lower House, driven by the constitutional status of the **Vice-President of India** as the *ex-officio* Chairman of the Council of States.

#### Structural Divergence of the Rajya Sabha Chairman and Deputy Chairman (Articles 89–92)
*   **Ex-Officio Non-Member Status (Article 89(1)):** Under **Article 89(1)**, the Vice-President of India is the *ex-officio* Chairman of the Council of States. In contrast, in a State having a bicameral legislature, the **Chairman of the State Legislative Council** under **Article 182** is elected by the Council *from amongst its own members* (as States do not have an office of Vice-Governor). Similarly, the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** (**Article 89(2)**) is elected by the Council of States from amongst its sitting members.
*   **Bicameral Removal Mechanics vs. Unicameral Removal (Article 67(b) vs. Article 90(c)):** Because the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha holds the Chair by virtue of being the Vice-President of India, the Chairman can be removed from office *only* if removed from the office of Vice-President under **Article 67(b)**—which requires a resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by a **majority of all the then members of the Council (Effective Majority)** and **agreed to by the House of the People (Simple Majority)**, following 14 days' notice. Conversely, the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** is removed under **Article 90(c)** by a resolution of the Council of States alone passed by an **Effective Majority**, without any involvement of the Lok Sabha.
*   **The Article 92(2) Voting Prohibition:** When a resolution for the removal of the Vice-President (Chairman) or the Deputy Chairman is under consideration, neither officer can preside (**Article 92(1)**). However, **Article 92(2)** introduces a critical textual distinction regarding the Chairman: while the Chairman has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of the Council of States during the consideration of his removal resolution, he **shall not be entitled to vote at all** on such resolution or on any other matter during such proceedings (whereas the Deputy Chairman, being an elected member of the Rajya Sabha, votes in the first instance). During ordinary sittings, the Chairman exercises a **casting vote** in the case of an equality of votes under **Article 100(1)** despite not being a member of the House.
*   **The Panel of Chairpersons / Vice-Chairpersons vs. Empty Chair Vacancy (Article 95 and Article 91):** Under the Rules of the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha, the Speaker and the Chairman respectively nominate from amongst the members a **Panel of not more than 10 Chairpersons** (in Lok Sabha) or a **Panel of Vice-Chairpersons** (in Rajya Sabha). Any member of this panel can preside when the Speaker and Deputy Speaker (or Chairman and Deputy Chairman) are temporarily **absent** from a sitting. Crucially, however, under **Article 95(2)** (and **Article 91(2)**), a member of the Panel of Chairpersons **cannot preside when the offices of both Speaker and Deputy Speaker are vacant**. In the event of simultaneous *vacancy* (as opposed to temporary *absence*), the **President** (or Governor under **Article 180(2)**) must appoint a member of the House to perform the duties of the office until an election is held.
*   **Presiding Over Joint Sittings (Article 118(4)):** At a joint sitting of both Houses convened under **Article 108**, the **Speaker of the Lok Sabha** presides. In the Speaker's absence, the **Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha** presides; if the Deputy Speaker is also absent, the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** presides; and if he is also absent, such other person as may be determined by the members present at the sitting presides. The **Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Vice-President) never presides over a joint sitting**, as he is not a member of either House of Parliament.

#### Independent Parliamentary and State Secretariats (Articles 98 and 187)
To prevent the executive branch (via the Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs or the Department of Personnel and Training) from controlling the administrative machinery, research staff, and legislative drafting apparatus of the legislature, **Article 98(1)** (and **Article 187(1)** for States) mandates that **each House of Parliament shall have a separate secretarial staff** (though the Proviso allows the creation of posts common to both Houses). Historically rooted in **Vithalbhai Patel's** 1928–1929 struggle as President of the Central Legislative Assembly to establish a separate Legislative Assembly Department independent of the Governor-General's Executive Council, **Article 98** places the Lok Sabha Secretariat under the exclusive administrative control of the Speaker and the Rajya Sabha Secretariat under the Chairman. Under **Article 98(2)**, Parliament may by law regulate the recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to the secretarial staff, and until such statutory provision is made, the **President may, after consultation with the Speaker of the House of the People or the Chairman of the Council of States**, make rules regulating their recruitment and service conditions (**Article 98(3)**), thereby insulating legislative staff from regular civil service control.

## II. Floor Accountability Mechanics and Parliamentary Privileges Jurisprudence (Articles 105, 194)

### 3. Procedural Devices of Executive Responsibility: Question Hour, Motions, and Floor Censure

Under the Westminster model codified in **Article 75(3)** (and **Article 164(2)**), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the House of the People. However, because the executive is formed by the very party or coalition that commands a majority on the floor of the Lok Sabha, the legislature's plenary power to remove the government is rarely triggered unless coalition partners defect. Consequently, the day-to-day enforcement of executive accountability relies upon calibrated procedural devices under the **Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business** framed pursuant to **Article 118**.

#### Diagnostic Breakdown of Floor Accountability Instruments
*   **Question Hour (11:00 AM to 12:00 Noon):** The first hour of every parliamentary sitting is dedicated to **Question Hour**, where Ministers are compelled to answer questions posed by private members regarding administrative acts and policy execution. Questions are classified into three distinct categories:
    1.  **Starred Questions** (distinguished by an asterisk): Require an **oral answer** on the floor of the House, which consequently permits members to ask immediate, unscripted **supplementary questions** to expose executive evasions (typically capped at 20 questions per day in Lok Sabha and 15 in Rajya Sabha).
    2.  **Unstarred Questions**: Receive a **written answer** laid on the Table of the House (up to 230 per day in Lok Sabha), following which **no supplementary questions** can be asked.
    3.  **Short Notice Questions**: Relate to a matter of urgent public importance and are asked with a notice period shorter than the mandatory 10 days (up to 15 days), answered orally followed by supplementaries, provided the Minister agrees to accept short notice. Questions can also be addressed to **Private Members** if the subject matter relates to some Bill, resolution, or other business of the House for which that member is responsible.
*   **Zero Hour (12:00 Noon):** Unlike Question Hour, **Zero Hour is not mentioned in the Rules of Procedure**. Emerging in the early 1960s as an indigenous **Indian parliamentary innovation**, Zero Hour begins immediately after Question Hour at 12:00 noon and lasts until the regular agenda of the day (the List of Business) is taken up at 1:00 PM. It serves as an informal procedural safety valve allowing members to raise urgent matters of public importance without prior 10-day notice (though notices are submitted to the Speaker/Chairman before 10:00 AM on the day of the sitting).
*   **Calling Attention Motion:** Also an **Indian procedural innovation** introduced in the Rules of Procedure in 1954, a Calling Attention Motion enables a member, with the prior permission of the Presiding Officer, to call the attention of a Minister to a matter of urgent public importance and demand an authoritative statement from him. Unlike Zero Hour, it is formally codified in the Rules; unlike an Adjournment Motion, it is **permissible in both the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha**, does not disrupt normal legislative business, and involves **neither a floor vote nor an element of censure**.
*   **Adjournment Motion:** An extraordinary procedural weapon introduced to **adjourn the regular business of the House** to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance. Because an Adjournment Motion involves a direct **element of censure against the Government**, the **Rajya Sabha is barred from using this device**; it is restricted exclusively to the Lok Sabha. To be admitted, it must secure the support of at least **50 members**, must be restricted to a single specific matter of recent occurrence (not framed in general terms), must not raise a question of privilege or a matter that is *sub judice*, and discussion on it cannot last for less than two hours and thirty minutes.
*   **Censure Motion vs. No-Confidence Motion (Rule 198):** Both motions are restricted exclusively to the **Lok Sabha** (and State Legislative Assemblies) as manifestations of collective responsibility under **Article 75(3)**, yet they operate on fundamentally distinct procedural thresholds:

| Procedural Parameter | No-Confidence Motion (Rule 198 of LS Rules) | Censure Motion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Constitutional Basis** | Direct operationalization of **Article 75(3)** collective responsibility | Procedural rule under **Article 118** to reprimand executive policy |
| **Requirement of Grounds** | **No reasons or grounds need to be stated** for its adoption; a single-line motion (*"This House expresses its want of confidence in the Council of Ministers"*) suffices | **Must explicitly state the specific reasons, policies, or acts** for which the executive is being censured |
| **Target of the Motion** | Can be moved **only against the entire Council of Ministers** collectively; never against an individual Minister | Can be moved against an **individual Minister, a group of Ministers, or the entire Council of Ministers** |
| **Leave of the House** | Requires the support of at least **50 members** in the Lok Sabha to be granted leave by the Speaker | No fixed 50-member threshold in the manner of Rule 198; governed by general motion admissibility rules and Speaker's consent |
| **Consequence of Passage** | **Mandatory Resignation:** The Prime Minister and the entire Council of Ministers are constitutionally bound to resign immediately | **No Mandatory Resignation:** The Council of Ministers need not resign instantly, though it suffers severe political delegitimation and must seek to restore confidence on the floor |

### 4. Constitutional Anatomy of Parliamentary Privileges: Codification Debates vs. Fundamental Rights (Articles 105 and 194)

Parliamentary privileges are special rights, immunities, and exemptions enjoyed by the two Houses of Parliament, their committees, and their members, designed to secure the independence, dignity, and fearless functioning of the legislature against executive intimidation or judicial interference. Governed by **Article 105** for Parliament and **Article 194** for State Legislatures, these privileges extend NOT only to elected and nominated members of the Houses, but also to persons who by virtue of the Constitution have the right to speak in and otherwise take part in the proceedings of a House or any of its committees—namely, the **Attorney General for India** (**Article 88**) and **Union Ministers** who may be members of the other House. Crucially, the **President of India**, despite being an integral constituent part of Parliament under **Article 79**, does **not** enjoy parliamentary privileges under **Article 105** (as the President does not sit or participate in the Chambers and is separately protected by **Article 361** executive immunity).

#### Textual Structure of Articles 105 and 194
*   **Clause (1) — Freedom of Speech in the Legislature:** *"Subject to the provisions of this Constitution and to the rules and standing orders regulating the procedure of Parliament, there shall be freedom of speech in Parliament."* Note the opening qualifier: **Article 105(1)** is explicitly made *"Subject to the provisions of this Constitution"*—including **Article 121** (and **Article 211** in the States), which strictly prohibits any discussion in Parliament with respect to the conduct of any Judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court in the discharge of his duties, except upon a motion for presenting an address to the President praying for the removal of the Judge.
*   **Clause (2) — Absolute Immunity for Speech, Vote, and Authorized Publication:** *"No member of Parliament shall be liable to any proceedings in any court in respect of anything said or any vote given by him in Parliament or any committee thereof, and no person shall be so liable in respect of the publication by or under the authority of either House of Parliament of any report, paper, votes or proceedings."* Unlike Clause (1), **Clause (2) is not prefaced by "Subject to the provisions of this Constitution."** It erects a protective shield against civil defamation or criminal prosecution for legislative speech and voting, complemented by **Article 361A** (inserted by the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978**), which grants constitutional immunity to the press for publishing substantially true reports of parliamentary or state legislative proceedings unless the publication is proved to have been made with **malice** (barring secret sittings).
*   **Clause (3) — Other Privileges and the 44th Amendment's Textual Shift:** In other respects, the powers, privileges, and immunities of each House, its members, and committees *"shall be such as may from time to time be defined by Parliament by law, and, until so defined, shall be those of that house and of its members and committees immediately before the coming into force of Section 15 of the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978."* Originally, **Article 105(3)** explicitly equated uncodified privileges with those of the **House of Commons of the Parliament of the United Kingdom** at the commencement of the Constitution (January 26, 1950). The **42nd Amendment (1976)** attempted to alter this so privileges would be "evolved" by each House, which was repealed by the **44th Amendment (1978)**. The 44th Amendment deleted the direct textual reference to the British House of Commons to satisfy nationalist sentiment, yet retained the exact substantive legal status quo by freezing the baseline at what existed immediately prior to June 20, 1979 (which itself was the 1950 House of Commons baseline).

#### Individual vs. Collective Privileges
1.  **Individual Privileges of Members:**
    *   **Freedom of Speech** on the floor and in committees (**Art. 105(1)–(2)**).
    *   **Freedom from Civil Arrest:** Under **Section 135A of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908**, a member cannot be arrested in a **civil case** during the continuance of a session of the House or a committee meeting, and during a period of **40 days before its commencement and 40 days after its conclusion**. Crucially, **this immunity does NOT extend to criminal offences, contempt of court, or preventive detention**; in criminal or preventive detention matters, the House only has the privilege of being immediately informed by the arresting magistrate/authority of the arrest, detention, conviction, and release of its member.
    *   **Exemption from Jury and Witness Duty:** A member cannot be summoned to attend as a witness in a court without the leave of the House while Parliament is in session.
2.  **Collective Privileges of the House as a Body:**
    *   The right to publish its reports, debates, and proceedings and the converse right to **prohibit others from publishing** the same (especially expunged portions or secret sittings).
    *   The right to **exclude strangers** from its galleries and hold secret sittings.
    *   The right to regulate its internal proceedings and adjudicate matters arising within its walls (**Articles 118 and 122**).
    *   The power to **punish members as well as outsiders for breach of privilege or contempt of the House** by admonition, reprimand, suspension, **expulsion** (for members), or **imprisonment** (terminating automatically upon the prorogation of the session).

#### The Non-Codification Paradox and the Fundamental Rights Collision
Parliament has deliberately refused to enact a comprehensive statute codifying privileges under the first limb of **Article 105(3)**. The institutional incentive for non-codification is straightforward: the moment Parliament enacts a statute defining privileges under **Article 105(3)**, that statute becomes ordinary *"law"* within the meaning of **Article 13(2)** and becomes fully testable against the entirety of **Part III (Fundamental Rights)**. So long as privileges remain uncodified and flow directly from the constitutional text of **Article 105(3) / Article 194(3)**, they claim co-equal constitutional status with Part III. This structural tension produced two foundational Supreme Court Constitution Bench rulings:

*   **M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha (*The Searchlight Case*, 1959 — 5-Judge Bench):** When the editor of *The Searchlight* newspaper published portions of a speech made in the Bihar Legislative Assembly that the Speaker had ordered to be expunged, the Assembly issued a notice for breach of privilege. The editor challenged the notice under **Article 32**, claiming that the uncodified privilege under **Article 194(3)** violated his fundamental right to freedom of speech and press under **Article 19(1)(a)** and personal liberty under **Article 21**. The 5-Judge Bench (by 4:1 majority, with Justice K. Subba Rao dissenting) applied the principle of **harmonious construction (special overrides general)**: it held that **Article 194(3)** is a special constitutional provision conferring specific privileges on the legislature, whereas **Article 19(1)(a)** is a general provision. Therefore, **Article 19(1)(a) must yield to Article 194(3)**. However, the Court simultaneously held that **Article 21 applies**: any deprivation of personal liberty for contempt of the House must follow the *"procedure established by law"* (i.e., the codified Rules of Procedure framed under **Article 208**).
*   **In Re: Keshav Singh (*The UP Assembly Case*, 1965 — 7-Judge Presidential Reference under Article 143):** The Uttar Pradesh Legislative Assembly ordered the imprisonment of a citizen, Keshav Singh, for seven days for printing a pamphlet criticizing an MLA and displaying disrespect at the Bar of the House. When two Judges of the Allahabad High Court (Lucknow Bench) entertained a *Habeas Corpus* petition under **Article 226** and granted interim bail to Keshav Singh, the UP Assembly passed an extraordinary resolution ordering that **the two High Court Judges, the advocate, and Keshav Singh be brought in custody before the Bar of the House** to answer for contempt. The two High Court Judges then petitioned a Full Bench of 28 Judges of the Allahabad High Court, which stayed the Speaker's warrants. To avert a constitutional collapse, President S. Radhakrishnan made a **Presidential Reference under Article 143(1)** to a **7-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by Chief Justice P.B. Gajendragadkar. The Supreme Court laid down five enduring axioms:
    1.  **Indian Legislatures Are Not Superior Courts of Record:** Unlike the British House of Commons, which historically functioned as part of the High Court of Parliament (a superior Court of Record), Indian legislatures owe their existence to a written Constitution with limited powers and cannot claim immunity for general, unspeaking warrants from judicial scrutiny.
    2.  **Supremacy of Articles 32 and 226:** The jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under **Article 32** and High Courts under **Article 226** to examine the legality of a legislative warrant when a citizen's fundamental right under **Article 21** is infringed cannot be ousted by **Article 194(3)**.
    3.  **Absolute Judicial Immunity under Article 211:** Neither the High Court Judges who entertained the *Habeas Corpus* petition nor the advocate who presented it committed any contempt of the legislature; **Article 211** functions as an absolute constitutional bar against legislative action targeting judicial conduct.

#### Cash-for-Query Expulsions: Raja Ram Pal (2007) and the 2023 Ethics Committee Precedent
*   **Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007 — 5-Judge Bench):** Following the *Operation Duryodhana* sting operation exposing 11 MPs (10 from Lok Sabha, 1 from Rajya Sabha) accepting cash to table parliamentary questions, both Houses passed resolutions expelling the members through special inquiry/ethics committees without routing the matter through the formal disqualification machinery of **Articles 102/103** and the Election Commission. The expelled MPs argued that "expulsion" creates a permanent vacancy not enumerated in **Articles 101 and 102**, and that Indian legislatures lack the punitive powers of a British Court of Record. A **5-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by CJI Y.K. Sabharwal (4:1, Justice R.V. Raveendran dissenting) upheld the expulsions, ruling that:
    1.  **Expulsion vs. Disqualification:** **Articles 101 and 102** are not exhaustive of the ways a seat falls vacant; **expulsion** is a self-protective collective privilege under **Article 105(3)** to rid the House of members whose conduct brings disrepute to the institution, and unlike disqualification, expulsion does not bar the person from immediately seeking re-election.
    2.  **Scope of Judicial Review over Article 122(1):** While **Article 122(1)** shields *"mere irregularity of procedure"* from judicial scrutiny, it does **not** bar the Supreme Court from examining legislative proceedings that suffer from **substantive unconstitutionality, gross illegality, violation of Fundamental Rights (Articles 14, 20, 21), or *mala fides***.
*   **The 2023 Lok Sabha Ethics Committee Expulsion Precedent:** In December 2023, the Lok Sabha expelled MP Mahua Moitra following a report by the **Ethics Committee** investigating allegations of sharing confidential parliamentary login credentials with an outside businessman to post questions in return for gifts (*quid pro quo*). This episode highlighted a critical institutional distinction: whereas the **Committee of Privileges** operates under quasi-judicial procedural rules permitting detailed cross-examination of witnesses to adjudicate a specific *"breach of privilege or contempt,"* the **Ethics Committee** (first constituted in Rajya Sabha in 1997 and in Lok Sabha in 2000, made permanent in 2015) exercises a broader, less procedurally formalized jurisdiction over *"unethical conduct"* violating the Code of Conduct for members.

#### Criminal Acts and Destruction of Public Property on the Floor: State of Kerala v. K. Ajith (2021)
In **State of Kerala v. K. Ajith (2021)**, opposition MLAs inside the Kerala Legislative Assembly disrupted the Finance Minister's budget presentation by climbing onto the Speaker's dais, uprooting the Speaker's chair, and destroying computer monitors and microphones, leading to an FIR under the Indian Penal Code and the **Prevention of Destruction to Public Property Act, 1984**. When the State Government sought to withdraw the criminal prosecution under **Section 321 CrPC** claiming the acts occurred on the floor of the House and were protected by legislative privilege under **Article 194(2)** and the exclusive jurisdiction of the Speaker, the Supreme Court (authored by Justice D.Y. Chandrachud) categorically rejected the claim. The Court ruled that:
*   **Privileges Are Not Exemptions from Criminal Law:** **Article 194(1) and (2)** protect legitimate legislative deliberation and voting; **destruction of public property and physical violence on the floor of the House are not "essential functions" of a legislator** and can never be equated with *"anything said or any vote given"* in the Assembly.
*   **No Sanctuary Principle:** The floor of the Legislature is not a sanctuary immune from the ordinary criminal law of the land, nor does the Speaker's disciplinary power to suspend a member oust the jurisdiction of criminal courts to try statutory offences.

#### The Landmark 7-Judge Constitution Bench Verdict in Sita Soren v. Union of India (March 2024)
For 26 years, Indian public law suffered under the anomalous doctrine of **P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (CBI/SPE) (*The JMM Bribery Case*, 1998 — 5-Judge Bench, 3:2)**. In *Narasimha Rao*, MPs from the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha (JMM) had accepted bribes to vote against a no-confidence motion in July 1993, enabling the P.V. Narasimha Rao government to survive. A 3:2 majority of the Supreme Court interpreted the phrase *"in respect of anything said or any vote given by him in Parliament"* in **Article 105(2)** literally, holding that any MP who accepted a bribe and **actually cast their vote** as paid enjoyed **complete constitutional immunity from criminal prosecution** under the Prevention of Corruption Act, whereas an MP (Ajit Singh) who accepted the bribe but **abstained from voting** lacked the nexus to a "vote given" and could be prosecuted.

In **Sita Soren v. Union of India (March 2024)**, an MLA of the JMM in Jharkhand was accused of accepting a bribe from an independent candidate to cast her vote in his favor during the 2012 **Rajya Sabha elections**. In fact, because Rajya Sabha elections utilize an **open ballot** system (introduced in 2003 and upheld in **Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India, 2006**), she did not vote for the bribe-giver and instead voted for her own party's official candidate. Relying on *P.V. Narasimha Rao*, she claimed absolute immunity under **Article 194(2)** on the ground that any prosecution would be *"in respect of"* her vote. A **7-Judge Constitution Bench** headed by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud **unanimously overruled *P.V. Narasimha Rao***, establishing the following constitutional doctrines:
*   **Rejection of Res Judicata / Stare Decisis for Manifest Error:** The 7-Judge Bench held that *P.V. Narasimha Rao* created a *"grave peril to the foundations of Indian representative democracy"* by rewarding corrupt legislators who fulfilled their illegal bargain while punishing only those who reneged on it, thereby warranting overruling despite 26 years of precedent.
*   **Purposive Construction and the Two-Pronged Necessity Test:** Tracing the deliberative ideals of the Constituent Assembly, the Court held that parliamentary privilege under **Articles 105(2) and 194(2)** is not a personal perquisite of rank, status, or individual immunity; it is strictly functional. Any claim to privilege by an individual member must pass a **Two-Pronged Test**:
    1.  It must be **tethered to the collective functioning of the House**; and
    2.  Its relationship to the **essential legislative functions** of the member must be such that without the immunity, the member cannot discharge their constitutional mandate of fearless speech and deliberation.
    Bribery fails both prongs: it does not advance fearless deliberation; rather, it destroys deliberative democracy by trading the representative's conscience for private pecuniary gain.
*   **Autonomous Completion of the Offence of Bribery:** Under **Section 7 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988**, the criminal offence of bribery is complete and crystallizes **at the exact point in time when the legislator accepts or agrees to accept undue advantage (illegal gratification)** outside the House. The offence does not depend on whether the legislator subsequently moves a question, delivers a speech, or casts a vote on the floor in accordance with the bribe-giver's direction. Consequently, criminal prosecution for bribery is completely decoupled from *"anything said or any vote given"* inside the House.
*   **Parallel Jurisdiction of House and Criminal Courts:** The power of the House to punish a member for contempt or breach of privilege is entirely distinct from the jurisdiction of a criminal court to prosecute the offence of bribery under penal statutes; both operate in parallel without double jeopardy (**Article 20(2)**).
*   **Extension to Rajya Sabha, Presidential, and Vice-Presidential Elections:** The Court also clarified that votes cast by MLAs in **Rajya Sabha elections** (as well as votes for Speaker/Deputy Speaker) fall within the structural ambit of **Article 194(2)** so as to protect honest legislators from civil intimidation over how they voted, *provided* such voting is untainted by bribery.

## III. The Parliamentary Committee System: Sub-Floor Scrutiny and Executive Audit

### 6. The Financial Triumvirate: PAC, Estimates Committee, and COPU

Modern governance involves vast technical complexity, regulatory delegation, and multi-trillion-rupee budgetary outlays that a plenary body of 543 or 245 legislators sitting on the open floor cannot systematically audit. Moreover, open floor proceedings are governed by **party whips** and television optics, polarizing every debate along government-versus-opposition lines. **Parliamentary Committees**—operating *in camera* without media cameras and freed from strict party whips—constitute the institutional engine where bipartisan legislative scrutiny actually occurs. Deriving their constitutional authority from **Article 105(3)** (powers and privileges of committees) and **Article 118(1)** (rules of procedure), Standing Committees are anchored at their apex by the three **Financial Committees**: the **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**, the **Estimates Committee**, and the **Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)**.

#### Structural and Functional Anatomy of the Three Financial Committees
*   **Public Accounts Committee (PAC):**
    *   **Historical Lineage and Composition:** The oldest parliamentary committee in India, first set up in **1921** under the provisions of the **Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)**. Today, it consists of **22 members**—**15 elected by the Lok Sabha** and **7 elected by the Rajya Sabha** from amongst their members every year according to the principle of **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote**, ensuring representation for all major political parties. Its term of office is **one year**, and **no Minister can be elected as a member** (if a member is appointed a Minister after election to the committee, they cease to be a member from the date of such appointment).
    *   **The Opposition Chairperson Convention:** Until 1966–67, the Chairperson of the PAC belonged to the ruling party (including initially the Finance Minister himself during the colonial era). Since **1967** (starting with **Minoo Masani**), an unbroken parliamentary convention dictates that the Speaker appoints the **Chairperson of the PAC exclusively from the Opposition**.
    *   **Audit Mandate and CAG Symbiosis:** The primary function of the PAC is to examine the annual audit reports of the **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG)** laid before Parliament under **Article 151**—specifically the Audit Report on Appropriation Accounts, the Audit Report on Finance Accounts, and the Audit Report on Public Undertakings (the last of which is now handled by COPU). The PAC does not merely verify legal and technical regularity (that money disbursed was legally available and applied to the service for which it was charged); it investigates public expenditure from the standpoint of **"wisdom, faithfulness, and economy"** to uncover waste, corruption, loss, or nugatory expenditure. In this investigation, the **CAG sits alongside the Committee as its *"friend, philosopher, and guide,"*** briefing members before they summon Union Secretaries (who appear personally as Accounting Officers) for examination.
    *   **Structural Limitations:** The PAC is an *ex-post facto* ("post-mortem") body: it cannot intervene while expenditure is being incurred, cannot question broad matters of policy, and its recommendations are advisory, though enforced through the mandatory mechanism of **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)**.
*   **Estimates Committee ("Continuous Economy Committee"):**
    *   **Origin and Exclusive Lok Sabha Composition:** Tracing its lineage to the Standing Financial Committee of 1921, the post-independence Estimates Committee was established in **1950** on the recommendation of then Finance Minister **John Mathai**. Originally comprising 25 members, its membership was expanded in 1956 to **30 members**, **all of whom are elected exclusively from the Lok Sabha** by proportional representation via single transferable vote for a one-year term. The **Rajya Sabha has zero representation** on the Estimates Committee, reflecting the exclusive constitutional monopoly of the Lower House over voting Demands for Grants under **Article 113(2)**. Ministers are barred from membership, and the **Chairperson is appointed by the Speaker invariably from the ruling party/coalition**.
    *   **Core Mandate and Structural Constraints:** Often described as a *"continuous economy committee,"* its function is to examine the budget estimates in detail and report what economies, improvements in organization, efficiency, or administrative reform consistent with the policy underlying the estimates may be effected, and to suggest the form in which the estimates shall be presented to Parliament. Crucially, the Estimates Committee examines budget estimates **only after they have been voted by the Parliament**, never before, and it selects only a few Ministries/Departments each year by rotation, deprived of the technical assistance of the CAG.
*   **Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU):**
    *   **Origin and Composition:** Created in **1964** on the recommendation of the **Krishna Menon Committee**, COPU initially had 15 members (10 LS + 5 RS), which was expanded in 1974 to **22 members** (**15 elected from Lok Sabha** and **7 elected from Rajya Sabha** via proportional representation by single transferable vote for a one-year term). Ministers are barred from membership, and the **Chairperson is appointed by the Speaker solely from amongst the members drawn from the Lok Sabha** (a Rajya Sabha member on COPU cannot be appointed Chairperson).
    *   **Mandate and Commercial Boundaries:** COPU examines the reports and accounts of Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) specified in the Fourth Schedule to the Rules of Procedure, examines the reports of the **CAG** on PSUs, and assesses whether the affairs of the public undertakings are being managed in accordance with sound business principles and prudent commercial practices. However, COPU is strictly barred from investigating matters of major government policy as distinct from business/commercial functions, matters of **day-to-day administration**, or matters for the consideration of which special statutory machinery is established under the statute governing a particular PSU.

### 7. Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) and Ad-Hoc Investigative Committees (JPCs)

#### The 24 Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs)
Prior to 1993, Parliament lacked a comprehensive system of subject-specific committees to scrutinize the Demands for Grants of all Union Ministries before the budget was voted upon. Following the experiment with three subject committees (Agriculture, Science & Technology, and Environment & Forests) in 1989, Parliament formally inaugurated **17 Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) in March 1993**, which were subsequently restructured and expanded to **24 DRSCs in July 2004**.

*   **Bicameral Composition and Nomination Mechanics:** Each of the **24 DRSCs** consists of **31 members**—**21 members from the Lok Sabha nominated by the Speaker** and **10 members from the Rajya Sabha nominated by the Chairman** from amongst the members of the respective Houses (unlike the three Financial Committees, DRSC members are **nominated** by the Presiding Officers on the recommendation of party floor leaders in proportion to party strength, rather than formally elected by STV ballot). The term of office of each DRSC is **one year** from the date of its constitution, and **a Minister is not eligible to be nominated** as a member of any DRSC.
*   **Jurisdictional Allocation Between Both Houses:** Out of the 24 DRSCs, **16 function under the administrative direction and control of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha** (e.g., Finance, Defence, External Affairs, Agriculture, Railways, Communications and IT, Energy, Rural Development) and **8 function under the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** (e.g., Home Affairs, Commerce, Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, Education, Health and Family Welfare, Science & Technology, Environment, Forests and Climate Change). Every single Ministry and Department of the Government of India falls within the jurisdiction of one of these 24 committees.
*   **Core Functions and the Budget Recess Mechanism:**
    1.  **Pre-Vote Scrutiny of Demands for Grants:** After the general discussion on the Union Budget in both Houses concludes, Parliament adjourns for a fixed recess of three to four weeks. During this recess, the **24 DRSCs** examine the Demands for Grants of all Ministries in detail and submit their reports before the Lok Sabha reconvenes to vote on the Demands. This significantly mitigates the pathology of the **Guillotine** (whereby over 80% of ministry budgets were historically voted upon on the last allotted day without a single minute of floor discussion).
    2.  **Legislative Scrutiny of Bills:** Examine Bills pertaining to the related Ministries that are referred to the Committee by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha or the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (though referral is **discretionary, not mandatory**, leading to a documented decline in referrals from 71% in the 15th Lok Sabha to 27% in the 16th and 16% in the 17th Lok Sabha, before recovering partially in the 18th Lok Sabha).
    3.  **Long-Term Policy and Annual Reports:** Consider the annual reports of Ministries and national basic long-term policy documents presented to the Houses and referred by the Presiding Officers.
    4.  **Strict Prohibition on Cut Motions and Day-to-Day Administration:** Under the Rules, DRSCs **shall not suggest anything of the nature of Cut Motions** while considering Demands for Grants, nor shall they consider matters of day-to-day administration of the concerned Ministries. While their recommendations are not legally binding, the Government must submit **Action Taken Reports (ATRs)** justifying any non-acceptance, and Supreme Court Constitution Benches (such as in **Kalpana Mehta v. Union of India, 2018 — 5-Judge Bench**) have explicitly held that **Parliamentary Standing Committee Reports are admissible in evidence** under **Section 57 of the Indian Evidence Act / Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam** as an external aid to statutory interpretation and to establish historical facts or the existence of a public mischief, provided their factual findings or validity are not directly contested or adjudicated in court without violate **Article 105**.

#### Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs) and Select Committees
Unlike the permanent Standing Committees, **Select Committees** (composed of members of one House) and **Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs)** (composed of members of both Houses, typically in a **2:1 ratio** favoring the Lok Sabha) are **Ad-Hoc Committees** created for a specific task that cease to exist the moment they present their final report. A JPC is constituted only when a motion is passed by one House and agreed to by the other House (or by mutual communication between the two Presiding Officers). JPCs serve two distinct constitutional functions:
1.  **Investigative JPCs:** Empowered to investigate major systemic financial or administrative scandals (such as the 1987 Bofors Contract JPC, the 1992 Harshad Mehta Securities Scam JPC, the 2001 Ketan Parekh Stock Market Scam JPC, the 2003 Pesticide Residues in Soft Drinks JPC, and the 2011 2G Spectrum Allocation JPC). Investigative JPCs possess quasi-judicial powers to summon witnesses, administer oaths, and compel the production of executive files (subject to a proviso permitting the Government to decline to produce a document on the ground that its disclosure would be prejudicial to the safety or interest of the State).
2.  **Legislative JPCs:** Constituted to examine highly complex, multi-ministry, or politically contentious Bills (such as the Joint Committees constituted in 2024 on the **Waqf (Amendment) Bill** and the **Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment) Bill, 2024** on Simultaneous Elections).

## IV. The Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule): Party Discipline, Tribunal Adjudication, and Constitutional Crises

### 8. Statutory Mechanics of the Tenth Schedule: The 52nd and 91st Constitutional Amendments

The **Anti-Defection Law** represents one of the most radical constitutional interventions in the history of representative democracy. In classical parliamentary theory—articulated by Edmund Burke in his 1774 *Speech to the Electors of Bristol*—an elected legislator is a **trustee**, not a mere mechanical delegate of a party machine, who owes the electorate their independent judgment and conscience on the floor of the House under **Article 105(1)**. However, beginning with the post-1967 general elections (infamously epitomized by Haryana MLA Gaya Lal switching parties three times in a single fortnight—*"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram"*), rampant floor-crossing driven by cash allurements and ministerial berths destabilized dozens of State Governments and subverted the electoral mandate.

Following the recommendations of the **Y.B. Chavan Committee on Defections (1969)** and abortive attempts via the 32nd (1973) and 48th (1978) Amendment Bills, Parliament enacted the **52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985**, which amended **Articles 101, 102, 190, and 191** and added the **Tenth Schedule** to the Constitution. Subsequently, based on the recommendations of the **Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)**, the **170th Law Commission Report (1999)**, and the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (Venkatachaliah Commission, 2002)**, Parliament enacted the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003** to close the "wholesale defection" loophole of the one-third split and curb the incentive of jumbo cabinets.

#### Granular Deconstruction of the Tenth Schedule Clauses
*   **Paragraph 2(1)(a) — Voluntarily Giving Up Membership and the *Ravi S. Naik* Doctrine:** Under **Paragraph 2(1)(a)**, a member belonging to any political party is disqualified if he *"voluntarily gives up his membership of such political party."* By **Explanation (a) to Paragraph 2(1)**, an elected member is deemed to belong to the political party, if any, by which he was set up as a candidate for election as such member. In the landmark judgment of **Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994)**, the Supreme Court held that the phrase *"voluntarily gives up his membership"* is **not synonymous with "resignation"** and has a much wider connotation: even in the complete absence of a formal letter of resignation, a Speaker can draw a legitimate inference from a member's **conduct inside or outside the Legislature** (such as accompanying the opposition leader to the Governor to claim the government has lost majority, campaigning for an opposing party's candidate, or publicly denouncing one's own party leadership, as reaffirmed in **Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya, 2007** and **Mahachandra Prasad Singh v. Chairman, Bihar Legislative Council, 2004**) that the member has voluntarily given up membership of their original party.
*   **Paragraph 2(1)(b) — Defiance of the Party Whip:** A member is disqualified if he votes or abstains from voting in such House contrary to any direction (**Whip**) issued by the political party to which he belongs or by any person or authority authorized by it in this behalf, without obtaining in either case the prior permission of such political party, person, or authority, and **such voting or abstention has not been condoned by such political party, person, or authority within 15 days** from the date of such voting or abstention. Note two critical constitutional boundaries:
    1.  **Condonation Window:** No disqualification under **Para 2(1)(b)** matures until the 15-day condonation window expires without party forgiveness.
    2.  **Inapplicability to Rajya Sabha and Presidential Elections:** As held in **Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2006 — 5-Judge Bench)**, voting in a **Rajya Sabha election** is an activity outside the floor proceedings of the Assembly ("in such House"); therefore, cross-voting by an MLA in a Rajya Sabha election does not trigger automatic disqualification under **Para 2(1)(b)** for defying a whip (though the party can discipline the member internally, or potentially pursue **Para 2(1)(a)** if accompanied by broader anti-party conduct). Similarly, **Presidential and Vice-Presidential elections** are conducted by secret ballot where party whips are legally unenforceable (**Section 171A IPC / BNS**).
*   **Paragraph 2(2) and Paragraph 2(3) — Asymmetric Rules for Independent vs. Nominated Members:**
    *   An **Independent Member** (**Para 2(2)**), having sought the electorate's mandate precisely on a non-party platform, is disqualified the instant they join any political party after election.
    *   A **Nominated Member** (**Para 2(3)**) is granted a **six-month grace period** from the date on which he takes his seat in the House (and complies with the oath requirement under **Article 99** or **Article 188**) to join a political party; if he joins any political party *after* the expiry of six months from that date, he incurs immediate disqualification.
*   **The 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003 — Abolition of Split (Para 3), Retention of Merger (Para 4), and Ministerial Caps:**
    1.  **Omission of Paragraph 3 (The 1/3rd Split):** Prior to 2003, **Paragraph 3** exempted defectors from disqualification if they claimed a "split" in their original political party and the faction comprised at least **one-third** of the members of the legislature party. The **91st Amendment completely omitted Paragraph 3**, ending all constitutional protection for splits.
    2.  **Retention of Paragraph 4 (The 2/3rd Merger):** Under **Paragraph 4(1)**, a member is not disqualified where his **original political party merges** with another political party, and under **Paragraph 4(2)**, such merger of the original political party shall be deemed to have taken place *if, and only if*, **not less than two-thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned have agreed to such merger**. If a valid 2/3rd merger occurs, **both** the two-thirds majority who join the new merged party **and** the remaining minority (up to one-third) who refuse to accept the merger and opt to function as a separate group are protected from disqualification.
    3.  **15% Ceiling on the Size of the Council of Ministers (Articles 75(1A) and 164(1A)):** To eliminate the lure of ministerial berths for potential defectors, the 91st Amendment inserted **Article 75(1A)** (capping the total number of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, in the Union Council of Ministers at **15% of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha**) and **Article 164(1A)** (capping the State Council of Ministers, including the Chief Minister, at **15% of the total strength of the Legislative Assembly**, with a **minimum floor of 12 Ministers** for smaller States).
    4.  **Bar on Ministerial Office and Remunerative Political Posts (Articles 75(1B), 164(1B), and 361B):** Any legislator disqualified under the Tenth Schedule is simultaneously disqualified from being appointed as a **Minister** (**Articles 75(1B) and 164(1B)**) or from holding any **remunerative political post** (**Article 361B**—defined as any office under the Central or State Government or a public body where the salary or remuneration is paid out of public revenue) from the date of disqualification until the expiry of their term of office as a member, **or until they contest a subsequent election and are declared re-elected, whichever is earlier**.

### 9. Judicial Review over the Speaker-Tribunal: Kihoto Hollohan, Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil, and Keisham Meghachandra Singh

When the 52nd Amendment enacted the Tenth Schedule, it attempted to vest absolute, unreviewable finality in the Presiding Officer through two insulating provisions: **Paragraph 6(1)** declared that the decision of the Chairman or the Speaker on whether a member has become subject to disqualification *"shall be final"* (**Paragraph 6(2)** further deemed all Tenth Schedule proceedings to be internal *"proceedings in Parliament"* within the meaning of **Article 122** or **Article 212**), and **Paragraph 7** explicitly ousted the jurisdiction of all courts: *"Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution, no court shall have any jurisdiction in respect of any matter connected with the disqualification of a member of a House under this Schedule."*

#### The Watershed 5-Judge Verdict in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)
In **Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992 — 5-Judge Constitution Bench, 3:2)**, the constitutional validity of the entire **52nd Amendment** and the **Tenth Schedule** was challenged. The majority judgment (authored by Justice M.N. Venkatachaliah for himself, K. Jayachandra Reddy, and S.C. Agrawal, JJ.) vs. the powerful dissent (authored by Justice J.S. Verma for himself and L.M. Sharma, J.) established the foundational framework of Tenth Schedule jurisprudence:
*   **Freedom of Speech vs. Anti-Defection:** The 5-Judge Bench unanimously held that the substantive provisions of **Paragraph 2** do not violate the basic structure of parliamentary democracy or the freedom of speech and vote under **Articles 105(2) and 194(2)**, because a political party acts as a collective conduit for popular representation and unprincipled floor-crossing subverts the democratic mandate. However, to reconcile intra-party dissent with **Paragraph 2(1)(b)**, the Court read down the reach of party whips, observing that disqualifying directions under **Para 2(1)(b)** should primarily govern votes crucial to the survival of the Government (votes of confidence or no-confidence) or core integral planks of the electoral manifesto on which the party sought the mandate.
*   **The Speaker as a Quasi-Judicial "Tribunal" under Paragraph 6(1):** The Court held that while resolving disputes under **Paragraph 6(1)**, the Speaker or Chairman exercises **judicial power to adjudicate rights and liabilities** and therefore acts as a statutory **Tribunal**. The finality clause in **Paragraph 6(1)** and the deeming fiction in **Paragraph 6(2)** (linking proceedings to **Articles 122/212**) protect the Speaker's proceedings only from judicial interference on grounds of *procedural irregularity*; they **do not oust the constitutional judicial review** of the Supreme Court under **Article 136** or the High Courts under **Articles 226 and 227**. Once a final order is passed by the Speaker-Tribunal, it is fully amenable to judicial review on four classic administrative law grounds: **violation of constitutional mandates, *mala fides*, non-compliance with the rules of natural justice, and perversity**.
*   **Striking Down Paragraph 7 via the Doctrine of Severability (and the 3:2 Split):** The Court unanimously held that **Paragraph 7**, by purporting to strip the Supreme Court and High Courts of their judicial review powers under **Articles 136, 226, and 227**, directly curtailed the powers of the judiciary listed in the entrenched federal clauses of the **Proviso to Article 368(2)** and violated the **Basic Structure Doctrine**. Because the 52nd Amendment had **never been sent to half of the State Legislatures for ratification** under the **Proviso to Article 368(2)**, **Paragraph 7** was unconstitutional. Here, the Bench split 3:2:
    *   **The 3-Judge Majority** applied the **Doctrine of Severability**, severing and striking down **Paragraph 7** alone while upholding the remaining provisions of the Tenth Schedule.
    *   **The 2-Judge Dissent (Verma and Sharma, JJ.)** held that the entire Tenth Schedule was void *ab initio* for want of **Article 368(2) Proviso** state ratification, and further held that **investing adjudicatory authority in the Speaker violated the Basic Structure principle of an independent adjudicator**, because the Speaker's own tenure under **Article 94(c) / Article 179(c)** depends on the continuous support of the majority party in the House, creating an inherent structural bias.
*   **The *Quia Timet* Bar on Pre-Decisional Judicial Interference:** Crucially, the *Kihoto Hollohan* majority ruled that judicial review is **not available at a stage prior to the making of a final decision by the Speaker/Chairman**, nor is *quia timet* (preventive interlocutory) action permissible except in grave cases of immediate, irreversible interlocutory suspension that has the effect of permanently altering the composition of the House.

#### Resignation vs. Disqualification: Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2019)
When *Kihoto Hollohan* barred courts from intervening *before* the Speaker passes a final order, and the **91st Amendment** barred disqualified defectors from becoming Ministers until re-elected (**Article 164(1B)**), ruling coalitions engineered a new bypass: **"Operation Lotus" resignations**. Instead of voting against the Government on the floor (which triggers disqualification), enough ruling MLAs abruptly tender their **voluntary resignations** under **Article 190(3)(b)** to artificially lower the effective strength of the Assembly, topple the incumbent government in a trust vote, get immediately inducted as Ministers in the new government without waiting for by-elections (since a member who *resigns* is not barred by **Article 164(1B)**, unlike a member who is *disqualified*), and then contest the subsequent by-elections within six months (**Article 164(4)**).

In **Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2019 — 3-Judge Bench)**, 17 Congress and JD(S) MLAs in Karnataka tendered resignations while disqualification petitions under **Para 2(1)(a)** were pending against them. The Speaker rejected their resignations and not only disqualified all 17 MLAs under the Tenth Schedule, but further ordered that they remain **disqualified from contesting any election until the end of the term of the 15th Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2023)**. The Supreme Court laid down a two-part synthesis:
1.  **Resignation Does Not Wipe Out Prior Disqualification:** A voluntary resignation under **Article 190(3)(b)** (where the Speaker's inquiry is restricted solely to verifying whether the resignation is *"voluntary and genuine"* under the Proviso added by the **33rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1974**) is constitutionally distinct from disqualification under the **Tenth Schedule**. If acts amounting to *"voluntarily giving up party membership"* occurred **prior** to the tendering of the resignation, the Speaker retains full jurisdiction to adjudicate and disqualify the member under the Tenth Schedule despite the subsequent resignation.
2.  **Speaker Cannot Impose a Fixed-Term Bar on Contesting By-Elections:** However, the Court **struck down the portion of the Speaker's order barring the MLAs until the end of the Assembly's term**. The Speaker-Tribunal's jurisdiction under **Paragraph 6** ends the moment it decides whether a member is disqualified; the consequences of disqualification are exhaustively governed by **Articles 164(1B) and 361B**, which explicitly empower a disqualified MLA to contest the immediate by-election and become a Minister once **"re-elected."** The Speaker cannot rewrite **Article 164(1B)** by fixing a punitive tenure ban.

#### Curbing the Speaker's "Pocket Veto": Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020)
The flip side of the *Kihoto Hollohan* bar on pre-decisional judicial interference was the **Speaker's Pocket Veto**: when opposition MLAs defected to the ruling party (even being sworn in as Cabinet Ministers while remaining formally on the opposition party's roster), the partisan Speaker simply sat on the disqualification petitions for three to five years until the entire term of the Legislative Assembly expired, rendering the Tenth Schedule a dead letter.

In **Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Hon'ble Speaker Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020 — 3-Judge Bench)**, where a Congress MLA in Manipur joined the BJP and became a Forest Minister in 2017 while the Speaker refused to decide the disqualification petition for nearly three years, Justice Rohinton Fali Nariman delivered two major interventions:
*   **Three-Month Outer Timeline for Speaker's Decision:** Harmonizing the *quia timet* bar of *Kihoto Hollohan* with the constitutional mandate against sitting indefinitely on petitions (and drawing upon **Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya, 2007**, where a 5-Judge Bench held that *failure to exercise jurisdiction* is itself a ground for judicial review), the Supreme Court held that **barring exceptional circumstances for which there is good reason, disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule must be decided by the Speaker within a period of THREE MONTHS** from the date on which the petition is filed (in that case, granting the Manipur Speaker four weeks to decide, and when he still failed to comply, the Supreme Court exercised its extraordinary powers under **Article 142** to directly restrain the Minister from entering the Assembly or holding ministerial office).
*   **Call for a Constitutional Amendment to Create an Independent Tribunal:** Acknowledging that Justice J.S. Verma's minority warning in *Kihoto Hollohan (1992)* had been completely vindicated by 28 years of partisan abuse by Speakers, the Court urged Parliament to amend the Constitution to **strip the Speaker of adjudicatory powers under the Tenth Schedule** and vest them in a **permanent Independent Tribunal** headed by a retired Supreme Court Judge or a retired Chief Justice of a High Court.

In June–July 2022, the constitutional architecture of the Tenth Schedule experienced its most sophisticated institutional stress test during the **Maharashtra Shiv Sena crisis**, culminating in the landmark **5-Judge Constitution Bench verdict in Subhash Desai v. #### The Five Core Holdings of Subhash Desai v. Governor of Maharashtra (2023)
1.  **Supremacy of the "Political Party" Over the "Legislature Party" in Appointing the Whip:** The rebel faction led by Eknath Shinde argued that because it commanded the support of 34 out of 55 Shiv Sena MLAs inside the Maharashtra Legislative Assembly, it constituted the majority of the **Legislature Party** and therefore possessed the legal authority to remove the existing Chief Whip (Sunil Prabhu, appointed by the Uddhav Thackeray-led **Political Party**) and appoint its own Chief Whip (Bharat Gogawale) under **Paragraph 2(1)(b)**. The 5-Judge Bench **unanimously rejected this argument**, holding that to read *"political party"* in **Paragraph 2(1)(b)** as *"legislature party"* would completely gut the Tenth Schedule:
    *   Under **Paragraph 1(b)** and **Paragraph 1(c)** of the Tenth Schedule, a **"Legislature Party"** (the group of all members of a House belonging to a party) is textually and conceptually distinct from the **"Original Political Party"** (the broader organizational party registered under **Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951** that fields candidates at the election).
    *   The Legislature Party has no independent corporate identity severed from the Political Party. Therefore, **the Direction (Whip) and the Leader of the Party in the House must be appointed by the Political Party, not by a numerical majority of the MLAs in the Legislature Party**. The newly elected Speaker's decision recognizing the rebel faction's Whip without conducting an inquiry into who was authorized by the *Original Political Party* was declared **illegal and unconstitutional**.
2.  **Inability to Plead "Split" as a Defense After the 91st Amendment:** The Court reaffirmed that after the deletion of **Paragraph 3** by the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003**, the existence of a "split" or ideological faction within a party—no matter how numerically overwhelming inside the Assembly—is **zero defense** against disqualification under **Paragraph 2(1)(a) or (b)**. Unless a faction formally **merges** its original political party with another political party supported by at least two-thirds of the legislature party under **Paragraph 4**, any breakaway faction remains fully liable to disqualification.
3.  **Concurrent Jurisdiction of the Speaker (Tenth Schedule) and the Election Commission (Para 15 of Symbols Order, 1968):** When two rival factions each claim to *be* the original political party, two distinct constitutional forums are triggered: the **Speaker** adjudicates individual disqualifications under **Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule** (based on the factual position existing at the time the alleged defection occurred), while the **Election Commission of India (ECI)** adjudicates which faction is entitled to the party name and symbol under **Paragraph 15 of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968** (as upheld in **Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India, 1972**). The 5-Judge Bench held that **the ECI cannot be barred from deciding a Paragraph 15 symbol dispute merely because Tenth Schedule disqualification petitions are pending before the Speaker**, as a freeze on the ECI would deprive the party of its symbol during intervening by-elections. However, the Court issued a stern constitutional caveat: in deciding a Para 15 dispute, the ECI need not rely exclusively on the "test of majority in the legislative wing" where those very legislators face pending Tenth Schedule disqualification proceedings, and must evaluate organizational tests and party constitutions.
4.  **Referral of *Nabam Rebia (2016)* to a 7-Judge Constitution Bench:** In **Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker, Arunachal Pradesh Legislative Assembly (2016 — 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court had held that once a notice of intention to move a resolution for the removal of the Speaker is given under **Article 179(c)**, it passes a **constitutional hiatus** disabling the Speaker from adjudicating any disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule until the removal motion is defeated on the floor (reasoning that a Speaker who lacks the confidence of the House should not alter the composition of the House to save his own chair). In Maharashtra, the rebels immediately weaponized *Nabam Rebia* by sending an unverified email notice of removal against the Deputy Speaker to freeze his hands from disqualifying them. In **Subhash Desai (2023)**, the 5-Judge Bench observed that *Nabam Rebia* failed to consider the countervailing constitutional peril: **allowing defectors to paralyze the Tenth Schedule simply by issuing a mechanical removal notice against the Speaker directly conflicts with the 5-Judge ruling in *Kihoto Hollohan (1992)***. Because a 5-Judge Bench cannot overrule a coordinate 5-Judge Bench, the Court **referred *Nabam Rebia (2016)* to a larger 7-Judge Constitution Bench** to settle whether the issuance of an **Article 94(c) / Article 179(c)** notice automatically suspends the Speaker's Tenth Schedule jurisdiction.
5.  **Gubernatorial Restraint on Floor Tests and the Bar on Restoring a Resigned Chief Minister:** Examining Governor Bhagat Singh Koshyari's June 2022 directive under **Article 175(2)** requiring Chief Minister Uddhav Thackeray to prove his majority on the floor of the House, the 5-Judge Bench held that **the Governor had no objective constitutional material indicating that the incumbent Chief Minister had lost the confidence of the House**. Relying on intra-party dissatisfaction, security threat letters by rebel MLAs, or a resolution by a faction of a coalition partner to order a floor test violates **S.R. Bommai (1994)** and **Shivraj Singh Chouhan (2020)**: **the Floor Test is an instrument to test the confidence of the House in the Government, NEVER a weapon to settle intra-party leadership disputes**. However, because Chief Minister Uddhav Thackeray **voluntarily resigned prior to facing the Floor Test**, the Court held that it could not quash a resignation that was never forced by an actual vote on the floor, and therefore **declined to restore the *status quo ante***. (Subsequently, in January 2024, when Maharashtra Speaker Rahul Narwekar dismissed all cross-petitions by treating the Shinde faction as the "real" political party based on legislative majority, the Supreme Court initiated fresh scrutiny of the Speaker's compliance with the *Subhash Desai* ratios).

### 11. Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 10: From Legislative Self-Regulation to Judicial Supremacy

Every structural fault line examined across Parts 8 and 9—whether the Speaker's certification of a **Money Bill** under **Article 110(3)** to bypass the Rajya Sabha, the clash between uncodified **Parliamentary Privileges** under **Articles 105/194** and citizen liberties under **Article 21**, the stripping of legislative bribery immunity in **Sita Soren (2024)**, or the transformation of the partisan Speaker into a judicially reviewable **Tribunal** under the **Tenth Schedule** in **Kihoto Hollohan (1992)** and **Subhash Desai (2023)**—demonstrates a single overarching constitutional trajectory: **the failure of legislative self-regulation inevitably triggers the expansion of judicial review**. In the Indian constitutional design, Parliament is sovereign within its legislative domain, yet it is a *constituted authority* bounded by a written text whose ultimate interpreter is the **Higher Judiciary**. We now transition directly into **Part 10: The Higher Judiciary: Jurisdiction, Collegium Appointments, Judicial Independence, and Accountability**, where we deconstruct the institutional architecture of the Supreme Court (**Articles 124–147**) and the High Courts (**Articles 214–231**), the evolution of the Collegium across the **Four Judges Cases**, and the plenary reach of **Articles 32, 131, 136, 141, and 142**.
