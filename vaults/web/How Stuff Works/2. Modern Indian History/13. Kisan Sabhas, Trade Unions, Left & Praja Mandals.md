# Part 13: Agrarian Radicalism (Kisan Sabhas), Working-Class Trade Unions, the Communist/Socialist Left, Princely Praja Mandals, and Socialist Realism (1920s–1940s)

## I. The Political Economy of Agrarian Radicalism and the Kisan Sabha Trajectory (1920–1939)

### 1. Post-WWI Rent-Squeeze, Taluqdari Feudalism, and Early Peasant Mobilizations (1920–1928)

#### The Awadh Taluqdari Matrix: Baba Ramchandra, Oudh Kisan Sabha (1920), and Madari Pasi’s Eka Movement (1921–22)

The structural genesis of organized peasant radicalism in the interwar period lay in the collision between post-World War I commodity inflation and the regressive statutory architecture of colonial landlordism. In Awadh (United Provinces), the post-1858 conservative alliance between the British Raj and the **Taluqdars**—codified under the **Oudh Rent Act of 1886**—had stripped the vast majority of cultivators of hereditary occupancy rights. Over 78% of Awadh peasants were reduced to statutory tenants-at-will holding precarious seven-year leases. Because the statutory cap limited legal rent enhancements to 6.25% (one anna in the rupee) every seven years, Taluqdars bypassed the law by systematically refusing rent receipts, evicting tenants at the end of the seven-year term (**Bedakhli**), and auctioning the vacated holdings to the highest bidder against exorbitant upfront renewal premiums (**Nazrana**). Simultaneously, landlords extracted dozens of illegal feudal cesses (**Abwabs** or **Lag-Bag**)—including **Motorawan** (a levy to purchase the Taluqdar’s motorcar), **Hathiawan** (elephant tax), **Murda-faroshi** (corpse-burning tax), and **Pakwan**—while enforcing unpaid compulsory labour (**Hari** and **Begar**) on the landlord’s home-farm (**Sir** land). When post-1918 wartime inflation doubled the prices of salt, kerosene, and coarse cloth, Taluqdars escalated **Nazrana** and **Bedakhli** to capture the inflationary surplus, pushing the lower-caste Kurmi, Pasi, and Ahir peasantry to the brink of subsistence collapse.

*   **Baba Ramchandra and the Grassroots Panchayat Network (1918–1920):** Born Sridhar Balwant Jodhpurkar, a Maharashtrian Brahmin who had worked for thirteen years as an indentured labourer (**Girmitiya**) in Fiji before settling in the Pratapgarh, Rae Bareli, and Sultanpur districts of Awadh, **Baba Ramchandra** bypassed elite legalistic petitions by deploying vernacular religious idioms for anti-feudal mobilization. Moving from village to village reciting verses from Tulsidas’s **Ramcharitmanas**, he transformed the salutations "*Sita-Ram*" into an instant collective mobilization alarm that could summon thousands of peasants within hours. He organized caste and village panchayats to enforce **Nai-Dhobi Bandh** (social boycotts denying barbers, washermen, and agricultural labourers to oppressive Taluqdars and their **Ziladars**) and led a historic peasant march on foot from Pratapgarh to Allahabad in June **1920**, drawing **Jawaharlal Nehru** directly into the agrarian hinterland.
*   **Formation of the Oudh Kisan Sabha (October 1920):** When the UP Kisan Sabha (founded in February 1918 by **Gauri Shankar Mishra**, **Indra Narayan Dwivedi**, and **Madan Mohan Malaviya**) split over the Congress decision to launch Non-Cooperation, **Baba Ramchandra**, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Gauri Shankar Mishra**, **Mata Badal Pande**, **Kedar Nath**, and **Deo Narayan Pande** established the radical **Oudh Kisan Sabha** at Pratapgarh on **October 17, 1920**. Within a month, the organization integrated **330 grassroots Kisan Sabhas**, culminating in a massive rally of over 100,000 peasants at Ayodhya in December 1920. Its core charter mandated that peasants refuse to cultivate **Bedakhli** land from which a fellow peasant had been evicted, refuse all **Hari**, **Begar**, and **Nazrana**, and settle all internal disputes through village panchayats rather than colonial courts. By January 1921, spontaneous peasant action in Rae Bareli (Fursatganj and Munshiganj firings), Faizabad, and Sultanpur escalated into direct seizures of Taluqdari granaries and the burning of **Karinda** (estate agent) account books, prompting the colonial state to pass the **Seditious Meetings Act** and hurriedly enact the **Oudh Rent (Amendment) Act of 1921**, which replaced seven-year leases with life-tenancies in exchange for enhanced rents.
*   **Madari Pasi and the Eka Movement (1921–1922):** As Congress leaders urged restraint and non-violence against Indian landlords, a second, more autonomous wave of peasant radicalism erupted in late **1921** across the northern Awadh districts of **Hardoi**, **Bahraich**, **Sitapur**, and **Kheri**, where **Thekadars** (revenue contractors who leased collection rights from Taluqdars) extracted rents exceeding 50% above the recorded revenue rate. Known as the **Eka** (Unity) Movement and led by subaltern leaders **Madari Pasi** and **Sahreb** from the untouchable Pasi and low-caste Ahir communities, the movement combined anti-colonial political consciousness with militant anti-feudal resistance. At village assemblies, peasants stood beside a pit representing the Ganga, paid a one-pice ritual fee to a priest, and took a solemn oath: to pay *only* the recorded statutory rent (**Parcha** rate) on time, to demand written rent receipts, to refuse to vacate **Bedakhli** land, to pay zero **Nazrana** or illegal cesses, to perform no **Begar**, and to abide strictly by the **Eka** panchayat. Because **Madari Pasi** did not exclude retaliatory violence against Taluqdari thugs and **Thekadars**, the movement lost Congress organizational patronage, enabling colonial police and military columns using armoured cars to suppress the insurgency and arrest Madari Pasi by mid-**1922**.

#### Ryotwari Anti-Enhancement Struggles: N.G. Ranga’s Andhra Ryots Association and Sardar Patel’s Bardoli Satyagraha (1928)

Whereas peasant insurgency in the Gangetic plains targeted the parasitic intermediary layer of Taluqdars and Zamindars, agrarian mobilization in the **Ryotwari** tracts of Madras and Bombay Presidencies directly confronted the colonial fiscal apparatus over periodic thirty-year revenue resettlements, arbitrary soil-reclassifications, and water-rate (**Fasli Jasti**) surcharges. Simultaneously, in the large **Zamindari** estates embedded within the Madras Presidency, peasants faced arbitrary forest-grazing fees (**Pullari**) and summary rent enhancements without survey records.

*   **N.G. Ranga and the Andhra Ryots Association:** Trained at Oxford where he absorbed Fabian socialism and Marxist agrarian theory, **Prof. N.G. Ranga** returned to coastal Andhra in the late 1920s to organize both the Ryotwari peasantry of the Krishna-Godavari deltas and the oppressed tenants of southern Zamindari estates. Building upon the earlier district ryot associations established in Guntur (1923), **N.G. Ranga** and **Maganti Bapineedu** consolidated the **Andhra Provincial Ryots Association** in **1928** (headquartered at Guntur/Vijayawada) and founded the **Andhra Zamindari Ryots Association** in **1929** (leading militant anti-feudal agitations in the **Venkatagiri**, **Munagala**, and **Challapalli** estates). Recognizing that peasant movements required an ideologically trained rural cadre, Ranga established the pioneering **Indian Peasant Institute** at **Nidubrolu** (Guntur district) in **1933**, which trained hundreds of peasant organizers across South India in colonial revenue law, anti-debt mobilization, and socialist political economy.
*   **Sardar Patel and the Bardoli Satyagraha (1928):** The archetype of disciplined, cross-class anti-revenue mobilization occurred in the **Bardoli** taluka of Surat district (Bombay Presidency). In January **1926**, British Settlement Officer **M.S. Jayakar** recommended a **30% enhancement** in the land revenue assessment of Bardoli (subsequently modified by the Bombay Government to **21.97%** in July 1927), basing his calculations on inflated post-WWI cotton prices while ignoring the subsequent price crash and rising cultivation costs. Invited by local constructive workers (**Kunwarji Mehta**, **Kalyanji Mehta**, and **Dayalji Desai** of the *Patidar Yuvak Mandal*), **Vallabhbhai Patel** assumed command in February **1928**, demanding either an independent, impartial judicial tribunal to examine the revenue calculations or the government’s acceptance of the existing revenue rate.
*   **Organizational Mechanics and the Kaliparaj–Ujliparaj Coalition:** Patel divided the 137 villages of Bardoli taluka into **13 Chhavanis** (workers’ camps), each commanded by seasoned lieutenants (**Darbar Gopaldas**, **Ravishankar Vyas**, **Mohanlal Pandya**, **Abbas Tyabji**, and **Imam Saheb**), backed by a daily intelligence and publicity bulletin, the **Bardoli Satyagraha Patrika**, edited by **Jugatram Dave** with a circulation of 10,000 copies. Crucially, Congress constructive workers since 1922 had run Ashrams among the **Kaliparaj** ("black-skinned people"—indigenous Dubla/Halpati tribal agricultural labourers bound in debt-servitude as **Halis** to upper-caste **Ujliparaj** Patidar, Bania, and Brahmin landowners), renaming them **Raniparaj** ("forest-dwellers") by Mahatma Gandhi in 1927. This prevented the British collector from splitting the movement along class/caste lines.
*   **Gendered Mobilization and the Broomfield-Maxwell Settlement:** Women formed the backbone of the resistance—led by **Mithuben Petit**, **Maniben Patel**, **Bhaktiba Desai**, **Shardaben Mehta**, and **Maniben Desai**—composing Gujarati *Bhajans*, picketing liquor shops, and persuading peasants to remain locked inside their homes with their cattle for months to block **Japti** (attachment) officers from seizing buffaloes and utensils. It was the peasant women of Bardoli who bestowed the title **"Sardar"** upon Vallabhbhai Patel. When village headmen (**Patels**) and accountants (**Talatis**) resigned en masse, a total social boycott denied food, transport, and labour to revenue officials, and eight members of the Bombay Legislative Council resigned in protest, Viceroy Lord Irwin forced the Bombay government to capitulate in August **1928**. The resulting **Broomfield-Maxwell Committee** (comprising Judicial Officer **R.S. Broomfield** and Revenue Officer **R.M. Maxwell**) conducted an empirical village-by-village inquiry, concluded that the original assessment was fundamentally flawed, slashed the revenue enhancement from 21.97% down to **6.03%**, and restored all confiscated **Khalsa** lands and auctioned buffaloes to the original owners.

### 2. The Great Depression Price Crash, Zamindari Coercion, and Institutional Kisan Consolidation (1929–1939)

#### Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, the Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (1929), and the Bihar Bakasht Land Agitation

The structural catalyst that transformed localized peasant grievances into an ideological, pan-Indian class movement was the **Great Depression of 1929–1934**. Across British India, wholesale agricultural commodity prices collapsed by over **50%** between 1929 and 1933, while colonial monetary policy—pegging the Rupee at an overvalued **1s 6d Sterling** ratio to protect British remittances—refused to devalue the currency or scale down fixed cash revenue and rent obligations. Consequently, the real burden of land revenue, Zamindari rents, canal water rates, and moneylender (**Mahajan/Sahukar**) interest doubled in physical terms. In Permanently Settled **Bihar**—where Zamindars paid a tiny, permanently fixed sum to the British state while extracting up to eight times that amount from tenants through cash rents, produce rents (**Danabandi** and **Bhaoli**), and illegal cesses (**Abwabs**)—hundreds of thousands of occupancy tenants (**Raiyats**) defaulted on rent during the Depression. Zamindars utilized colonial courts to execute mass eviction decrees, purchasing the defaulted holdings at nominal auction prices and converting them into **Bakasht** land (literally "under the cultivation of the landlord"). The dispossessed peasants were then forced to cultivate their own ancestral plots as precarious sharecroppers (**Bataidars**) without occupancy rights.

*   **Swami Sahajanand Saraswati and the Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS, 1929):** Originally a Dashnami Sanyasi from Ghazipur (UP) who had organized Bhumihar Brahmins before establishing his ashram at **Bihta** (Patna district), **Swami Sahajanand Saraswati** rapidly transcended caste reform to become India’s foremost revolutionary peasant tribune. In **November 1929**, at the **Sonepur Mela**, he founded the **Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS)** as its President, with **Sri Krishna Sinha** as Secretary, supported by **Jamuna Karjee**, **Jadunandan Sharma**, **Karyanand Sharma**, and **Panchanan Sharma**. Initially formed to defeat a proposed government Tenancy Amendment Bill that favored landlords, the BPKS was radicalized after 1933 through its tactical alliance with the newly formed **Congress Socialist Party (CSP)** (**Jayaprakash Narayan**, **Rambriksh Benipuri**, **Ganga Sharan Sinha**, and **Awadheshwar Prasad Singh**). By 1935, the BPKS had adopted the fundamental demand for the **complete abolition of the Zamindari system without compensation**, growing to **80,000 members in 1935** and **250,000 members by 1938**—making it the largest provincial peasant organization in India.
*   **The Bihar Bakasht Land Agitation (1936–1939):** When the Congress formed a provincial ministry in Bihar under Premier **Sri Krishna Sinha** in July **1937**, peasant expectations surged. However, because the Bihar Congress leadership was heavily penetrated by small and medium landlords, the ministry signed a formal **Congress-Zamindar Pact** (brokered by **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** and **Rajendra Prasad**), diluting tenancy reforms and excluding lands auctioned after 1937 or held by "petty Zamindars" from restoration. In response, the BPKS launched direct physical Satyagraha—armed with the peasant **Lathi** (*Danda*) as a symbol of self-defence ("*Kisanon ki Lathi Zindabad*" / "*Kaise loge malguzari, lathi hamari zindabad*")—in which peasants marched onto **Bakasht** lands to forcibly sow and harvest the crops against the Zamindars’ private militias (**Lathiyals**) and the Congress Ministry’s police:
    1.  **Barahiya Tal (Munger District):** Led by **Karyanand Sharma**, peasants resisted the powerful Zamindars of Barahiya across thousands of acres of inundated *Tal* pulse-growing tracts from **1936 to 1939**, forcing district authorities into partial arbitration.
    2.  **Reora and Majiawan (Gaya District):** Led by **Yadunandan Sharma** in **1938–1939**, where peasants fought the rapacious **Bhaoli** (appraised produce-rent) and **Bakasht** expropriations of the Tekari Raj and local landlords, eventually winning the restitution of roughly 850 bighas of Bakasht land.
    3.  **Amwari (Saran District) and Darbhanga/Champaran:** Led by the polymath Buddhist scholar and Marxist revolutionary **Rahul Sankrityayan** ( alongside **Nagarjun** and **Ramnandan Mishra**) in **early 1939**. At Amwari, **Rahul Sankrityayan** commenced harvesting sugarcane on disputed Bakasht land, was brutally struck on the head by a Zamindar’s *Lathiyal*, and was arrested while bleeding profusely by the Congress government’s police—crystallizing the profound class rupture between the right-wing Congress ministerial leadership and the militant Kisan Sabha movement.

#### The All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS, Lucknow April 1936) and the Agrarian Manifesto

The convergence of provincial peasant movements in Bihar, Andhra, United Provinces, Punjab (**Baba Sohan Singh Bhakna**, **Teja Singh Swatantar**, and the *Kirti Kisan* network), Bengal (**Muzaffar Ahmad**, **Bankim Mukherjee**), Malabar (**E.M.S. Namboodiripad**, **P. Krishna Pillai**, **K.A. Keraleeyan** mobilizing against **Jenmi** landlords), and Central Provinces culminated in the creation of a unified national peasant federation.

*   **Foundation of the All India Kisan Sabha (April 11, 1936):** Following a preparatory meeting of Congress Socialists and peasant leaders at **Meerut** in January 1936 (presided over by **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** and **Jayaprakash Narayan**), the first session of the **All India Kisan Congress**—shortly renamed the **All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)**—was convened at **Lucknow** on **April 11, 1936**, coinciding with the Lucknow Session of the Indian National Congress presided over by **Jawaharlal Nehru**. **Swami Sahajanand Saraswati** was elected its first President and **N.G. Ranga** its General Secretary, with active participation from **Indulal Yagnik** (who edited the **All India Kisan Bulletin**), **Mohanlal Gautam**, **Sohan Singh Josh**, **Jayaprakash Narayan**, and **Acharya Narendra Deva**. September 1 was declared **All-India Kisan Day**.
*   **The AIKS Manifesto (August 1936) and the Faizpur Congress Impact:** Formulated at Bombay in August **1936**, the **Kisan Manifesto** bifurcated peasant demands into a *Minimum Programme* (immediate 50% cut in rent and revenue, abolition of all feudal levies and **Begar**, a five-year moratorium on agricultural debts, restitution of **Bakasht** and forfeited lands, graduated agricultural income tax on landlords, and recognition of Kisan Sabhas for collective bargaining) and a *Maximum Programme* (complete abolition of **Zamindari**, **Taluqdari**, **Malguzari**, **Inamdari**, and **Jenmi** landlordism without compensation, and vesting of land ownership directly in the tillers). This radical pressure directly compelled the Indian National Congress at its **December 1936 Faizpur Session** (where Kisan marchers walked 200 miles from Mulshi to the session venue) to adopt its first comprehensive **Thirteen-Point Faizpur Agrarian Programme**, promising substantial rent and revenue reductions, abolition of feudal levies, and fixity of tenure, though stopping short of Zamindari abolition. In October **1937**, at its Niyamatpur (Gaya) committee meeting, the AIKS formally adopted the **Red Flag (Lal Jhanda)** with the hammer and sickle as its official banner.

## II. Late-Colonial Subaltern and Armed Agrarian Insurrections (1945–1951)

### 3. Sharecropper, Adivasi, and Coir-Worker Militancy on the Eve of Transfer of Power (1945–1947)

#### Godavari Parulekar and the Warli Adivasi Revolt in Maharashtra (1945–47)

In the post-World War II conjuncture (1945–1947), as the Indian National Congress entered high-level constitutional negotiations for the Transfer of Power, Communist-led Kisan Sabhas shifted the epicentre of agrarian struggle downward from middle/rich occupancy tenants to the most marginalized strata of rural society: bonded tribal labourers (**Adivasis**), unrecorded sharecroppers (**Bargadars**), and rural agro-industrial workers. In the **Umbergaon**, **Dahanu**, and **Palghar** talukas of **Thane district** (Maharashtra), approximately 150,000 indigenous **Warli Adivasis** had been dispossessed of their forest and agricultural lands during the late nineteenth century by absentee Parsi, Marwari, Gujarati, and Brahmin landlords (**Sawkars**) and timber contractors. The Warlis were locked into a brutal regime of feudal bondage Resting on three coercive pillars:

*   **Vethbigar (Compulsory Unpaid Labour):** To retain a tiny subsistence patch of leased land, every Warli family was legally and physically coerced to plough, sow, harvest, and transport the landlord’s crops without wages for up to four months a year, while landlords leased out their own Warli tenants as unpaid human chattel to timber contractors to fell forest trees.
*   **Lagin-Gadi (Marriage-Debt Slavery):** Because destitute Warli youths possessed no cash to pay the customary bride-price and wedding expenses (amounting to mere Rs 50 to Rs 100), they borrowed the sum from the landlord. Under the **Lagin-Gadi** (*marriage-bond*) system, the bridegroom—and frequently his wife—became the hereditary bonded slave (**Gadi**) of the landlord for life, working purely for a handful of coarse grain while the principal debt descended to their children.
*   **Khand (Usurious Grain Loans) and Physical Terror:** Landlords advanced seed-paddy under the **Khand** system at compound interest rates of 150% to 200% in kind, while enforcing torture (tying Warlis to trees and flogging them with whips) and systematic sexual exploitation of Warli women through private estate overseers.
*   **The Kisan Sabha Intervention and Mass Insurrection (1945–1947):** In **May 1945**, Maharashtra Kisan Sabha leaders **Godavari Parulekar** (popularly revered by the tribals as *Godutai*, who later chronicled the struggle in her Marathi classic **Jevha Manus Jaga Hoto** — *When the Human Awakes*) and **Shamrao Parulekar** convened a historic conference of 5,000 Warlis at **Zari** (Umbergaon taluka). Adopting the Red Flag, the Warlis instantaneously abolished **Vethbigar** and **Lagin-Gadi** across the entire tract, liberating thousands of marriage-slaves within weeks. In October **1945**, when the grass-harvesting season began, 30,000 Warlis launched a total strike demanding a minimum wage of **Rs 2-8-0 for cutting 500 lbs of grass**. Despite a police firing on peaceful Warli strikers at **Talawada** (October 11, 1945) where five Adivasis died, the landlords surrendered. In **October–November 1946**, a second massive strike forced timber contractors to pay a standardized daily wage of **Rs 1-4-0 for forest tree-felling**. Even after the Bombay Congress Ministry declared a state of emergency in Thane in November 1946, externed **Godavari Parulekar**, and deployed the Maratha Light Infantry—leading to fatal police firings at **Nanivali** and **Narpad** in January 1947—the Warlis successfully shattered generational debt-slavery and forced statutory tenancy protection.

#### The Tebhaga Movement in Bengal (1946–47): Jotedar Surplus Extraction vs. Bargadar Two-Thirds Share

The largest sharecropper insurrection in modern Indian history erupted in **late 1946** across nineteen districts of Bengal. Beneath the statutory layer of the **Zamindars** and intermediate **Patnidars** created by the 1793 Permanent Settlement, actual agrarian power in the countryside—particularly in North Bengal (**Dinajpur**, **Rangpur**, **Jalpaiguri**, **Malda**) and coastal districts (**Midnapore**, **24 Parganas**, **Khulna**, **Mymensingh**)—was exercised by rich peasant-landlords known as **Jotedars**. While Jotedars held thousands of acres on low, fixed cash rents under the Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885, they sublet their lands on an oral, seasonal crop-sharing basis (**Adhiari** or **Barga**) to landless and poor peasants—predominantly **Rajbansis** (Scheduled Castes), **Santhals**, **Oraons**, **Hajongs**, and impoverished **Muslim** cultivators—known as **Bargadars**, **Adhiars**, or **Bhagchasis**.

*   **The Khamar Extraction Mechanism and the Floud Commission (1940):** Under the traditional **Adhiari** system, although the **Bargadar** bore 100% of the cultivation costs (labour, bullocks, ploughs, and manure), the harvested paddy had to be carried to and stacked inside the **Jotedar’s Khamar** (private threshing floor). Before dividing the crop 50:50, the Jotedar deducted compound interest of 50% to 100% on seed and subsistence paddy loans (**Dera-bari** and **Karja**) along with arbitrary **Abwabs** (threshing-floor watchman charges, charity levies, and weighing fees), leaving the Bargadar with barely one-fourth of the harvest. In **1940**, the **Bengal Land Revenue Commission** chaired by **Sir Francis Floud** (**Floud Commission**) officially recommended the abolition of the Permanent Settlement and stipulated that **Bargadars** be recognized as legal tenants entitled to **two-thirds (Tebhaga)** of the gross crop, leaving only **one-third** as rent to the Jotedar.
*   **Operationalizing the Tebhaga Insurrection (September 1946 – March 1947):** After the **1943 Great Bengal Famine** decimated rural sharecroppers while Jotedars hoarded rice for black-market profiteering, the Communist-led **Bengal Provincial Kisan Sabha (BPKS)**—spearheaded by **Bhowani Sen**, **Sunil Sen**, **Muzaffar Ahmad**, **Bankim Mukherjee**, **Krishna Binod Ray**, **Moni Singh** (leading the *Tanka* rent-in-kind revolt of Hajong tribals in Mymensingh), **Abani Lahiri**, and **Kansari Halder**—launched the **Tebhaga Movement** in **September 1946** during the *Aman* (winter paddy) harvest. Over **6 million peasants** across 19 districts mobilized around two revolutionary operational slogans: **"Tebhaga Chai"** (*We want two-thirds share*) and **"Nij Khamare Dhan Tolo"** (*Stack the paddy in your own threshing floor*). By harvesting the paddy collectively under the protection of volunteer **Lal Fauj** (Red Guard) squads armed with *Lathis* and carrying it to the Bargadar’s own courtyard or a collective Kisan Sabha *Khamar*, the peasants Physically broke the Jotedar’s power to make illegal deductions, subsequently sending a formal notice inviting the Jotedar to collect his one-third share.
*   **Subaltern Women’s Agency (Nari Bahini) and State Repression:** When H.S. Suhrawardy’s Muslim League Ministry dispatched armed police into the villages at the behest of Jotedars to seize the harvested paddy and arrest male Kisan Sabha activists, rural Rajbansi, Santhal, and Muslim women organized autonomous **Nari Bahini** (*Women’s Militias*). Sounding conch shells, beating brass plates, and wielding household implements—**Boti** (curved fish-cutting blades), **Gayen** (heavy wooden rice-husking levers), broomsticks, and pouches of red chilli powder—women led by figures such as **Bimala Maji** (Midnapore), **Ila Mitra** (*Rani Ma* of the Santhals in Nachole, Rajshahi), **Dipsri**, and **Pokorani** disarmed police raiding parties. At **Khanpur** village (Dinajpur) on **January 20, 1947**, Rajbansi widow **Jashoda Rani Sarkar** and Santhal leader **Chiarai Murmu** were among 22 peasants shot dead by police; at **Thumnia** and **Kakdwip** (24 Parganas, where **Kansari Halder** and **Jatin Maity** sustained the struggle until 1950), fierce clashes ensued. The uprising forced the Bengal Government to publish the **Bengal Bargadars Temporary Regulation Bill** in the official gazette on **January 22, 1947**, conceding the two-thirds principle, which ultimately laid the foundation for the **West Bengal Bargadars Act of 1950** and post-independence **Operation Barga**.

#### The Punnapra-Vayalar Uprising in Travancore (1946): Working-Class and Agrarian Armed Resistance to Autocracy

In the southern Princely State of **Travancore**, a unique socio-political fusion occurred where militant trade unionism among agro-industrial workers (coir mat weavers, coconut-husk beaters, fishermen, and toddy tappers) merged with the anti-feudal struggle of landless **Pulaya** and **Ezhava** agricultural labourers (**Kudikidappukar** tenants-at-will living on landlord homesteads) and the political campaign for responsible democratic government.

*   **Dewan C.P. Ramaswami Iyer’s "American Model" Constitution:** In January **1946**, seeking to pre-empt both popular democracy and Travancore’s accession to a democratic Indian Union, Dewan **Sir C.P. Ramaswami Iyer** announced a proposed constitutional scheme dubbed the **"American Model."** While introducing universal franchise for an advisory legislature, it vested absolute, irremovable executive authority in the Maharaja’s appointed **Dewan** (modeled superficially on the US Presidency, without democratic election or accountability to the legislature). The Communist Party and the **All-Travancore Trade Union Congress (ATTUC)** rejected the scheme with the famous slogan: **"Throw the American Model into the Arabian Sea!"**
*   **The Armed Confrontation at Punnapra and Vayalar (October 1946):** Centered in the **Ambalapuzha** and **Cherthala (Shertallai)** taluks of **Alappuzha (Alleppey)** district, the **Travancore Coir Factory Workers Union** (led by **T.V. Thomas**, **K.C. George**, **R. Sugathan**, **P. Krishna Pillai**, and **C.K. Kumara Panicker**) organized a general political and economic strike in October **1946** against acute postwar rice scarcity, landlord evictions of **Kudikidappukar** hutment dwellers, and military terror. To defend themselves against the Dewan’s Reserve Police and landlords’ thugs, thousands of workers established fortified communal camps at **Punnapra**, **Vayalar**, **Muhamma**, and **Mararikulam**, training with sharpened wooden **Areca-nut spears** (*Vayalar spears*). On **October 24, 1946** (the Maharaja’s birthday), worker volunteers armed with spears and stones stormed the armed police camp at **Punnapra**, seizing rifles at the cost of dozens of lives while killing the police camp commander. Declaring martial law across Alappuzha on October 25 and assuming supreme command of the state military, Dewan **C.P. Ramaswami Iyer** dispatched the Travancore Infantry via motor-boats to encircle the water-locked camp at **Vayalar Island** on **October 27, 1946**. Machine-gun fire mowed down between 400 and over 1,000 workers and peasants armed only with wooden spears. Although brutally crushed in blood, the **Punnapra-Vayalar Uprising** shattered the legitimacy of the Dewan’s autocratic regime, galvanized the final phase of the Travancore anti-monarchical struggle, and precluded Travancore’s bid for sovereign independence in 1947.

### 4. The Telangana Peasant Armed Struggle in Hyderabad State (1946–1951)

#### Deshmukh-Jagirdar Feudalism (Vetti and Bhagela), Sangham Guerrilla Squads, and Land Redistribution

The most sustained, geographically expansive, and structurally transformative peasant guerrilla revolution in modern South Asian history unfolded between **July 1946 and October 1951** in the Telugu-speaking **Telangana** region (primarily **Nalgonda**, **Warangal**, and **Khammam** districts) of the Princely State of **Hyderabad**. Ruled by the autocratic **Nizam Mir Osman Ali Khan**, Hyderabad’s 53 million acres of land were bifurcated into three fiscal-tenurial zones:
1.  **Diwani or Khalsa Lands (60% of area):** Under direct state revenue administration, but dominated at the village level by hereditary revenue tax-farmers turned landlords—known as **Deshmukhs** and **Deshpandes** (or *Doras* / "Lords" in Telugu)—alongside village officials (**Patels** and **Patwaris**). By manipulating land survey records (**Pahani**), individual Deshmukhs accumulated colossal estates: the **Jana Reddy Pratap Reddy** family in Suryapet owned **150,000 acres**, the **Kalluru Deshmukh** owned **100,000 acres**, and **Visnoor Ramachandra Reddy** in Jangaon controlled **40,000 acres** across forty villages.
2.  **Jagirdari, Ijaradari, and Samsthanam Lands (30% of area):** Feudal fiefdoms granted to Muslim nobles and Hindu Rajas who maintained their own private courts, police, and taxation systems untouched by state law.
3.  **Sarf-e-Khas Lands (10% of area):** The Nizam’s personal crown estate covering 8,000 square miles, whose entire revenue of Rs 20 million went directly into the Nizam’s private privy purse.

*   **The Anatomy of Vetti and Bhagela Bondage:** Across Telangana, the **Doras** (Deshmukhs and Jagirdars) enforced the **Vetti** system—a pervasive feudal institution of compulsory unpaid labour and tribute that subordinated every working caste. **Mala** and **Madiga** Dalit families were forced to supply one male daily to sweep the Deshmukh’s fortress (**Gadhi**), carry post, run in front of his bullock cart or motorcar, and cultivate his home-farm (**Khudkasht**) without payment; **Goud** (toddy-tapper) families had to surrender the best palm trees for free toddy; **Chakali** (washermen), **Mangali** (barbers), **Kummari** (potters), and **Kammari** (blacksmiths) rendered free services and utensils; and peasant families were forced to supply their bullocks and ploughs to till the landlord’s fields before they were permitted to sow their own plots after the monsoon rains. In the forest tracts of Warangal and Khammam, indigenous **Koya**, **Chenchu**, and **Lambada** tribals were bound under the **Bhagela** system—hereditary debt-serfs who signed thumb-impression bonds for petty grain or marriage loans and worked across generations for the landlord. Furthermore, landlords practiced the custom of **Adi-bapa** (taking teenage peasant girls as lifelong domestic concubines inside the *Gadhi*). During World War II, Deshmukhs also monopolized the village grain-levy committees, exempting their own hoards while confiscating the subsistence grain of small peasants.
*   **The Andhra Mahasabha, Sanghams, and the Spark at Kadavendi (1946):** Since 1930, cultural and social reform in Telangana had been led by the **Andhra Mahasabha**. At its **1941 Chilkur** and **1944 Bhongir Sessions**, young Communist leaders—**Ravi Narayana Reddy**, **Baddam Yella Reddy**, **Puchalapalli Sundarayya**, **Devulapalli Venkateswara Rao**, **Bheemireddy Narasimha Reddy**, **Arutla Ramachandra Reddy**, **Arutla Kamala Devi**, and **Mallu Swarajyam**—transformed the Andhra Mahasabha into a militant mass peasant union, organizing village cells called **Sanghams**. The first flashpoint occurred in **Palakurthi** village (Jangaon taluka, Nalgonda) in early 1946, when the dreaded **Visnoor Deshmukh (Ramachandra Reddy)** sent a hundred goons to seize the standing harvest on four acres of land leased by a fearless washerwoman, **Chakali Ailamma** (Chityala Ailamma); **Sangham** volunteers armed with *Lathis* and slings (**Vadisela**) routed the Deshmukh’s thugs and harvested Ailamma’s grain into her house. On **July 4, 1946**, in **Kadavendi** village, as a procession of peasants marched past the fortress of the Visnoor Deshmukh’s mother (Janakamma), estate gunmen opened fire from the ramparts, assassinating the young village **Sangham** leader **Doddi Komaraiah**. His martyrdom ignited a region-wide insurrection across Nalgonda, Warangal, and Khammam.
*   **Gram Rajyams, Dalams, and the One-Million-Acre Land Redistribution (1946–1948):** When the Nizam banned the Communist Party and the Andhra Mahasabha in late 1946 and unleashed both the state military and **Qasim Razvi’s** fanatical 200,000-strong paramilitary **Razakars** to burn rebel villages, the peasantry organized a three-tiered armed guerrilla architecture:
    1.  **Village Defence Squads:** Part-time peasant militias in every village armed with **Vadisela** (stone-slings), *Lathis*, spears, and country-made muzzle-loaders (**Bharamarams**).
    2.  **Destruction Squads:** Specialized units tasked with digging up roads, destroying culverts, and cutting telegraph lines to block Razakar and police trucks.
    3.  **Regular Guerrilla Dalams:** Full-time, mobile partisan squads (numbering up to 2,000 regular fighters backed by 10,000 village squad members) armed with modern `.303` rifles, Sten guns, and Bren guns captured from Nizam police stations and Razakar camps.
    By mid-**1948**, the insurgency had liberated **3,000 villages** covering **16,000 square miles (41,000 sq km)** and a population of **4 million people**. Inside this liberated zone, Deshmukhs and Patwaris fled to the cities; elected five-to-seven-member village committees (**Gram Rajyams** or **Panchayats**) burned all **Patwari** land records and moneylender bonds, abolished **Vetti** and **Bhagela** forever, raised agricultural wages, established People’s Courts, enforced strict prohibitions against wife-beating, imposed a land ceiling (initially 500 acres, rapidly lowered to **100 acres of dry land and 10 acres of wet land**), and **redistributed 1 million acres of confiscated landlord, waste, and forest land** among landless Dalits, tribals, and poor tenants.
*   **Operation Polo and the Second Phase in the Forest Sanctuaries (September 1948 – October 1951):** When the Government of India launched **Operation Polo** (September 13–17, **1948**) to overthrow the Nizam, undo the Razakar terror, and integrate Hyderabad into the Indian Union, the armed peasant squads initially cooperated in crushing Razakar strongholds. However, under the ultra-left **B.T. Ranadive line** adopted at the Second Congress of the CPI in Calcutta (February 1948)—and the **Andhra Thesis** (June 1948), which sought to replicate Mao Zedong’s Chinese path of protracted peasant guerrilla war against the newly independent Nehruvian state—the CPI refused to surrender its weapons when the Indian Army ordered restored landlords back to their villages. Under the command of **Major-General J.N. Chaudhuri** (Military Governor) and Civil Administrator **M.K. Vellodi**, over 50,000 Indian Army and armed police personnel swept the plains of Nalgonda and Warangal. Forced out of the open plains, the surviving **Dalams** retreated into the dense **Nallamala**, **Amrabad**, and **Godavari** forest belts of Khammam, Warangal, and Karimnagar, mobilizing **Koya** and **Chenchu** Adivasis for three more years. Recognizing the exhaustion of the peasantry, the isolation of the forest squads, and the Government’s passage of the **Hyderabad (Abolition of Jagirs) Regulation (1949)** and the **Hyderabad Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act (1950)** (which granted legal protection to protected tenants), the CPI leadership—following secret discussions with **Joseph Stalin** in Moscow—unconditionally withdrew the Telangana armed struggle on **October 21, 1951** to contest the **1951–52 First General Elections** (where the CPI-led People’s Democratic Front swept Nalgonda and Warangal, with **Ravi Narayana Reddy** polling more votes than Jawaharlal Nehru himself).

## III. Industrial Proletarianization, Trade Unionism, and the Colonial Statutory Containment Grid (1918–1939)

### 5. Post-WWI Inflation, the Madras Labour Union, and the Genesis of AITUC (1918–1926)

#### The Jobber-Millowner Nexus, B.P. Wadia’s Madras Labour Union (1918), and the AITUC Foundation (1920)

The structural transformation of the Indian industrial working class from sporadic, localized rioters into an organized, trade-unionized proletariat occurred in the immediate aftermath of World War I (**1918–1920**). During the war (1914–1918), the disruption of shipping lanes from Britain had granted indigenous cotton textile mills in Bombay, Ahmedabad, Madras, and Nagpur—alongside British-owned jute mills in Calcutta and railway workshops—unprecedented windfall profits ranging from 60% to 200% per annum. Simultaneously, wartime deficit financing and supply bottlenecks triggered a **70% to 100% surge in the cost of basic foodgrains and cloth**, while nominal industrial wages stagnated, causing a catastrophic collapse in real wages. Inside the colonial factory, workers were subjected to a two-tier despotic hierarchy: at the apex stood the European or Indian managing agency, while on the shop floor and in the working-class tenements (**Chawls** in Bombay, **Bustees** in Calcutta, **Cheris** in Madras), total control over hiring, firing, leave, housing, and petty credit was monopolized by the **Jobber** (**Sardar** in Bengal/Jute, **Mukadam** or **Naikin** in Bombay Cotton, **Maistry** in Madras and Plantations), who extracted regular monthly bribes (**Dasturi**) from every worker’s wage packet.

*   **B.P. Wadia and the Madras Labour Union (April 1918):** While philanthropic associations like **Sasipada Banerji’s** *Workingmen’s Club* (1870), **N.M. Lokhande’s** *Bombay Millhands Association* (1890), and the *Amalgamated Society of Railway Servants* (1897) had existed in the late nineteenth century, the **first modern trade union in India**—defined by permanent, regularized membership registers, fixed monthly subscriptions (one anna per worker), elected office-bearers, and strike relief funds—was the **Madras Labour Union**, founded on **April 27, 1918** by **B.P. Wadia** (a Parsi Theosophist, associate of Annie Besant in the Home Rule League, and assistant editor of *New India*), alongside Tamil labour activists **V. Kalyanasundaram Mudaliar (Thiru. Vi. Ka.)** and **G. Ramanujulu Naidu**. Organized among the textile workers of the British-owned **Buckingham and Carnatic (B&C) Mills** (managed by Binny & Co.) in Perambur, Madras, the union fought against racial humiliation by European supervisors, arbitrary wage fines, and the inhuman 25-minute midday recess during a 12-hour workday. During the **1920 B&C Mills lockout and strike**, Binny & Co. sued **B.P. Wadia** in the Madras High Court (*Binny & Co. vs. B.P. Wadia*), which issued an injunction against Wadia and imposed damages of **Rs 75,000** on the common-law tort ground that organizing a strike constituted a *"malicious conspiracy to induce workers to commit a breach of contract."* Although the suit was dropped after Wadia agreed to leave the union, the judgment shocked the British Trades Union Congress and exposed the complete absence of statutory legal protection for trade unions in British India.
*   **Foundation of the All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC, October 31, 1920):** Between late 1918 and 1920, an unprecedented strike wave swept every industrial centre in India—125,000 Bombay textile workers struck in January 1919, followed by 200,000 in January 1920, alongside strikes by Jamshedpur TISCO steelworkers, North-Western Railway workers, and Calcutta jute workers. The immediate institutional catalyst for a national federation was the establishment of the **International Labour Organization (ILO)** at Geneva in **1919** under the Treaty of Versailles. When the Government of India nominated **N.M. Joshi** as the Indian worker representative to the Washington ILO Conference without consulting labour organizations, nationalist and labour leaders convened at the **Empire Theatre in Bombay on October 31, 1920** to establish the **All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC)**, affiliating 64 unions with a combined membership of 140,854 workers.
*   **Leadership and Ideological Orientation of Early AITUC:** The inaugural session was presided over by the veteran Extremist nationalist **Lala Lajpat Rai**, with **Dewan Chaman Lal** as General Secretary, **Joseph Baptista** as Chairman of the Reception Committee (and Vice-President alongside **Annie Besant** and **C.F. Andrews**), and attended by **Motilal Nehru**, **M.A. Jinnah**, **Vithalbhai Patel**, **B.P. Wadia**, and **N.M. Joshi**. In his historic presidential address, **Lala Lajpat Rai** forged the foundational ideological link between anti-colonial nationalism and the working class, declaring: *"Imperialism and militarism are the twin children of capitalism; they are one and the same... Indian labour needs to organize itself along international lines."* At its subsequent sessions—**Jharia (1921)** under Joseph Baptista, **Lahore (1923)** under **C.R. Das** (who urged the Congress to organize workers and peasants directly lest "Swaraj be for the 2%" elite), and **Calcutta (1924)** under C.R. Das—the AITUC functioned as a broad platform uniting Congress nationalists, Fabian social reformers (**N.M. Joshi**, **Diwan Chaman Lal**, **V.V. Giri**), and emerging Marxists.

#### The Trade Unions Act of 1926: Statutory Regularization and Containment

Confronted by the rapid expansion of AITUC and the international scrutiny triggered by the **1920 B.P. Wadia judgment** (and persistent legislative resolutions moved by **N.M. Joshi** in the Central Legislative Assembly in 1921), the colonial state enacted the **Indian Trade Unions Act of 1926** (enforced on June 1, 1927). The statute operated as a classic colonial instrument of **dual containment**: it granted legal recognition and narrow civil/criminal immunities to "bonafide" trade unions while simultaneously erecting administrative firewalls to prevent trade unions from morphing into revolutionary anti-colonial political organizations.

*   **Registration and Corporate Personality:** Any seven or more members of a trade union could apply for registration under a provincial **Registrar of Trade Unions**, transforming the registered union into a body corporate with perpetual succession, a common seal, and the legal capacity to hold property and enter into contracts.
*   **Civil and Criminal Immunities (Sections 17 & 18):** Reversing the *Binny & Co. vs. B.P. Wadia* precedent, **Section 18** granted registered trade unions, their office-bearers, and members complete **immunity from civil suits** in respect of any act done in contemplation or furtherance of a trade dispute on the ground *only* that such act induced another person to break a contract of employment. **Section 17** granted registered unionists **immunity from criminal prosecution** for conspiracy under **Section 120-B(2) of the Indian Penal Code** for agreements made between members to further legitimate trade union objectives (unless the agreement was to commit an independent criminal offence).
*   **Statutory Restrictions on Leadership and Political Funds:** To restrict the entry of radical socialist and nationalist "outsiders," **Section 22** stipulated that **not less than one-half (50%) of the total number of office-bearers** of every registered trade union must be persons *actually engaged or employed* in an industry with which the union was connected. Furthermore, **Section 15** strictly limited the expenditure of general union funds to specified welfare and trade-dispute items; **Section 16** mandated that if a union wished to spend money for **political or civic purposes**, it had to constitute a completely separate **Political Fund** financed purely by *voluntary* contributions, from which no member could be compelled to contribute or penalized for refusing to pay.

### 6. The Militant Strike Wave, Girni Kamgar Union, and the Coercive Counter-Offensive (1928–1939)

#### The Girni Kamgar Union (1928) and the Great Bombay Textile Strike

By **1927–1928**, the Indian working-class movement entered a militant phase driven by the attempts of British and Indian capitalists to resolve their post-war profitability crisis through **"Rationalization"** and **"Standardization"** schemes—slashing wage rates, carrying out mass retrenchments, and forcing individual weavers to operate three or four looms instead of two without commensurate wage increases. In early 1928, prolonged strikes erupted on the **East Indian Railway (Liluah Workshop)**, the **South Indian Railway**, the **Jamshedpur TISCO steel plant** (where **Subhas Chandra Bose** brokered a settlement), and across the **Bombay cotton textile mills**.

*   **Rise of the Girni Kamgar Union (Lal Bavta, May 1928):** When Bombay millowners attempted to introduce the three-loom/two-frame rationalization system in April **1928**, a spontaneous general strike of **150,000 textile workers** paralyzed all 84 mills in Bombay for **six months (April to October 1928)**. While moderate unionists led by **N.M. Joshi** initially hesitated, the Communist and Workers and Peasants Party (WPP) leaders—**S.A. Dange**, **K.N. Joglekar**, **S.V. Ghate**, **R.S. Nimbkar**, **S.S. Mirajkar**, and British communist **Ben Bradley**—teamed up with proletarian millworker leaders **A.A. Alwe** and **G.R. Kasle** to register the **Girni Kamgar Union (GKU)** (*Red Flag / Lal Bavta Millworkers’ Union*) on **May 23, 1928**.
*   **Shop-Floor Mill Committees and Mass Mobilization:** Operating through a joint Strike Committee and publishing the Marathi workers’ weekly **Kranti**, the **Girni Kamgar Union** revolutionized Indian labour organization. Starting with a mere **324 members in May 1928**, the GKU’s membership exploded to **54,000 by December 1928** and over **65,000 by early 1929**, making it the largest and most militant single-industry trade union in Asia. Even after the strike ended in October 1928 with the appointment of the **Fawcett Inquiry Committee** (before which **S.A. Dange** presented a masterly technical and economic defence of the workers against the Millowners’ Association), the GKU established elected **Mill Committees** inside every individual factory. These grass-roots shop-floor committees bypassed the despotic authority of the **Jobbers** (*Mukadams*) and forced mill managers to negotiate directly with elected workers over daily piece-rates and working conditions—terrifying both the Bombay bourgeoisie and the colonial state.

#### The Trade Disputes Act (1929) and the Whitley Commission on Labour (1929)

In 1928 alone, British India experienced 203 major industrial strikes involving 506,851 workers and a staggering loss of **31,647,404 working days**—exceeding the total man-days lost during the entire preceding five years combined. To crush the revolutionary wing of the labour movement while separating moderate reformist unions from the Left, Viceroy **Lord Irwin’s** government executed a three-pronged counter-offensive in **early 1929**: launching the **Meerut Conspiracy Case** (March 1929), pushing through the draconian **Trade Disputes Act** (April 1929), and appointing the **Royal Commission on Labour (Whitley Commission)** (May 1929).

*   **The Trade Disputes Act (April 1929):** Enacted on **April 8, 1929**—the very day **Bhagat Singh** and **Batukeshwar Dutt** hurled two non-lethal smoke bombs into the Central Legislative Assembly benches to protest this statute and the Public Safety Bill—the **Trade Disputes Act of 1929** (initially enacted for five years and made permanent in **1934**) borrowed the most repressive clauses of the British Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act of 1927 (passed after the 1926 UK General Strike) without including British social welfare protections:
    1.  **Ad-Hoc Conciliation Without Enforcement:** Part I authorized the Central or Provincial Governments to refer any trade dispute to an ad-hoc **Court of Inquiry** or **Board of Conciliation**, though their findings were non-binding.
    2.  **Criminalization of Public Utility Strikes (Section 15):** It made any strike or lockout in **Public Utility Services**—explicitly defined as **Railways**, **Postal**, **Telegraph**, or **Telephone** services, any industry supplying **water, light, or power** to the public, or any system of public conservancy/sanitation—a **criminal offence** punishable with imprisonment and fines if commenced **without giving one month’s prior written notice**.
    3.  **Ban on Sympathetic, General, and Political Strikes (Section 16):** It declared any strike or lockout wholly **illegal** if it had *"any object other than the furtherance of a trade dispute within the trade or industry in which the strikers or employers are engaged"* AND was *"designed or calculated to inflict severe, general and prolonged hardship upon the community and thereby to compel the Government to take or abstain from taking any course of action."* Participants and organizers of such sympathetic or political strikes were stripped of all legal immunities under the 1926 Trade Unions Act.
*   **The Whitley Commission on Labour (1929–1931):** Appointed on **May 24, 1929** under the chairmanship of **John Henry Whitley** (former Speaker of the British House of Commons), the **Royal Commission on Labour in India** was tasked with investigating existing conditions of labour in industrial undertakings and plantations in British India, including health, efficiency, standard of living, and employer-employee relations. While moderate AITUC leaders **N.M. Joshi** and **Diwan Chaman Lal** accepted seats as members on the Commission (alongside **B. Shiva Rao**, **Kabeer-ud-Din Ahmed**, and **G.D. Birla**), the left-nationalist and Communist majority at the **December 1929 Nagpur Session of the AITUC** (presided over by **Jawaharlal Nehru**) passed a resolution **boycotting the Whitley Commission**, precipitating the walkout of N.M. Joshi and V.V. Giri to form the **Indian Trade Union Federation (ITUF)**.
*   **Diagnostic Findings and Statutory Fallout of the Whitley Report (1931):** Publishing its exhaustive report in **March 1931**, the Whitley Commission documented the structural pathologies of colonial industrialization:
    1.  **Eradication of the Jobber Monopoly:** It exposed how the **Jobber** (*Sardar/Mukadam*) controlled recruitment and extracted systematic bribes (**Dasturi**) from starving rural migrants, recommending that factories abolish Jobber recruitment and appoint qualified **Labour Officers** directly responsible to the management for transparent hiring and grievance redressal.
    2.  **Reduction of Working Hours (Factories Act 1934):** It recommended slashing the statutory weekly working hours in perennial factories from 60 hours to **54 hours per week** (with a daily cap of **10 hours** in perennial and 11 hours in seasonal factories), which was codified into the **Indian Factories Act of 1934**.
    3.  **Protection of Wages against Arbitrary Fines and Deductions (Payment of Wages Act 1936):** It exposed how millowners routinely withheld wages for weeks and deducted up to 30% of a worker’s pay as "fines" for damaged cloth or tardiness, directly leading to the **Payment of Wages Act of 1936**, which mandated prompt wage payment within 7 to 10 days and capped permissible deductions.
    4.  **Indebtedness, Housing, and Workmen’s Compensation:** Finding that over two-thirds of industrial workers were trapped in permanent debt to Pathan and Marwari moneylenders at interest rates of 75% to 150% (*Sawai*), the Commission recommended outlawing the attachment of worker wages for debt recovery, banning the besetting of factory gates by moneylenders on payday, expanding the **Workmen’s Compensation Act (amended 1933)**, and establishing permanent statutory **Industrial Councils** and Conciliation Officers.

## IV. The Communist Party, Workers and Peasants Parties, State Conspiracy Trials, and the Congress Socialist Party (1920–1940s)

### 7. The Comintern Nexus, Domestic Communist Genesis, Workers and Peasants Parties, and Bolshevik Conspiracy Cases (1920–1933)

#### M.N. Roy at Tashkent (1920), Early Domestic Communist Circles, and the Workers and Peasants Parties (WPPs)

The ideological and institutional implantation of Marxism-Leninism in India emerged from the convergence of two distinct historical streams after the **1917 Bolshevik Revolution**: first, a transnational stream of exiled Indian revolutionaries and pan-Islamic **Hijrat Muhajirs** who encountered Soviet anti-imperialism in Moscow and Central Asia; and second, an indigenous stream of left-wing Non-Cooperators, Ghadarites, and trade unionists inside Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Lahore, and Kanpur who sought a structural class alternative after the abrupt suspension of the Non-Cooperation Movement at Bardoli in February 1922.

*   **M.N. Roy, the Lenin–Roy Debate, and the Tashkent Foundation of the CPI (October 17, 1920):** Born **Narendranath Bhattacharya** in Bengal, **M.N. Roy** (Manabendra Nath Roy) had begun his career as a militant **Jugantar** revolutionary involved in the 1915 Indo-German *Zimmerman Plan* with Bagha Jatin. Escaping to the United States and then Mexico—where he founded the Mexican Communist Party (the first Communist Party outside Soviet Russia)—Roy was invited by **Vladimir Lenin** to Moscow for the **Second Congress of the Communist International (Comintern) in July–August 1920**. There, a famous theoretical debate unfolded over the **National and Colonial Question**: while **Lenin’s Preliminary Theses** argued that Communist parties in colonial countries must form temporary tactical alliances with "bourgeois-democratic liberation movements" (such as the Indian National Congress under Gandhi), **M.N. Roy’s Supplementary Theses** (adopted alongside Lenin’s by the Comintern) contended that the colonial bourgeoisie would inevitably compromise with British imperialism out of fear of agrarian and proletarian revolution, and that the Comintern must prioritize building autonomous **Communist Parties** and **Workers’ and Peasants’ Soviets** from below. Proceeding to Soviet Turkestan as head of the Comintern’s Central Asian Bureau, **M.N. Roy** established an Indian Military Training School (*Indusky Kurs*) and, on **October 17, 1920 at Tashkent**, formally founded the **Communist Party of India (CPI)** alongside **Evelyn Trent Roy**, **Abani Mukherji** (co-author with Roy of *India in Transition*, 1922), **Rosa Fitingof**, **Mohammad Ali (Ahmed Hasan)**, **Mohammad Shafiq** (elected Secretary), and **M.P.B.T. Acharya**, recruiting radicalized young **Muhajirs** (**Shaukat Usmani**, **Rafiq Ahmad**, **Ferozuddin Mansur**) who had trekked across Afghanistan during the Khilafat *Hijrat*. From Berlin and Zurich, Roy subsequently smuggled his influential Marxist journal **The Vanguard of Indian Independence** (later *The Masses of India*) into Indian ports via maritime lascars.
*   **Autonomous Domestic Communist Circles and the 1925 Kanpur Foundation:** Inside British India, four autonomous Marxist nuclei crystallized simultaneously between **1921 and 1923**:
    1.  **Bombay (S.A. Dange):** **Shripad Amrit Dange** published his pioneering comparative pamphlet **Gandhi vs. Lenin** in April **1921** (critiquing Gandhian anti-industrial trusteeship and advocating nationalization of big industry and ceilings on landholding) and launched India’s first English Marxist weekly, **The Socialist**, in August **1922**, gathering a committed circle including **S.V. Ghate**, **K.N. Joglekar**, **R.S. Nimbkar**, and **S.S. Mirajkar**.
    2.  **Calcutta (Muzaffar Ahmad):** **Muzaffar Ahmad**, working closely with the rebel poet **Kazi Nazrul Islam** and **Hemanta Kumar Sarkar**, launched the Bengali evening daily **Navayug** (1920) and later the organ **Langal** (*The Plough*, 1925) and **Ganavani** (1926).
    3.  **Madras (M. Singaravelu Chettiar):** Veteran lawyer and trade unionist **M. Singaravelu Chettiar** addressed the 1922 Gaya Congress calling yourself a communist, organized the **first celebration of May Day in India at Madras on May 1, 1923**—hoisting the **Red Flag** for the first time in India at Triplicane Beach and High Court Beach—and founded the **Labour Kisan Party of Hindustan** with its manifesto and journal *Labour Kisan Gazette*.
    4.  **Lahore (Ghulam Hussain and Inqilab):** Professor **Ghulam Hussain**, alongside **Shamsuddin Hassan**, launched the Urdu Marxist journal **Inqilab** in **1922** and organized North-Western Railway workers, parallel to the Ghadar-inspired **Kirti** movement in Punjab led by **Santokh Singh** and **Sohan Singh Josh**.
    On **December 25–28, 1925**, at the **First Indian Communist Conference in Kanpur** (convened initially by **Satyabhakta** concurrently with the Kanpur Congress Session), delegates from these four regional centres united under the presidency of **M. Singaravelu Chettiar** to establish the domestic **Communist Party of India** on Indian soil, with **S.V. Ghate** and **J.P. Bagerhatta** as General Secretaries and its central office in Bombay.
*   **The Workers and Peasants Parties (WPPs, 1925–1929):** Because the colonial intelligence apparatus (**IPI** and **DIB**) systematically intercepted Comintern funds, proscribed Marxist literature under the **Sea Customs Act**, and arrested open Communists for treason, early Indian Marxists created legal, open, dual-class political parties—the **Workers and Peasants Parties (WPPs)**—which functioned simultaneously as autonomous mass organizations of industrial workers and peasants *and* as an organized left-wing bloc working *inside* the Indian National Congress to radicalize it toward *Purna Swaraj* and socio-economic revolution:
    1.  **Bengal (1925–1926):** Founded at Krishnanagar on November 1, **1925** as the **Labour-Swaraj Party of the Indian National Congress** by **Hemanta Kumar Sarkar**, **Qazi Nazrul Islam**, **Qutubuddin Ahmad**, and **Muzaffar Ahmad**, with **Langal** ( featuring Nazrul Islam’s famous poem *Samyabadi* — "The Egalitarian") as its weekly organ; renamed the **Peasants and Workers Party of Bengal** in February **1926** (with **Ganavani** as its organ).
    2.  **Bombay (1927):** Founded in February **1927** as the **Workers and Peasants Party of Bombay** with **D.R. Thengdi** as President and **S.S. Mirajkar** as General Secretary (alongside **S.A. Dange**, **K.N. Joglekar**, and **R.S. Nimbkar**), publishing the Marathi weekly **Kranti** and spearheading the massive 1928 **Girni Kamgar Union** strike and the anti-Simon Commission demonstrations.
    3.  **Punjab (1926–1928) and UP (1928):** Founded in Amritsar/Hoshiarpur around the Punjabi journal **Kirti** (launched in Feb 1926 by Ghadar veteran **Bhai Santokh Singh** and **Bhag Singh Canadian**) as the **Kirti Kisan Party** in April **1928** under **Sohan Singh Josh** and **Abdul Majid**; followed in October **1928** by the **WPP of United Provinces and Delhi** at Meerut with **P.C. Joshi** as General Secretary.
    4.  **All-India WPP Conference (Calcutta, December 1928):** Representatives of all four provincial WPPs united in Calcutta under the presidency of **Sohan Singh Josh** to constitute the **All-India Workers and Peasants Party**, demonstrating massive street mobilization when 25,000 workers led by the WPP marched into the pandal of the 1928 Calcutta Congress Session to demand complete independence rather than the Nehru Report’s Dominion Status.

#### The Peshawar (1922–27), Kanpur Bolshevik (1924), and Meerut Conspiracy Cases (1929–33)

Viewing the spread of Bolshevism as an existential geopolitical and domestic threat to the British Empire in Asia, the colonial state deployed **Section 121-A of the Indian Penal Code** (*"Conspiracy to wage war against the King-Emperor or to deprive the King-Emperor of the sovereignty of British India"*) in three landmark judicial counter-insurgency trials during the 1920s:

*   **The Peshawar Conspiracy Cases (1922–1927):** A series of **five successive criminal trials** held at Peshawar (North-West Frontier Province) between **1922 and 1927** (frequently referred to in colonial intelligence files as the *Moscow-Tashkent Conspiracy Cases*). As young Indian **Muhajirs** trained at M.N. Roy’s Tashkent Military School and at the **Communist University of the Toilers of the East (KUTV)** in Moscow infiltrated back into India across the Pamir mountains, Chitral, and the Khyber Pass to build an underground revolutionary network, British border intelligence arrested them in batches. Prominent accused included **Mohammad Akbar Khan** (tried repeatedly in the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd Peshawar cases), **Rafiq Ahmad**, **Habib Ahmad**, **Ferozuddin Mansur**, **Abdul Majid**, **Sultan Mohammad**, **Gaurakhpuria**, and **Fazal Ilahi Qurban** (in the 1923–24 main trial), followed by **Mian Mohammad Akbar Shah** and **Gohar Rahman** (1924), and **Fazal Elahi** (1927). Sentenced to rigorous imprisonment ranging from one to seven years, their trials exposed the overland Pamir-Kabul revolutionary corridor.
*   **The Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case (May 1924):** Determined to crush the domestic Communist nuclei before they could unite, the Government of India filed a conspiracy indictment in **March–May 1924** at Kanpur against **eight pioneering Indian Communists** under **Section 121-A IPC**, alleging that they had entered into a treasonable conspiracy with **M.N. Roy** and the Communist International to overthrow British sovereignty via violent revolution. Of the eight named accused, **M.N. Roy** was in Europe (later arrested upon his secret return to India in July **1931** and sentenced to 12 years’ transportation, reduced on appeal to 6 years), **Ramcharan Lal Sharma** had taken political asylum in French **Pondicherry**, **M. Singaravelu Chettiar** was granted bail and dropped from the trial due to acute illness, and **Ghulam Hussain** of Lahore turned state approver in exchange for pardon. The remaining four—**S.A. Dange** (Bombay), **Muzaffar Ahmad** (Calcutta), **Shaukat Usmani** (arrested earlier after returning from Tashkent/Moscow), and **Nalini Gupta** (Comintern courier)—were tried in May 1924 before the notoriously harsh Sessions Judge **H.E. Holme** (who had earlier sentenced 172 peasants to death in the Chauri Chaura trial) and sentenced to **four years’ rigorous imprisonment** each. Ironically, the Kanpur trial publicized Communist doctrines across the nationalist press for the first time.
*   **The Meerut Conspiracy Case (March 1929 – January 1933):** The most famous and protracted political trial of the interwar era was launched on **March 20, 1929**, when colonial police conducted simultaneous dawn raids across Bombay, Calcutta, Poona, Allahabad, and Lahore, arresting **31 (eventually 32) top trade unionists, WPP organizers, and Communist leaders** under **Section 121-A IPC**.
    1.  **Strategic Selection of Meerut:** Although the primary union activities of the accused had occurred in the Presidency metropolises of **Bombay** and **Calcutta**, the colonial government deliberately chose the small cantonment town of **Meerut** (where the UP WPP had held a conference in Oct 1928) as the trial venue because trials in High Court Presidency towns mandated a **Jury trial** (which might have acquitted the popular labour leaders), whereas in the mofussil district court of Meerut, the accused could be tried without a jury using merely five assessors.
    2.  **Composition of the Accused:** The 31 prisoners represented the entire leadership of the militant working-class movement: Communists and WPP leaders (**S.A. Dange**, **Muzaffar Ahmad**, **P.C. Joshi**, **S.V. Ghate**, **K.N. Joglekar**, **S.S. Mirajkar**, **R.S. Nimbkar**, **Sohan Singh Josh**, **Shaukat Usmani**, **Gopen Chakravarty**, **Dharani Goswami**, **Radharaman Mitra**, **G.M. Adhikari**, **Ajudhya Prasad**), radical trade unionists and Congressmen (**Kedar Nath Sehgal**, **Shibnath Banerjee**, **Kishorilal Ghosh**, **D.R. Thengdi**—who died during the trial, **S.H. Jhabvala**, **A.A. Alwe**, **G.R. Kasle**, **Lakshman Rao Kadam**, **Viswanath Mukherjee**, **Dharamvir Singh**, **Gauri Shankar**), and **three British Communists** who had come to India as fraternal organizers to assist the trade union movement: **Philip Spratt**, **Ben Bradley**, and **Lester Hutchinson**.
    3.  **Nationalist Defence and the Courtroom as Propaganda Tribune:** Rather than isolating the Communists as Viceroy Irwin had calculated, the Meerut arrests provoked universal nationalist outrage. The Congress Working Committee established a **Central Defence Committee** chaired by **Motilal Nehru** (contributing Rs 1,500 personally), while **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Kailash Nath Katju**, **Farid-ul-Haq Ansari**, and **M.C. Chagla** appeared as defence counsel, and **Mahatma Gandhi** personally visited the Meerut prisoners in jail in October 1929. Turning the dock into an ideological classroom for **four and a half years** (producing over 10,000 pages of printed court records and 3,500 prosecution exhibits), the accused delivered systematic, book-length Joint Statements analyzing colonial political economy, agrarian feudalism, and scientific socialism, which were reported daily in Indian newspapers. Internationally, the trial was condemned by **Albert Einstein**, **Romain Rolland**, **H.G. Wells**, **Harold Laski**, and the Archbishop of York. In January **1933**, Sessions Judge R.L. Yorke sentenced **Muzaffar Ahmad** to transportation for life, **Dange, Spratt, Ghate, Joglekar, and Nimbkar** to 12 years’ transportation, and others to 3 to 10 years, though international pressure forced the **Allahabad High Court** (Chief Justice Sir Shah Muhammad Sulaiman) in August 1933 to drastically slash the sentences to 1 to 3 years.
*   **P.C. Joshi and the Popular Front Expansion (1935–1947):** In July **1934**, the colonial government formally declared the **Communist Party of India** and its affiliated unions and Kisan Sabhas **unlawful associations** under the Criminal Law Amendment Act (a ban that remained in force until July **1942**). Following the Seventh Congress of the Comintern (1935) and the **Dutt-Bradley Thesis** (*"The Anti-Imperialist People’s Front in India"*, February **1936**, authored by British Communists **Rajani Palme Dutt** and **Ben Bradley**), the CPI abandoned the sectarian isolation of its 1928–34 phase. Under the dynamic leadership of **P.C. Joshi**—who served as **General Secretary of the CPI from late 1935 to 1947**—Communists joined the **Congress Socialist Party (CSP)** and the **Indian National Congress**, built the **All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)**, the **All India Students’ Federation (AISF, 1936)**, the **Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA, 1936)**, and the **Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA, 1943)**, and transformed the CPI into a mass political force. When World War II broke out in September 1939, the CPI initially waged militant anti-war strikes against the **"Imperialist War"** (leading to mass arrests under the Defence of India Rules), before shifting in December **1941**—six months after Nazi Germany’s invasion of the Soviet Union—to the **"People’s War"** line, which legalized the Party in July 1942 and enabled rapid expansion of its trade union, peasant, and cultural apparatus, though alienating it from the nationalist mainstream during the **August 1942 Quit India Movement**.

### 8. The Congress Socialist Party (CSP, 1934) and the Dialectics of Left Unity

#### The Nasik Jail Crucible, Patna and Bombay Conferences (1934), and Core Leadership

While the Meerut Conspiracy prisoners were locked in court and the CPI remained momentarily isolated from the Congress during the 1930–32 Civil Disobedience Movement due to the Comintern’s ultra-left Sixth Congress line, a powerful new current of indigenous Marxist and democratic socialist thought crystallized among young Congress nationalists. Disillusioned by the abrupt suspension of Civil Disobedience, the constitutionalist drift toward the **revived Swaraj Party** (May 1934), and the bourgeois-landlord caution of the Congress Right Wing (Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, C. Rajagopalachari), a group of young Congress prisoners incarcerated inside **Nasik Road Central Prison** during **1932–1933** formulated a blueprint for an organized Socialist caucus operating strictly *within* the Indian National Congress.

*   **The Nasik Group and the 1934 Patna & Bombay Conferences:** Inside **Nasik Jail** (1932–33), **Jayaprakash Narayan** (who had absorbed Marxism during seven years of university study in the United States), **Minoo Masani** (trained at the London School of Economics), **Achyut Patwardhan**, **Asoka Mehta**, **N.G. Goray**, **S.M. Joshi**, and **M.L. Dantwala** drafted the foundational thesis for a Congress Socialist party. Upon their release, building on regional socialist groups already formed in Bihar (1931, by **Jayaprakash Narayan**, **Phulan Prasad Varma**, and **Abdul Bari**), Punjab (1933, the *Punjab Socialist Party*), and Bombay/UP (**Sampurnanand**, **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay**, **Purushottam Tricumdas**), they convened the **First All-India Congress Socialist Conference at Patna on May 17, 1934** (immediately preceding the AICC session called to ratify the revival of council entry). Presided over by the erudite Marxist scholar **Acharya Narendra Deva** (Principal of Kashi Vidyapith), the Patna Conference resolved to form an all-India party. Formally inaugurated at its **First Conference in Bombay on October 21–22, 1934** under the presidency of **Sampurnanand**, the **Congress Socialist Party (CSP)** elected **Jayaprakash Narayan** as its General Secretary and **Minoo Masani** as Joint Secretary, supported by a stellar national executive including **Acharya Narendra Deva**, **Achyut Patwardhan**, **Ram Manohar Lohia** (who edited the party’s influential weekly organ, **The Congress Socialist**, from Calcutta and later Bombay), **Yusuf Meherally** (founder of the *Bombay Youth League*, leader of the 1928 anti-Simon black-flag demonstration who coined the immortal slogans **"Simon Go Back"** and later **"Quit India"** in 1942), and **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** (who presided over the 1936 Meerut CSP Conference). While **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** never formally joined the CSP, both extended warm ideological patronage and financial/political support to it.
*   **Ideological Synthesis and the Fifteen-Point Bombay Programme (1934):** As articulated in **Jayaprakash Narayan’s** seminal theoretical treatise **Why Socialism? (1936)** and **Acharya Narendra Deva’s** *Socialism and the National Revolution*, the CSP synthesized **Marxian scientific socialism**, **Fabian democratic socialism**, and **Gandhian mass mobilization ethics**. The CSP argued that because the Indian National Congress was the historic mass vehicle of the anti-imperialist struggle, walking out of the Congress (as the CPI had done between 1929 and 1934) was sectarian suicide; conversely, leaving the Congress under the unchallenged control of the bourgeois Right Wing would end in a compromising constitutional deal with British imperialism that left landlordism and capitalism intact. Therefore, every CSP member was required by constitution to be an active member of the **Indian National Congress**, working to:
    1.  **Democratize and Radicalize the Congress Constitution:** Replace individual middle-class membership with **collective institutional affiliation** of Kisan Sabhas and Trade Unions to the Congress committees.
    2.  **Uncompromising Anti-Imperialism:** Reject the **Government of India Act of 1935** in its entirety, oppose acceptance of Ministerial office in the provinces (though accepting election campaigns as mass propaganda), demand a sovereign **Constituent Assembly** elected by universal adult franchise, and oppose Indian participation in any British imperialist war.
    3.  **The Socialist Reconstruction Charter:** Transfer all power to the producing masses; nationalize key industries (steel, railways, shipping, mines, banks, insurance, and plantations); **abolish Zamindari, Taluqdari, and Princely feudalism without compensation**; redistribute land to actual tillers; encourage cooperative and collective farming; liquidate all rural and workers’ debts owed to usurers; and apply the socialist principle *"To everyone according to his needs and from everyone according to his capacity."*
*   **The Meerut Thesis (January 1936), Left Unity, and the 1940 Rupture:** At its **Second Conference in Meerut (January 20, 1936)**, drafted largely by **Jayaprakash Narayan** and **Acharya Narendra Deva**, the CSP adopted the famous **Meerut Thesis**, which declared the CSP an explicitly Marxist party and called for the unification of all socialist and anti-imperialist forces in India into a single front. Crucially, despite warnings from **Minoo Masani**, **Ram Manohar Lohia**, **Achyut Patwardhan**, and **Asoka Mehta**, **Jayaprakash Narayan** threw open the membership of the CSP to members of the **banned Communist Party of India (CPI)**. Between **1936 and 1939**, operating legally under the umbrella of the CSP, brilliant CPI organizers—including **E.M.S. Namboodiripad** (elected Joint Secretary of the All-India CSP), **Puchalapalli Sundarayya** (who built the Andhra unit), **P. Jeevanandam** and **P. Ramamurti** (in Tamil Nadu), **Sajjad Zaheer**, **Z.A. Ahmad**, and **K.M. Ashraf** (elevated to the CSP National Executive and key posts in the AICC)—entered the Congress apparatus, captured the entire CSP provincial branches in **Kerala (Malabar)**, **Andhra**, and **Tamil Nadu**, and gained major influence in the AIKS, AITUC, and AISF. When **Minoo Masani** published secret CPI circulars in 1937–38 exposing how the CPI was systematically absorbing CSP cadres, and after disagreements erupted over the **Pant Resolution at the 1939 Tripuri Congress** (where the CSP abstained from voting, effectively allowing the Right Wing to oust Subhas Chandra Bose) and wartime strategy, the CSP National Executive at the **March 1940 Ramgarh Congress** formally **expelled all Communists from the CSP**. The departing CPI units took the entire southern organizational machinery of the CSP in Kerala, Andhra, and Tamil Nadu with them into the Communist Party, while the core CSP leadership (**Jayaprakash Narayan**, **Ram Manohar Lohia**, **Achyut Patwardhan**, **Aruna Asaf Ali**, **Yusuf Meherally**) went on to spearhead the heroic underground resistance of the **1942 Quit India Movement**.

## V. The Princely States Peoples’ Movement (Praja Mandals) and Socialist Realist Cultural Mobilization (1927–1947)

### 9. Paramountcy, the AISPC (1927), Congress Integration (Haripura to Ludhiana), and Regional Praja Mandal Struggles

#### The Architecture of Princely Autocracy, AISPC (1927), and the Haripura–Ludhiana Shift (1938–1939)

Across the territorial map of colonial India, **562 (over 560) Princely States** ("Native States")—ranging from Hyderabad (as large as Italy with 16 million subjects) and Kashmir down to microscopic Saurashtra estates covering a few villages—occupied **two-fifths (45%) of the subcontinent’s land area** and governed **nearly one-fourth (93 million by 1941) of its total population**. Since Queen Victoria’s 1858 Proclamation, the British Crown had preserved these states as conservative *"breakwaters in the storm"* against anti-colonial nationalism. Shielded by British military **Paramountcy**—which guaranteed that British Indian troops would crush any internal rebellion against a Prince—and reaffirmed by the **Butler Committee (Indian States Committee, 1927–1929)** (which ruled that *"Paramountcy must remain paramount"* and could never be transferred to a democratically elected Indian Cabinet in British India without the Princes’ explicit consent), most rulers governed as unbridled personal autocrats. Inside the states, there was no separation between state revenue and the ruler’s personal **Privy Purse** (squandered on European luxury hotels, Rolls-Royces, and polo stables), zero civil liberties or independent judiciary, an absolute ban on public meetings and newspapers, and a medieval fiscal regime of rack-renting, state trade monopolies, and dozens of feudal forced-labour exactions (**Begar**, **Vetti**, **Bethi**, **Rasad**, and **Lag-Bag**).

*   **Foundation of the All India States Peoples’ Conference (AISPC, December 1927):** Inspired by the Non-Cooperation Movement, local democratic associations—variously named **Praja Mandals** (*Peoples’ Associations*), **Lok Parishads**, or **State Congresses**—sprang up in Baroda, Kathiawar, Mysore, Hyderabad, Indore, and Bikaner during the early 1920s. When the colonial government appointed the **Butler Committee** in **1927** to perpetuate Princely isolation from British India, democratic activists from across the states convened at **Bombay on December 17, 1927** (presided over by **Dewan Bahadur M. Ramachandra Rao**) to establish the **All India States Peoples’ Conference (AISPC)**. Its permanent organizational architects were three tireless organisers from Western India: **Balwantrai Mehta** (from Bhavnagar, who served for decades as General Secretary), **Maniklal Kothari** (a fiery Kathiawar nationalist and close lieutenant of Gandhi and Patel), and Professor **G.R. Abhyankar** (from Sangli, specialist in states’ constitutional law). Coordinating over **700 local Praja Mandals**, the AISPC demanded that Princely States be made integral units of a single, democratic, federated India, with fundamental civil liberties, an independent judiciary, a fixed limit of 10% on the ruler’s privy purse, and fully elected responsible legislatures.
*   **From Non-Interference to Complete Integration (1920 Nagpur → 1938 Haripura → 1939 Tripuri/Ludhiana):** From 1920 to 1937, Mahatma Gandhi and the Indian National Congress adhered to a cautious policy of **organizational non-interference** in the internal affairs of Princely States, reasoning that subjects of the states lacked training in disciplined non-violent Satyagraha and that premature Congress intervention would provoke joint British-Princely armed repression before the main colonial fortress in British India was breached. However, the assumption of office by **Congress Ministries in eight provinces in July 1937** triggered a political earthquake inside the bordering Princely States.
    1.  **The 1938 Haripura Resolution:** At the **February 1938 Haripura Congress Session** (presided over by **Subhas Chandra Bose**), the Congress emphatically declared that **Purna Swaraj** encompassed the whole of India—inclusive of the Princely States—and that the states were integral parts of the Indian nation. While temporarily directing that internal Satyagraha movements in the states should be conducted in the name of independent **Praja Mandals** rather than in the organizational name of the Congress, the Haripura resolution encouraged **individual Congressmen** to render full moral, material, and personal leadership to the states’ peoples’ struggles. Immediately, top Congress leaders (**Vallabhbhai Patel** in Rajkot/Baroda/Mysore, **Jamnalal Bajaj** in Jaipur, **Harekrushna Mahatab** in Odisha, and **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** in South India) plunged directly into Praja Mandal campaigns.
    2.  **The 1939 Ludhiana AISPC and Tripuri Congress Integration:** By early **1939**, as mass Satyagrahas swept Rajkot, Mysore, Jaipur, Travancore, Hyderabad, and the Odisha states, Gandhi Himself declared in *Harijan* (January 1939) that the awakening of the states’ people was irreversible and abolished all distinction between British and Princely India. In **February 1939**, at the landmark **Ludhiana Session of the AISPC**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** accepted election as **President of the AISPC** (with **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** and **Balwantrai Mehta** heading its Standing Committee, later succeeded as President by **Sheikh Abdullah** in 1946). A month later, at the **March 1939 Tripuri Congress Session**, the Congress formally jettisoned the policy of non-intervention, welding the Praja Mandals into the central command of the national liberation movement.

#### Major Regional Praja Mandal Agitations: Rajkot, Mysore, Hyderabad, Travancore, Odisha Garjat States, and Kashmir

Between **1938 and 1947**, the Praja Mandal movement transformed the political geography of Princely India through six decisive regional confrontations:

*   **Rajkot (1938–1939):** In the small Kathiawar state of **Rajkot**, the dissolute ruler **Thakore Saheb Dharmendrasinhji** and his scheming Dewan **Durbar Virawala** squandered state finances and auctioned monopolies on matches, sugar, and cloth while brutally suppressing protests led by **U.N. Dhebar**. Directed personally by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** (assisted by **Maniben Patel**, **Mridula Sarabhai**, and **Kasturba Gandhi**, who courted arrest in Rajkot in February 1939), the **Rajkot Praja Parishad** organized a total fiscal and trade boycott that forced the Thakore Saheb on December 26, 1938 to sign an agreement promising a 10-member Reform Committee with seven members nominated by **Sardar Patel**. Prompted by the British Resident and **Durbar Virawala**, the ruler subsequently repudiated his word and rejected Patel’s nominees. Viewing this breach of faith as a test of *Ahimsa*, **Mahatma Gandhi** travelled to Rajkot—his boyhood home—and commenced a **fast unto death on March 3, 1939**, compelling Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** to intervene and refer the dispute to the Chief Justice of the Federal Court, **Sir Maurice Gwyer**, who gave his arbitral award in April 1939 wholly upholding Patel’s interpretation (though Gandhi later renounced the award after feeling that appealing to the British Paramount power had tainted the spiritual purity of his fast).
*   **Mysore (1937–1939):** Although often praised by colonial officials as a "model state," the administration of Dewan **Sir Mirza Ismail** in **Mysore** ruthlessly suppressed the **Mysore State Congress** (founded in October 1937 under **T. Siddalingaiya**, **K.T. Bhashyam**, **K.C. Reddy**, and **H.C. Dasappa**). Defying a blanket ban on hoisting the Congress Tricolour, over 30,000 peasants and activists gathered at **Shivapura** (Maddur taluk, Mandya) on **April 11–13, 1938** to launch the historic **Shivapura Flag Satyagraha**, hoisting the national flag continuously despite mass arrests. Two weeks later, on **April 25, 1938**, at the sacred pilgrimage village of **Vidurashwatha** (Kolar district), state armed police opened indiscriminate rifle and machine-gun fire on an unarmed gathering of 10,000 satyagrahis, killing **32 people on the spot** in what came to be known as the **"Vidurashwatha Massacre"** (*the Jallianwala Bagh of South India*). Following this bloodbath, **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** and **J.B. Kripalani** rushed to Bangalore in May 1938 and forced the Mysore Durbar to officially recognize the **Mysore State Congress**, include Congress nominees on the Constitutional Reforms Committee, and legalize the hoisting of the National Flag alongside the Mysore state flag—culminating later in the **1942 Isoor (Esuru) "Swatantra Halli" uprising** and the **1947 "Mysore Chalo" Satyagraha** that installed **K.C. Reddy** as the first democratic Chief Minister.
*   **Hyderabad (1938–1939):** Under **Nizam Mir Osman Ali Khan**, the majority Hindu population (87%—speaking Telugu in Telangana, Marathi in Marathwada, and Kannada in Karnataka) was ruled by a narrow Urdu-speaking feudal oligarchy while public political assemblies were prohibited under Circular **Gashti Nishan 53**. In mid-**1938**, progressive nationalists from all three linguistic regions—led by **Swami Ramanand Tirtha** (a disciple of N.M. Joshi and Gandhi who became the supreme ascetic architect of the movement), **Govindbhai Shroff**, **Burgula Ramakrishna Rao**, **Kashinathrao Vaidya**, **Digambarrao Bindu**, **M. Narsing Rao**, and **Ravi Narayana Reddy**—founded the secular **Hyderabad State Congress**. When the Nizam’s Executive Council immediately banned the organization in September 1938 on the false pretext that it was communal, **Govindbhai Shroff** and **Swami Ramanand Tirtha** launched an individual civil disobedience Satyagraha in October 1938. Simultaneously, at **Osmania University**, Hindu students were expelled from hostels in November **1938** for singing **Vande Mataram** in their prayer hall, sparking the famous **Vande Mataram Student Movement** (in which students migrated en masse to **Nagpur University** to complete their degrees). Because the **Arya Samaj** and the **Hindu Mahasabha** (led by V.D. Savarkar and Bhai Parmanand) were concurrently conducting a separate religious-communal Satyagraha in Hyderabad, **Mahatma Gandhi** and theHyderabad State Congress suspended their civil disobedience in December 1938 so that their secular democratic struggle for responsible government would never be conflated with communal agitation, shifting toward constructive work and the **Andhra**, **Maharashtra**, and **Karnataka Parishads** until the **1946–48 final Join-India struggle**.
*   **Travancore (1938–1947):** In **February 1938**, leading lawyers and public figures representing the Christian, Nair, and Ezhava communities—**Pattom A. Thanu Pillai** (President), **T.M. Varghese**, **C. Kesavan**, **A.J. John**, **E. John Philipose**, and **Annie Mascarene**—founded the **Travancore State Congress** at Trivandrum to challenge the autocracy of Dewan **Sir C.P. Ramaswami Iyer**. In May 1938, the State Congress submitted a famous **Memorial** to the Maharaja demanding immediate responsible government together with a heavily documented **Memorandum of Charges** personally indicting **Sir C.P. Ramaswami Iyer** for nepotism, corruption, and crushing newspaper freedom. Launching mass civil disobedience in **August 1938**, the movement galvanized thousands of youth and working people despite police firings at **Neyyattinkara**, **Quilon**, **Puthuppally**, and **Kadakkal** (where peasants set up a parallel administration for a week). Under personal pressure from **Mahatma Gandhi**—who feared that the personal corruption charges against the Dewan were overshadowing the core constitutional demand for democratic governance—the State Congress withdrew the personal Memorandum against C.P. Ramaswami Iyer in late 1938. This provoked a radical Youth League split (led by **P. Krishna Pillai**, **E.M.S. Namboodiripad**, **K.C. George**, and **M.N. Govindan Nair**) that shifted the working-class base to the Communist Party, culminating in the **1946 Punnapra-Vayalar Uprising** and the final **1947 mass upsurge** (where an assassination attempt on C.P. Ramaswami Iyer in July 1947 prompted the Maharaja to immediately sign the **Instrument of Accession** to India).
*   **Odisha Garjat States (1938–1939):** Encircling the British-ruled coastal districts of Orissa lay **26 feudal Princely States** known as the **Garjat States** (including **Dhenkanal**, **Talcher**, **Nilgiri**, **Ranpur**, **Nayagarh**, **Athgarh**, and **Mayurbhanj**). Here, tribal and peasant subjects were subjected to horrific medieval exactions: **Bethi** (unpaid forced labour on roads and palaces), **Rasad** (compulsory free provisioning of food to the Raja’s hunting parties and officials), **Magan** (forced monetary contributions whenever a member of the royal family married, died, or bought a car), **Bheti** (compulsory ceremonial gifts), and a total monopoly on salt, kerosene, betel leaves, and even hiding animal skins. Following the convening of the **Orissa States Peoples’ Conference** at Cuttack in June 1937 (presided over by **Pattabhi Sitaramayya**) and the **Orissa States Enquiry Committee** chaired by **Harekrushna Mahatab** (assisted by **Sarangadhar Das** and **Balwantrai Mehta**, whose 1939 report recommended cancelling the *Sanads* of the Garjat Rajas and merging their estates into Orissa Province), explosive Praja Mandal rebellions erupted in **1938–1939**:
    1.  **Nilgiri and Dhenkanal (Baji Rout’s Martyrdom, Oct 11, 1938):** After **Sarangadhar Das** and **Kailash Chandra Mohanty** forced the Raja of **Nilgiri** to recognize the Praja Mandal in August 1938, over 50,000 peasants in **Dhenkanal** (led by **Harmohan Patnaik**, **Baisnab Charan Patnaik**, and **Maheswar Subahu Singh**) rose against Raja Shankar Pratap Singh Deo. On the rainy night of **October 11, 1938**, at **Nilakanthapur Ghat** on the Brahmani river, when a detachment of British Indian troops and state police ordered a **12-year-old Banapua (Praja Mandal youth volunteer) boatman named Baji Rout** to ferry them across the river after they had shot villagers in Bhuban, the fearless boy blocked the boat and cried out that the Praja Mandal had forbidden ferrying the oppressors. A policeman shattered **Baji Rout’s** skull with a rifle butt and shot him dead alongside five companions (**Laxman Malik**, **Fagu Sahoo**, **Hrushi Pradhan**, **Nata Malik**, and **Guri Naik**)—immortalizing 12-year-old **Baji Rout** as the **youngest martyr of the Indian freedom struggle** (celebrated in Jnanpith poet **Sachi Routray’s** epic poem *Baji Rout*).
    2.  **The Great Talcher Hijrat (1938–1939):** In **Talcher**, against the sadistic rule of Raja Kishore Chandra Birabar Harichandan, the **Talcher Praja Mandal** led by **Pabitra Mohan Pradhan** launched an extraordinary non-violent weapon: a mass **Hijrat** (collective exodus). Between November 1938 and March 1939, **over 26,000 men, women, and children**—nearly one-third of Talcher’s entire population—abandoned their standing crops and ancestral homes and migrated into temporary bamboo refugee camps at **Angul** in British-ruled Orissa (where the Congress Ministry of **Biswanath Das**, **Harekrushna Mahatab**, **Malati Choudhury**, and **Nabakrushna Choudhury** organized food and medical relief) until British political officers forced administrative reforms.
    3.  **Ranpur and the Killing of Major Bazelgette (January 5, 1939):** In **Ranpur**, when the British Political Agent for the Odisha States, **Major R.L. Bazelgette**, arrived at the palace on **January 5, 1939** to disperse a crowd of 25,000 Praja Mandal peasants demanding the release of arrested leaders (**Raghunath Mohanty** and **Dibakar Parida**), Bazelgette shot dead a peasant named **Arjun Rout** with his revolver. The enraged crowd overpowered and beat **Major Bazelgette** to death—triggering a massive military invasion of the Garjat states by British troops and the hanging of young Praja Mandal leaders **Raghunath Mohanty** and **Dibakar Parida** in 1941.
*   **Kashmir: Sheikh Abdullah, the National Conference, Naya Kashmir (1944), and Quit Kashmir (1946):** In the northernmost Princely State of **Jammu and Kashmir**—created by the **1846 Treaty of Amritsar** under which the East India Company sold the Kashmir Valley to Dogra Raja Gulab Singh for **75 lakh Nanakshahee rupees**—the overwhelming Muslim majority of peasant cultivators (**Haalis**), shawl weavers (**Shal-bafs**), and silk-factory workers lived under acute feudal exploitation, heavy land taxation, and **Begar** (forced porterage to Gilgit) beneath the rule of Maharaja **Hari Singh** and a predominantly Dogra Jagirdari elite. Returning from Aligarh Muslim University with an M.Sc. in Chemistry in 1930, **Sheikh Mohammad Abdullah** (*Sher-e-Kashmir*) founded the *Reading Room Party*. Following the **July 13, 1931 Srinagar Central Jail firing** (where 22 protesters were killed by the Maharaja’s police during the trial of Abdul Qadir, prompting the appointment of the **Glancy Commission**), **Sheikh Abdullah** and **Chaudhry Ghulam Abbas** founded the **All Jammu and Kashmir Muslim Conference** in October **1932**. However, deeply influenced by his personal friendship with **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan**, and left-wing Kashmiri Pandit intellectuals (**Prem Nath Bazaz**, **Jia Lal Kilam**, **Kashyap Bandhu**) and Sikh leaders (**Sardar Budh Singh**), Sheikh Abdullah recognized that an anti-feudal struggle against autocracy required the unity of all exploited peasants and workers regardless of religion. At a historic Special Session in **June 1939**, **Sheikh Abdullah** dissolved the communal framework and transformed the party into the secular, nationalist **All Jammu and Kashmir National Conference**. In **September 1944**, at its Sopore Session, the National Conference adopted its revolutionary manifesto, **Naya Kashmir** (*New Kashmir*, drafted with the assistance of Punjab Communist **B.P.L. Bedi** and **Freda Bedi**)—the most radical socialist constitutional and economic blueprint produced by any political party in colonial India, mandating the **abolition of Jagirdari and Chakdari landlordism without compensation**, distribution of **land to the tiller**, a planned socialist economy, and a comprehensive **Women’s Charter** guaranteeing equal wages and political rights. In **May 1946**, as the British Cabinet Mission negotiated in Delhi, **Sheikh Abdullah** launched the militant **"Quit Kashmir" Movement**, challenging the moral and legal validity of the **1846 Treaty of Amritsar** ("*Sale Deed of Kashmir*") and demanding that Maharaja Hari Singh quit the valley and surrender sovereignty to the people. When Abdullah was arrested on May 20, 1946, **Jawaharlal Nehru** defied the Maharaja’s prohibitory orders to rush to his defence, was arrested at the **Kohala Bridge** border post in June 1946, and arranged **Asaf Ali** as Abdullah’s defence counsel—forging the unbreakable ideological bond between the **National Conference** and the Indian Union that proved decisive during the October 1947 Pakistani tribal invasion.

### 10. Socialist Realism, Subaltern Visual Modernity, the Progressive Writers’ Association (1936), and IPTA (1943)

#### Amrita Sher-Gil’s Rural Canvases and the Graphic Linocuts of the 1943 Bengal Famine and Tebhaga (Chittaprosad, Somnath Hore, Zainul Abedin)

Just as the Kisan Sabhas, Trade Unions, CSP, CPI, and Praja Mandals shattered the elite bourgeois-landlord monopoly over nationalist politics during the 1930s and 1940s, an equally profound ideological and material rupture transformed Indian visual art, print culture, literature, and theatre. Up to the early 1930s, Indian visual modernity had been polarized between two aesthetic paradigms: on one side stood colonial **Academic Naturalism** (taught in British Government Art Schools and popularized through **Raja Ravi Varma’s** mythological oil paintings and oleographs); on the other stood the **Bengal School of Art** (**Abanindranath Tagore** and **E.B. Havell**), whose delicate Japanese-wash watercolours of medieval Mughal and Ajanta subjects had increasingly degenerated among second-generation imitators into sentimental, nostalgic, and otherworldly escapism divorced from the material misery of depression-era and famine-stricken India.

*   **Amrita Sher-Gil (1913–1941) and the Subaltern Rural Canvas:** Born in Budapest to a Sikh aristocrat father (Umrao Singh Sher-Gil Majithia) and a Hungarian Jewish opera-singer mother, and rigorously trained in oil painting and fresco at the **École des Beaux-Arts in Paris** (where she became the youngest Associate of the Grand Salon in 1933 with *Young Girls*), **Amrita Sher-Gil** returned to India in late **1934** (living at Simla, Saraya in Gorakhpur district, and Lahore until her tragic death at age 28 in December 1941). Travelling to **Ajanta**, **Ellora**, **Mattancherry (Cochin)**, and across rural Punjab and the Himalayan hills, she mounted a fierce theoretical critique against the Bengal School for reducing Ajanta’s monumental structural power into weak, decorative linearism. Synthesizing the sculptural volume and stark emotional directness of European **Post-Impressionism** (**Paul Gauguin**, **Paul Cézanne**, and **Modigliani**) with the flat, earthy vermilion, ochre, and emerald colour planes of **Basohli/Pahari miniatures** and **Ajanta murals**, Sher-Gil declared her artistic mission: *"To interpret the life of Indians, and particularly the poor Indians, pictorially; to paint those silent images of infinite submission and patience, to depict their angular brown bodies, strangely beautiful in their ugliness."* In landmark canvases such as **Three Girls (1935)**, **Hill Men** and **Hill Women (1935)**, **Mother India (1935)**, **The South Indian Trilogy — Bride’s Toilet, Brahmacharis, and South Indian Villagers Going to Market (1937)**, **Village Scene (1938)**, and **Woman Resting on a Charpoy (1940)**, she refused to exoticize the Indian village as an idyllic pastoral Arcadia, depicting instead the gaunt skeletal frames, hollowed eyes, fatalistic exhaustion, and claustrophobic patriarchal confinement of rural peasants and women.
*   **Graphic Print Capitalism and Visual Reportage of the 1943 Bengal Famine and Tebhaga:** In **1943–1944**, when colonial wartime inflation, ruthless grain procurement for the Allied military, and the scorched-earth **Rice Denial and Boat Denial Policies** engineered the **Great Bengal Famine**—starving **3.5 million rural Bengalis** (landless agricultural labourers, Bargadars, fishermen, weavers, and potters) to death on the streets of Calcutta and across the districts—a new generation of socially committed artists concluded that bourgeois easel painting in secluded studios was morally bankrupt. Mobilized by **P.C. Joshi** and the Communist Party’s periodicals (**People’s War** in English and **Janayuddha** in Bengali), three master artists turned to cheap, immediate, mechanically reproducible graphic media—**linocuts**, **woodcuts**, and **black ink on brown packing paper**—to turn art into a weapon of anti-imperialist indictment and peasant solidarity:
    1.  **Chittaprosad (Chittaprosad Bhattacharya, 1915–1978):** Rejecting enrollment in the colonial Government School of Art, **Chittaprosad** became a full-time visual chronicler for the CPI during World War II. Walking on foot through the worst famine-ravaged villages of **Midnapore (Contai Subdivision)** in November **1943**, he kept a devastating notebook of eyewitness sketches and personal names of starving peasants, published in December 1943 as the famous illustrated book **Hungry Bengal** (*A Tour through Midnapur District, by Chittaprosad, in November 1943*). Terrified by the book’s visual indictment of colonial administration, British police immediately proscribed **Hungry Bengal** and seized and **burned nearly 5,000 copies**. Mastering the high-contrast medium of the **linocut** and **scraperboard** (inspired by Mexican revolutionary printmaker Leopoldo Méndez and Chinese woodcuts), Chittaprosad carved unforgettable images of vultures and jackals tearing at unburied peasant corpses alongside heroic, muscular depictions of red-flag peasant resistance during the **1945–47 Warli Revolt**, the **1946 RIN Naval Mutiny**, the **1946–47 Tebhaga Movement**, and the **Telangana Armed Struggle**, as well as his poignant children’s book on child labour, **Kingdom of Rasagolla** and **Angels Without Fairy Tales**.
    2.  **Somnath Hore (1921–2006):** Born in Chittagong and brought into the Communist Party by **P.C. Joshi** during the 1943 famine while studying at the Government School of Art in Calcutta, **Somnath Hore** produced searing ink-and-wash famine drawings for *Janayuddha*. In **December 1946**, at the height of the **Tebhaga Movement**, Hore travelled to the rebel sharecropper villages of **Rangpur and Dinajpur** in North Bengal, living among the **Rajbansi** and **Adhiar** peasants and maintaining a remarkable illustrated journal known as the **Tebhaga Diary** (*Tebhagar Diary*). Executed in rapid pen-and-ink sketches and **wood-engravings (woodcuts)**, Hore’s *Tebhaga Diary* shifted the representation of the Indian peasant from a passive, skeletal famine victim into an active historical agent: showing peasant men and women debating tenurial strategy in midnight **Baithaks** (councils), **Nari Bahini** volunteers harvesting paddy under the Red Flag, and villagers erecting martyr columns for comrades killed in the Khanpur police firing—a lifelong obsession with social trauma that later culminated in his iconic white-on-white paper-pulp print series, **Wounds (1970s)**.
    3.  **Zainul Abedin (1914–1976):** Then a young teacher at the Government School of Art in Calcutta (later celebrated as *Shilpacharya* and founder of modernist art in Bangladesh), **Zainul Abedin** walked the pavements of Calcutta during **1943** as starving rural refugees collapsed beside overflowing garbage bins outside European restaurants. Because wartime rationing had made imported art paper and oil paints prohibitively expensive, Abedin bought **cheap brown coarse packing paper** at a few annas and used **ordinary black Chinese ink applied with a dry brush and burnt charcoal twigs** to create his world-famous **1943 Famine Sketches**. With just four or five razor-sharp, calligraphic brush-strokes on bare brown paper, Abedin captured skeletal mothers with infants nursing at dry breasts, emaciated figures fighting street dogs and crows over scraps in dustbins, and dying families collapsing on city kerbs. Exhibited in 1943–44 by the **Anti-Fascist Writers’ and Artists’ Association** and reproduced in **Ela Sen’s** book *Darkening Days (1944)*, Abedin’s famine sketches became the definitive visual icon of colonial entitlement failure in the twentieth century.

#### The Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA, 1936): Premchand, Sajjad Zaheer, Mulk Raj Anand, Faiz, Chughtai, and Manto

In the sphere of Indian literature across Urdu, Hindi, Bengali, Malayalam, Telugu, Marathi, and Punjabi, the structural break with feudal courtly romanticism, religious orthodoxy, and bourgeois aestheticism ("Art for Art’s sake") was institutionalized by the **All-India Progressive Writers’ Association (PWA / Anjuman Tarraqi Pasand Musannifin-e-Hind)** in **April 1936**.

*   **From "Angarey" (1932) and the London Manifesto (1935) to Lucknow (April 1936):** The immediate literary precursor of the PWA was the publication in **December 1932** at Lucknow of **Angarey** (*Burning Coals*)—a daring collection of ten Urdu short stories and a one-act play authored by four young radical intellectuals: **Sajjad Zaheer** (*Banne Bhai*), **Dr. Rashid Jahan** (a pioneer Communist gynaecologist and feminist writer), **Ahmed Ali**, and **Mahmuduzzafar**. Utilizing stream-of-consciousness and unflinching realism to attack religious hypocrisy, purdah, feudal patriarchy, and colonial exploitation, **Angarey** provoked fatwas from conservative ulema and was banned by the United Provinces government in March 1933 under **Section 295-A of the IPC**. Travelling to Europe—where **Sajjad Zaheer** and **Mulk Raj Anand** participated in the 1935 Paris World Congress of Writers for the Defence of Culture against Fascism (led by Maxim Gorky, Romain Rolland, and André Malraux)—a group of Indian students and writers in London (**Sajjad Zaheer**, **Mulk Raj Anand**, **Jyoti Ghosh**, **Pramod Sengupta**, and **M.D. Taseer**) met at the **Nanking Restaurant in Denmark Street, London in November 1934** and drafted the foundational **Manifesto of the Indian Progressive Writers’ Association** (published in *Left Review*, February 1936). Returning to India, **Sajjad Zaheer** secured the enthusiastic endorsements of **Rabindranath Tagore**, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Sarojini Naidu**, **Maulvi Abdul Haq**, and **Munshi Premchand**.
*   **The Inaugural Lucknow Session (April 9–10, 1936) and Premchand’s Presidential Address:** Convened at the **Rifa-i-Aam Club in Lucknow on April 9–10, 1936**—simultaneously with the Lucknow sessions of the Indian National Congress and the **All India Kisan Sabha**—the first All-India Conference of the **Progressive Writers’ Association** elected **Sajjad Zaheer** as its General Secretary and was presided over by the titan of modern Hindi-Urdu fiction, **Munshi Premchand** (Dhanpat Rai Shrivastava, 1880–1936). In his celebrated presidential address, **Sahitya ka Uddeshya** (*The Purpose / Aim of Literature*), delivered just six months before his death—the same year he published his supreme realist masterpiece on peasant debt-ruin, **Godaan** (*The Gift of a Cow*, 1936), and his chilling short story on Dalit dehumanization, **Kafan** (*The Shroud*, 1936)—**Premchand** issued the manifesto of Indian Socialist Realism:
    *   Literature can no longer be a courtly narcotic of *Shringara* (eroticism), magical fantasies (*Tilism-e-Hoshruba*), or passive fatalism serving the leisure of kings, nawabs, and landlords.
    *   The writer is by nature a progressive who stands at the vanguard of society, wielding his pen as a **torchbearer (Mashalchi)** for the oppressed, the exploited peasant, the industrial worker, the untouchable, and the subjugated woman.
    *   Crucially, Premchand declared: **"Hamein Husn ka Mayar Badalna Hoga"** (*"We must change the very standard of beauty"*): hitherto, the standard of beauty was dictated by the wealthy and the luxurious, viewing a pale, richly adorned aristocratic woman inside a palace as beautiful; henceforth, true beauty must be seen in the **sweat-soaked village peasant woman cutting wheat in the blazing sun with her infant sleeping on the field-ridge**.
*   **The Literary Vanguard of the PWA:** Over the next two decades, the PWA reshaped every major Indian language:
    1.  **Mulk Raj Anand (1905–2004):** Pioneered subaltern social realism in Indian English fiction with **Untouchable (1935)** (chronicling a single day in the life of a young Dalit manual scavenger, **Bakha**, with a preface by E.M. Forster), **Coolie (1936)** (tracing the exploitation and death of an orphaned hill-boy, **Munoo**, in textile mills and colonial Simla), and **Two Leaves and a Bud (1937)** (exposing the brutal indenture and sexual violence on British-owned **Assam Tea Plantations**, immediately banned by the British for "obscenity").
    2.  **Faiz Ahmad Faiz (1911–1984):** Revolutionized classical Urdu *Ghazal* and *Nazm* aesthetics by infusing the traditional tropes of the Beloved, the Rival (*Raqeeb*), and the Tavern (*Maikhana*) with anti-imperialist and socialist revolutionary urgency. In his first collection **Naqsh-e-Faryadi (1941)**—containing the watershed poem **Mujh Se Pehli Si Mohabbat Mere Mehboob Na Maang** (*Do not ask of me, my love, that love I once had for you*, transitioning from romantic absorption to the festering sores of workers’ bodies Sold in alleyways) and **Bol Ke Lab Azaad Hain Tere** (*Speak, for your lips are free*)—and later **Dast-e-Saba** and his ambivalent lament on the 1947 Partition, **Subh-e-Azadi** (*This stained, scarred dawn*), Faiz became the premier poetic voice of the Asian Left.
    3.  **Ismat Chughtai (1915–1991):** Using the razor-sharp, idiomatic *Begumati Zubaan* (women’s colloquial Urdu of UP Muslim households), **Chughtai** tore open the claustrophobic hypocrisy of middle-class domesticity, female sexual repression, and class exploitation in works like her autobiographical novel **Terhi Lakeer** (*The Crooked Line*, 1944), **Chauthi ka Jaura**, and her fearless 1942 short story **Lihaaf** (*The Quilt*)—which depicted female homoerotic desire inside an neglected *Zenana* and led to her famous **1944–45 Lahore Crown obscenity trial** alongside Manto.
    4.  **Saadat Hasan Manto (1912–1955):** Though maintaining a fierce artistic autonomy from party dogma, **Manto** worked at the heart of the progressive literary milieu, training an unsparing surgical scalpel on the hypocrisy of "respectable" society by centering prostitutes, pimps, tangawallahs, and outcasts (*Hatak*, *Kali Shalwar*, *Bu*, *Naya Qanoon* on the 1935 Act), and subsequently producing the greatest literary indictments of the **1947 Partition holocaust**: **Toba Tek Singh** (using the lunatic asylum of Lahore to expose the madness of cartographic vivisection), **Thanda Gosht** (*Cold Meat*), and **Khol Do** (*Open It*).
    5.  **Pan-Indian Vernacular Expansion:** Alongside Urdu/Hindi luminaries (**Krishan Chander** with *Annadata* on the Bengal Famine, **Rajinder Singh Bedi**, **Kaifi Azmi**, **Ali Sardar Jafri**, **Sahir Ludhianvi**, **Majrooh Sultanpuri**, **Khwaja Ahmad Abbas**, **Yashpal**, **Rahul Sankrityayan**, **Nagarjun**, and **Rambriksh Benipuri**), the PWA ignited transformative movements in Bengali (**Manik Bandopadhyay’s** *Padma Nadir Majhi*, **Tarasankar Bandyopadhyay**, **Bishnu Dey**, **Subhas Mukhopadhyay**, **Sukanta Bhattacharya**), Malayalam (*Jeeval Sahitya* / Progressive Literature led by **Thakazhi Sivasankara Pillai’s** *Thottiyude Makan* and *Randidangazhi*, **Vaikom Muhammad Basheer**, **Kesavadev**), and Telugu (*Abhyudaya Rachayitala Sangham* / **Arasam** led by the revolutionary poet **Sri Sri [Srirangam Srinivasa Rao]** and his epic collection *Maha Prasthanam*).

#### The Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA, 1943): Bijon Bhattacharya’s Nabanna, Balraj Sahni, and Salil Chowdhury

Recognizing that in a country where colonial rule had left over 88% of the population illiterate, printed books and journals could reach only the educated urban strata, the Socialist and Communist cultural vanguard created the **Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA)**—the most dynamic mass-performing-arts movement in modern Indian history.

*   **Genesis of IPTA (1941–1943) and the Bombay Foundation Conference (May 25, 1943):** Preceded by the **Youth Cultural Institute** in Calcutta (1940: **Chinmohan Sehanavis**, **Jyotirindra Moitra**, **Debabrata Biswas**, **Bijon Bhattacharya**) and the **People’s Theatre** started in **Bangalore** in **1941** and **Bombay** in **1942** by the Sri Lankan Marxist journalist **Anil de Silva** (alongside **Hima Kesarcodi**, **Dada Sarkalkar**, and trade-union choirs), the **First All-India Conference of the Indian People’s Theatre Association (IPTA)** was convened at the **Marwari Vidyalaya Hall in Bombay on May 25, 1943**. The name **"People’s Theatre"** had been personally suggested to Anil de Silva by the visionary nuclear physicist **Dr. Homi J. Bhabha** (inspired by **Romain Rolland’s** 1903 treatise *Le Théâtre du Peuple*), who also designed part of its visual emblem, while **Chittaprosad** drew its iconic logo of the **Tribal Drummer (Nagada player)** summoning the masses, beneath the motto: **"People’s Theatre Stars the People."** Presided over by **Prof. Hiren Mukherjee** with **Anil de Silva** as its first General Secretary and **N.M. Joshi** as President, IPTA broke out of the commercial urban proscenium stage (such as melodramatic Parsi Theatre). Operating on open-air makeshift wooden platforms lit by kerosene *Petromax* lamps in mill compounds, railway yards, and peasant threshing floors, IPTA revived and weaponized dying indigenous regional **folk performance forms** for anti-imperialist, anti-fascist, and anti-feudal education:
    *   **Bengal:** *Jatra*, *Kabigan* (improvised poetic duels by **Ramesh Shil** and **Gumani Dewan**), *Gambhira*, and *Baul/Bhatiyali* folk melodies.
    *   **Maharashtra:** *Tamasha* and *Powada* (heroic ballads of Shivaji and peasant martyrs sung by the *Lal Bawta Kalapathak* trinity: Dalit revolutionary **Annabhau Sathe**, working-class balladeer **Amar Shaikh**, and **Gavankar**).
    *   **Andhra & Telangana:** *Burrakatha* (three-person narrative balladry with the *Tambura* and *Gummeta* drums, transformed by **Sunkara Satyanarayana** and **Vasireddy Bhaskara Rao’s** play *Maabhoomi* [Our Land, 1947], which was watched by over 2 million peasants during the Telangana Armed Struggle), *Veedhi Natakam*, and *Harikatha*.
    *   **Kerala, Assam, and Gujarat:** *Ottamthullal* and *Kathakali-based* poor-man’s theatre (*Pattabakki* by K. Damodaran in Malabar), Assamese *Bihu* and *Ojapali* (**Hemanga Biswas** and **Bhupen Hazarika**), and Gujarati *Bhavai* (**Dina Pathak / Dina Gandhi’s** *Maina Gurjari*).
*   **Bijon Bhattacharya’s "Nabanna" (New Harvest, October 1944):** The aesthetic and political watershed of modern Indian theatre was IPTA Bengal Squad’s landmark production of **Nabanna** (*New Harvest*), written by **Bijon Bhattacharya** and co-directed by **Bijon Bhattacharya** and **Sombhu Mitra**, which premiered on **October 24, 1944** at the Sri Rangam Theatre in Calcutta (featuring **Bijon Bhattacharya**, **Tripti Bhaduri [Mitra]**, **Sombhu Mitra**, **Gangapada Basu**, **Sobha Sen**, and **Charuprakash Ghosh**, with a revolving minimalist stage designed by **Manoranjan Bhattacharya**).
    *   **Dramatic Architecture of Nabanna:** Rejecting individual bourgeois heroes, **Nabanna** made the collective Bengal peasantry its protagonist, tracking the family of an elderly patriarch, **Pradhan Samaddar**, from the village of **Aminpur**. Crushed in rapid succession by the August 1942 political repression, the catastrophic **October 1942 Midnapore Cyclone**, the hoarding of grain by black-marketeers and **Jotedars**, and the **1943 Great Bengal Famine**, Pradhan’s starving family is forced to abandon their ancestral soil and trek to **Calcutta**, where they are reduced to begging for leftover rice-starch (*Phan*) in city drains, watching their children die on the streets, and rescuing young peasant women from brothel-traffickers. In the final act, rejecting urban beggary and death, the surviving peasants return to **Aminpur**, unite across Hindu-Muslim lines under the Kisan Sabha, and collectively harvest the **New Harvest (Nabanna)** with a vow never again to let landlords and hoarders steal the grain grown by their sweat.
    *   **The "Voice of Bengal" All-India Cultural Squad (1944–1945):** Organized by **P.C. Joshi** and choreographer **Shanti Bardhan** (alongside **Ravi Shankar**, **Abani Dasgupta**, **Binoy Roy**, **Rekha Jain**, **Dina Pathak**, and **Gul Bardhan**), IPTA’s Central Cultural Squad toured across Gujarat, Maharashtra, Punjab, and Delhi performing **Nabanna** and the shadow-ballet *Spirit of India* (*Bharat ki आत्मा*) and *India Immortal*, selling tickets to working-class and middle-class audiences to raise **over Rs 2 lakhs for Bengal famine relief and rural medical rehabilitation**.
*   **Balraj Sahni, Salil Chowdhury, and the Cinematic-Musical Legacy of IPTA:**
    1.  **Balraj Sahni (1913–1973):** After teaching at Rabindranath Tagore’s **Visva-Bharati (Santiniketan)**, working closely with Mahatma Gandhi at Sevagram (1938), and serving as a BBC Hindi broadcaster in London during World War II, **Balraj Sahni** (along with his wife **Damayanti Sahni**) plunged into the Bombay branch of **IPTA** in **1944**. Directing plays such as **K.A. Abbas’s** *Zubeida* and *Jadoo ki Kursi*, Sahni brought rigorous Stanislavskian naturalism and subaltern empathy to the Indian stage and screen. In **1946**, when **IPTA** produced its first feature film—**Dharti Ke Lal** (*Children of the Earth*, directed by **Khwaja Ahmad Abbas**, based on Bijon Bhattacharya’s *Nabanna* and Krishan Chander’s *Annadata*, with music by **Ravi Shankar**)—**Balraj Sahni** and **Sombhu Mitra**, alongside **Tripti Mitra** and **Zohra Sehgal**, starred in the lead peasant roles. This paved the way for **Chetan Anand’s** IPTA-infused masterpiece **Neecha Nagar** (1946, winner of the Grand Prix / Palme d’Or at the first **Cannes Film Festival**) and **Bimal Roy’s** classic on peasant dispossession, **Do Bigha Zamin (1953)**, in which **Balraj Sahni** delivered his immortal performance as the dispossessed peasant rickshaw-puller **Shambhu Mahato**. Actively marching in working-class rallies in Bombay, Sahni was arrested during a Communist protest in 1949 and was escorted in handcuffs directly from Arthur Road Jail to the film studio floor to shoot his scenes in *Hulchul*.
    2.  **Salil Chowdhury (1925–1995):** Raised on a tea garden in Assam where he listened to both tribal work-songs and his father’s collection of Western classical symphonies (Beethoven, Mozart, Chopin), **Salil Chowdhury** joined **IPTA** and the peasant movement in the 24 Parganas while a college student in **1944**. Travelling from village to village through the paddy fields of Bengal with an IPTA squad during the **1945–46 INA Trials** and the **1946–47 Tebhaga Insurrection**, **Salil Chowdhury** revolutionized Indian political and popular music by fusing Bengal folk tunes (*Bhatiyali*, *Baul*, *Sari*) with complex Western polyphonic harmony, counterpoint, and dynamic choral orchestration (**Ganasangeet** / *People’s Songs*). His iconic IPTA compositions—**Bicharpati Tomar Bichar Korbe Jara** (*O Judge, those who will judge you have woken up today*, written during the 1945 INA Red Fort Trials), **Dheu Uthchhe Kara Tutchhe** (*Waves are rising, prisons are breaking*, celebrating the 1946 RIN Mutiny), **He Samalo Dhan Ho, Kaste Ta Dao Shaan Ho** (*Guard the paddy, sharpen your sickle!*—the roaring battle-anthem of the **Tebhaga Movement**), **Nauniyer Ma**, and his musical setting of Sukanta Bhattacharya’s **Runner** (*The Postal Runner*, sung by Hemanta Mukherjee)—became the permanent sonic repertoire of subaltern resistance across Bengal and later reshaped Bombay cinema when Chowdhury composed the score for **Do Bigha Zamin (1953)** and **Madhumati (1958)**.

### 11. Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 14: From Subaltern Mass Radicalization to Imperial Constitutional Counter-Strategies, Wartime Rupture, and the Transfer of Power (1935–1947)

By the mid-1930s, the simultaneous consolidation of the **All India Kisan Sabha (1936)**, the militant **AITUC** and **Girni Kamgar** working-class networks, the **Communist Party** and **Congress Socialist Party (1934)**, the pan-Indian **Praja Mandal (AISPC)** upsurge across the Princely States, and the cultural counter-hegemony of the **PWA** and **IPTA** had permanently altered the structural balance of forces on the Indian subcontinent. Confronted by this bottom-up radicalization of peasants, industrial workers, and states’ subjects, the British imperial state designed its most elaborate statutory instrument of "divide and rule" and conservative containment: the **Government of India Act of 1935**. By proposing an **All-India Federation** that conditioned central responsibility on the voluntary accession of reactionary **Princely States** (designed to act as a permanent conservative bloc neutralizing the nationalist Left) while granting **Provincial Autonomy** to draw the Congress into the administrative management of colonial law and order, Whitehall sought to fracture the anti-imperialist united front. This structural dialectic directly propels our inquiry into **Part 14 (1935–1947)**, where the 28-month **Congress Provincial Ministries (1937–1939)**, the ideological collision between **Subhas Chandra Bose** and the Congress High Command at **Haripura (1938)** and **Tripuri (1939)**, the dragooning of India into **World War II**, the **1942 Quit India Movement** and its underground parallel governments (**Prati Sarkars**), the man-made catastrophe of the **1943 Great Bengal Famine**, the military offensive of the **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)**, and the post-war volcanic eruptions of the **INA Red Fort Trials** and the **1946 Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny** ultimately shattered the coercive pillars of the Raj and forced the final **1947 Transfer of Power**.
