# Part 1: The Fragmentation of the Mughal Imperium, 18th-Century Regional Polities, and European Mercantilist Penetration (1707–1765)

## I. Fiscal-Military Crisis of the Mughal Imperium and the 18th-Century Historiographical Debate (1707–1761)

### 1. Structural Anatomy of Imperial Disintegration: Jagirdari Crisis and Court Factionalism

#### The Khalisa-Jagir Imbalance, Wars of Succession, and the Sayyid Brothers

The structural disintegration of the centralized Mughal Empire following the death of **Aurangzeb** on **March 3, 1707** was neither a sudden dynastic accident nor a product of personal royal decadence; it was the culmination of an acute systemic contradiction within the Mughal military-fiscal apparatus. For nearly a century and a half, imperial stability rested upon the **Mansabdari-Jagirdari system**, an agrarian extraction mechanism that converted land revenue (*Mal*) into military cavalry power (*Sawar*) via transferable revenue assignments (**Jagirs**). However, **Aurangzeb’s** protracted twenty-six-year military campaign in the Deccan (**1681–1707**) against the **Marathas** and the Deccan Sultanates of **Bijapur** (**1686**) and **Golkonda** (**1687**) triggered an irreversible disequilibrium between available revenue-yielding land (**Paibaqi**) and the rapidly expanding roster of imperial nobles (**Mansabdars**). To co-opt Deccani and Maratha elites into the imperial apparatus without expanding the physical agrarian base at a commensurate rate, the imperial exchequer inflated the numerical ranks (**Zat** and **Sawar**) while depleting the crown lands (**Khalisa Sharifa**), resulting in an acute condition of **Be-Jagiri** (landlessness among newly appointed Mansabdars) and a widening chasm between paper revenue assessment (**Jama**) and actual realization (**Hasil**).

As the central surplus pool contracted, the post-1707 imperial court fractured along ethno-political fault lines as competing aristocratic factions fought zero-sum wars of succession to monopolize the dwindling **Khalisa** revenues and prime **Jagirs** of the Indo-Gangetic plain:

*   **The Four-Way Aristocratic Factional Matrix**: The Mughal nobility splintered into four armed court coalitions: the **Turani** faction (Sunni Central Asian nobles tracing descent from the Timurid heartland, led by **Ghazi-ud-din Khan Firuz Jung**, **Chin Qilich Khan / Nizam-ul-Mulk**, and **Muhammad Amin Khan**); the **Irani** faction (Shia Persian administrators and military commanders, led by **Zulfiqar Khan**, **Asad Khan**, and later **Saadat Khan** and **Safdar Jung**); the **Afghani** faction (settled Rohilla and Bangash Pathan military entrepreneurs dominating the middle Gangetic Doab); and the **Hindustani** faction (Indian-born Muslims, prominently the Barha **Sayyid Brothers**, allied with Rajput Rajas and Khatri/Kayastha financial scribes).
*   **Wars of Succession and Fiscal Exhaustion (1707–1713)**: The succession war of **1707–1709** brought **Bahadur Shah I** (**Shah Alam I**, **1707–1712**) to the throne only after he distributed lavish rank promotions totaling **Rs 3 to 4 crore** from the imperial treasury, exhausting the reserves accumulated at Agra. Following his death in **1712**, the powerful Irani Wazir **Zulfiqar Khan** engineered the accession of the puppet emperor **Jahandar Shah** (**1712–1713**). To liquidate immediate treasury deficits, **Zulfiqar Khan** abolished the fixed cash assessment Regulation (**Zabti**) system and institutionalized imperial **Ijaradari** (speculative revenue farming), auctioning the right to collect land tax to the highest-bidding contractors (**Ijaradars** and *Mahajans*) who extracted rack-rent from the peasantry (*Reza Riaya*) without long-term investment in agrarian reproduction.
*   **The Ascendancy of the Sayyid Brothers (1713–1720)**: In **1713**, the **Hindustani** faction led by the **Sayyid Brothers**—**Sayyid Abdullah Khan** (appointed Wazir) and **Sayyid Hussain Ali Khan** (appointed Mir Bakhshi)—overthrew **Jahandar Shah** and installed **Farrukhsiyar** (**1713–1719**). Operating as the "Kingmakers" of the Mughal state, the **Sayyid Brothers** attempted to construct a broad-based pluralist coalition: they abolished the **Jizya** (poll tax on non-Muslims) and pilgrimage taxes, reconciled the estranged Rajput rulers (**Ajit Singh of Marwar** and **Jai Singh II of Amber**), and negotiated the landmark **Mughal-Maratha Treaty of 1719** with Peshwa **Balaji Vishwanath**. Under this pact, the Sayyids formally recognized the Maratha right to collect **Chauth** (25% of revenue) and **Sardeshmukhi** (an additional 10% levy) across the six Mughal Subas of the Deccan in exchange for a 15,000-strong Maratha cavalry contingent to back the Hindustani faction in Delhi.
*   **Regicide and the Turani Counter-Coup (1719–1724)**: When **Farrukhsiyar** conspired against them, the **Sayyid Brothers**, backed by Peshwa **Balaji Vishwanath’s** Maratha troops, blinded and executed the Emperor in **April 1719**—the first regicide by nobles in Mughal history. After rapidly cycling through two consumptive princes (**Rafi-ud-Darajat** and **Rafi-ud-Daulah / Shah Jahan II**), they enthroned **Muhammad Shah 'Rangila'** (**1719–1748**). Alarmed by the Hindustani-Maratha monopoly of state patronage, the **Turani** and **Irani** factions united under **Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah**, **Muhammad Amin Khan**, and **Saadat Khan**, assassinating **Hussain Ali Khan** in **October 1720** and defeating **Abdullah Khan** at the **Battle of Hasanpur** (**November 1720**). When **Nizam-ul-Mulk’s** subsequent attempt as Wazir (**1722–1724**) to abolish **Ijaradari**, re-discipline the corrupt court nobility, and reclaim illegally alienated **Jagirs** was sabotaged by **Muhammad Shah’s** courtiers, the Nizam abandoned Delhi in **1724** to carve out an autonomous principality in the Deccan, signaling the irreversible transformation of imperial provinces (**Subas**) into autonomous regional states.

### 2. Exogenous Military Shocks: Nadir Shah (1739) and Ahmad Shah Abdali’s Invasions

#### The Battle of Karnal (1739), Durrani Raids, and the Panipat Rupture (1761)

While internal fiscal decay hollowed out the Mughal core, the geopolitical vacuum along the Kabul-Qandahar-Ghazni scientific frontier exposed the Indo-Gangetic heartland to catastrophic exogenous military-fiscal shocks from Persia and Afghanistan. Since the reign of **Akbar**, the security of northern India had depended upon controlling the Hindu Kush mountain passes and paying regular subsidies to the frontier Afghan tribes. By the **1730s**, imperial bankruptcy halted these frontier remittances, leaving the governor of Kabul unpaid for five years and dismantling the empire's northwestern defensive glacis just as **Nadir Shah Afshar** consolidated a modernized, firearm-intensive military state in Iran.

The mechanics and consequences of these northwestern invasions shattered both the symbolic legitimacy and the bullion reserves of the Mughal centre:

*   **Nadir Shah’s Invasion and the Battle of Karnal (February 24, 1739)**: Provoked by Delhi’s failure to prevent Afghan rebels from crossing into Mughal Kabul, **Nadir Shah** crossed the Khyber Pass and confronted **Muhammad Shah’s** massive but logistically paralyzed host at the **Battle of Karnal** (**February 24, 1739**). While the Mughal camp dragged along heavy siege ordnance and hundreds of thousands of non-combatant retainers, its command was paralyzed by rivalry between the Turani **Nizam-ul-Mulk**, the Hindustani Mir Bakhshi **Khan-i-Dauran**, and the Irani **Saadat Khan** of Awadh. **Nadir Shah** deployed mobile camel-mounted swivel guns (**Zamburaks**) and disciplined heavy musketeers (**Jazayerchis**) to ambush **Saadat Khan’s** baggage train and lure **Khan-i-Dauran’s** elephant-mounted cavalry into a concentrated crossfire kill-zone. Within three hours, **Khan-i-Dauran** was mortally wounded, **Saadat Khan** was captured, and the Mughal army was blockaded into starvation.
*   **The Sack of Delhi and the Bullion Siphon (March–May 1739)**: Although **Nizam-ul-Mulk** initially negotiated a **Rs 50 lakh** indemnity for **Nadir Shah’s** withdrawal, **Saadat Khan**—furious that the Nizam had secured the post of Mir Bakhshi—informed the Persian monarch that the imperial vaults of Shahjahanabad held vastly greater wealth. **Nadir Shah** occupied Delhi, read the **Khutba** and struck coins (**Sikka**) in his own name (suspending Mughal sovereignty for 57 days), and ordered a retaliatory general massacre (*Qatl-i-Aam*) on **March 22, 1739** that killed over 20,000 citizens. He departed in **May 1739** with an estimated **Rs 70 crore** in bullion, jewels, Shah Jahan’s **Peacock Throne** (*Takht-i-Taus*), and the **Koh-i-Noor** diamond, alongside thousands of master masons and artisans. Territorially, **Muhammad Shah** was forced to cede all Mughal provinces west of the River Indus (Kabul, Sindh, and western Punjab), permanently stripping North India of its strategic mountain barrier.
*   **Ahmad Shah Abdali’s Invasions (1748–1767)**: Following **Nadir Shah’s** assassination in **1747**, his Durrani Afghan commander **Ahmad Shah Abdali** (**Ahmad Shah Durrani**) established an independent kingdom at Qandahar and launched eight successive invasions of northern India between **1748 and 1767**. Viewing the rich alluvial plains of Punjab and the Gangetic Doab as an external fiscal reservoir to finance his tribal confederacy, **Abdali** defeated the Mughals after an initial setback at **Manupur** (**1748**), annexed Lahore and Multan in **1752**, and sacked Delhi and Mathura in **1757**, installing the Rohilla chief **Najib-ud-Daulah** as his Mir Bakhshi and supreme agent over the puppet emperor **Alamgir II** (**1754–1759**).
*   **The Third Battle of Panipat (January 14, 1761)**: When the Maratha Peshwa’s brother **Raghunath Rao** marched into Punjab in **1758**, expelled **Abdali’s** son **Timur Shah**, and planted the Maratha banner at Attock, **Ahmad Shah Abdali** returned in **1759**, forging an Indo-Afghan coalition with **Najib-ud-Daulah** (Rohilkhand) and **Shuja-ud-Daula** (Nawab of Awadh). At the **Third Battle of Panipat** (**January 14, 1761**), the Maratha expeditionary army under **Sadashivrao Bhau** and **Vishwasrao**—isolated by scorched-earth logistics, alienated from the local Jat (**Suraj Mal**) and Rajput rulers due to predatory Maratha tribute demands, and immobilized by heavy French-style siege artillery under **Ibrahim Khan Gardi**—was enveloped and annihilated by **Abdali’s** mobile cavalry and **Zamburak** camel-gun formations. Panipat did not decide who would rule India, but who would *not*: it shattered the Maratha bid for pan-Indian imperial succession while leaving a power vacuum in Bengal and Hindustan that the **English East India Company** systematically exploited between **1757 and 1765**.

### 3. The 18th-Century Historiographical Debate: Imperial Decay vs. Regional Commercial Dynamism

#### Traditionalist "Dark Age" Models vs. Revisionist Portfolio Capitalism

The interpretation of the 18th century constitutes one of the most fiercely contested ideological battlegrounds in modern Indian historiography. Rather than a monolithic era, the century is bifurcated by two structural transitions: the **first transition (c. 1707–1750s)** from the centralized Mughal Empire to decentralized regional polities, and the **second transition (1757–1818)** from regional Indian polities to the colonial mercantilist hegemony of the **English East India Company**.

| Analytical Dimension | Traditionalist / Imperialist & Early Nationalist School | Aligarh Marxist School (Structural Agrarian Crisis) | Revisionist & Neo-Revisionist School (Regional Dynamism) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Key Historians** | **William Irvine**, **Jadunath Sarkar**, **Ishwari Prasad** | **Irfan Habib**, **M. Athar Ali**, **Satish Chandra**, **Shireen Moosvi** | **C.A. Bayly**, **Muzaffar Alam**, **Frank Perlin**, **B.R. Grover**, **P.J. Marshall**, **Sanjay Subrahmanyam** |
| **Core Analytical Thesis** | The 18th century was a chaotic "Dark Age" of political anarchy, moral decay, and religious bigotry triggered by **Aurangzeb’s** orthodoxy and the weak personal character of Later Mughals. | Systemic collapse driven by the **Mansabdari-Jagirdari crisis** and tripolar agrarian exploitation (State vs. *Mansabdar* vs. *Zamindar* vs. *Ryot*), causing peasant rebellions that ruptured the empire. | The 18th century was not a generalized economic collapse; imperial decentralization coincided with vibrant regional commercialization, urban growth (*Qasbahs*), and "Portfolio Capitalism." |
| **View of Regional States** | Fragmentary warlord states incapable of maintaining law, order, or pan-Indian unity, thereby justifying or inviting British colonial intervention. | Symptom of political fragmentation that weakened Indian resistance to European mercantilism and initiated economic stagnation prior to **1757**. | Dynamic, centralizing "military-fiscal" states (Bengal, Awadh, Mysore, Marathas) that deepened revenue penetration and integrated merchant-bankers into state finance. |
| **Timing of the Colonial Rupture** | **1757 (Plassey)** viewed as a progressive restoration of administrative order over native anarchy. | **1757/1765 (Plassey/Diwani)** viewed as an abrupt, catastrophic structural rupture that replaced indigenous surplus circulation with unrequited colonial **Drain of Wealth**. | Continuity thesis: the **English East India Company** succeeded by inserting itself into existing Indian commercial networks, military labour markets, and revenue-farming (*Ijara*) systems. |

A structural synthesis of these competing historiographical models reveals three distinct analytical layers:

*   **The Aligarh Marxist Political Economy Critique**: Spearheaded by **Irfan Habib** (*The Agrarian System of Mughal India*), **Satish Chandra** (*Parties and Politics at the Mughal Court*), and **M. Athar Ali** (*The Mughal Nobility under Aurangzeb*), this school demonstrates that the Mughal state extracted nearly the entire agrarian surplus above bare subsistence. Because **Jagirdars** were transferred every three to four years to prevent them from developing local feudal roots, they lacked any incentive to invest in long-term agricultural productivity. Instead, they maximized short-term extraction, provoking armed peasant insurrections led by hereditary rural intermediaries (**Zamindars**) among the **Jats**, **Satnamis**, **Sikhs**, and **Marathas**. For the Aligarh school, and later **Sushil Chaudhury** and **Om Prakash**, the British conquest of **1757–1765** constituted a violent discontinuity that terminated indigenous textile export growth and halted bullion inflows.
*   **The Revisionist Commercial and "Portfolio Capitalist" Thesis**: Challenging the "Dark Age" orthodoxy, **C.A. Bayly** (*Rulers, Townsmen and Bazaars*), **Muzaffar Alam** (*The Crisis of Empire in Mughal North India*), and **Sanjay Subrahmanyam** argued that the decline of Delhi merely redistributed wealth and skilled personnel to flourishing regional capitals—such as **Murshidabad**, **Faizabad/Lucknow**, **Hyderabad**, **Poona**, and **Tanjore**—and intermediate rural market towns (**Qasbahs**). **Zamindari** uprisings were reinterpreted not as pauperized peasant revolts, but as assertions by newly prosperous rural gentry seeking to retain a larger share of rising agricultural wealth. Crucially, the 18th century witnessed the rise of **"Portfolio Capitalists"** (such as the **Jagat Seths** and **Omichand** in Bengal, or **Gopaldas Manohardas** in Banaras)—hybrid elites who simultaneously straddled wholesale trade, state revenue farming (**Ijaradari**), bullion minting, continental credit transfer (**Hundis**), and military entrepreneurship.
*   **The Structural Synthesis**: While the Revisionists correctly proved that pre-1757 regional India was commercially monetized and militarily innovative rather than an economic wasteland, their attempt to portray the rise of the **English East India Company** as a mere "continuity" of indigenous regional state-building obscures the fundamental fiscal rupture of **1765**. Unlike indigenous regional rulers (**Alivardi Khan** or **Tipu Sultan**) who spent extracted land revenue within the domestic economy, the Company's acquisition of the **Diwani** fused state taxation with an external corporate monopoly, converting domestic tax surplus into unrequited European export cargoes and inaugurating the colonial drain.

## II. The Three-Tiered Regional State System: Revenue Farming, Portfolio Capitalism, and Military Modernization

### 4. Tier I — The Successor States: Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad

The **Successor States** (**Bengal**, **Awadh**, and **Hyderabad**) emerged when high-ranking Mughal provincial governors (**Subadars**) combined the offices of civil revenue administration (**Diwani**) and military-executive command (**Nizamat**)—splitting the institutional check-and-balance instituted by **Akbar**—and rendered their offices hereditary. Crucially, none of these rulers formally repudiated Mughal sovereignty; they continued to mint coins in the name of the Mughal Emperor, recite the imperial **Khutba**, and purchase formal deeds of investiture (**Sanads**), even as they ceased regular revenue remittances to Delhi, curtailed **Khalisa** and imperial **Jagir** lands within their provinces, and forged autonomous regional alliances with indigenous banking houses and local **Zamindars**.

#### Nawabi Bengal (Murshid Quli Khan, Shuja-ud-din, Alivardi Khan) and the Jagat Seth Fiscal-Banking Nexus

Bengal was the most commercially monetized and fiscally lucrative province of the 18th-century world economy, absorbing massive inflows of silver bullion from Europe and Asia in exchange for fine cotton muslins, calicoes, raw silk, and saltpetre:

*   **Murshid Quli Khan (1717–1727) and the Malzamini Revenue Architecture**: Appointed Diwan of Bengal by **Aurangzeb** in **1700** and formally made Nazim (Governor) of Bengal and Orissa in **1717**, **Murshid Quli Khan** shifted the provincial capital from Dacca to **Murshidabad** and executed a comprehensive agrarian reorganization. He resumed the majority of Mughal **Jagirs** in Bengal, transferring the displaced Mansabdars to the poorer, unpacified province of Orissa, and converted the reclaimed Bengal lands directly into crown land (**Khalisa**). Through the **Malzamini** system (instituted in **1722**), he bypassed small defaulting intermediaries and consolidated revenue collection into the hands of fifteen to twenty mega-**Zamindaris** (such as **Rajshahi** under Ramjivan, **Burdwan**, **Dinajpur**, and **Nadia**), which together accounted for over half of Bengal’s total **Jama**. Defaulting Muslim and Hindu Zamindars were subjected to harsh physical coercion (the notorious *Baikuntha* filth-pit) or dispossessed in favor of compliant Hindu Khatri and Bengali Kayastha/Brahmin officials, while landless peasants were provided agricultural credit (**Taqavi** loans) to expand cultivation.
*   **Shuja-ud-din Muhammad Khan (1727–1739) and the Incorporation of Bihar**: Succeeding his father-in-law, **Shuja-ud-din** annexed Bihar to the Bengal Subadari in **1733** (appointing **Alivardi Khan** as Naib Nazim in Patna), thereby bringing the entire lower Gangetic trade artery and the Bihar saltpetre/opium production zones under a single regional administration. He governed through a triumvirate Advisory Council comprising **Haji Ahmad**, **Alamchand** (Rai Rayan of the Diwani), and the banking magnate **Jagat Seth Fatehchand**, while imposing additional fiscal cesses (**Abwabs**) on top of **Murshid Quli Khan’s** base assessment.
*   **Alivardi Khan (1740–1756) and the Maratha Bargi Wars**: After overthrowing Shuja-ud-din’s son **Sarfaraz Khan** at the **Battle of Giria** (**April 1740**) with the financial backing of the **Jagat Seths** and purchasing an imperial **Sanad** from **Muhammad Shah** for **Rs 2 crore**, **Alivardi Khan** halted all further revenue remittances to Delhi. His sixteen-year reign was dominated by grueling military defense against annual invasions (**1742–1751**) by the Nagpur **Bhonsle Marathas** (**Raghuji Bhonsle’s** *Bargi* light cavalry), which devastated western Bengal’s silk and weaving districts and drove refugees across the Hooghly into British Calcutta. In **1751**, an exhausted **Alivardi Khan** signed a peace treaty ceding the revenues of **Orissa** (*de facto* annexation) and agreeing to pay **Rs 12 lakh** annually as **Chauth** for Bengal and Bihar. Fully aware of the **English East India Company’s** military triumphs in the Carnatic, **Alivardi Khan** likened the Europeans to *"a hive of bees whose honey could be reaped if left undisturbed, but who would sting to death if provoked"*—rigorously forbidding both the British and French from fortifying their factories at Calcutta and Chandernagore.
*   **The House of Jagat Seth as State Treasurer and Mint Monopoly**: Founded by the Marwari Oswal Jain banker **Manikchand** and elevated by Emperor **Farrukhsiyar** and **Muhammad Shah** under his nephew **Fatehchand** (conferred the hereditary title **Jagat Seth** or "Banker of the World" in **1723**), this banking house operated as the central bank of Nawabi Bengal. The **Jagat Seths** exercised an absolute monopoly over the Murshidabad royal mint, earning immense seigniorage and exchange commissions (**Batta**) by assaying and recoining all foreign silver bullion imported by the **English**, **Dutch**, and **French** companies into **Murshidabad Sicca Rupees**. They received the land revenue deposits from Bengal's **Zamindars**, provided security bonds (*Zamin*) for tax arrears, financed the Nawab’s military campaigns, and remitted Bengal’s annual **Rs 1 crore** tribute to Delhi via a single paper promissory note (**Hundi**). When **Siraj-ud-Daulah** alienated **Jagat Seth Mahtab Chand** in **1756–1757**, this very financial oligarchy bankrolled the **Plassey** conspiracy with **Robert Clive**.

#### Nawabi Awadh (Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk and Safdar Jung) and the Muqarrari-Ijaradari Matrix

Positioned strategically across the fertile alluvial Gangetic Doab linking Delhi to Bengal, **Awadh** controlled the lucrative Himalayan and trans-Gangetic trade routes as well as the richest recruiting ground for peasant-infantry (**Baksariya** musketeers) in North India:

*   **Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk (1722–1739)**: An Irani (Khorasani) noble appointed Subadar of Awadh in **1722** following the overthrow of the **Sayyid Brothers**, **Saadat Khan** immediately crushed the autonomous military power of the rebellious local **Shaikhzadas** of Lucknow and the recalcitrant Rajput **Zamindars** of Baiswara, dismantling hundreds of mud-forts (*Garhis*). In **1723**, he executed a fresh revenue settlement that curbed arbitrary **Jagirdari** exactions while raising provincial revenue from **Rs 70 lakh** to over **Rs 2 crore**. By converting imperial **Jagirs** within Awadh into **Muqarrari** (fixed-rent) holdings and **Ijaradari** contracts supervised directly by the Subadar’s agents, he rendered the Mughal nobles holding assignments in Awadh wholly dependent on his treasury. Following his capture by **Nadir Shah** at **Karnal** and his inability to deliver the promised **Rs 20 crore** ransom in Delhi, **Saadat Khan** committed suicide by taking poison in **March 1739**.
*   **Safdar Jung (1739–1754) and the Ganga-Jamuni Synthesis**: Succeeding his uncle and father-in-law after paying **Nadir Shah** **Rs 2 crore** for confirmation, **Abul Mansur Khan Safdar Jung** consolidated Awadh’s internal administration, annexed the strategic fortress of Allahabad (**1748**), and served simultaneously as the imperial **Wazir** of the Mughal Empire (**1748–1753**) under Emperor **Ahmad Shah Bahadur**. **Safdar Jung** waged protracted campaigns against the adjacent **Rohilla** and **Bangash Pathans** of Farrukhabad (employing **Maratha** and **Jat** mercenaries to crush them in **1751–1752**). Internally, he institutionalized a syncretic **Ganga-Jamuni** administrative culture from his capital at **Faizabad**, appointing a Hindu Kayastha, **Maharaja Nawab Rai**, as his highest deputy (*Naib*) and Diwan, before passing a consolidated, prosperous realm to his son **Shuja-ud-Daula** (**1754–1775**).

#### Asaf Jahi Hyderabad (Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah) and Deccan Subadari Consolidation

In the Deccan peninsula, **Mir Qamar-ud-din Chin Qilich Khan**—awarded the titles **Nizam-ul-Mulk** (**1713**) and **Asaf Jah** (**1725**)—established the autonomous **Asaf Jahi Dynasty** of **Hyderabad** (**1724–1948**):

*   **The Battle of Shakar Kheda (October 1724)**: Disgusted by the factional paralysis of **Muhammad Shah’s** court in Delhi, **Nizam-ul-Mulk** marched south in **1724** without imperial permission. At the **Battle of Shakar Kheda** (**October 11, 1724**) in Berar, he defeated and killed **Mubariz Khan**, the rival imperial governor of the Deccan who had been secretly incited by Delhi to destroy him. Compelled by military reality, **Muhammad Shah** recognized the Nizam as the permanent Viceroy of the six Deccan Subas and conferred the title **Asaf Jah**.
*   **Internal Consolidation and Maratha Containment**: While maintaining the fiction of allegiance to the Mughal Emperor—never formally declaring independence or assuming royal insignia—**Nizam-ul-Mulk** conducted independent wars, signed treaties, and conferred **Mansabs** and **Jagirs** across the Deccan. He co-opted powerful Hindu warrior chiefs (**Deshmukhs** and **Samasthan** rajas in Telangana) and appointed the Hindu administrator **Puran Chand** as his Diwan. However, Hyderabad’s agrarian base was perpetually squeezed by the expanding **Maratha Confederacy** under Peshwa **Baji Rao I**: defeated by Baji Rao’s mobile cavalry warfare at the **Battle of Palkhed** (**February 1728**) and the **Battle of Bhopal** (**December 1737**), the Nizam was forced by the **Treaty of Mungi-Shevgaon** (**1728**) to recognize Maratha rights to **Chauth** and **Sardeshmukhi** in the Deccan. Following **Nizam-ul-Mulk’s** death in **May 1748**, a bitter war of succession between his son **Nasir Jung** and grandson **Muzaffar Jung** opened the floodgates for **French** and **British** military intervention during the **Second Carnatic War**.

### 5. Tier II — Warrior and Rebel Polities: Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, and Rohilla-Bangash Pathans

Unlike the Successor States, which inherited pre-existing Mughal provincial administrative machinery intact, the **Warrior and Rebel Polities** originated through armed anti-imperial agrarian and military insurgencies led by non-Mughal rural elites (**Zamindars**, peasant-caste brotherhoods, and tribal military entrepreneurs) who constructed entirely new fiscal-military structures from below.

#### The Maratha Confederacy: Peshwa Centralization, Chauth-Sardeshmukhi Extraction, and Northern Expansion

Following **Aurangzeb’s** death in **1707**, **Zulfiqar Khan** released **Shahu** (grandson of **Shivaji**) from Mughal captivity to ignite a debilitating civil war against **Tarabai** (widow of **Rajaram**, ruling from Kolhapur in the name of **Shivaji II**). **Shahu’s** triumph at the **Battle of Khed** (**October 1707**) was engineered by the diplomatic and financial genius of **Balaji Vishwanath**, a Chitpavan Brahmin revenue official (*Sarsubedar*) whom **Shahu** appointed as **Peshwa** (Prime Minister) in **1713**, transforming the Maratha kingdom from a centralized royal monarchy under the **Ashtapradhan** into a decentralized, expansionist **Maratha Confederacy** directed from **Poona**:

*   **Balaji Vishwanath (1713–1720) and the Saranjami Fiscal-Feudal System**: To rally fractious Maratha warlords (*Sardars*) behind **Shahu**, **Balaji Vishwanath** institutionalized the **Saranjami** system (analogous to the Mughal *Jagir*), granting hereditary revenue assignments (**Saranjams** and **Watans**) to military commanders in exchange for maintaining cavalry contingents. By securing the **Mughal-Maratha Treaty of 1719** from the **Sayyid Brothers** (ratified by **Muhammad Shah**), he legalized the collection of **Chauth** (25% of gross revenue assessed to prevent Maratha raids, divided into **25% Babti** for the Chhatrapati, **6% Sahotra** for the Pant Sachiv, **3% Nadgaunda**, and **66% Mokasa** distributed directement to the Maratha *Sardars* maintaining troops) and **Sardeshmukhi** (an additional 10% hereditary levy claimed entirely by the Chhatrapati as head *Deshmukh*). Because **66% (Mokasa)** of external **Chauth** went directly to the campaigning generals, it created a self-propelling structural incentive for autonomous military expansion across India, simultaneously laying the seeds of regional warlord autonomy.
*   **Baji Rao I (1720–1740) and the Doctrine of Northern Expansion**: Succeeding his father at age twenty, **Baji Rao I** articulated the strategic doctrine of striking directly at the withered trunk of the Mughal tree so that its provincial branches would fall of their own weight, championing the ideal of **Hindu Pad Padshahi** (though pragmatically allying with Muslim and Hindu rulers alike). Mastering high-velocity light cavalry warfare (*Ganimi Kava*), **Baji Rao I** outmaneuvered **Nizam-ul-Mulk** at **Palkhed** (**1728**) and **Bhopal** (**1737**), conquered **Malwa** and **Bundelkhand** (assisted by **Chhatrasal**), wrested **Gujarat** from Mughal control, defeated the Portuguese to capture **Salsette and Bassein** (**1739** under his brother **Chimaji Appa**), and raided the suburbs of Delhi in **1737**. To administer these vast conquests outside the Maharashtra Swarajya core, **Baji Rao I** elevated loyal non-Brahmin military commanders who evolved into the five semi-autonomous houses of the **Maratha Confederacy**: the **Gaekwads of Baroda** (**Pilaji** and **Damaji Gaekwad**), the **Holkars of Indore** (**Malhar Rao Holkar**), the **Scindias of Gwalior** (**Ranoji Scindia**), the **Bhonsles of Nagpur** (**Raghuji Bhonsle**, initially aligned with Shahu’s court at Satara), and the **Peshwa at Poona**.
*   **Balaji Baji Rao / Nana Saheb (1740–1761) and the Apex of Peshwa Hegemony**: Following **Shahu’s** death in **1749**, the **Sangola Agreement (1750)** formally transferred supreme sovereign authority from the Chhatrapati at Satara to the **Peshwa** at **Poona**. Under **Balaji Baji Rao**, Maratha armies overran Karnataka, extracted **Chauth** from Bengal/Orissa (**1751**), signed the **Ahamdiya Treaty (1752)** placing the Mughal Emperor under Maratha protection against internal and external foes in exchange for the **Chauth** of the northwest, and pushed to **Attock** in **1758**. Yet this rapid territorial overstretch lacked an integrated bureaucratic administrative apparatus: outside Maharashtra and parts of Malwa, Maratha rule operated as a predatory tribute-extracting mechanism (**Khandani** and **Chauth**) that alienated the **Rajputs**, **Jats**, **Sikhs**, and **Awadh**, culminating in the catastrophic encirclement at the **Third Battle of Panipat** (**January 14, 1761**)—though the young Peshwa **Madhav Rao I** (**1761–1772**), aided by generals **Mahadji Scindia** and **Nana Phadnavis**, rapidly restored Maratha power in North India within a decade (reinstalling Emperor **Shah Alam II** in Delhi in **1772**).

#### Sikh Agrarian Insurgency (Banda Bahadur) to the Dal Khalsa and the Twelve Misls

In the Punjab plains, the transformation of the **Sikh Panth** into an armed egalitarian commonwealth (**Khalsa**, instituted by the tenth Guru, **Guru Gobind Singh**, in **1699**) fused lower-caste and **Jat** peasant resistance against Mughal **Faujdars** and high-caste hill rajas into a revolutionary anti-feudal state-building movement:

*   **Banda Singh Bahadur’s Agrarian Revolution (1708–1716)**: Commissioned by **Guru Gobind Singh** at Nanded shortly before the Guru’s assassination in **1708**, **Banda Singh Bahadur** (**Lachhman Dev / Madho Das**) rallied the **Jat** peasantry, low-caste artisans, and untouchables of Punjab. In **May 1710**, he defeated and killed **Wazir Khan**, the Mughal Faujdar of **Sirhind**, establishing the first sovereign Sikh territorial state between the Sutlej and Yamuna rivers with his fortified capital at **Lohgarh** (Mukhlispur). **Banda Bahadur** struck coins and issued official seals in the names of **Guru Nanak** and **Guru Gobind Singh**, and executed a radical anti-feudal agrarian revolution: he abolished the Mughal **Zamindari** system across his domains, conferring direct proprietary rights over the soil onto the actual peasant cultivators (*Halwahaks*). Besieged by Emperor **Farrukhsiyar’s** forces at the fortress of **Gurdas Nangal**, **Banda Bahadur** and over 700 followers were captured in **December 1715** and executed under torture in Delhi in **June 1716**.
*   **Persecution, Kapur Singh, and the Dal Khalsa (1716–1748)**: Despite relentless genocidal campaigns launched by the Mughal governors of Lahore, **Abdus Samad Khan** and **Zakariya Khan** (**1726–1745**), the Sikh guerrilla bands (*Jathas*) survived in the Lakhi jungle and Siwalik hills. Taking advantage of the administrative paralysis caused by **Nadir Shah’s 1739** invasion and **Ahmad Shah Abdali’s** raids, **Nawab Kapur Singh** and **Jassa Singh Ahluwalia** unified the scattered *Jathas* at Amritsar on Baisakhi Day in **1748** into a single unified military army, the **Dal Khalsa**, governed through bi-annual democratic assemblies at the Akal Takht known as the **Sarbat Khalsa**, which passed binding collective resolutions (**Gurmata**).
*   **The Rakhi System and the Twelve Misls (1753–1799)**: To finance the **Dal Khalsa** and protect the Punjab peasantry from both Mughal tax collectors and Afghan marauders, the Sikhs instituted the **Rakhi** system in **1753**—a protective levy of one-fifth (**20%**) of agricultural produce paid twice a year by villages in exchange for guaranteed military security against all external extortion. Over the **1750s and 1760s**, the **Dal Khalsa** crystallized into **Twelve Misls** (confederate warrior brotherhoods based on equality and shared territory, derived from the Arabic *Misl* meaning "equal" or "alike"): **Ahluwalia** (led by **Jassa Singh Ahluwalia**), **Bhangi** (the largest and most powerful in early decades, controlling Amritsar, Lahore, and Multan under **Hari Singh Bhangi**), **Sukerchakia** (based at Gujranwala under **Charat Singh**, **Maha Singh**, and later **Ranjit Singh**), **Kanhaiya** (**Jai Singh** and **Sada Kaur**), **Ramgarhia** (**Jassa Singh Ramgarhia**), **Phulkian** (Patiala, Nabha, Jind under **Ala Singh**—operating south of the Sutlej as the *Cis-Sutlej* states outside the Dal Khalsa), **Faizullapuria / Singhpuria** (**Nawab Kapur Singh**), **Nakai**, **Dallewalia**, **Karorsinghia**, **Nishanwalian**, and **Shahid**.

#### Jat State-Building under Suraj Mal of Bharatpur and the Rohilla-Bangash Pathans

Simultaneously, the strategic Agra-Mathura-Delhi corridor and the Rohilkhand Doab were seized by two fiercely autonomous warrior formations:

*   **The Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur under Churaman, Badan Singh, and Suraj Mal**: Originating as an agrarian peasant-caste rebellion against **Aurangzeb’s** revenue collectors in the Mathura-Agra region under the leadership of the *Zamindars* **Gokula** (**1669**), **Rajaram** (**1685**), and **Churaman** (**1695–1721**), the Jats constructed mud-bastion jungle forts (**Garhis**) to disrupt the imperial highway between Delhi and Agra. After **Churaman’s** nephew **Badan Singh** (**1721–1756**) received the title of *Braj-Raj* from **Jai Singh II of Amber** and consolidated the four central forts of **Deeg**, **Kumher**, **Weir**, and **Bharatpur**, the Jat state reached its zenith under his adopted son and successor **Maharaja Suraj Mal** (**1756–1763**). Celebrated as the *"Plato of the Jat Tribe"* and the *"Jat Ulysses"* for his political sagacity and fiscal acumen, **Suraj Mal** expanded the Bharatpur kingdom across Agra, Mathura, Meerut, Aligarh, Etawah, and Gurgaon, generating an annual revenue exceeding **Rs 1.75 crore**. He excluded his own Jat clan kinsmen from holding independent military fiefs, instituted a centralized cash revenue bureaucracy closely modeled on the Mughal **Zabti** system, and engineered the impregnable mud-walled ramparts of **Lohagarh Fort** at Bharatpur (whose thick clay bastions absorbed European cannonballs without shattering). Before **Panipat (1761)**, **Suraj Mal** presciently advised **Sadashivrao Bhau** to leave heavy artillery and non-combatants behind at Bharatpur and wage light guerrilla warfare against **Abdali**; when Bhau arrogantly rejected this counsel, **Suraj Mal** withdrew his forces, though he later sheltered and fed tens of thousands of surviving Maratha refugees fleeing Panipat, before dying in battle against the Rohilla chief **Najib-ud-Daulah** in **December 1763**.
*   **The Rohilla and Bangash Pathan Principalities**: Exploiting the collapse of Mughal control across the upper Gangetic plain, migrant **Yusufzai** and **Bangash** Afghan horse-traders and mercenary soldiers carved out two contiguous military states. At **Farrukhabad** (around Kanauj and Aligarh), **Muhammad Khan Bangash** (**1713–1743**) established an autonomous **Bangash Pathan** principality covering the central Doab between Allahabad and Delhi. Immediately north of the Ganga in the fertile Himalayan foothills (modern Bareilly, Moradabad, Badaun, and Rampur), **Ali Muhammad Khan** (**1721–1748**) consolidated the Afghan **Rohilla** kingdom of **Rohilkhand** during the chaos of **Nadir Shah’s 1739** invasion. Governed after **1748** by the regent **Hafiz Rahmat Khan** and allied with the kingmaker **Najib-ud-Daulah** at Najibabad and Delhi, the **Rohillas** introduced a highly efficient agricultural and commercial regime, protecting peasant property and trans-Himalayan trade while locked in perpetual warfare against the **Marathas** and **Nawabi Awadh** until **Shuja-ud-Daula**, backed by **Warren Hastings’** British brigade, annexed Rohilkhand in the **Rohilla War of 1774**.

### 6. Tier III — Modernizing Southern Military-Fiscal Polities: Mysore and Travancore

In sharp contrast to both the revenue-farming **Successor States** and the decentralized **Warrior Confederacies**, the southern polities of **Mysore** and **Travancore** pioneered full-scale **Military-Fiscalism**: the systematic elimination of feudal landlord intermediaries (**Poligars**, **Deshmukhs**, **Ettuveetil Pillamar**), the imposition of direct state taxation on the peasantry, and the establishment of state commercial monopolies over global export commodities to finance permanent, European-drilled standing infantry armies and indigenous ordnance industries.

#### Mysore under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan: Direct Ryotwari Taxation, State Commercial Monopolies, and Iron-Cased Rocket Artillery

Originally a viceroyalty of the **Vijayanagara Empire**, the **Wodeyar** kingdom of **Mysore** under **Chikka Krishnaraja Wodeyar II** (**1734–1766**) had fallen under the oligarchic dominance of two brothers—the Dalavayi (Commander-in-Chief) **Devaraja** and the Sarvadhikari (Finance Minister) **Nanjaraja**. Exposed to perpetual **Maratha** tribute raids and the Anglo-French **Carnatic Wars**, the Mysore military machine was revolutionized from within by **Hyder Ali** (**c. 1721–1782**), an illiterate but brilliant military commander (*Faujdar* of **Dindigul** in **1755**) who seized *de facto* sovereign power in **1761**, and his son **Tipu Sultan** (**1782–1799**):

*   **Military Modernization under Hyder Ali (1761–1782)**: Recognizing while serving in the **Second Carnatic War** that traditional Indian feudal levies could not withstand rapid-firing European flintlock musketry and mobile field artillery, **Hyder Ali** hired **French** engineers and artillery experts to establish a modern munitions arsenal and gun-foundry at **Dindigul** (**1755**). Upon assuming power at **Seringapatam** in **1761**, he crushed the autonomous feudal **Poligars** (**Palaiyakkarars**) and **Deshmukhs**, conquered **Bednur** (renamed **Hyder Nagar**), **Canara**, **Sunda**, and the **Malabar** coast (securing maritime ports and coastal trade), and reorganized the Mysore army along **Risala** divisions equipped with French-drilled infantry (*Juzailburdars*) and rapid light cavalry, successfully dictating terms to the British at the gates of Madras in the **First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769)**.
*   **Tipu Sultan’s Direct Agrarian Taxation and Feudal Expropriation**: Succeeding his father in **December 1782**, **Tipu Sultan** explicitly broke with the Mughal legitimist fiction: he dropped the Mughal Emperor’s name from the **Khutba**, struck coins in his own sovereign right without mentioning **Shah Alam II**, introduced a new **Mauludi** lunisolar calendar, and established direct diplomatic relations with **Louis XVI’s** France, Ottoman Constantinople, Afghanistan, and Mauritius, planting a **Tree of Liberty** at Seringapatam as "Citizen Tipu" of the French Jacobin Club. In the agrarian sphere, **Tipu Sultan** executed the most radical anti-feudal fiscal reform in 18th-century India: he abolished **Jagirdari** and stripped the hereditary **Poligars** and **Deshmukhs** of their tax-collecting privileges and unauthorized cesses, establishing a direct **Ryotwari**-style relationship between the state bureaucracy (*Amildars*) and the peasant cultivator. State revenue agents provided **Taqavi** advances to bring wasteland (*Banjar*) under the plough, penalized officials who extorted forced labor (**Begari**), introduced **sericulture** (mulberry and silkworm cultivation) into Mysore, and laid the foundation of the Kaveri river dam infrastructure.
*   **State Commercial Monopolies and Trading Factories**: To prevent the **English East India Company** at Tellicherry and Madras from draining Mysore’s wealth or utilizing commercial access for political subversion, **Tipu Sultan** established a strict **State Commercial Monopoly** (**1785–1793**) over high-value export commodities—**sandalwood**, **betel nut**, **black pepper**, **cardamom**, **elephants**, **gold**, and **timber**. In **1785**, he imposed a total trade embargo halting all pepper and sandalwood exports from the Malabar coast to the English Company. By **1793–1794**, he promulgated a comprehensive Commercial Code establishing thirty state-run trading corporations (**Malikut-Tujjar**) across Mysore and overseas factories at **Muscat** (Oman), **Ormuz**, **Jeddah**, and **Pegu** (Burma), inviting private subjects to buy dividend-bearing shares (paying up to 50% returns for small investors) while building a blue-water **Mysore Navy** of 40 warships across dockyards at **Mangalore**, **Bhatkal**, and **Wajidabad**.
*   **Mysorean Iron-Cased Rocket Artillery and Industrial Karkhanas**: Under **Hyder Ali** and **Tipu Sultan**, state-owned industrial **Karkhanas** at **Seringapatam**, **Bangalore**, **Chitaldurg**, and **Nagar** manufactured high-grade Wootz steel sword blades, flintlock muskets, bronze cannon, paper, and clockwork mechanisms. Their crowning military-technological innovation was the **Mysorean iron-cased rocket**. Whereas European armies still used fragile pasteboard or wooden rocket cylinders, Mysorean metallurgists hammered soft **iron tubes** (roughly 8 inches long and 1.5 to 3 inches in diameter) bound with leather thongs to bamboo stabilizing shafts up to 10 feet long, or fitted directly with curved steel sword-blades. The iron casing withstood vastly higher internal black-powder combustion pressures, enabling a range of **1.5 to 2.4 kilometers**—far outranging British smoothbore field artillery. Deployed by specialized **Kushoon** rocket brigades (expanded by Tipu from 1,200 to **5,000 rocket-men**) firing massed salvoes from wheeled carts, these rockets detonated British ammunition tumbrils at the **Battle of Pollilur (1780)** and during the sieges of **Seringapatam (1792 and 1799)**, directly inspiring **Sir William Congreve** at the Royal Arsenal in Woolwich to reverse-engineer captured Mysorean rockets into the British **Congreve Rocket** (**1804**).

#### Travancore under Marthanda Varma: Pepper Monopolies, the Battle of Colachel (1741), and Devaswom Centralization

At the southern tip of the Malabar Coast, **Marthanda Varma** (**1729–1758**) transformed the diminutive principality of **Venad** into the centralized military-fiscal kingdom of **Travancore**, sustained under his successor **Rama Varma** (**Kartika Thirunal / Dharma Raja**, **1758–1798**):

*   **Crushing Feudal Oligarchy and the Pepper Monopoly**: Upon ascending the throne in **1729**, **Marthanda Varma** eradicated the feudal power of the **Ettuveetil Pillamar** (the arrogant Nair nobles of the "Eight Houses") and the **Yogakkar** (the Brahmin temple trustees of the Padmanabhaswamy Temple) who had hitherto paralyzed royal authority. Assisted by his brilliant Diwan **Ramayyan Dalawa**, he confiscated the estates of the rebellious Nair nobility, annexed neighboring pepper-producing principalities (**Attingal**, **Kayamkulam**, **Kottarakkara**, **Quilon**, **Ambalapuzha**), conducted a systematic cadastral land survey (**1739**), and declared a **Royal State Monopoly** (**1743**) over the trade in **black pepper**, **areca nut**, **tobacco**, and **salt**, compelling European companies to purchase pepper directly from state warehouses (*Pandakasalas*) in exchange for firearms and cash.
*   **The Battle of Colachel (August 10, 1741) and Eustachius De Lannoy**: Alarmed that **Marthanda Varma’s** annexations and state pepper monopoly were destroying the **Dutch East India Company’s (VOC)** exclusive commercial contracts with the petty Malabar rajas, the Dutch Governor of Ceylon, **Gustaaf Willem van Imhoff**, dispatched a naval and amphibious force that seized the port of **Colachel**. **Marthanda Varma’s** forces blockaded the Dutch fortifications, and on **August 10, 1741**, a red-hot cannonball fired by Travancore’s gunners exploded the Dutch gunpowder magazine, forcing the **Dutch VOC** command to surrender unconditionally. The **Battle of Colachel (1741)** stands as a landmark in Asian military history: the first decisive military defeat of a European colonial power by an organized Indian state, permanently shattering Dutch territorial ambitions in Kerala and forcing the VOC to sign the **Treaty of Mavelikkara (1753)** (pledging non-interference and supplying Travancore with annual quotas of European weaponry). Crucially, **Marthanda Varma** pardoned the captured Flemish-Dutch commander **Captain Eustachius De Lannoy**, appointing him *Valiya Kappithan* (Senior Admiral/General) of the Travancore army. **De Lannoy** drilled a 50,000-strong standing army along European infantry lines, built the stone bastion fortress of **Udayagiri Fort** (complete with a cannon foundry), and engineered the **Nedumkotta** (Travancore Lines)—a 30-mile-long fortified earthen and stone defensive wall stretching from the Arabian Sea to the Anamalai Hills that later halted **Tipu Sultan’s** initial assault in **1789**.
*   **Thrippadidanam (January 3, 1750)**: To insulate the centralized crown from future feudal rebellions or sacrilegious treason, **Marthanda Varma** executed the **Thrippadidanam** in **January 1750**, formally surrendering the entire kingdom of Travancore to the tutelary deity **Lord Padmanabha** (Vishnu) at Trivandrum and assuming the ideological title of **Padmanabhadasa** ("Servant of Lord Padmanabha"), thereby converting any political rebellion against the royal tax-state into a religious sin against the deity.

## III. European Mercantilist Penetration, Enclave Urbanism, and the Architecture of Fortified Extraction (1498–1750s)

### 7. The Iberian, Dutch, and Scandinavian Mercantilist Regimes and Their Spatial-Architectural Imprints

The arrival of European maritime powers in the Indian Ocean was driven by the core structural imperative of **European Mercantilism**: the accumulation of precious bullion within the metropolitan nation-state through state-chartered commercial monopolies that bypassed Levantine, Arab, and Venetian middlemen. However, because early modern Europe manufactured virtually no commodities that could compete in price or quality with Indian cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre, and Malabar pepper, European trade with pre-1757 India suffered from a structural balance-of-payments deficit. To acquire Asian goods without perpetually draining European silver reserves, the **Portuguese**, **Dutch**, **Danish**, **French**, and **English** deployed three successive strategies: **coercive maritime protection rackets** (Portuguese *Cartaz*), **intra-Asian country trade** (Dutch spice-for-textile triangular circuits), and ultimately **territorial revenue capture** (the Anglo-French wars and the 1765 Bengal *Diwani*). Crucially, each mercantilist model engineered a distinct coastal enclave urbanism and architectural morphology to project ideological dominance and physically secure extracted commodities.

#### Portuguese Estado da Índia: The Cartaz-Armada-Cafila Coercive Grid, the 1556 Printing Press, and Iberian Baroque/Manueline Architecture in Goa and Diu

Following **Vasco da Gama’s** arrival at Calicut in **May 1498** (piloted across the Arabian Sea from Malindi), the Portuguese Crown established the **Estado da Índia** (**1505**) as a royal vice-regal military-mercantilist enterprise:

*   **Francisco de Almeida, Afonso de Albuquerque, and the Blue-Water Chokepoint Strategy**: The first Viceroy, **Francisco de Almeida** (**1505–1509**), articulated the **Blue Water Policy** (*Cartaze* naval supremacy), destroying the combined Egyptian Mamluk, Gujarati, and Calicut Zamorin fleet at the **Battle of Diu (1509)**. His successor, **Afonso de Albuquerque** (**1509–1515**), grounded this naval dominance in a network of fortified coastal chokepoints spanning the Indian Ocean: he conquered **Goa** from the Bijapur Sultanate in **1510** (making it the capital from **1530** under **Nuno da Cunha**), seized **Malacca** (**1511**) and **Hormuz** (**1515**), abolished *Sati* in Goa, and encouraged Portuguese marriages with local women. By the mid-16th century, Portuguese enclaves extended across the western coast (**Goa**, **Diu** acquired in **1535**, **Daman** in **1559**, **Salsette**, **Bassein**, **Chaul**, **Bombay** ceded to England in **1661** as Catherine of Braganza's dowry, **Cochin**, **Cannanore**, and **Quilon**) and the eastern coast (**San Thome** near Madras and **Hooghly** in Bengal, from which they were expelled by **Shah Jahan’s** forces under Qasim Khan in **1632** for piracy and slave-raiding).
*   **The Cartaz-Armada-Cafila Extraction Mechanism**: Lacking the capital to out-trade Asian merchants in open markets, the **Estado da Índia** declared King Manuel I "Lord of the Conquest, Navigation, and Commerce of Ethiopia, Arabia, Persia, and India," criminalizing all unarmed indigenous navigation in the Indian Ocean. Under the **Cartaz-Armada-Cafila** system, every Indian or Arab merchant vessel (including ships owned by the Mughal royal family traveling to Mecca) was forced to purchase a naval pass (**Cartaz**) from a Portuguese fortress captain. The **Cartaz** prohibited the vessel from carrying spices (black pepper, cinnamon), firearms, iron, or Christian slaves, and compelled it to call at a Portuguese port (**Goa**, **Diu**, or **Daman**) to pay customs duties of **3.5% to 5%** before proceeding to its destination. Any ship caught at sea without a **Cartaz** was confiscated as lawful prize, its cargo seized, and its crew enslaved or executed. Under the complementary **Cafila** system (instituted in the **1570s**), small coastal merchant vessels trading along the Konkan, Kanara, and Gujarat coasts were forced to sail in convoys (**Cafilas**) escorted by Portuguese naval **Armadas** both to protect them from Malabar *Kunjali Marakkar* corsairs and to ensure they unloaded their cargoes exclusively at **Goa** and **Diu** customs houses.
*   **Technological and Agrarian Introductions (The 1556 Printing Press and New World Crops)**: Operating under the **Padroado Real** (papal patronage uniting Crown mercantilism with Jesuit Counter-Reformation proselytization following **St. Francis Xavier’s** arrival in **1542** and the **Goa Inquisition** established in **1560**), the Jesuits brought the **first movable-type printing press to India at St. Paul’s College in Old Goa in 1556** (producing *Conclusiones Philosophicas* in **1556**, St. Francis Xavier’s *Doutrina Christa* in **1557**, and **Garcia da Orta’s** monumental pharmacological treatise on Indian medicinal plants, *Colóquios dos simples e drogas da Índia*, in **1563**, followed by the first vernacular Tamil types at Punnaikayal/Ambalakad in **1577–1578**). Simultaneously, Portuguese trans-oceanic networks permanently altered Indian agriculture by introducing New World crops from Brazil and Mesoamerica: **tobacco**, **potato**, **tomato**, **chilli pepper**, **maize**, **groundnut (peanut)**, **cashew nut**, **papaya**, **pineapple**, **guava**, and ** custard apple**, alongside advanced mango grafting (*Alfonso*/*Afonso* mango).
*   **Embedded Portuguese Architecture in Goa and Diu (Manueline, Iberian Baroque, and Luso-Indian Patio-Houses)**: To project Catholic Counter-Reformation majesty and coastal military impregnability in **Old Goa** (*Velha Goa*, "Rome of the Orient") and the island fortress of **Diu**, the Portuguese engineered three distinct architectural typologies:
    1.  **Military Bastion Architecture (Diu Fort, 1535–1546, and Fort Aguada, 1612)**: Constructed in partnership with Bahadur Shah of Gujarat and reinforced by **Dom João de Castro** after the **1546 Siege of Diu**, **Diu Fort** exemplifies early Renaissance *Trace Italienne* coastal fortification—featuring double moat channels cut into solid sandstone bedrock, low-profile thick angled bastions capable of withstanding heavy Ottoman artillery, and massive vaulted underground fresh-water cisterns.
    2.  **Manueline and Iberian Baroque Ecclesiastical Monuments**: While the **Church of St. Francis of Assisi** preserves a rare **Manueline** (Portuguese late-Gothic) portal sculpted with maritime rope-mouldings, armillary spheres, and the Cross of the Order of Christ, the monumental churches of Old Goa deploy **Iberian Baroque** and **Mannerist** idioms to induce awe among indigenous populations. The **Se Cathedral (Cathedral of St. Catherine, 1562–1619)**—the largest church in Asia—was built in a severe **Portuguese-Tuscan Mannerist** exterior housing a barrel-vaulted interior and a gilded Baroque altar (*Talha Dourada*) celebrating Albuquerque’s conquest of Goa on St. Catherine’s feast day. Immediately adjacent stands the **Basilica of Bom Jesus (1594–1605)**, a masterpiece of Jesuit **Iberian Baroque** architecture constructed predominantly of unplastered reddish-brown local **laterite stone** trimmed with basalt columns across a three-tiered classical facade (Ionic, Doric, and Corinthian orders). Inside, the Basilica houses the mortal remains of **St. Francis Xavier** within an ornate Florentine marble and bronze mausoleum designed by Giovanni Battista Foggini (**1698**). Similarly, the **Church of St. Paul in Diu (1601–1610)** represents one of the finest surviving examples of pure **Iberian Baroque** in India, featuring an elaborate whitewashed facade of curvilinear volutes, shell-motifs, and spiral columns carved by Indian artisans.
    3.  **Luso-Indian Domestic Architecture (Goan Patio-Houses / Sobrados)**: In the residential quarters of Margao, Panjim, and Chandor, Christianized Saraswat Brahmin and Chardó elites synthesized European neoclassical/Baroque manor layouts with tropical Konkan micro-climatic engineering to create the **Goan Patio-House**. Raised on high laterite plinths (*Porão*) to prevent monsoon dampness, these mansions were organized around an internal open-to-sky central courtyard (**Patio** / *Rajangan* derived from the Hindu *Angan*) that generated convective thermal ventilation. The street-facing elevation featured an external reception porch (**Balcão**) with built-in masonry seats, deep overhanging mangalore-tiled eaves supported by wooden corbels, and full-length casement windows glazed not with imported European glass, but with translucent **Capiz / Carepa oyster shells** slotted into teakwood lattices to diffuse harsh tropical glare while maintaining privacy.

#### The Dutch VOC and Danish East India Company: Joint-Stock Spice/Textile Circuits and Coastal Factory Morphology

By the early 17th century, the feudal-royal Portuguese monopoly was eclipsed by Northern European joint-stock corporations backed by modern Amsterdam and London capital markets:

*   **The Dutch Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie (VOC, 1602)**: Chartered by the States-General of the Netherlands in **March 1602** as the world's first permanently capitalized, publicly traded multinational corporation empowered to wage war, coin money, and build forts, the **Dutch VOC** targeted the **Indonesian Spice Islands** (Batavia/Jakarta) as its primary imperial anchor. However, the Dutch rapidly discovered that Indonesian spice cultivators demanded **Indian cotton textiles** (from the Coromandel Coast and Gujarat) rather than European silver coins. Consequently, the **VOC** integrated India into a lucrative intra-Asian triangular trade network: establishing fortified coastal factories at **Masulipatnam (1605)**, **Pulicat (1610, Fort Geldria—their early Coromandel headquarters with its own gold *Pagoda* mint)**, **Surat (1616)**, **Bimlipatnam (1641)**, **Karikal (1645)**, **Chinsurah in Bengal (1653, Fort Gustavus)**, **Cochin (conquered from the Portuguese in 1663)**, and **Nagapattinam (1658, replacing Pulicat as the Coromandel capital in 1690)**. From India, the Dutch exported Coromandel piece-goods, Bengal raw silk, Bihar **saltpetre** and **opium**, and Malabar pepper. Following their defeat by **Marthanda Varma** at the **Battle of Colachel (1741)** in Kerala and their decisive routing by **Robert Clive** and **Colonel Forde** at the **Battle of Bedara / Chinsurah (November 1759)** in Bengal, the Dutch were reduced to minor commercial enclaves before liquidating their Indian assets to the British in **1824–1825**.
*   **The Danish East India Company (Ostindisk Kompagni, 1616)**: Founded by King Christian IV of Denmark in **1616**, the Danes established two primary Indian enclaves: **Tranquebar (Tharangambadi)** on the Tamil Coromandel coast in **1620** (leased from Raghunatha Nayak of Tanjore, where Admiral **Ove Gjedde** constructed the trapezoidal coastal bastion **Fort Dansborg**), and **Serampore (Frederiksnagore)** on the Hooghly River in Bengal in **1755**. Lacking the military-fiscal scale to compete in the Carnatic or Bengal wars, the Danish enclaves operated as neutral commercial entrepôts during Anglo-French conflicts and as pioneering centers of Protestant missionary print culture—first under the German Lutheran Pietists **Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg** and **Heinrich Plütschau** at **Tranquebar** (**1706**, printing the Tamil New Testament in **1714–1715**), and later under the Baptist **Serampore Trio** (**William Carey**, **Joshua Marshman**, and **William Ward**, **1800**), before Denmark sold Serampore and Tranquebar to the British in **1845**.
*   **Dutch and Danish Coastal Factory Morphology**: Architecturally, **Dutch** (**Fort Geldria** at Pulicat, **Fort Gustavus** at Chinsurah, Dutch Cochin) and **Danish** (**Fort Dansborg** at Tranquebar) coastal factories functioned as self-contained, bastioned warehousing engines. Ringed by brick-and-laterite curtain walls with four corner diamond bastions, the interior compound rigidly segregated the damp-proof ground-floor vaulted **godowns** (warehouses for storing cloth bales and saltpetre), cloth-sorting halls, and gunpowder magazines from the upper-story living quarters of the Factor/Governor, while the adjacent orthogonal street grid (such as **King’s Street** in Tranquebar or **Fort Cochin**) blended Dutch stepped-gable rooflines and Danish neoclassical townhouse facades with deep tropical verandahs.

### 8. The Anglo-French Mercantilist Rivalry, Sovereign Charters, and Enclave Town Planning

#### The French Compagnie des Indes Orientales, Dupleix’s Political-Military Doctrine, and Pondicherry’s Cartesian Gridiron Urbanism

The last major European power to enter the Indian Ocean trade was **France**, whose mercantilist architecture suffered from a fatal structural flaw: unlike the privately owned, commercially autonomous English and Dutch companies, the French company was an arm of the absolutist Bourbon state:

*   **Colbert’s 1664 Charter and State-Dependent Mercantilism**: Founded in **1664** by **Jean-Baptiste Colbert**, Finance Minister to **Louis XIV**, the **Compagnie des Indes Orientales** was heavily financed by the French Crown, managed by royal bureaucrats, and subordinated to Versailles' European dynastic and geopolitical priorities. The French established their first Indian factory at **Surat (1668)** under **François Caron**, followed by **Masulipatnam (1669)**, and in **1673–1674**, **François Martin** acquired the coastal village of **Pondicherry** from Sher Khan Lodi (the Bijapuri governor of Valikondapuram), transforming it along with **Fort Louis** into the headquarters of French India. Subsequent French enclaves were acquired at **Chandernagore** in Bengal (**1690–1692**, granted by Mughal Subadar Shaista Khan), **Mahe** on the Malabar Coast (**1725**), **Yanam** in Andhra (**1731**), and **Karaikal** on the Coromandel Coast (**1739**), backed by the strategic naval base of **Mauritius (Île de France)** and **Bourbon (Réunion)** in the Indian Ocean under **Mahé de La Bourdonnais**.
*   **Joseph François Dupleix (Governor-General, 1742–1754) and the Proto-Imperial Subsidiary Model**: Appointed Governor-General at Pondicherry in **1742**, **Dupleix** recognized that French trade was chronically under-capitalized compared to the **English East India Company**. To overcome this commercial deficit, **Dupleix** pioneered the foundational blueprint of European territorial imperialism in India:
    1.  Deploying small, disciplined regiments of **European-drilled Indian infantry (Sepoys)** equipped with rapid-firing flintlock muskets and bayonets alongside mobile field artillery to decisive effect in indigenous succession disputes;
    2.  Trading this decisive military intervention to rival Indian claimants (**Chanda Sahib** in the Carnatic and **Muzaffar Jung** / **Salabat Jung** in Hyderabad) in exchange for sovereign territorial revenue grants (**Jagirs** such as the **Northern Circars**);
    3.  Using indigenous Indian land revenue—rather than scarce European bullion imports—to finance both French military garrisons and export textile cargoes, while installing a French political resident backed by a French-officered force (under **Marquis de Bussy** at Hyderabad) at the Indian ruler's court.
*   **Embedded French Cartesian Gridiron Town Planning in Pondicherry**: Following the Dutch occupation of Pondicherry (**1693–1697**, after which it was restored to France by the **Treaty of Ryswick, 1697**) and its reconstruction after the **1761** British demolition, French military engineers (notably **Jean Law de Lauriston** and **Bourcet**) executed a masterwork of Enlightenment **Cartesian Gridiron Town Planning** bounded by a semi-circular fortified boulevard. Pondicherry was spatially organized around an orthogonal grid of straight streets intersecting at precise **90-degree angles**, bisected North-to-South by a monumental drainage and spatial boundary canal (**Canal des Français** / Grand Canal) into two racially and culturally distinct urban zones:
    1.  **Ville Blanche (The White Town)**: Occupying the breezy eastern seaside strip facing the Bay of Bengal and centered on the **Place du Gouvernement** and **Fort Louis**, the **Ville Blanche** housed French administrators, merchants, and religious orders. Its domestic architecture consisted of high-ceilinged, flat-terraced (*Madras terrace*) neoclassical mansions painted in pale yellow and white lime plaster, hidden from the street behind high continuous boundary walls pierced by monumental arched carriage gateways. Inside, colonnaded courtyards and arcaded loggias regulated coastal humidity.
    2.  **Ville Noire (The Black Town)**: Positioned west of the Grand Canal, the **Ville Noire** housed the Tamil weaver, merchant (*Dubash*), and artisan populations within an equally strict rectilinear street grid, where indigenous Tamil vernacular houses—featuring street-side pillared verandahs (**Thinnai**) for public commerce and internal open courtyards (**Mutram**) for family life—were hybridized with French arched windows and classical pilasters (**Franco-Tamil architecture**).

#### The English East India Company: The 1600 Charter, Thomas Roe’s Embassy, the 1717 Farrukhsiyar Farman, Dastak Abuse, and British Factory-Forts (Fort St. George and Fort William)

In contrast to its Portuguese and French rivals, the **English East India Company (EIC)** combined corporate autonomy and deep access to London’s private financial markets with aggressive fiscal-legal opportunism inside the Mughal Empire:

*   **The 1600 Royal Charter and Early Mughal Concessions**: On **December 31, 1600**, **Queen Elizabeth I** granted a fifteen-year monopoly charter over all trade east of the Cape of Good Hope to *"The Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies,"* governed by a **Court of Directors** (24 directors elected annually by the **Court of Proprietors**). In **1608**, **Captain William Hawkins** arrived at **Surat** in the ship *Hector* and travelled to the Agra court of Emperor **Jahangir** (**1609**), though Portuguese intrigue blocked a permanent concession until **Captain Thomas Best** defeated a Portuguese naval squadron off **Swally (Suvali)** near Surat in **1612**, prompting **Jahangir** to issue a *Farman* permitting the first permanent English factory at **Surat (1613)** (supplemented on the east coast by a trading post at **Masulipatnam in 1611**). Between **1615 and 1619**, **Sir Thomas Roe**, dispatched as official ambassador by **King James I** to **Jahangir’s** court, abandoned the failed quest for a formal bilateral commercial treaty and instead secured imperial *Farmans* and princely *Nishans* (from Prince Khurram/Shah Jahan) granting the English permission to establish factories across the Mughal Empire (Agra, Ahmedabad, Broach).
*   **Acquisition of the Three Presidency Enclaves and Factory-Fort Architecture**: Over the 17th century, the EIC shifted its strategic center of gravity from interior Mughal cities (where it was vulnerable to imperial *Subadars*) to three autonomous, fortified coastal **Presidency Towns**:
    1.  **Madras and Fort St. George (1639–1640)**: In **1639**, **Francis Day** leased the coastal strip of **Madraspatnam** from the local Damarla Venkatadri Nayak (representative of the Vijayanagara Raja of Chandragiri) and in **1640** constructed **Fort St. George**—the first British bastioned factory-fort in India, elevated to a Presidency in **1652/1684**.
    2.  **Bombay (1661–1668)**: Acquired by **King Charles II** from Portugal in **1661** as part of **Catherine of Braganza’s** marriage dowry, the seven islands of **Bombay** were leased to the EIC in **1668** for a nominal annual rent of **£10 in gold**, replacing vulnerable **Surat** (sacked twice by **Shivaji** in **1664** and **1670**) as the western headquarters in **1687** under **Gerald Aungier**.
    3.  **Calcutta and Fort William (1690–1700)**: After the EIC joined the **Dutch** and **French** in the Bengal delta (establishing factories at **Balasore 1633**, **Hooghly 1651**, **Kasimbazar**, and **Patna**), **Sir Josiah Child’s** premature military war against **Aurangzeb** (**1686–1690**) ended in humiliating English defeat. Pardoned in **1690**, the EIC agent **Job Charnock** established a new settlement at the marshy village of **Sutanuti** on the Hooghly River. Exploiting the **Sobha Singh** *Zamindari* rebellion of **1696**, the English received permission from Subadar Azim-ush-Shan to fortify their factory, and in **1698** purchased the *Zamindari* rights over the three contiguous villages of **Sutanuti**, **Kalikata**, and **Govindapur** for **Rs 1,200**, completing the original **Fort William** (named after King William III) in **1700**.
*   **Spatial Morphology of British Factory-Forts (Fort St. George and Fort William)**: Both **Fort St. George (Madras)** and **Fort William (Calcutta)** operationalized a bifurcated spatial and military-architectural grid that prefigured high-colonial racial segregation. At the core stood the **Trace Italienne** bastioned star-fortress (**White Town** / *Christian Town*), enclosed by thick brick-and-stone curtain walls with angled corner bastions providing interlocking fields of enfilading cannon fire across a cleared glacis (esplanade). Inside the **White Town** of **Fort St. George** stood the Governor’s House, the Exchange, warehouses, barracks, and **St. Mary’s Church (consecrated 1680—the oldest surviving Anglican church in India)**, laid out along neat streets inhabited exclusively by British covenanted servants and licensed free merchants. Immediately outside the northern wall of the fort lay the **Black Town** (*George Town* in Madras; the northern *Sutanuti-Bazar* native quarter in Calcutta)—a dense, high-density settlement of Indian textile weavers, dyers, washer-men, **Banians** (in Bengal) or **Dubashes** (in Madras: bilingual Indian commercial brokers and translators such as **Ananda Ranga Pillai** in Pondicherry or **Pachaiyappa Mudaliar** in Madras), and *Mahajan* financiers, encircled by an outer mud rampart or ditch (such as the **1742 Maratha Ditch** in Calcutta). Whenever military conflict erupted (as in **Calcutta in 1757–1758** when Clive began constructing the massive octagonal **New Fort William** after Plassey), the British demolished neighboring native settlements to create a vast, unobstructed **Maidan** (esplanade fire-zone) separating the European citadel from the Indian city.
*   **The 1717 Farrukhsiyar Farman ("Magna Carta of the Company") and Dastak Abuse**: In **1715**, the EIC dispatched an embassy led by **John Surman** (accompanied by the Armenian merchant **Khwaja Sarhad** and surgeon **William Hamilton**, who cured Emperor **Farrukhsiyar** of a painful groin abscess) to Delhi. In **1717**, **Farrukhsiyar** issued three imperial **Farmans** directed to the Subadars of **Bengal**, **Hyderabad (Deccan)**, and **Gujarat**:
    1.  In **Bengal**, in exchange for a minuscule fixed annual tribute (*Peshkash*) of **Rs 3,000**, the Company’s official import and export trade was exempted from all imperial customs duties;
    2.  The Company’s President at Calcutta was authorized to issue stamped paper passes—**Dastaks**—certifying that goods in transit belonged to the Company, thereby entitling them to pass through all provincial toll stations (*Chaukis*) duty-free;
    3.  The Company was permitted to rent **38 additional villages** around Calcutta and to use the royal mint at Murshidabad.
    4.  In **Surat**, all customs duties were commuted for an annual payment of **Rs 10,000**, and coins minted by the Company at **Bombay** were declared legal tender throughout the Mughal Empire.
*   **The Mechanics of Dastak Abuse**: While **Murshid Quli Khan** and **Alivardi Khan** successfully blocked the Company from acquiring the 38 villages and denied it free access to the **Jagat Seth**-controlled Murshidabad mint, the **Dastak** became an explosive fiscal weapon. The **1717 Farman** granted duty-free trade strictly to the **East India Company’s corporate external import-export cargoes**; it never exempted either the **internal country trade** in domestic staples (salt, betel nut, tobacco, grain) or the **private trade** conducted by individual Company servants for their personal enrichment. Yet poorly paid Company officials systematically issued Company **Dastaks** to cover their own private internal trade and sold **Dastaks** for a high cash bribe to Indian merchants seeking to evade the Nawab’s customs collectors. This illicit practice inflicted a dual catastrophe on Nawabi Bengal: it defrauded the Nawab’s treasury of legitimate customs revenues while ruining honest indigenous Bengali merchants who still had to pay **10% to 40%** transit duties, setting the direct collision course for the **Battle of Plassey (1757)** and the **Battle of Buxar (1764)**.

## IV. The Anglo-French Carnatic Crucible, the Bengal Revolution, and the Diwani Watershed (1746–1765)

### 9. The Three Carnatic Wars (1746–1763): The Infantry Revolution and Subsidiary Proto-Imperialism

The **Carnatic**—the Coromandel coastal plain and hinterland stretching between the Eastern Ghats and the Bay of Bengal under the nominal suzerainty of the **Nizam of Hyderabad** and governed from **Arcot** by the Nawab of the Carnatic (**Anwar-ud-din Khan**)—served as the geopolitical laboratory where the **English** and **French** trading companies fought a twenty-year struggle (**1746–1763**) for subcontinental mastery. What began as an overseas spillover of European dynastic wars rapidly mutated into a decisive contest of **military-fiscal mobilization**, testing whether state-controlled French territorial diplomacy (**Dupleix** and **Lally**) could withstand the naval supremacy and Bengal-funded credit power of the **English East India Company** (**Robert Clive** and **Eyre Coote**).

#### The First Carnatic War (1746–1748) and the Tactical Watershed of the Battle of St. Thome (1746)

Triggered in Europe by the **War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748)** (which pitted Britain against France in **1744**), hostilities in India commenced when a British naval squadron under **Commodore Curtis Barnett** seized French merchant ships off the Coromandel coast in **1745**:

*   **Capture of Madras and the Anglo-French Conflict**: Lacking a local naval fleet, French Governor-General **Dupleix** summoned **Mahé de La Bourdonnais**, the French Governor of **Mauritius**, who arrived with an armed squadron in **July 1746** and forced the British at **Fort St. George (Madras)** to surrender in **September 1746** (taking a young Company clerk, **Robert Clive**, prisoner before Clive escaped in disguise to **Fort St. David** at Cuddalore). While **La Bourdonnais** wanted to ransom Madras back to the British for cash, **Dupleix** insisted on retaining the captured Presidency to obliterate English competition on the Coromandel coast.
*   **The Battle of St. Thome / Adyar (November 4, 1746) and the European Infantry Revolution**: Before the siege, **Dupleix** had pacified **Anwar-ud-din Khan**, the Nawab of the Carnatic, by promising to hand Madras over to him once captured. When **Dupleix** reneged, **Anwar-ud-din** dispatched a **10,000-strong Mughal-style army** commanded by his eldest son **Mahfuz Khan** to enforce his sovereignty and seize Madras. On **November 4, 1746**, at **St. Thome** on the banks of the **Adyar River**, a tiny French detachment led by **Captain Louis Paradis**—comprising merely **230 French soldiers and 700 French-trained Indian Sepoys** without even field artillery—waded across the river and shattered **Mahfuz Khan’s** 10,000 troops in minutes. **The Battle of St. Thome (1746)** exposed a seismic military-technological chasm: traditional Indian armies relied on undisciplined feudal cavalry charges and slow-firing matchlocks (firing 1 round every 2–3 minutes), whereas **Paradis’s** infantry used flintlock muskets equipped with socket bayonets and pre-packaged paper cartridges to deliver disciplined, synchronized **platoon volley fire** (3–4 rounds per minute) accompanied by rapid reload-and-advance bayonet charges that panicked Indian war-elephants and cavalry. When the **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)** ended the War of the Austrian Succession in Europe, **Madras** was handed back to the English in exchange for the French fortress of **Louisbourg** in North America (Cape Breton Island), leaving **Dupleix** convinced that his new **Sepoy-and-artillery** army could remake the political map of South India.

#### The Second Carnatic War (1749–1754), Dupleix’s Succession Interventions, and Clive’s Siege of Arcot (1751)

Although Britain and France were officially at peace in Europe, **Dupleix** engineered the **Second Carnatic War (1749–1754)** by inserting French military power into two simultaneous succession disputes in **Hyderabad** (following the death of **Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah** in **May 1748**) and the **Carnatic**:

*   **Dupleix’s Tripartite Coalition and the Battle of Ambur (August 1749)**: In Hyderabad, the throne was contested between the Nizam’s second son **Nasir Jung** and his grandson **Muzaffar Jung**; in the Carnatic, the sitting Nawab **Anwar-ud-din Khan** was challenged by **Chanda Sahib** (son-in-law of the former Nawab Dost Ali, newly ransomed from Maratha captivity). **Dupleix** forged a secret pact with **Muzaffar Jung** and **Chanda Sahib**, while the alarmed British under **Stringer Lawrence** and **Saunders** backed **Nasir Jung** and **Anwar-ud-din’s** son **Muhammad Ali Wallajah**. At the **Battle of Ambur (August 3, 1749)**, the combined forces of **Dupleix**, **Chanda Sahib**, and **Muzaffar Jung** defeated and killed the 77-year-old **Anwar-ud-din Khan**. **Muhammad Ali** fled south to the rock-fortress of **Trichinopoly**, while **Nasir Jung** was assassinated by his own rebellious Pathan *Nawabs* of Kadapa and Kurnool in **December 1750**.
*   **The Zenith of French Power and Bussy in Hyderabad (1750–1751)**: Installing **Muzaffar Jung** (and following his accidental death in early **1751**, his uncle **Salabat Jung**) as Nizam of Hyderabad, **Dupleix** stationed his brilliant lieutenant **Marquis Charles de Bussy** with a permanent French corps at **Aurangabad/Hyderabad**. **Dupleix** was appointed nominal Mughal Governor of all territories south of the Krishna River, received **Masulipatnam**, **Divi**, and lucrative *Jagirs* around Pondicherry, and in **1753**, **Bussy** secured the grant of the four rich coastal **Northern Circars** (**Mustafanagar**, **Ellore**, **Rajahmundry**, and **Chicacole**, yielding **Rs 30 lakh** annually) to pay the French subsidiary force.
*   **Robert Clive’s Siege of Arcot (August–November 1751) and Dupleix’s Recall**: By mid-**1751**, the last British ally, **Muhammad Ali**, was besieged inside **Trichinopoly** by **Chanda Sahib** and the French. To break the stranglehold, the 26-year-old **Robert Clive** proposed a daring strategic diversion: striking directly at **Chanda Sahib’s** unguarded capital of **Arcot**. With only **210 Europeans and 300 Sepoys**, **Clive** marched through a monsoon thunderstorm to seize **Arcot** in **August 1751**, forcing **Chanda Sahib** to detach 4,000 troops from Trichinopoly under his son **Raza Sahib**. For **53 days** (**September 23 – November 14, 1751**), **Clive’s** starving garrison held the crumbling ramparts of **Arcot** against repeated assaults until relief forces led by **Stringer Lawrence** and Maratha cavalry under **Murari Rao** routed the besiegers. In **June 1752**, the French force besieging Trichinopoly surrendered to **Lawrence** and **Clive**, and **Chanda Sahib** was captured and beheaded by the Raja of Tanjore. Furious at the mounting financial losses and unauthorized territorial wars that disrupted commercial dividends, the French Directors and **Louis XV** recalled **Dupleix** in **1754**, replacing him with **Charles Robert Godeheu**, who signed the **Treaty of Pondicherry (1754–1755)** acknowledging the British candidate **Muhammad Ali Wallajah** as Nawab of the Carnatic and surrendering Dupleix’s hard-won *Jagirs*.

#### The Third Carnatic War (1756–1763), the Battle of Wandiwash (1760), and the Treaty of Paris (1763)

The outbreak of the global **Seven Years’ War (1756–1763)** in Europe immediately reignited open warfare in India, transforming the **Third Carnatic War** into an existential struggle that ended French political ambitions:

*   **Count de Lally’s Strategic Blunders and theBengal Financial Advantage**: In **April 1758**, the French government dispatched an expeditionary force under the courageous but autocratic and tactically rigid Irish-born general **Comte de Lally (Thomas Arthur, Count de Lally)**. Although **Lally** rapidly captured **Fort St. David** (**June 1758**), his campaign collapsed under three structural failures: first, his arrogant treatment of Indian caste norms (forcing Brahmins and upper-caste Tamil locals at gunshot to drag heavy cannon barrels during his failed siege of Tanjore) destroyed French logistics; second, Admiral **d’Aché’s** French naval squadron was repeatedly outfought by British Admiral **George Pocock**, abandoning the Indian Ocean to the Royal Navy; and third, in a fatal strategic blunder, **Lally** recalled **Marquis de Bussy** from **Hyderabad** in **1758** to assist in his failed siege of **Madras (1758–1759)**. The moment **Bussy** departed Hyderabad, **Robert Clive**—now flush with the immense treasury plunder of **Plassey (1757)** in Bengal—dispatched **Colonel Francis Forde** from Calcutta into the **Northern Circars**. **Forde** routed the remaining French garrison at the **Battle of Condore (December 1758)**, captured **Masulipatnam (April 1759)**, and compelled Nizam **Salabat Jung** to switch allegiance from France to the **English East India Company**.
*   **The Battle of Wandiwash (January 22, 1760) and the Treaty of Paris (1763)**: On **January 22, 1760**, at the decisive **Battle of Wandiwash** (*Vandavasi* in Tamil Nadu), the British army commanded by **General Sir Eyre Coote** decisively crushed the French forces under **Comte de Lally** and captured **Bussy**. Starved of naval supplies and bankrupt, **Pondicherry** was besieged by **Coote** for eight months until **Lally** surrendered unconditionally on **January 16, 1761** (followed by the British capture of **Jinji** and **Mahe**). The British systematically leveled the fortifications and palaces of **Pondicherry** to the ground. Although the **Treaty of Paris (February 10, 1763)** restored **Pondicherry**, **Chandernagore**, **Mahe**, **Yanam**, and **Karaikal** to France as isolated commercial *loges*, Article XI explicitly prohibited the French from ever fortifying their settlements, maintaining troops in Bengal, or engaging in territorial politics—leaving the **English East India Company** without a European rival in India.

### 10. The Conquest of Bengal: From the Plassey Coup (1757) to the Buxar-Allahabad Settlement (1764–1765)

#### Siraj-ud-Daulah’s Sovereignty Crisis, the Fort William Fortification Dispute, and the Battle of Plassey (1757)

While the Carnatic Wars forged the **English East India Company’s** military instrument, the conquest of **Bengal** (**1757–1765**) provided the immense fiscal engine that paid for the subjugation of the entire subcontinent. When **Alivardi Khan** died in **April 1756**, he was succeeded by his twenty-three-year-old grandson **Siraj-ud-Daulah** (**1756–1757**). From the outset, **Siraj-ud-Daulah’s** sovereignty was contested from within by a hostile court oligarchy: his aunt **Ghaseti Begum** (at the Motijheel palace), his cousin **Shaukat Jung** (Faujdar of Purnea), the veteran military commander (*Mir Bakhshi*) **Mir Jafar**, and the Hindu commercial-banking elite led by **Jagat Seth Mahtab Chand**, **Rai Durlabh**, **Raja Rajballabh** (former Diwan of Dacca), and the merchant **Omichand (Amirchand)**. Simultaneously, the British at **Calcutta** under Governor **Roger Drake** directly violated **Siraj-ud-Daulah’s** sovereign authority across three axes:

*   **The Three Structural Triggers of 1756**: First, anticipating the outbreak of the **Seven Years’ War**, both the **English** at Calcutta and the **French** at Chandernagore began mounting heavy cannon and expanding their fortifications without the Nawab’s permission—When **Siraj-ud-Daulah** ordered both companies to demolish their new fortifications, the French complied via diplomacy, whereas the English sent an insulting refusal; second, Company servants continued their rampant abuse of the **1717 Dastaks** to evade internal customs duties; and third, the British at Calcutta granted political asylum to **Krishna Das** (son of **Raja Rajballabh**), who had fled Dacca with misappropriated state treasury funds, and sheltered him inside **Fort William**.
*   **Capture of Calcutta (June 1756), the "Black Hole" Episode, and the Treaty of Alinagar (February 1757)**: Asserting his sovereign jurisdiction, **Siraj-ud-Daulah** seized the English factory at **Kasimbazar** (**June 4, 1756**), marched on **Calcutta**, and captured **Fort William** on **June 20, 1756** after Governor **Roger Drake** and the military commanders deserted down the Hooghly in boats. Renaming Calcutta **Alinagar** and placing it under **Manikchand**, the Nawab returned to Murshidabad (leaving behind the controversial **"Black Hole of Calcutta"** incident on the night of **June 20, 1756**, later sensationally publicized by survivor **John Zephaniah Holwell** as an atrocity in which 123 of 146 British prisoners allegedly suffocated in a cramped **18-by-14-foot** guardroom—a figure modern historians like **Brijen Gupta** and **J.H. Little** exposed as grossly exaggerated to justify imperial retaliation, though a smaller number of prisoners died of heat exhaustion without the Nawab's personal knowledge). When news reached **Madras**, the Company dispatched **Robert Clive** and **Admiral Charles Watson** with a Madras army and Royal Navy squadron that recaptured **Calcutta** on **January 2, 1757**. Intimidated by **Ahmad Shah Abdali’s** simultaneous capture of Delhi, **Siraj-ud-Daulah** signed the **Treaty of Alinagar (February 9, 1757)**, restoring all privileges of the **1717 Farrukhsiyar Farman**, permitting the Company to fortify **Calcutta** and mint its own coins (**Siccas**), and paying war compensation.
*   **The Plassey Conspiracy and the Battle of Plassey (June 23, 1757)**: Determined to eliminate French presence in Bengal and replace the independent-minded **Siraj-ud-Daulah** with a compliant puppet, **Clive** and **Watson** attacked and captured French **Chandernagore** in **March 1757**. Through the British Resident at Murshidabad, **William Watts**, **Clive** entered into a secret conspiracy with the disaffected Murshidabad oligarchy: **Mir Jafar** (Mir Bakhshi, promised the *Masnad* of Bengal), **Jagat Seth Mahtab Chand** (alienated by Siraj’s demands for forced war-loans and personal insults), **Rai Durlabh** (Diwan), **Yar Lutuf Khan**, and **Omichand** (whom Clive famously deceived by forging two versions of the treaty—a real "White Paper" omitting Omichand’s **Rs 30 lakh** commission and a fake "Red Paper" containing Admiral Watson’s forged signature). On **June 23, 1757**, in the mango grove of **Plassey (Palashi)** on the banks of the Bhagirathi River, **Clive’s** force of **950 European troops and 2,100 Indian Sepoys** faced **Siraj-ud-Daulah’s** 50,000-strong army. Throughout the battle, roughly 45,000 of the Nawab’s troops commanded by the conspirators **Mir Jafar**, **Yar Lutuf Khan**, and **Rai Durlabh** stood completely motionless. Only a small loyal vanguard led by **Mir Madan** and **Mohan Lal** (supported by a small detachment of French artillerymen) fought heroically until a stray cannonball killed **Mir Madan** after a midday monsoon downpour soaked the Nawab’s uncovered gunpowder. Deceived by **Mir Jafar’s** treacherous advice to halt hostilities and recall his advancing troops, **Siraj-ud-Daulah** saw his army rout, fled to Murshidabad, and was captured and murdered on **July 2, 1757** by **Mir Jafar’s** son **Miran**. **Plassey** was thus not a military battle, but a transactional corporate-banking coup d'état that handed the keys of the Murshidabad treasury to **Robert Clive**.

#### Mir Jafar’s Fiscal Exhaustion, Mir Qasim’s Internal Customs Abolition, and the Battle of Buxar (1764)

The installation of **Mir Jafar** (**1757–1760**) inaugurated the **"Plassey Plunder"**, rapidly bankrupting the Murshidabad state and forcing a second, far more violent military showdown:

*   **The Fiscal Bleed of Mir Jafar (1757–1760) and the 1760 Coup**: Under the secret Plassey treaty, **Mir Jafar** was forced to pay **Rs 1.77 crore** (**£2.75 million**) in immediate war restitution and private "gifts" to Company officials (**Robert Clive** personally pocketed **Rs 23.4 lakh** plus an annual **Jagir** worth **£30,000 / Rs 2.34 lakh** per year representing the quit-rent of the **24 Parganas** south of Calcutta, which Mir Jafar granted to the Company as a *Zamindari* in **December 1757**). Between **1757 and 1765**, over **£6 million** was extorted directly from the Bengal rulers as personal presents and indemnities, while Company servants used **Dastaks** and physical coercion (*Gomasthas* flogging Bengali weavers to accept below-market advances) to monopolize Bengal's internal trade. Unable to meet the Company’s insatiable financial demands or pay his own mutinous troops, **Mir Jafar** secretly opened correspondence with the **Dutch VOC** at **Chinsurah**. After **Clive** and **Colonel Forde** crushed the Dutch fleet and army at the **Battle of Bedara (November 1759)**, the interim British Governor **Henry Vansittart** staged a bloodless palace coup in **October 1760**, deposing **Mir Jafar** in favor of his energetic son-in-law **Mir Qasim** (**1760–1763**).
*   **Mir Qasim’s Administrative Modernization and the 1763 Internal Customs Rupture**: To purchase the *Masnad*, **Mir Qasim** rewarded **Vansittart’s** Select Committee with **Rs 20 lakh** in bribes and ceded the three rich revenue-producing districts of **Burdwan**, **Midnapore**, and **Chittagong** to the **East India Company** to cover military maintenance costs. However, **Mir Qasim** proved to be the most capable and determined ruler since **Alivardi Khan**, resolved not to remain a British puppet. To escape the suffocating military surveillance of Calcutta, he shifted his capital from **Murshidabad** upstream to **Munger (Monghyr)** in Bihar (**1762**), overhauled the provincial bureaucracy, audited and extracted arrears from corrupt revenue officials (such as **Ram Narayan** of Patna), and established a modern gun-foundry and musket factory at **Munger**, training a disciplined infantry army along European lines under the Armenian commander **Gurgin Khan (Khwaja Gregory)** and **Sumru (Walter Reinhardt)**. The decisive rupture occurred over **Dastak** abuse in internal trade: when **Vansittart** negotiated a compromise in **1762** under which Company servants would pay a reduced **9%** transit duty (compared to **25%–40%** paid by Indian merchants), the Calcutta Council led by **William Ellis** angrily rejected the agreement, insisting on total tax exemption for their private trade while forcing Indian merchants to pay full duties. In a brilliant stroke of fiscal statesmanship in **March 1763**, **Mir Qasim** **abolished all internal transit duties across Bengal and Bihar for two years**, placing his own Indian subjects on a completely equal tax-free footing with the British. Enraged that they had lost their unfair preferential monopoly over Indian merchants, the British Council declared war in **July 1763**, reinstalled the senile **Mir Jafar** (**1763–1765**) on the throne, and defeated **Mir Qasim’s** forces in hard-fought engagements at **Katwa**, **Giria**, **Sooty**, and **Udaynala** (after which an embittered **Mir Qasim** executed the **Jagat Seth** brothers Mahtab Chand and Swarup Chand along with British prisoners at **Patna** before fleeing into **Awadh**).
*   **The Battle of Buxar (October 22, 1764)**: In **Awadh**, the exiled **Mir Qasim** forged an unprecedented tripartite imperial coalition with **Shuja-ud-Daula** ( the Nawab-Wazir of Awadh) and the fugitive Mughal Emperor **Shah Alam II** (**Ali Gauhar**, **1759–1806**). On **October 22, 1764**, on the plains of **Buxar** (in western Bihar on the banks of the Ganga), the combined imperial army of roughly **40,000 to 50,000 troops** confronted the **East India Company’s** army of **7,072 men** (857 Europeans, 5,297 trained Indian Sepoys, and 918 Indian cavalry) commanded by **Major Hector Munro**. Unlike the treacherouscharade of **Plassey (1757)**, **Buxar (1764)** was a fiercely contested, five-hour conventional military battle. **Hector Munro’s** disciplined hollow-square infantry formations and rapid-fire field artillery broke **Shuja-ud-Daula’s** heavy Durrani cavalry charges and turned the allied flank, capturing 133 cannon and routing the coalition. **Buxar** established the unchallenged military supremacy of the **English East India Company** across the entire Gangetic plain from Calcutta to Allahabad: in a single afternoon, the Company defeated not merely the Nawab of Bengal (**Mir Qasim**), but the strongest regional military power in North India (**Awadh**) and the titular sovereign of the entire Mughal Empire (**Shah Alam II**).

#### Clive’s Treaty of Allahabad (1765): The Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa and the Structural Inversion of Bullion Flows

Returning to Bengal for his second governorship (**May 1765 – January 1767**) to craft a durable political-fiscal settlement out of the **Buxar** victory, **Robert Clive** deliberately rejected the reckless temptation to march on Delhi or annex **Awadh** outright—recognizing that annexing Awadh would saddle the Company with an indefensible 600-mile frontier exposed to **Maratha** and **Afghan (Abdali)** invasions. Instead, in **August 1765**, **Clive** signed two interlocking **Treaties of Allahabad** that engineered a buffer-state defensive shield in North India and legalized corporate tax extraction in Bengal:

*   **The First Treaty of Allahabad with Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh (August 16, 1765)**: **Clive** restored the conquered kingdom of **Awadh** to **Shuja-ud-Daula**, transforming Awadh into a dependent **imperial buffer state** shielding Bengal’s western border, subject to four stringent terms:
    1.  **Shuja-ud-Daula** surrendered the strategic districts of **Kora and Allahabad** (to be handed over to Emperor **Shah Alam II**);
    2.  He agreed to pay a war indemnity of **Rs 50 lakh** to the Company;
    3.  He confirmed full possession of the estate (*Zamindari*) of **Banaras** to the British ally **Balwant Singh**; and
    4.  He entered into a defensive military alliance under which the Company pledged to defend Awadh’s borders provided the Nawab paid the full maintenance costs of the British troops (the embryonic prototype of the **Subsidiary Alliance** system), while granting the Company duty-free trade throughout Awadh.
*   **The Second Treaty of Allahabad with Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II (August 12, 1765)**: Treating the defeated Emperor as a sovereign rubber-stamp whose *Farman* would confer unimpeachable constitutional legitimacy upon the Company in the eyes of both Indian subjects and rival European powers (**France** and **Holland**), **Clive** concluded the second treaty:
    1.  **Shah Alam II** was taken under the Company’s protection and assigned the districts of **Kora and Allahabad** (wrested from Awadh) as his royal demesne;
    2.  In exchange, **Shah Alam II** issued a formal imperial **Farman** granting the **Permanent Diwani (the right to collect and administer land revenue and civil justice) of the provinces of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa** to the **English East India Company**;
    3.  In return for the **Diwani**, the Company agreed to pay the Emperor an annual tribute of **Rs 26 lakh** from the revenues of Bengal and to provide **Rs 53 lakh** toward the expenses of the **Nizamat** (criminal administration, police, and defense) of the three provinces.
*   **Subjugation of the Nizamat and the Structural Inversion of Bullion Flows**: Having already compelled the new teenaged Nawab of Bengal, **Najm-ud-Daulah** (**1765–1766**, son of Mir Jafar, who died in February 1765), by the **Treaty of February 1765** to disband most of his army and delegate all **Nizamat** functions to a **Naib Nazim** (Deputy Subadar, **Muhammad Reza Khan**) who could only be appointed or dismissed by the British, **Clive** now united both **Diwani** (via the Emperor’s *Farman*) and **Nizamat** (via control of the Nawab’s deputy) under the Company’s invisible grip. From the standpoint of global political economy, the **1765 Diwani Grant** executed the single most consequential structural inversion in modern Indian economic history. For two thousand years since Pliny the Elder, and throughout the first 150 years of European mercantilism (**1600–1757**), India had functioned as the *"sink of the world’s precious metals,"* importing silver and gold bullion in exchange for exported manufactured textiles. Overnight after **August 1765**, the **English East India Company** ceased importing silver bullion from Britain to purchase Indian goods. Instead, after deducting administrative and military expenses from the **Rs 3 to 4 crore** gross land revenue extracted from the peasantry of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, the Company allocated the net fiscal surplus—euphemistically termed **"The Investment"**—to purchase Bengali cotton muslins, raw silk, and saltpetre for export to London, and to ship Bengal silver bullion to **Canton (China)** to purchase Chinese tea for the British domestic market. India was thus forced to buy its own exports with its own taxes, converting a reciprocal commercial exchange into an unrequited colonial **Drain of Wealth**.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge to Part 2: From Mercantilist Company to the Diwani Dual State

The signing of the **Treaties of Allahabad in August 1765** closed the pre-colonial era of competitive regional state-building and coastal mercantilist enclaves, inaugurating the paradoxical monster of the **Company-State**. By vesting the **Diwani** (fiscal sovereignty) in a London joint-stock trading corporation while leaving the hollow shell of **Nizamat** (administrative responsibility) with a powerless Murshidabad Nawab and Indian deputies (**Muhammad Reza Khan** and **Shitab Rai**), **Robert Clive** erected the predatory mechanism of the **Dual Government of Bengal (1765–1772)**—a regime of power without responsibility that extracted maximal agrarian revenue to fund the Company’s export **"Investment"**, culminating directly in the catastrophic **Great Bengal Famine of 1770** (which wiped out one-third of Bengal’s population) and the near-bankruptcy of the East India Company in London. How the British Parliament intervened via the **Statutory Charter and Regulating Acts (1773–1853)** to subordinate this rogue corporate sovereign, how **Warren Hastings**, **Cornwallis**, **Wellesley**, and **Dalhousie** weaponized Bengal’s agrarian surplus into a continental military-fiscal machine to crush **Mysore**, the **Maratha Confederacy**, **Sindh**, and the **Sikh Empire**, and how this expanding Company state projected its legitimacy through **Neo-Classical Palladian urbanism** and the **Company School of Painting** forms the direct analytical architecture of **Part 2**.
