# Part 7: Print Capitalism, Colonial Education Commissions, Socio-Religious Reform, and Bazaar/Lithographic Visual Modernity (1800–1920s)

## I. Print Capitalism, the Vernacular Public Sphere, and the Coercive Statutory Gag Grid (1780–1910)

### 1. The Political Economy of Print Capitalism and the Anti-Colonial Press Network

The emergence of print capitalism in colonial India was neither an altruistic diffusion of European Enlightenment technologies nor a neutral communicative evolution. It originated at the intersection of three structural forces: intra-mercantile factionalism within the English East India Company, missionary and Orientalist linguistic codification of vernacular typefaces, and the rise of an indigenous urban *Bhadralok* and *Mahajan*intelligentsia seeking a public sphere to contest colonial administrative arbitrariness and traditional social hierarchies. Movable metal type and lithography transformed the economics of ideological reproduction. Whereas pre-colonial manuscript culture restricted textual access to courtly scribes and Brahmanical or *Ulama* custodians, the steam and hand-operated printing press commodified information, standardised regional dialects into supra-local print-languages (standardized Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Gujarati, and Khari Boli Hindi), and enabled horizontal political solidarity across geographically dispersed presidencies.

#### Pioneer Anglo-Indian and Reformist Press Formations (1780–1850s)

The earliest newspapers in India emerged from disgruntled European employees and private traders exposing the private corruption and peculation of high Company officials, before being appropriated by early Indian bourgeois reformers to debate scriptural rationalism, social customs, and administrative accountability.

*   **Hickey’s Bengal Gazette (1780)**: Founded in Calcutta on **January 29, 1780**, by the Irishman **James Augustus Hickey** as *The Bengal Gazette or Calcutta General Advertiser*, this two-sheet weekly was India’s first printed newspaper. Functioning as an organ of intra-European factional opposition against Governor-General **Warren Hastings** and Chief Justice **Elijah Impey**, Hickey exposed private nabob profiteering, illicit contracts, and judicial collusion. Hastings retaliated by withdrawing the paper's postal privileges through the General Post Office in **November 1780**, culminating in Hickey’s imprisonment and the seizure of his printing types in **March 1782**.
*   **Rammohan Roy’s Sambad Kaumudi (1821) and Mirat-ul-Akbar (1822)**: Recognizing print as the primary lever for socio-religious and constitutional reform, **Raja Rammohan Roy** launched the Bengali weekly **Sambad Kaumudi** (*Moon of Intelligence*) in **December 1821** to mobilize public opinion for the abolition of *Sati*, promote Western scientific education, and critique orthodox *Dharma Sabha* apologetics. In **April 1822**, he established the Persian weekly **Mirat-ul-Akbar** (*Mirror of News*), targeting the Persianate judicial and administrative elite across North India with critical analyses of the British constitution, Irish coercion, and Company judicial administration, before shutting it down in **1823** in protest against the **Adams Regulations**.
*   **Harish Chandra Mukherjee’s Hindu Patriot (1853)**: Initially started by Madhusudan Ray and Girish Chandra Ghosh in **1853**, the English weekly **Hindu Patriot** was acquired and edited from **1855** by **Harish Chandra Mukherjee**, a clerk in the Military Auditor-General’s office. Mukherjee transformed the journal into an investigative tribunal during the **1857 Revolt** (critiquing both sepoy violence and Lord Canning’s retaliatory European terror) and the **Indigo Revolt (1859–60)**. Turning his Bhowanipore residence into a legal aid bureau for Nadia and Jessore *ryots*, Mukherjee systematically documented how European planters used fraudulent advances (*dadon*) and kidnapping to enforce unremunerative indigo cultivation, compelling the appointment of the **Indigo Commission (1860)**.
*   **Naoroji’s Rast Goftar (1851)**: Founded in Bombay in **1851** by **Dadabhai Naoroji** with financial backing from Kharshedji Nasarvanji Cama following Parsi-Muslim riots, **Rast Goftar** (*The Truth Teller*) operated as an Anglo-Gujarati fortnightly (and later weekly). Distributed initially free of cost, it served as the ideological organ of the **Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha**, attacking Parsi priestly orthodoxy (*Parsi Panchayat* oligarchy), championing female education, and laying the bourgeois civic foundations of Western Indian economic nationalism.

#### Nationalist Print Capitalism and Vernacular Mobilization (1860s–1890s)

By the late 1860s, the expansion of the telegraph, the uniform half-anna postal rate introduced under **Lord Dalhousie (Post Office Act of 1854)**, and the growing supply of unemployed or underemployed university graduates transformed Indian journalism from elite petitioning into an autonomous political industry. Even though individual newspaper circulation rarely exceeded a few thousand copies, the institución of collective reading in village *chaupals*, bazaar tea stalls, and urban libraries created a multiplier effect where a single newspaper reached dozens of illiterate or semi-literate listeners.

*   **Sisir Kumar Ghosh’s Amrita Bazar Patrika (1868)**: Established on **February 20, 1868**, by **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** and his brothers (including **Motilal Ghosh**) in the village of Amrita Bazar in Jessore district (before moving to Calcutta in **1871**), it began as a Bengali weekly exposing the exploitation of indigo planters and lower bureaucracy. Originally bilingual, it executed the most famous legal maneuver in colonial press history on **March 21, 1878**: within hours of **Lord Lytton** enacting the **Vernacular Press Act**, it converted overnight into an exclusively English-language newspaper using borrowed typefaces, instantly placing itself outside the statutory jurisdiction of the Act.
*   **G. Subramania Iyer’s The Hindu (1878) and Swadesamitran (1882)**: In Madras Presidency, the monopoly of the pro-official Anglo-Indian press (*Madras Mail* and *Madras Times*) was broken by the **"Triplicane Six"** led by **G. Subramania Iyer** and **M. Veeraraghavachariar**, who founded **The Hindu** as a weekly on **September 20, 1878** (initially to defend the appointment of **T. Muthuswami Iyer** to the Madras High Court bench), converting it into a tri-weekly in **1883** and a daily in **1889**. Recognizing that English print could not penetrate the non-English-speaking Tamil *mufassil*, **G. Subramania Iyer** launched the Tamil weekly **Swadesamitran** in **1882** (converted into a daily in **1899**), creating a vernacular political vocabulary to translate budget deficits, salt taxes, and forest regulations for rural Tamil audiences.
*   **Surendranath Banerjea’s The Bengalee (1879)**: Originally founded by Girish Chandra Ghosh in **1862**, the English newspaper **The Bengalee** was purchased on **January 1, 1879**, by **Surendranath Banerjea** for Rs 10 (plus Rs 1,600 for its press). Serving as the journalistic arm of the **Indian Association (1876)**, *The Bengalee* spearheaded all-India campaigns against the reduction of the ICS maximum age limit and the **Ilbert Bill** racial backlash. In **1883**, Banerjea became the first Indian nationalist leader imprisoned for journalism when Justice Richard Garth convicted him of contempt of court for an editorial in *The Bengalee* condemning Justice John Freeman Norris for ordering a sacred Hindu *Saligram* idol brought into the Calcutta High Court.
*   **Tilak’s Kesari and Mahratta (1881)**: Launched in Poona in **January 1881** by a collective of Deccan Education Society pioneers including **Bal Gangadhar Tilak**, **Gopal Ganesh Agarkar**, **Vishnushastri Chiplunkar**, and M.B. Namjoshi, the two weeklies executed a deliberate linguistic division of labour. **Mahratta** (published in English, initially edited by Tilak) addressed the constitutional, legal, and pan-Indian educated classes across other provinces, while **Kesari** (*The Lion*, published in Marathi, initially edited by Agarkar and taken over completely by Tilak in **1887–90**) employed a militant, direct, and idiomatic Marathi prose aimed at politicizing Deccan peasants, artisans, and lower-middle-class youth against colonial famine mismanagement, plague regulations, and Moderate mendicancy.
*   **Agarkar’s Sudharak (1888)**: Following an irreconcilable ideological rupture with **Tilak** over whether social reform (specifically state legislation against child marriage and caste disabilities) should precede or follow political emancipation, **Gopal Ganesh Agarkar** resigned from *Kesari* in **October 1887** and founded the Anglo-Marathi weekly **Sudharak** (*The Reformer*) in **October 1888** (with **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** editing its English columns for four years). Embracing Herbert Spencer’s positivism and John Stuart Mill’s utilitarian rationalism, *Sudharak* mercilessly attacked Hindu scriptural orthodoxy, untouchability, enforced widowhood, and caste endogamy, enduring mock funerals staged by Poona conservatives outside Agarkar’s house.
*   **Naoroji’s Voice of India (1883)**: Started in Bombay in **1883** by **Dadabhai Naoroji** (with the operational assistance of **Behramji Malabari** and **William Wedderburn**), **Voice of India** functioned as a monthly digest and meta-clearinghouse of Indian public opinion. It systematically compiled, translated, and synthesized editorials from the major vernacular and English nationalist papers across all provinces to demonstrate to the British Parliament and Whitehall that Indian grievances regarding the **Drain of Wealth**, military expenditure, and civil service exclusion represented a unified national consensus rather than isolated provincial agitation.
*   **Bharatendu Harishchandra (1850–1885)**: Operating from Banaras, **Bharatendu Harishchandra** pioneered modern Khari Boli Hindi prose, drama, and journalism through three foundational journals: **Kavi Vachan Sudha (1868)** (which transitioned from poetic anthology to political commentary calling for the boycott of foreign cloth well before 1905), **Harishchandra Magazine / Harishchandra Chandrika (1873)**, and **Bala Bodhini (1874)**, the first Hindi journal dedicated exclusively to women's education. In his seminal satirical plays **Bharat Durdasha (1880)** and **Andher Nagari (1881)**, Bharatendu fused popular folk-theatrical tropes with Dadabhai Naoroji’s drain theory, personifying British colonial taxation, price inflation, and legal bureaucracy as demonic forces (*Bharat Durdaiv*) bleeding Hindustan dry behind a facade of imperial peace.

### 2. The Statutory Architecture of Colonial Censorship and Sedition Codification

The colonial state’s posture toward the press oscillated between utilitarian confidence during periods of military security and draconian statutory suppression during geopolitical or internal crises. Because a colonial regime rules without democratic consent, its survival depends on maintaining an ideological monopoly over the narrative of its own benevolence and invincibility. Whenever print capitalism threatened to link urban intellectual critique with peasant, sepoy, or working-class discontent, the state deployed pre-censorship, executive licensing, security-deposit forfeiture, and sedition prosecutions.

#### From Pre-Censorship to Licensing and Metcalfe's Repeal (1799–1835)

During the early expansionist phase of Company rule, press controls were designed primarily to discipline refractory European editors (whom the Company could deport to Europe through summary cancellation of residence licenses) and to prevent military intelligence from leaking to French or Maratha adversaries.

*   **1799 Censorship Act (Wellesley’s Censorship of Press Act, May 1799)**: Enacted by **Lord Wellesley** amid fears of a French Napoleonic invasion via Egypt and alliance with **Tipu Sultan**, this regulation imposed strict wartime pre-censorship. Every newspaper was legally required to print the full names of its printer, editor, and proprietor at the bottom of the paper and submit all material—including advertisements and shipping intelligence—to the **Chief Secretary to the Government** for prior inspection and deletion before publication. The penalty for breach was immediate deportation to Europe. In **1818**, **Lord Hastings** abolished pre-censorship and replaced it with general editorial guidelines forbidding critiques of the Company’s public measures or discussions casting suspicion on the interference with native religions.
*   **1823 Adams Regulations (John Adams’ Licensing Regulations, March 1823)**: Drafted by Acting Governor-General **John Adams** (a reactionary civil servant deeply hostile to both European radicals like **James Silk Buckingham** of the *Calcutta Journal* and Indian reformers like **Rammohan Roy**), this ordinance shifted control from post-publication penalty to mandatory prior licensing. Every printer and publisher had to obtain an explicit license from the Governor-General in Council before operating a press or publishing any book or paper; publishing without a license invited a fine of **Rs 400** or imprisonment, and the government retained absolute discretion to revoke any license without assigning cause. Following the enactment of these regulations, **Buckingham** was deported to England and **Rammohan Roy** ceased publication of **Mirat-ul-Akbar** after his historic memorial to the Supreme Court and Privy Council ("*Areopagitica* of Indian History") was rejected.
*   **Metcalfe’s Press Act 1835 (Act XI of 1835)**: Reflecting the temporary ascendancy of Benthamite liberalism under **William Bentinck** and Acting Governor-General **Charles Metcalfe** (supported by Law Member **Thomas Babington Macaulay**), Act XI of **1835** repealed the noxious **1823 Adams Regulations** in Bengal and the **1825/1827 Bombay Press Regulations**. Earning Metcalfe the title **"Liberator of the Indian Press"**, the 1835 Act replaced executive licensing with a simple registration procedure: a printer and publisher needed only to sign a formal declaration before a magistrate specifying the exact physical premises of the printing press, remaining free to publish unless convicted of specific offences under ordinary law. Although the **Court of Directors** furious with Metcalfe denied him confirmation as permanent Governor-General, his registration regime survived until **Lord Canning’s Licensing Act of 1857** (Act XV of 1857, temporarily restoring emergency licensing for one year across both European and vernacular papers during the 1857 uprising) and the **Press and Registration of Books Act of 1867** (Act XXV of 1867, a regulatory measure requiring every printed book/newspaper to carry the printer's/publisher's name and deliver complimentary copies to the local government within one month).

#### Imperial Gagging, Section 124A Sedition Trials, and Security Forfeiture (1870–1910)

During the post-1858 Crown regime, as the vernacular press systematically exposed the structural link between **Lord Lytton’s** imperial pageantry (**1877 Delhi Durbar**), the **Second Anglo-Afghan War**, and the starvation of millions during the **Great Famine of 1876–78**, the colonial state abandoned liberal neutrality and engineered a two-pronged coercive apparatus: discriminatory executive confiscation of vernacular presses and criminalization of political dissent as "sedition."

*   **Vernacular Press Act 1878 (Act IX of 1878, "The Gagging Act")**: Passed in a single sitting of the Imperial Legislative Council on **March 14, 1878**, under Viceroy **Lord Lytton**, this statute explicitly discriminated between the English-language press (which was wholly exempted) and the vernacular press. It empowered any District Magistrate or Commissioner of Police, with the prior sanction of the Local Government, to call upon the printer and publisher of any vernacular newspaper to enter into a formal bond undertaking not to publish anything likely to excite feelings of disaffection against the government or antipathy between persons of different races, castes, or religions. The Magistrate could further demand a monetary security deposit, which could be forfeited—along with the physical confiscation of the printing press, paper, and types—if an offending article appeared. Crucially, the Magistrate's executive decision was declared final, with **zero right of appeal to any Court of Law**. While a vernacular newspaper could escape the Act only by submitting proofs to a government censor before publication, proceedings were immediately launched against **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar’s** *Som Prakash*, *Bharat Mihir*, *Dacca Prakash*, and *Sahachar*, while **Sisir Kumar Ghosh’s Amrita Bazar Patrika** bypassed the Act overnight by becoming an English weekly. Following intense agitation led by the **Indian Association** and British Liberal leader **William Ewart Gladstone**, the Act was repealed in **January 1882** by **Lord Ripon**.
*   **Section 124A Sedition Trials**: Originally drafted by Macaulay in 1837 but omitted from the **1860 Indian Penal Code**, **Section 124A** was inserted into the IPC by **Act XXVII of 1870** at the insistence of Law Member **James Fitzjames Stephen** amid anxieties over the **Wahabi movement**. It criminalized whoever by words, either spoken or written, or by signs or visible representation, excited or attempted to excite feelings of "disaffection" towards the Government established by law in British India, carrying a maximum punishment of **transportation for life**.
    *   *The Bangobasi Case (1891)*: The first major prosecution under Section 124A targeted the orthodox Bengali weekly *Bangobasi* (edited by Jogendra Chandra Bose) for its militant campaign against the **Age of Consent Bill (1891)**; though Chief Justice Petheram defined "disaffection" broadly as "contrary to affection," the jury deadlocked and the prosecution was dropped after an apology.
    *   *Tilak’s First Sedition Trial (1897)*: Following the assassination of Poona Plague Commissioner **W.C. Rand** and Lt. Ayerst by the **Chapekar Brothers**, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** was prosecuted under **Section 124A** for publishing in **Kesari** (June 15, 1897) a metaphorical poem (*Shivaji’s Utterances*) and a report of a Shivaji festival speech defending Shivaji’s killing of Afzal Khan on ethical grounds derived from the *Bhagavad Gita*. Presiding Judge **Arthur Strachey** radically expanded colonial jurisprudence by instructing the European-majority jury (6 Europeans, 3 Indians) that "disaffection" meant **"simply the absence of affection"**—meaning no incitement to armed rebellion or physical violence was required to constitute sedition. Convicted by a 6–3 racial vote, Tilak was sentenced to **18 months’ rigorous imprisonment**.
    *   *The 1898 IPC Amendment and Tilak’s Second Trial (1908)*: In **1898**, the colonial legislature codified Strachey’s ruling into **Section 124A** (explicitly defining disaffection to include *"disloyalty and all feelings of enmity"*) and added **Section 153A** (promoting enmity between classes) and **Section 505**. Following the **1908 Muzaffarpur Bomb Case**, Tilak wrote two articles in **Kesari** (*"The Country's Misfortune"* and *"These Remedies Are Not Lasting"*), arguing that revolutionary bomb-making was the structural outcome of repressive colonial autocracy. Tried before Justice **Dinshaw Davar** and a special jury (7 Europeans, 2 Parsis), Tilak was convicted by a 7–2 vote and sentenced to **6 years’ transportation to Mandalay Jail (Burma)** and a fine of Rs 1,000, triggering the historic **6-day political strike by Bombay textile mill workers** in July 1908.
*   **Press Acts of 1908 and 1910**: To crush the **Swadeshi and Boycott Movement** and the burgeoning revolutionary underground, the colonial state replaced slow jury trials with summary administrative confiscation:
    *   **Newspaper (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908 (Act VII of 1908)**: Empowered District Magistrates to confiscate any printing press used to publish material that, in the Magistrate’s opinion, incited murder, offences under the Explosive Substances Act, or any act of violence, allowing the police to attach the press via conditional order even before a formal hearing. Nine presses were prosecuted within a year, forcing radical revolutionary organs like **Sandhya**, **Yugantar**, and **Bande Mataram** to cease open publication and go underground.
    *   **Indian Press Act, 1910 (Act I of 1910)**: Reviving the worst features of Lytton’s 1878 Act and applying them to **both English and Vernacular newspapers**, this statute required every keeper of a new printing press to deposit a mandatory financial security ranging from **Rs 500 to Rs 2,000** (and existing presses up to **Rs 5,000** if ordered). If the Local Government deemed any publication to contain words tending directly or indirectly to bring the government into hatred or contempt, seduce soldiers from duty, or intimidate public servants, it could forfeit the security deposit by executive order. Upon a second offence, the new heightened security (**Rs 1,000 to Rs 10,000**) AND the physical printing press itself were confiscated to the Crown, while Customs and Postal officers were authorized to detain and search any suspected packages. Over nearly a decade of operation (before repeal in **1922** following the **Tej Bahadur Sapru Press Committee** recommendations), the 1910 Act penalized nearly 1,000 newspapers and presses and extracted over Rs 5 lakh in securities and forfeitures.

## II. The Colonial Pedagogical Apparatus: From Orientalist Pragmatism to Nationalist Basic Education (1781–1944)

### 3. Early Company Orientalism, Anglicist Hegemony, and Wood’s Despatch (1781–1857)

The trajectory of colonial education in India was dictated by the shifting fiscal and administrative imperatives of the British Empire across R.P. Dutt’s stages of colonialism. During the late-18th-century phase of mercantile conquest, when the Company was consolidating its grip over newly acquired *Diwani* territories and feared provoking a religious backlash among traditional elites, it pursued an **Orientalist** strategy: patronizing classical Arabic, Persian, and Sanskrit learning to train indigenous judicial assessors (*Muftis*, *Qazis*, and *Pandits*) capable of interpreting Islamic and Hindu personal laws for British magistrates. By the 1820s and 1830s, however, the transition to **Industrial Free-Trade Capitalism** created two new structural necessities: first, slashing the prohibitive fiscal cost of importing Covenanted European civil servants by training a cheap supply of English-literate Indian subordinate clerks; and second, cultivating an Anglicized indigenous consumer class whose cultural tastes would align with British manufactured imports.

#### The Orientalist-Anglicist Rupture and Macaulay’s Downward Filtration

Although the **Charter Act of 1813 (Section 43)** directed the Company to set apart a sum of not less than **Rs 1 lakh (Rs 100,000) per annum** for *"the revival and improvement of literature, and the encouragement of the learned natives of India, and for the introduction and promotion of a knowledge of the sciences,"* the Company left this statutory grant unspent until **1823**, when the **General Committee of Public Instruction (GCPI)** was constituted in Calcutta. Over the next decade, the ten-member GCPI deadlocked into two five-member ideological factions: the **Orientalists** (led by **H.T. Prinsep** and **H.H. Wilson**), who argued that the Rs 1 lakh grant should continue funding Arabic, Persian, and Sanskrit stipends and printing classical texts while "grafting" Western science onto traditional learning; and the **Anglicists** (led by Charles Trevelyan and supported from outside by Indian modernizers like **Raja Rammohan Roy**, who petitioned Lord Amherst in **December 1823** against the founding of a new Sanskrit College in favour of teaching Baconian philosophy, chemistry, anatomy, and mathematics).

*   **Calcutta Madrasa (1781)**: Founded in **October 1781** by Governor-General **Warren Hastings** (initially at his own expense before being charged to Company revenues), the **Calcutta Madrasa** (*Aliah Madrasah*) was designed to conciliate displaced Muslim *Ashraf* gentry and train qualified officers versed in *Fiqh* (Islamic jurisprudence) and Persian to assist British judges in the *Sadar Nizamat Adalat* and *Faujdari* courts.
*   **Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784)**: Established on **January 15, 1784**, in Calcutta by Supreme Court Puisne Judge **Sir William Jones** (under the patronage of Warren Hastings), the **Asiatic Society** institutionalized scholarly Orientalism. By translating foundational legal and literary texts—such as Charles Wilkins’s *Bhagavad Gita* (1785) and Jones’s *Abhijnanashakuntalam* (1789) and **Manusmriti** (*Institutes of Hindu Law*, 1794)—the Society sought to break the interpretive monopoly of indigenous *Pandits*, enabling British judges to administer codified textual law directly.
*   **Banaras Sanskrit College (1791)**: Established in **1791** by the British Resident at Banaras, **Jonathan Duncan**, with the dual objective of endearing the British government to the Hindu population of the sacred city and preserving a nursery of native *Pandits* and *Shastris* to expound *Dharmashastra* inheritance and contract law to European judges.
*   **Macaulay’s Minute (February 2, 1835) and the Downward Filtration Theory**: The GCPI deadlock was broken when **Thomas Babington Macaulay**, Law Member of the Governor-General’s Council and appointed President of the GCPI, submitted his famous **Minute on Indian Education on February 2, 1835**, which Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck** formally ratified through the **English Education Act / Resolution of March 7, 1835**.
    *   *Epistemic Dismissal and Resource Reallocation*: Asserting infamously that *"a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia,"* Macaulay argued that the 1813 Charter Act left the government entirely free to stop printing Sanskrit and Arabic books that nobody bought and cease paying monetary stipends to students at the Calcutta Madrasa and Banaras Sanskrit College. Under Bentinck’s **March 7, 1835 Resolution**, all government educational funds were henceforth to be spent strictly on imparting knowledge of English literature and European science through the **English medium alone**.
    *   *The Downward Filtration Theory*: Because the colonial state refused to allocate fiscal resources to educate the broader peasant and artisanal masses, Macaulay articulated the **Downward Filtration Theory**: the state would invest its limited funds exclusively in educating a microscopic urban upper- and middle-class elite in English. This elite—explicitly defined by Macaulay as *"a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect"*—would serve as subordinate administrative intermediaries between the British rulers and the millions they governed, while theoretically filtering Western knowledge downward to the masses by enriching the vernacular dialects. In **1844**, Governor-General **Lord Hardinge** structurally cemented the economic premium on English education by issuing an official resolution guaranteeing preference in all public government appointments to candidates educated in English schools.

#### Wood’s Despatch (1854) and the 1857 Presidency University Triad

By the early 1850s, the complete neglect of vernacular primary schooling and the unregulated proliferation of rote-learning English academies prompted a comprehensive parliamentary review prior to the renewal of the Company’s Charter in 1853. In **July 1854**, **Sir Charles Wood**, President of the Board of Control, sent a landmark educational policy document to Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie** known as **Wood’s Despatch of 1854** (often styled the **"Magna Carta of English Education in India"**).

*   **Repudiation of Downward Filtration (in Theory)**: Wood’s Despatch formally rejected the Downward Filtration Theory, declaring for the first time that the state bore moral responsibility for the education of the general masses, though fiscal allocations on the ground remained heavily skewed toward higher urban education.
*   **Five-Tiered Structural Hierarchy and Medium of Instruction**: The Despatch established an integrated, pyramidal educational grid across every province:
    1.  **Primary Schools** at the village bottom, teaching solely through the **Vernacular medium**;
    2.  **Anglo-Vernacular High Schools** at the sub-divisional and district levels, utilizing both vernaculars and English;
    3.  **Affiliated Colleges** at the district headquarters, teaching in the **English medium**;
    4.  **Presidency Universities** at the apex; and
    5.  **Departments of Public Instruction (DPI)** established in **1855** across each of the five provinces (Bengal, Bombay, Madras, North-Western Provinces, and Punjab) under a Director of Public Instruction assisted by an inspectorate, replacing the old provincial boards and committees.
*   **Founding of the 1857 Universities of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras**: Modelled explicitly on the **University of London** (then a purely examining and degree-conferring senate rather than a teaching or residential institution), the Despatch mandated the creation of the first three modern Indian universities—incorporated by Legislative Acts in **January, July, and September 1857** at **Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras** respectively—comprising a Chancellor (the Governor-General or Governor), a Vice-Chancellor, and a Senate of nominated Fellows who prescribed syllabi and conducted examinations for affiliated arts, law, medicine, and civil engineering colleges.
*   **Grants-in-Aid, Teacher Training, and Female Education**: To expand educational infrastructure without burdening the imperial treasury, Wood’s Despatch instituted the **Grants-in-Aid system**, providing partial state financial subsidies to private (both Indian and missionary) schools that charged tuition fees, submitted to government inspection, and maintained secular instruction hours. It also explicitly endorsed female education (building on **J.E.D. Bethune’s** 1849 Calcutta Female School and Dalhousie’s personal support), vocational/technical instruction, and Normal Schools for teacher training on the English model.

### 4. Imperial Commissions, Curzonian Centralization, and Late-Colonial Pedagogical Debates (1882–1944)

By the late 19th century, the unintended dialectical consequence of colonial higher education became fully visible to British administrators: rather than producing docile clerks loyal to the Raj, the Presidency universities and private Indian colleges were generating a politically militant nationalist intelligentsia armed with John Stuart Mill, Edmund Burke, Mazzini, and political economy critiques of British taxation. From 1882 to 1929, colonial education commissions attempted to contain this political threat by starving higher education of state funds, tightening bureaucratic control over university senates, and restricting admission under the guise of "improving quality." Conversely, Indian nationalists responded first by defending autonomous private colleges and eventually by formulating anti-colonial pedagogical models (**Nai Talim**) alongside late-colonial state planning (**Sargent Plan**).

#### De-Evolution, Bureaucratic Capture, and University Restructuring (1882–1929)

*   **Hunter Commission (1882–83)**: Appointed by **Lord Ripon** on **February 3, 1882**, under the chairmanship of **Sir William Wilson Hunter** (comprising 20 members, including Indian representatives such as **Syed Mahmud**, **Ananda Mohan Bose**, **K.T. Telang**, and **Bhudeb Mukherjee**), the Commission was restricted by its mandate to reviewing the progress of **primary and secondary education** since Wood’s 1854 Despatch, deliberately excluding university functioning.
    *   *Primary Education and Local Boards*: Declaring that primary education of the masses through the vernacular must have the first claim on public funds, the Hunter Commission integrated educational administration with Ripon’s **1882 Local Self-Government Resolution** by transferring the management and financing of primary schools to newly created **District and Municipal Boards**, stipulating that local educational cesses be kept in a separate statutory fund not divertible to other municipal works.
    *   *Bifurcation of Secondary Education and Privatization*: For high schools, the Commission recommended bifurcating the curriculum in the upper classes into two distinct streams: **(A) a Literary stream** leading to the University Entrance/Matriculation examination, and **(B) a Commercial/Vocational stream** preparing students for non-literary business and technical careers. Crucially, the Commission advised the government to gradually withdraw from the direct management of secondary schools and arts colleges, turning them over to private Indian enterprise via liberalized Grants-in-Aid—a policy that inadvertently triggered a massive boom in low-fee, nationalist-run private schools and colleges across Bengal and Maharashtra in the 1880s and 1890s.
*   **Raleigh Commission (1902) and Indian Universities Act (1904)**: Viewing the rapid expansion of private Indian colleges as *"factories of sedition"* and political nurseries for the Indian National Congress, Viceroy **Lord Curzon** convened a secret bureaucratic educational conference at **Shimla in September 1901** (excluding all Indian educators) and subsequently appointed the **Indian Universities Commission on January 27, 1902**, chaired by Law Member **Sir Thomas Raleigh**. Initially containing no Hindu member, the Commission included **Syed Hussain Bilgrami** and later added **Justice Gurudas Banerjee** of the Calcutta High Court, who submitted a sharp **Note of Dissent** defending affordable higher education against elitist restriction. Based on the Raleigh report, Curzon pushed through the **Indian Universities Act of 1904 (Act VIII of 1904)** against fierce nationalist opposition led in the Imperial Legislative Council by **Gopal Krishna Gokhale**:
    *   *Officialization of University Senates*: The Act drastically reduced the total number of Fellows of a University Senate (fixing the minimum at 50 and maximum at 100) and reduced their tenure from life to **5 years**. More dangerously, at least **80% of the Senate Fellows** (all except 20 in Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, and 15 in Allahabad and Punjab) were henceforth to be **nominated directly by the Government**, converting the universities into extensions of the colonial executive.
    *   *Draconian Affiliation Conditions and Veto Powers*: The Act vested the ultimate power of affiliating or disaffiliating any private college in the **Government** (rather than the Senate), subjected private colleges to periodic bureaucratic inspection by the Syndicate, empowered the Government to veto or unilaterally alter any university regulation passed by the Senate, and empowered the Governor-General in Council to define the territorial limits of each university.
    *   *Fiscal Carrot*: While nationalists rightly condemned the Act as a retrogressive attempt to strangle nationalist private colleges by raising fees and affiliation thresholds, Curzon sanctioned the first substantial imperial grant for higher education: **Rs 5 lakh per annum for 5 years** for the improvement of university libraries, laboratories, and postgraduate teaching.
*   **Sadler Commission (Calcutta University Commission, 1917–19)**: Appointed in **1917** under the chairmanship of **Dr. Michael E. Sadler** (Vice-Chancellor of Leeds University) and including two distinguished Indian academics—**Sir Ashutosh Mukherjee** and **Dr. Ziauddin Ahmad**—this Commission was ostensibly constituted to inquire into the overcrowding and governance of Calcutta University, but its 13-volume report redesigned the entire structural interface between secondary and university education across India:
    *   *The 12 + 3 Structural Formula and Intermediate Boards*: Identifying that university standards were collapsing because poorly prepared matriculates entered colleges too early, the Sadler Commission recommended that the dividing line between school and university should be drawn at the **Intermediate examination** (after 12 years of schooling), rather than at Matriculation (10 years). It mandated the creation of autonomous **Boards of Secondary and Intermediate Education** to administer high schools and two-year Intermediate Colleges, followed by a rigorous **3-year Honours and Pass degree course** at the university level.
    *   *Residential and Unitary Universities*: Critiquing the sprawling, purely affiliating 1857 London model, Sadler advocated establishing centralized, **unitary, residential teaching universities** (catalyzing the rapid creation of **Dacca, Lucknow, Aligarh, Osmania, Delhi, and Nagpur Universities** between 1916 and 1923 alongside earlier **BHU 1916** and **Mysore 1916**), greater flexibility in internal university governance, the creation of an **Inter-University Board (1924)** to coordinate standards, and a **Special Board of Women’s Education** at Calcutta University to allow purdah women to take examinations and access professional training in medicine and teaching.
*   **Hartog Committee (1929)**: Following the **Government of India Act 1919**, which transferred provincial education to Indian Ministers under **Dyarchy** while starving them of financial resources (held as a Reserved subject by the Governor’s Executive Council), a quantitative expansion of primary schools occurred alongside falling literacy retention. In **1928–29**, the **Simon Commission** appointed an auxiliary committee chaired by **Sir Philip Hartog** (former Vice-Chancellor of Dacca University) to review educational growth:
    *   *Diagnosis of "Wastage" and "Stagnation"*: The Hartog Committee coined two foundational analytical terms for primary education: **"Wastage"** (the premature withdrawal of children from primary school before completing Class IV/V, resulting in a relapse into complete illiteracy because rural economic distress required child agricultural labour) and **"Stagnation"** (the retention of a child in the same lower class for more than one academic year due to poor teaching and single-teacher schools). It advocated a policy of **"consolidation and stabilization"** over hasty quantitative expansion.
    *   *Selective University Restriction*: At the secondary and higher levels, noting the rising problem of educated middle-class unemployment (which fed revolutionary and nationalist ranks), Hartog recommended diverting rural and average students away from the Matriculation stream after middle school into industrial, commercial, and agricultural vocations, restricting university admission strictly to students proven fit for higher literary and scientific training.

#### The Gandhian Counter-Pedagogy vs. Post-War Technocratic Planning (1937–1944)

When the **Indian National Congress** formed provincial ministries in **1937** under the **Government of India Act 1935**, it confronted a severe fiscal paradox: Congress was pledged to both universal mass education and total prohibition (which eliminated provincial excise revenue, the main source of educational funding).

*   **Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (Nai Talim, 1937)**: Writing a series of articles in his weekly **Harijan** in **1937**, **Mahatma Gandhi** attacked the Macaulayite educational apparatus for alienating Indian youth from manual labour, despising village crafts, and imposing the psychological tyranny of a foreign language. At the **All-India National Education Conference convened at Wardha in October 1937**, a committee chaired by **Dr. Zakir Husain** (Principal of Jamia Millia Islamia) formulated the **Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (Nai Talim)**, endorsed at the **1938 Haripura Congress Session**:
    *   *Core Pedagogical Principles*: It mandated **free and compulsory nationwide education for 7 years (ages 7 to 14)** imparted exclusively in the child's **Mother Tongue**, with **zero English** in the basic curriculum and no religious instruction.
    *   *Learning Through a Productive Manual Craft*: Rather than treating vocational skill as an extra subject, the entire academic curriculum (mathematics, history, geography, general science, and social studies) was organically correlated around a central **productive manual handicraft** suited to the local environment—such as spinning and weaving (*Takli* and *Charkha*), carpentry, agriculture, leatherwork, or pottery. Gandhi initially proposed that the sale of student-produced craft goods to the state would make schools financially self-supporting (covering teachers' salaries) while eradicating the caste prejudice separating intellectual (*Brahmin/Bhadralok*) from manual (*Shudra/Dalit*) labour. The outbreak of World War II and the **October 1939 resignation of Congress Ministries** halted its wide-scale provincial implementation.
*   **Sargent Plan (1944)**: Formulated by **Sir John Sargent** (Educational Adviser to the Government of India) and submitted by the **Central Advisory Board of Education (CABE)** in **1944** as the *Report on Post-War Educational Development in India*, this was the colonial state's final technocratic blueprint. It proposed a comprehensive plan to bring Indian educational standards up to the level achieved in England as of 1939 within a span of **40 years** (later criticized by nationalists as an unacceptably slow timeline and compressed to 16 years by the Kher Committee):
    *   *Multi-Stage Architecture*: The Sargent Plan envisaged **(1) Pre-Primary education** (nursery schools for ages 3–6); **(2) Universal, free, and compulsory Basic Education** for all children aged **6 to 14**, divided into a **Junior Basic stage (ages 6–11)** and a **Senior Basic stage (ages 11–14)**; **(3) Six-year High School education (ages 11–17)** strictly for selected students (roughly 20% of the Senior Basic cohort), bifurcated into **Academic High Schools** (teaching arts and pure sciences) and **Technical/Vocational High Schools** (teaching applied sciences and industrial/commercial skills); and **(4) Abolition of the Intermediate course**, transferring the first year to High Schools and the second year to a **3-year University Degree course**, coordinated by an all-India **University Grants Committee** alongside expanded technical, commercial, and adult literacy institutions and removal of adult illiteracy within 20 years.

## III. Bourgeois-Bhadralok Reform, Textual Rationalism, and Revivalist Counter-Mobilizations (1815–1905)

### 5. The Bengal Renaissance: Monotheistic Rationalism, Young Bengal, and Neo-Vedanta

Socio-religious reform in 19th-century Bengal emerged from the structural transformation of the Calcutta *Bhadralok*—a composite social class born from the **1793 Permanent Settlement** (absentee *Zamindars* and *Patnidars*), Company agency-house compradors (*banians*), and newly educated professionals (lawyers, doctors, journalists, and teachers). Confronted simultaneously by the aggressive evangelical proselytization of Christian missionaries (permitted entry under the **Charter Act of 1813**) and the utilitarian critique of Hindu society as inherently superstitious and stagnant, the *Bhadralok* engineered an ideological defense of indigenous modernity. By deploying **textual hermeneutics**—bypassing medieval Puranic ritualism and *Smriti* custom to anchor social reform in ancient *Shruti* texts (the **Upanishads**) coupled with Enlightenment rationalism—reformers sought to purge patriarchal and caste practices (*Sati*, polygamy, child marriage, female illiteracy, and idol worship) that fractured middle-class domesticity and delegitimized Indian claims to self-governance.

#### Rammohan Roy and the Triadic Fracturing of the Brahmo Samaj

*   **Rammohan Roy’s Atmiya Sabha (1815) and Brahmo Samaj (1828)**: Mastering Persian, Arabic (studied at Patna), Sanskrit (at Banaras), Hebrew, Greek, and English, **Raja Rammohan Roy (1772–1833)** inaugurated his intellectual critique with the Persian treatise **Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin (1803–04, *A Gift to Monotheists*)**, which used Islamic *Mutazilite* rationalism to argue that pure monotheism was the natural state of all humanity whereas priestly intermediaries fabricated miracles and polytheistic rituals for material gain. Settling in Calcutta in **1814**, he established the **Atmiya Sabha (Society of Friends) in 1815** to debate Vedantic monotheism and social evils, translated the **Vedanta Sutra** and five principal **Upanishads** into Bengali and English to prove that Hindu scriptures mandated the worship of one formless Supreme Being (*Ekamevadwitiyam*), and published **The Precepts of Jesus (1820)** (accepting Christ’s ethical humanism while rejecting Trinitarian divinity and miracles, sparking a polemical debate with Serampore Baptist missionary Joshua Marshman and leading to the brief formation of the Calcutta Unitarian Committee in 1821). On **August 20, 1828**, Rammohan founded the **Brahmo Sabha** (soon renamed **Brahmo Samaj**), institutionalizing it via its **January 1830 Trust Deed** as a non-sectarian congregation for the worship of the Eternal, Unsearchable, and Immutable Being without any graven image, statue, carving, painting, or animal sacrifice. His relentless scriptural and statistical campaign against widow burning directly enabled **Lord William Bentinck** to enact **Regulation XVII of December 1829**, declaring *Sati* illegal and punishable as culpable homicide, which Rammohan defended against **Radhakanta Deb’s orthodox Dharma Sabha (1830)** before the Privy Council in London.
*   **Debendranath Tagore’s Tattvabodhini Sabha (1839)**: Following Rammohan’s death at Bristol in **1833** (where he had travelled as envoy of Mughal Emperor **Akbar II**, who conferred on him the title *Raja*), the Brahmo Samaj languished as a small prayer club funded by **Dwarkanath Tagore**. In **October 1839**, Dwarkanath’s son **Maharshi Debendranath Tagore (1817–1905)** founded the **Tattvabodhini Sabha** (*Truth-Searching Society*), accompanied by a school (*Tattvabodhini Pathshala*, 1840) and its influential Bengali monthly organ **Tattvabodhini Patrika (1843)** edited by the rationalist **Akshay Kumar Datta**. Merging the Tattvabodhini Sabha into the Brahmo Samaj in **1842**, Debendranath gave the movement its first formal organizational structure, compiled the **Brahmo Dharma** devotional manual, and introduced the **Brahmo Covenant** (1843). Crucially, under logical pressure from Akshay Kumar Datta (who famously demonstrated via a public vote of members that the Vedas could not be considered divinely infallible), Debendranath formally repudiated the doctrine of **Vedic infallibility in 1850**, grounding Brahmoism instead in intuitive conscience and Upanishadic selection while retaining conservative social boundaries within the larger Hindu community.
*   **Keshab Chandra Sen and the Two Brahmo Schisms (1866 and 1878)**: Joining the Brahmo Samaj in **1857**, the charismatic orator **Keshab Chandra Sen (1838–1884)** radicalized the movement by founding the *Sangat Sabha* (1860), launching the *Indian Mirror* (1861), and undertaking missionary tours to Madras and Bombay (catalyzing the *Veda Samaj* 1864 and *Prarthana Samaj* 1867).
    *   *The First Schism (1866)*: Keshab’s insistence on radical anti-caste practices—specifically demanding that Brahmo *Acharyas* discard the sacred thread (*Upanayana*), promoting inter-caste marriages, and integrating teachings from Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, and Zoroastrianism alongside Hindu texts—alienated the conservative **Debendranath Tagore**. In **1865–66**, Debendranath dismissed Keshab from the office of *Acharya*, splitting the organization into two: Debendranath’s conservative wing adopted the name **Adi Brahmo Samaj** (adhering to the slogan *"Brahmoism is Hinduism"* and limiting itself to monotheistic prayer without disrupting Hindu caste rules), while Keshab founded the radical **Brahmo Samaj of India in November 1866**. Keshab subsequently established the **Indian Reform Association (1870)** (running cheap literature presses, female normal schools, and anti-liquor campaigns) and pressured the colonial state to enact the **Native Marriage Act of 1872 (Act III of 1872 / Civil Marriage Act)**, which legalized inter-caste marriages, banned polygamy, and fixed the minimum marriageable age at **14 for girls and 18 for boys**, provided the couple declared they did not belong to the Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, or Parsi religions.
    *   *The Second Schism (1878)*: By the mid-1870s, Keshab’s growing authoritarianism, claims of receiving direct divine commands (*Adesh*), and mystical devotion caused friction with rationalist younger members. The rupture occurred in **March 1878** when Keshab violated his own **1872 Native Marriage Act** by marrying his 13-year-old daughter Suniti Devi to the 15-year-old minor **Maharaja of Cooch Behar** using traditional Hindu idolatrous rites, justifying the act via divine *Adesh*. Disgusted by this betrayal, the rationalist and constitutionalist faction—led by **Ananda Mohan Bose**, **Shibnath Shastri**, and **Umesh Chandra Dutta**—walked out in **May 1878** to form the **Sadharan Brahmo Samaj** (instituting a democratic constitution, universal adult franchise within the Samaj, and active involvement in the **Indian Association** and Congress), while Keshab retreated into his syncretic **Naba Bidhan (New Dispensation, 1881)**.

#### Radical Iconoclasm, Legal Humanism, and Spiritual Service

*   **Derozio and Young Bengal (1826–1831)**: Operating concurrently with Rammohan’s pragmatic reformism, a far more radical, iconoclastic current erupted at **Hindu College, Calcutta**, led by the Anglo-Indian poet and rationalist **Henry Louis Vivian Derozio (1809–1831)**, who taught English literature and history there from **1826 until his forced resignation in April 1831** (and tragic death from cholera in December 1831 at age 22). Inspired by the French Revolution, Thomas Paine’s *Age of Reason*, David Hume’s skepticism, and Jeremy Bentham, Derozio founded the **Academic Association (1828)** and inspired student journals such as the *Parthenon*, *Hesperus*, *Enquirer*, *Jnananvesan*, and *Bengal Spectator*. His disciples—known collectively as **Young Bengal** or Derozians, including **Rasik Krishna Mallick**, **Krishnamohan Banerjee**, **Ramgopal Ghosh**, **Dakshinaranjan Mukherjee**, **Peary Chand Mitra**, and **Radhanath Sikdar**—openly defied Hindu orthodoxy by eating beef, drinking wine, refusing to bow to idols, and tearing off their sacred threads. After Derozio’s death, they formed the **Society for the Acquisition of General Knowledge (1838)** and the **Bengal British India Society (1843)**, demanding trial by jury, abolition of press restrictions, Indianization of civil services, protection of *ryots* from *Zamindari* oppression, and abolition of slavery and Mauritius coolie traffic. However, because their Westernized cultural iconoclasm completely alienated them from the rural peasantry and traditional urban society without building a mass institutional base, their radicalism remained an isolated bourgeois vanguard.
*   **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891)**: Representing the synthesis of deep Sanskritic scholarship and secular humanist rationalism, **Pandit Ishwar Chandra Bandyopadhyay (Vidyasagar)** rose from rural poverty to become **Principal of the Sanskrit College, Calcutta, in 1850**.
    *   *Pedagogical and Print Democratization*: Vidyasagar broke the millennia-old Brahmanical monopoly over Sanskrit learning by opening the Sanskrit College to **non-Brahmin students**, introducing Western logic (Mill) alongside Vedanta (which he privately dismissed as false philosophy in his 1853 letter to the Council of Education), and composing a simplified Sanskrit grammar (*Upakramanika*) and the foundational Bengali primer **Barnaparichay (1855)**, which standardized modern Bengali prose and typography.
    *   *Widow Remarriage Act (1856) and Female Education*: Discovering a sanction for widow remarriage in the **Parashara Samhita** (*"Nachté mrité pravrajité klivé cha patité patau, panchasu apatsu narinam patir anyo vidhiyaté"*), Vidyasagar launched a massive pamphlet and petition campaign in **1855**, compelling **Lord Dalhousie** and **Lord Canning** to enact the **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856 (Act XV of 1856)** on **July 26, 1856**, legalizing the marriage of Hindu widows and declaring their offspring legitimate (despite a counter-petition signed by over 30,000 orthodox Hindus led by Radhakanta Deb). Vidyasagar personally supervised and funded the first legal widow remarriage in Calcutta on **December 7, 1856** (between Srishchandra Vidyaratna and 10-year-old widow Kalimati Devi), fought a lifelong battle against **Kulin Brahmin polygamy** (*Bahubivaha*), and served as Secretary of the **Bethune School (1849)** while personally organizing and inspecting **35 girls’ schools** across Hooghly, Burdwan, Midnapore, and Nadia districts.
*   **Ramakrishna Paramahamsa and Swami Vivekananda’s Ramakrishna Mission (1897)**: By the late 19th century, the hyper-rationalist, Anglicized posture of the fractured Brahmo Samaj had lost emotional resonance among the broader Bengali middle class, who felt humiliated by colonial racial superiority yet alienated from elitist Westernization.
    *   *Gadadhar Chattopadhyay / Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (1836–1886)*, a humble temple priest at the **Dakshineswar Kali Temple** built by the lower-caste (*Kaivarta*) Rani Rashmoni, revitalized popular devotionalism through direct mystical experience (*Bhakti*) and rustic parables. Rejecting bookish pedantry, Ramakrishna asserted the fundamental unity of all religious paths (*"Jato mat, tato path"* — as many faiths, so many paths), validating both idol worship (*Sakar*) and formless meditation (*Nirakar*) as legitimate stages of spiritual realization.
    *   *Narendranath Datta / Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902)* translated his master’s mystical universalism into a muscular, activist **Neo-Vedanta**. After traversing the Indian subcontinent on foot (1888–1893) and witnessing the crushing material destitution of the masses, Vivekananda electrified the **World’s Parliament of Religions at Chicago on September 11, 1893**, positioning Indian Advaita Vedanta as the spiritual counterpart to Western material science. Returning to India in **1897**, he founded the **Ramakrishna Mission on May 1, 1897** (headquartered at **Belur Math**, Howrah, registered in 1909, distinct from the monastic *Ramakrishna Math*). Reinterpreting the Upanishadic doctrine of *Tat Tvam Asi* through the concept of **Daridra Narayana** (*Jiva is Shiva* — service to starving, diseased humanity is the highest worship of God), Vivekananda condemned upper-caste orthodoxy as a degrading *"religion of the kitchen and the cooking-pot"* (*"Don't-touchism"*), famously declaring that *"it is an insult to a starving man to offer him metaphysics"* and establishing permanent networks of hospitals, famine/plague relief camps, orphanages, and educational centres.

### 6. Western Indian Social Reform and Institutional Philanthropy

Unlike Bengal, where the 1793 Permanent Settlement created a rentier *Bhadralok* class separated from pre-colonial statecraft by nearly a century of British rule, the British conquest of the Peshwa’s dominion in **1818** occurred within living memory in the Bombay Presidency. Consequently, social reform in Maharashtra and Gujarat—spearheaded initially by English-educated *Chitpavan* and *Saraswat* Brahmins alongside Parsi and Gujarati mercantile elites—operated through two distinct modalities: secret iconoclastic societies in the 1840s and evolutionary, institutional reform from within Hindu society (*"reform from within"*) from the 1860s onward.

#### Anti-Caste Secret Societies, Prarthana Theism, and Vernacular Critique

*   **Paramahansa Mandali (1849)**: Growing out of the earlier *Manav Dharma Sabha* founded at Surat in **1844**, the **Paramahansa Mandali** was established in Bombay in **1849** by **Dadoba Pandurang Tarkhadkar** (author of *Dharma Vivechan*), **Mehtaji Durgaram Manchharam**, and Balshastri Jambhekar’s circle. Operating as a **secret socio-religious society** to avoid immediate social ostracism, its members subscribed to one God, human brotherhood, widow remarriage, and the destruction of caste hierarchy. As an initiation rite to physically break caste pollution taboos, members gathered secretly behind closed doors to eat bread baked by a Christian baker and drink water brought by a Muslim or lower-caste person. When the Mandali’s secret membership registers were stolen and published in **1860**, intense orthodox persecution caused the society to collapse, proving the vulnerability of clandestine elitist iconoclasm.
*   **Prarthana Samaj (1867)**: Catalyzed by **Keshab Chandra Sen’s** missionary visits to Bombay in 1864 and 1867, the **Prarthana Samaj** (*Prayer Society*) was founded in Bombay on **March 31, 1867**, by **Dr. Atmaram Pandurang**, and subsequently elevated into Western India’s premier reform institution when **Mahadev Govind Ranade (1842–1901)**, **Ramkrishna Gopal Bhandarkar (1837–1925)**, and **Narayan Ganesh Chandavarkar** joined it in **1869–70**.
    *   *Ideological Distinction from the Brahmo Samaj*: Learning directly from the 1866 Brahmo schism in Bengal, **M.G. Ranade** and **R.G. Bhandarkar** deliberately avoided breaking away from the larger Hindu society as a separate sect. Instead of anchoring their monotheism exclusively in abstract Upanishadic rationalism, they rooted the Prarthana Samaj in the indigenous **Maharashtrian Varkari Bhakti tradition** of **Sant Namdev, Eknath, and Tukaram**, singing Marathi *Abhangas* during congregational worship.
    *   *Four-Point Social Programme and the Indian National Social Conference (1887)*: The Prarthana Samaj focused on four concrete social reforms: **(1) disapproval and gradual abandonment of caste distinctions**, **(2) raising the marriage age of both males and females**, **(3) promotion of widow remarriage**, and **(4) women’s education** (supplemented by night schools for working-class labourers, orphanages at Pandharpur, and the **Depressed Classes Mission** later founded by Prarthana missionary **Vitthal Ramji Shinde in 1906**). In **1887**, **M.G. Ranade** and **Raghunath Rao** founded the **Indian National Social Conference**, which met annually alongside the Indian National Congress session in the same pandal (until **Tilak** forced its eviction in Poona in **1895**), launching the *Pledge Movement* against child marriage.
*   **Gopal Hari Deshmukh 'Lokahitawadi' (1823–1892)**: Serving as a judge under the British judicial service in Maharashtra and Gujarat, **Gopal Hari Deshmukh** authored his famous **100 Letters (Shatapatre)** published between **1848 and 1850** in the Marathi weekly **Prabhakar** under the pen-name **Lokahitawadi** (*Advocate of the People's Welfare*). Launching a blistering secular and utilitarian indictment of traditional Brahmanical priesthood, Deshmukh argued that the *Bhats* (orthodox priests) had paralyzed Indian society with mindless ritualism and Ignorance of modern geography, history, and political economy. He famously declared that if religious scriptures obstructed human welfare and social progress, those scriptures must be discarded and new ones written, while advocating parliamentary governance, female liberation, and indigenous industrialization.
*   **Karsondas Mulji’s Satya Prakash (1852) and the Maharaja Libel Case (1862)**: A Gujarati Elphinstone College alumnus and member of the *Gnan Prasarak Mandali*, **Karsondas Mulji** launched the Gujarati weekly **Satya Prakash** in **1852** to campaign against child marriage, exorbitant wedding expenditures, and the taboo on foreign travel. In an explosive editorial published on **October 21, 1860** (*"The Primitive Religion of the Hindus and the Present Heterodox Opinions"*), Mulji exposed how the hereditary *Maharajas* (spiritual preceptors of the Pushtimarg **Vallabhacharya** sect in Gujarat and Bombay) exploited the doctrine of *Tan-Man-Dhan* dedication to sexually abuse female devotees inside temples. When **Jadunathji Brijratanji Maharaj** sued Mulji for Rs 50,000 in damages before the **Bombay Supreme Court in 1862 (The Maharaja Libel Case)**, Mulji—supported by **Dr. Bhau Daji Lad** and reformist witnesses—proved the truth of his allegations in open court, winning a landmark judgment from Justices Sir Matthew Sausse and Sir Joseph Arnould that subordinated sectarian religious authority to secular bourgeois morality and Anglo-Saxon tort law.

#### Institutionalized Widow Rehabilitation and Civic Nationalism

*   **D.K. Karve (Maharshi Dhondo Keshav Karve, 1858–1962)**: Recognizing that legislative permission for widow remarriage remained meaningless so long as widows were economically dependent and socially ostracized, Professor **D.K. Karve** of Fergusson College, Poona, personally married a 23-year-old Brahmin widow, **Godubai Joshi (Anandibai Karve)**, in **March 1893** and revived the **Widow Marriage Association (Vidhava Vivaha Pratibandh Nivarak Mandali)** in the same year. Realizing that the vast majority of Hindu widows could not or would not remarry, Karve shifted focus to making widows self-reliant educators and nurses: he founded the **Hindu Widows’ Home Association (Anath Balikashram) at Hingne near Poona in 1896/1899**, the **Mahila Vidyalaya (1907)**, and finally established the **Indian Women’s University at Poona in June 1916** (modelled on the Japan Women’s University, utilizing the vernacular mother tongue as the medium of instruction, and renamed **SNDT Women’s University** in **1920** following a generous endowment of Rs 15 lakh from Bombay industrialist Sir Vithaldas Thackersey in memory of his mother, **Shreemati Nathibai Damodar Thackersey**).
*   **Behramji Malabari’s Seva Sadan (1908)**: A Parsi poet, journalist (editor of the *Indian Spectator*), and social reformer, **Behramji Merwanji Malabari (1853–1912)** published his famous **Notes on Infant Marriage and Enforced Widowhood in India (1884)** and campaigned tirelessly in both India and Britain—particularly after the tragic death of 11-year-old Bengali child-bride **Phulmoni Dasi** in 1889 and the **Rukhmabai restitution of conjugal rights case (1884–88)**—forcing the colonial legislature to enact the **Age of Consent Act of 1891** (raising the age of consent for girls from 10 to 12). In **July 1908**, Malabari joined hands with **Diwan Dayaram Gidumal** to establish the **Seva Sadan** in Bombay (with a parallel Poona Seva Sadan launched by **Ramabai Ranade** and G.K. Devadhar in **1909**), a non-sectarian philanthropic institution dedicated to providing medical care, shelter, industrial training, and teaching credentials to destitute, abused, and widowed women across all castes and creeds.
*   **Gokhale’s Servants of India Society (1905)**: Founded at Poona on **June 12, 1905**, by **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** (building on the institutional philosophy of his mentor **M.G. Ranade**, who had severed ties with the Deccan Education Society), the **Servants of India Society** was conceived as a secular order of political and social *Sannyasins* dedicated to "spiritualizing public life." Every member took seven solemn vows—including putting country first, serving without personal financial gain on a subsistence allowance, treating all Indians as brothers regardless of caste or creed, and undergoing a rigorous **5-year novitiate training** in economics, constitutional law, and social statistics before speaking publicly. Explicitly remaining aloof from the Indian National Congress as an autonomous civic think-tank and service corps, the Society launched its English organ **The Hitavada** from Nagpur in **1911** and produced stalwarts such as **V.S. Srinivasa Sastri**, **G.K. Devadhar**, **N.M. Joshi** (who later founded the **Social Service League in 1911** and co-founded the **AITUC in 1920**), and **Hriday Nath Kunzru**, executing pioneering famine relief, sanitation, and tribal/labor welfare work.

### 7. Pan-Indian Revivalism, Vedic Infallibility, and Orthodox Consolidation

By the 1870s, a powerful counter-current known as socio-religious **Revivalism** challenged the Anglicized, colonial-state-dependent nature of early bourgeois reform. Revivalism was not a simple reactionary return to the pre-colonial past; rather, it was an aggressive modern project that appropriated colonial printing presses, voluntary associations, missionary proselytization techniques, and Western scientific terminology to assert that ancient Indian civilization already possessed all modern scientific and social truths, thereby delegitimizing both Christian missionary critique and British imperial rule.

#### Arya Samaj, Shuddhi, and the DAV–Gurukul Pedagogical Schism

*   **Dayananda Saraswati’s Arya Samaj (1875) and Satyarth Prakash**: Born **Mool Shankar** in an orthodox Audichya Brahmin family in **Tankara (Morvi State, Gujarat) in 1824**, **Swami Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883)** wandered for fifteen years as a *Sannyasin* before studying ancient Sanskrit grammar under the blind sage **Swami Virjananda at Mathura (1860–63)**. After touring Bengal in **1872–73** (where **Keshab Chandra Sen** advised him to abandon Sanskrit and preach in **Hindi** while wearing full clothing to reach the urban middle classes), Dayananda published his foundational Hindi treatise **Satyarth Prakash (The Light of Truth, 1875)** and formally established the first **Arya Samaj unit at Bombay on April 10, 1875**, shifting its permanent organizational headquarters and ten simplified doctrinal principles to **Lahore in June 1877**.
    *   *Vedic Infallibility and Anti-Brahmanical Radicalism*: Raising the battle cry **"Back to the Vedas"**, Dayananda drew a sharp hermeneutic line between the four **Vedas (Samhitas)**—which he declared to be the eternal, infallible repository of all spiritual and physical science—and all post-Vedic texts (the *Puranas*, *Smritis*, *Mahabharata*, and Tantras), which he condemned as degenerate fabrications concocted by selfish priests. Crucially, by asserting that the infallible Vedas sanctioned **neither idol worship (*Murti Puja*), nor polytheism, nor animal sacrifice, nor untouchability, nor child marriage (fixing minimum marriage age at 25 for men and 16 for women), nor pilgrimage, nor priestly mediation**, Dayananda weaponized scriptural fundamentalism to champion radical social reform—including **female education, widow remarriage (and *Niyoga*), and the replacement of birth-based *Jati* with merit-based occupational *Varna***.
    *   *Socio-Economic Base in Punjab and Western UP*: While the Arya Samaj gained limited traction in Gujarat, it achieved massive dominance in **Punjab and Western United Provinces** among the rising Hindu mercantile and professional castes (**Khatris, Aroras, and Banias**) and peasant proprietors (**Jats**), who utilized Arya Samaj Vedic egalitarianism to break the ritual hegemony of orthodox Brahmins while defending their youth against aggressive Christian and Islamic proselytization.
    *   *The Shuddhi Movement and Communal Polarization*: Drawing on traditional purification rites, the Arya Samaj institutionalized **Shuddhi** (*Purification/Reconversion*)—initially to uplift lower-caste and untouchable groups (*Meghs*, *Odhs*, *Rahtias*, and *Chamars*) into full Vedic status with the sacred thread, and increasingly to reconvert Hindus who had converted to Islam or Christianity (such as the **Malkana Rajputs** in the 1920s). Coupled with Dayananda’s **Gaurakshini Sabhas (Cow Protection Societies, 1882)** and aggressive polemics against Islam and Christianity in Chapters 13 and 14 of *Satyarth Prakash* (which culminated in the assassination of militant Arya preacher **Pandit Lekh Ram** in 1897 and **Swami Shraddhanand** in 1926), *Shuddhi* simultaneously democratized Hindu caste boundaries and hardened communal fault lines in North India against Islamic *Tabligh* and *Tanzim* movements.
*   **The DAV vs. Gurukul Split (1892–1893)**: Following Dayananda’s death at Ajmer in **October 1883**, the **Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) High School and College** was established at Lahore in **1886–1889** under the leadership of **Lala Hansraj** (who served for 25 years as honorary Principal without salary). By **1892–1893**, the Punjab Arya Samaj split formally into two rival factions over two core issues—**educational curriculum** and **mandatory vegetarianism**:
    *   *The College ("Cultured") Party*: Led by **Lala Hansraj**, **Lala Lajpat Rai**, and Lal Chand, this faction controlled the **DAV College Trust and Management Society**. They argued that to compete for administrative and professional jobs in the modern world, Arya youth required an **Anglo-Vedic curriculum** combining Western natural sciences and English literature with Sanskrit/Hindi studies, remaining affiliated to the colonial **Punjab University**, and held that vegetarianism versus meat-eating was a personal dietary choice rather than a binding religious dogma of Dayananda’s ten principles.
    *   *The Mahatma ("Gurukul") Party*: Led by **Munshi Ram (later Swami Shraddhanand)**, **Pandit Lekh Ram**, and **Guru Datta Vidyarthi**, this purist faction captured the *Arya Pratinidhi Sabha*, enforced strict **mandatory vegetarianism** (*Ghas Party*), and rejected colonial university affiliation as culturally enslaving. In **March 1902**, **Swami Shraddhanand** founded the **Gurukul Kangri near Haridwar**, an alternative residential academy where boys entered at age 7–8 and observed strict *Brahmacharya* for 16 years until age 24, studying ancient Vedic Sanskrit literature alongside modern sciences and history entirely through the **Hindi medium**, while pioneering girls' higher education at the **Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Jalandhar (1896)**.

#### Occult Orientalism and Sanatan Dharma Defense

*   **Blavatsky, Olcott, and Annie Besant’s Theosophical Society**: Founded in **New York City on November 17, 1875**, by the Russian occultist **Madame Helena Petrovna Blavatsky (1831–1891)** and the American Civil War veteran **Colonel Henry Steel Olcott (1832–1907)**, the **Theosophical Society** shifted its movement to India in **1879** (initially partnering with Dayananda’s Arya Samaj as the *Theosophical Society of the Arya Samaj* from 1878 to 1882 before splitting over spiritualism) and established its permanent international headquarters at **Adyar, near Madras, in December 1882**.
    *   *Ideological Validation of Traditional Hinduism*: Combining Western esotericism with Upanishadic and Buddhist metaphysics, Theosophy preached three core objectives: **(1) forming a universal brotherhood of humanity without distinction of race, creed, sex, caste, or colour**, **(2) encouraging the comparative study of religion, philosophy, and science**, and **(3) investigating unexplained laws of nature and latent psychic powers in man**. Crucially, by glorifying the Hindu doctrines of **Karma, Reincarnation (*Transmigration of Souls*), Maya, Yoga, and even the occult spiritual efficacy of Puranic idol worship and caste *Varna* order**, Europeans like Blavatsky and Olcott provided educated middle-class Hindus with a powerful psychological shield against Christian missionary attacks, though at the cost of defending conservative social orthodoxy.
    *   *Annie Besant (1847–1933) and the Central Hindu College (1898)*: Arriving in India in **November 1893** (after abandoning Fabian Socialism, atheism, and birth-control activism in Britain) and elected **President of the Theosophical Society in 1907** following Olcott’s death, **Annie Besant** transformed the society from an occult coterie into an educational and political force across the Madras and United Provinces. In **1898**, she founded the **Central Hindu College at Banaras**, where Hindu religious instruction was integrated with Western arts and sciences—an institution she formally handed over in **1915–16** to **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya** to serve as the nucleus of the **Banaras Hindu University (BHU, 1916)**.
*   **Bharat Dharma Mahamandala (1902)**: As the **Arya Samaj** systematically attacked idol worship, *Shraddha* ancestor rites, pilgrimage, and Brahmanical authority across North India, orthodox *Sanatani* Hindus organized defensive counter-associations—beginning with **Pandit Din Dayalu Sharma’s Sanatan Dharma Sabhas** (founded at Jhajjar and Haridwar in **1887–1895**), the **Dharma Maha Parishad** in South India, and the **Dharma Mahamandali** in Bengal. In **1902**, these regional orthodox bodies merged at **Varanasi** to form the pan-Indian **Bharat Dharma Mahamandala**, supported by conservative princely rulers (such as the Maharaja of Darbhanga) and leaders like **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya**. Headquartered at Varanasi, the Mahamandala sought to defend orthodox **Sanatan Dharma**, preserve temple worship and Puranic rituals, manage Hindu religious endowments, establish *Sanatan Dharma* schools and colleges across Punjab and UP to counter DAV schools, and allow limited social adjustments (such as raising marriage ages and educating Hindu girls in *Pativrata* domesticity) strictly within the bounds of traditional *Varnashrama Dharma*.

## IV. Islamic, Sikh, and Parsi Confessional Modernities and Bazaar/Lithographic Visual Culture (1850–1925)

### 8. Islamic, Parsi, and Sikh Socio-Religious Reconfigurations

The socio-religious transformations within Islamic, Parsi, and Sikh communities between 1850 and 1925 followed sharply divergent class and political trajectories conditioned by their structural relationship to the British colonial state. Whereas the urban Parsi mercantile bourgeoisie of Bombay rapidly assimilated Western education and reformed its internal domestic laws to match its commercial ascendancy, the North Indian Muslim *Ashraf* elite—devastated by the loss of Mughal sovereignty in **1857**, the replacement of Persian by English and vernaculars in **1835–37**, and post-Revolt British suspicion—polarized into two competing ideological camps: **Aligarh Loyalist Modernism** (seeking reconciliation with the Raj and Western scientific education to regain administrative share) versus **Deobandi Scripturalist Anti-Imperialism** (rejecting British patronage to build an autonomous network of Islamic seminaries allied politically with the Indian National Congress). Simultaneously, in Punjab, the **Sikh Singh Sabha** and **Akali movements** evolved from cultural boundary-marking against Arya Samaj *Shuddhi* and Christian proselytization into a militant anti-feudal and anti-imperial mass struggle to wrest the massive landed revenues of historic Gurdwaras from British-backed *Mahants*.

#### Islamic Modernism vs. Scripturalist Deobandi Anti-Imperialism

*   **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan’s Aligarh Movement and MAO College (1875)**: Born into a noble Mughal family of Delhi in **1817**, **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898)** entered the East India Company’s judicial service as a *Sarishtadar* and *Sadar Amin* in **1838**. During the **1857 Revolt** at Bijnor, he saved European lives while witnessing the retaliatory British destruction of Delhi’s Muslim aristocracy.
    *   *Political Reconciliation with the Raj*: To dismantle the colonial paranoia that Muslims were inherently bound by religion to rebel against a Christian government, Sir Syed authored *Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind* (**The Causes of the Indian Revolt, 1858**—attributing 1857 to the exclusion of Indians from legislative councils and administrative insensitivity rather than a Muslim conspiracy), *The Loyal Muhammadans of India (1860)*, and a rebuttal to W.W. Hunter’s *The Indian Musalmans (1871)*.
    *   *Rationalist Quranic Exegesis and Institutional Modernity*: Asserting that the **Quran** (the Word of God) could never contradict the **Laws of Nature** (the Work of God), Sir Syed advocated rational *Ijtihad* (independent reasoning), attacked *Taqlid* (blind adherence to medieval jurisprudence), condemned polygamy, *piri-muridi* mysticism, and slavery, and launched his reformist Urdu journal **Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq (Social Reformer, 1870)** after returning from England. To translate Western sciences into Urdu, he founded schools at Moradabad (1859) and Ghazipur (1863) and established the **Scientific Society at Ghazipur in 1864** (shifted to Aligarh in 1866). On **May 24, 1875** (formally laid by Lord Lytton in Jan 1877), he founded the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College at Aligarh** (modelled on Oxford and Cambridge, open initially to both Muslim and Hindu students under European Principals like **Theodore Beck**, and elevated into **Aligarh Muslim University in 1920**), alongside the **Muhammadan Educational Conference (1886)**.
    *   *Opposition to the Indian National Congress*: After the **1867 Hindi-Urdu controversy** in the North-Western Provinces and the formation of the **INC in 1885**, Sir Syed (influenced by Principal **Theodore Beck**) underwent a conservative political turn. Arguing that until Muslims caught up with Hindus in Western education, representative parliamentary elections and competitive ICS examinations would result in permanent Hindu majority domination over the Muslim minority, he advised Muslims in his famous **Lucknow (1887) and Meerut (1888) speeches** to shun Congress agitation and founded the **United Indian Patriotic Association (1888)** in alliance with **Raja Shiv Prasad of Banaras**.
*   **The Deoband School (Darul Uloom Deoband, 1866)**: Representing the exact polar opposite of Aligarh’s Anglicized loyalism, the orthodox *Ulama* linked to the anti-colonial reformist legacy of **Shah Waliullah** and veterans of the **1857 Revolt** (specifically the Battle of Shamli) retreated from Delhi to the provincial town of **Deoband in Saharanpur district (UP)**, founding the **Darul Uloom Deoband on May 30, 1866**, under the leadership of **Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi (1832–1880)** and **Rashid Ahmad Gangohi (1829–1905)**.
    *   *Fiscal Autonomy and Scripturalist Pedagogy*: Operating entirely through voluntary popular donations (*Chanda*) and strictly refusing any British government grant-in-aid or state interference, Deoband rejected English education and Western sciences altogether. Instead, it imparted rigorous training in the **Quran, Hadith, and Hanafi Fiqh** (*Dars-i-Nizami* curriculum) through the **Urdu medium**, aiming to purge Indian Islam of un-Islamic folk customs, tomb worship (*Bida'ah*), and Sufi excesses while training a vast cadre of disciplined religious scholars to preserve Muslim community identity under non-Muslim rule.
    *   *Anti-Colonial Political Alignment*: Unlike Sir Syed, the Deoband *Ulama* remained implacably hostile to British imperialism. In **1888**, **Rashid Ahmad Gangohi** and the Deoband scholars issued a landmark religious decree (**Fatwa**, published as *Nusrat-ul-Ahrar*) explicitly endorsing Muslim cooperation with the **Indian National Congress** in secular worldly matters and condemning Sir Syed’s *United Indian Patriotic Association* and his heterodox theology. Under Nanautavi’s successor **Mahmud-ul-Hasan (1851–1920, *Shaykh-ul-Hind*)**, Deoband bridged relations with Aligarh youth via the *Jamiat-ul-Ansar* (1909) and launched the transnational anti-British **Silk Letter Conspiracy (*Tehrek-e-Reshmi Rumal*, 1916)** with **Ubaidullah Sindhi** in Kabul, laying the foundation for the nationalist *Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind* (1919).
*   **Nadwatul Ulama (1894)**: Recognizing that the bitter polarization between **Aligarh** (which produced Westernized graduates ignorant of Arabic and Islamic theology) and **Deoband** (which produced orthodox *Maulvis* completely alienated from modern history, English, and contemporary politics) was fracturing the community, a conclave of scholars led by **Maulana Shibli Nomani (1857–1914)**—a brilliant historian who had taught Persian and Arabic at Aligarh for sixteen years alongside Sir Syed before breaking with Aligarh’s slavish pro-British loyalism—founded the **Nadwatul Ulama** at Kanpur in **1894**, establishing its permanent seminary (**Darul Uloom Nadwatul Ulama**) at **Lucknow in 1898**. Nadwa sought a middle ground: reforming the traditional Arabic theological curriculum by stripping out medieval scholastic logic while introducing modern history, English, and contemporary disciplines, and encouraging political alignment with the Indian National Congress.
*   **Ahmadiyya Movement (1889)**: Founded in **1889 at Qadian (Gurdaspur district, Punjab)** by **Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (1835–1908)**, author of **Barahin-i-Ahmadiyya (1880)**, this movement emerged amid the intense tri-cornered religious polemics in Punjab between Christian missionaries, the **Arya Samaj** (specifically **Pandit Lekh Ram**), and orthodox *Ulama*. Basing itself on liberal principles similar to the Brahmo Samaj—such as universal religious tolerance, Western education, separation of mosque and state, and the strict repudiation of violent military *Jihad* against the British—the movement generated deep theological controversy within mainstream Islam because **Mirza Ghulam Ahmad** claimed to be not only the promised Islamic **Mahdi** and Christian **Messiah** (*Masih-i-Maud*), but also an avatar of **Lord Krishna** (*Buruz*) and a non-law-bearing subordinate prophet (*Ummati Nabi*), leading eventually to a split in **1914** between the **Qadiani** and **Lahori (*Ahmadiyya Anjuman Ishaat-i-Islam*)** factions.

#### Parsi Urban Bourgeois Reform and Sikh Institutional Liberation

*   **Parsi Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851)**: Following the conversion of two Parsi boys at John Wilson’s Scottish Mission School in 1839, a group of English-educated Elphinstone College graduates—led by **Naoroji Furdonji**, **Dadabhai Naoroji**, **S.S. Bengalee (Sorabji Shapoorji Bengalee)**, and the oriental scholar **K.R. Cama**—founded the **Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (Religious Reform Association)** in Bombay on **August 1, 1851**, using **Rast Goftar** as its journalistic organ. The Sabha waged a two-front campaign: first, purifying Zoroastrianism of Hindu ritual accretions and training educated priests via **K.R. Cama’s** philological studies of the *Zend Avesta*; and second, emancipating Parsi women from purdah/seclusion, founding Parsi girls’ schools, abolishing infant marriage, and securing the **Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act and Parsi Intestate Succession Act of 1865**, which outlawed bigamy, instituted jury-assisted civil divorce courts, and enhanced female inheritance rights.
*   **Sikh Singh Sabha Movement (1873)**: Following the British annexation of Punjab in **1849**, Sikh identity faced an existential crisis: **Maharaja Dalip Singh** had converted to Christianity in 1853, Christian missionaries opened a mission inside Amritsar, **Baba Ram Singh’s Kuka (Namdhari) rebellion** was crushed in 1872, and in **1873** four Sikh students at the Amritsar Mission School announced their intention to convert to Christianity.
    *   *The Amritsar vs. Lahore Singh Sabha Split*: In **October 1873**, conservative Sikh aristocrats, *Gurbans* (descendants of the Gurus like **Baba Khem Singh Bedi**), and *Gianis* led by **Thakur Singh Sandhawalia** founded the first **Sri Guru Singh Sabha at Amritsar**. However, because the Amritsar faction espoused a conservative **Sanatan Sikhism**—treating Sikhs as a martial sect within the broader Hindu fold, tolerating caste distinctions against untouchable Sikhs (*Mazhabis* and *Ramdasias*), and claiming divine status for Guru descendants—a radical middle-class and plebeian faction led by **Professor Gurmukh Singh** and the Dalit (*Ramdasia*) scholar **Giani Ditt Singh** founded the **Lahore Singh Sabha in November 1879** (establishing the *Khalsa Akhbar* in Gurmukhi).
    *   *Ascendancy of the Tat Khalsa*: Championing the purist **Tat Khalsa** (*True Khalsa*) ideology, the Lahore Sabha rejected Hindu Puranic rituals and idol worship (**Kahn Singh Nabha’s** famous 1898 treatise *Ham Hindu Nahin* — *We Are Not Hindus*), repudiated Arya Samaj attacks on the Sikh Gurus after breaking with the Lahore Arya Samaj in **1888**, promoted the **Gurmukhi script** and Punjabi literature, established the **Khalsa College at Amritsar in 1892**, consolidated over a hundred Sabhas under the **Chief Khalsa Diwan (1902)**, and secured the **Anand Marriage Act of 1909**, which gave statutory legal validity to the distinct Sikh marriage ceremony (*Anand Karaj*) without Brahmanical rites.
*   **The Akali / Gurdwara Reform Movement (1920–1925), the SGPC, and the Sikh Gurdwaras Act (1925)**: While the **Chief Khalsa Diwan** remained politically loyal to the British Raj, the post-WWI **Ghadar movement**, the **1919 Jallianwala Bagh Massacre**, and the **Non-Cooperation Movement** fused Sikh religious reform with militant anti-imperialism.
    *   *The Structural Grievance against British-Backed Mahants*: During the 18th-century Mughal and Afghan persecutions when Khalsa warriors fought in the jungles, the day-to-day management of historic Sikh Gurdwaras had passed to **Udasi Mahants** (celibate ascetics who did not wear the five Khalsa symbols). Under British rule, colonial civil courts and land revenue settlements recognized these *Mahants* as virtually **hereditary private proprietors** of the massive tax-free *Jagir* lands and cash offerings attached to the shrines. Protected by British Deputy Commissioners, corrupt *Mahants* squandered shrine revenues on personal debauchery, installed Hindu idols inside Gurdwaras, barred lower-caste Sikhs from offering *Karah Prasad* at the Golden Temple, and—in the ultimate provocation after April 1919—the government-appointed *Sarbrah* (manager) of the **Akal Takht** and **Golden Temple**, Arur Singh, honoured **Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer** (perpetrator of the Jallianwala Bagh massacre) with a *Siropa* (robe of honour).
    *   *Formation of the SGPC and Akali Dal (November–December 1920)*: Mobilized by the radical **Central Sikh League (1919)** and the daily newspaper *Akali*, thousands of reformers (including low-caste Sikhs) marched to Amritsar in **October 1920** and took peaceful possession of the **Golden Temple** and **Akal Takht**. On **November 15–16, 1920**, a representative congregation constituted the **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC)**—a 175-member central managing body—followed on **December 14, 1920**, by its volunteer task force, the **Shiromani Akali Dal**, which organized disciplined, non-violent *Akali Jathas* under leaders like **Baba Kharak Singh**, **Master Tara Singh**, and **Kartar Singh Jhabbar** to liberate Gurdwaras across Punjab.
    *   *Major Morchas (Nankana Sahib, Keys Affair, Guru-ka-Bagh, and Jaito)*:
        1.  **Nankana Sahib Tragedy (February 20, 1921)**: At the birthplace of Guru Nanak, the notorious **Mahant Narain Das** hired armed Pathan and mercenary thugs, locked the gates when a peaceful *Jatha* led by **Lachhman Singh Dharowali** entered for prayer, and massacred over **130 unarmed Akalis**, burning their bodies with kerosene. Shocked by the atrocity and public outcry (visited by **Mahatma Gandhi** and the Ali Brothers), the British administration was forced to hand over the shrine keys to the SGPC.
        2.  **The Toshakhana "Keys Affair" (November 1921 – January 1922)**: When the British Deputy Commissioner of Amritsar seized the keys of the *Toshakhana* (treasury) of the Golden Temple to assert state control, the SGPC launched a massive non-cooperation and civil disobedience campaign; **Baba Kharak Singh** and nearly 200 leaders were arrested, and Sikh soldiers threatened unrest. In **January 1922**, the Punjab Government capitulated unconditionally, releasing all prisoners and returning the keys wrapped in red cloth to Baba Kharak Singh, prompting **Mahatma Gandhi** to send his famous telegram: *"First decisive battle for India's freedom won. Congratulations."*
        3.  **Guru-ka-Bagh Morcha (August–November 1922)**: When police arrested Akalis for cutting dry *kikar* wood from Gurdwara land for the community kitchen (*Langar*) on a complaint by Mahant Sundar Das, daily *Jathas* of 100 black-turbaned Akalis vowed to total non-violence marched to Guru-ka-Bagh (near Amritsar) and endured brutal police iron-tipped *lathi*-charges until they collapsed unconscious—an epic display of Gandhian *Satyagraha* witnessed by **C.F. Andrews**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, and **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, until Sir Ganga Ram leased the land from the Mahant and allowed free access.
        4.  **Jaito Morcha (1923–1925)**: Sparked when the British deposed the nationalist **Maharaja Ripudaman Singh of Nabha** in July 1923 and disrupted an unbroken reading (*Akhand Path*) of the Guru Granth Sahib at Gangsar Gurdwara in **Jaito**, the SGPC and Akali Dal (declared illegal organizations in Oct 1923) sent continuous 500-man *Shahidi Jathas* (one witnessed by **Jawaharlal Nehru**, who was arrested at Nabha) that braved state machine-gun fire in February 1924 until the government lifted the ban on religious readings.
    *   *The Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 (Act VIII of 1925)*: Facing an unbreakable rural-military mobilization (over 30,000 Akalis arrested, 400 killed, and Rs 15 lakh in fines forfeited) that threatened the loyalty of Sikh troops in the Indian Army, पंजाब Governor **Sir Malcolm Hailey** negotiated a settlement through the **Sikh Gurdwaras Act passed in July 1925 (effective November 1, 1925)**. The Act legally dispossessed the hereditary *Mahants*, established a Judicial Tribunal to adjudicate shrine properties, defined a Sikh legally by exclusive devotion to the ten Gurus and the **Guru Granth Sahib**, and vested supreme democratic management of all scheduled historic Gurdwaras in an elected central board (**SGPC**), elected via **universal adult Sikh franchise (including both men and women over age 21)**.

### 9. Bazaar Visual Modernity, Colonial Art Schools, and Lithographic Mass Iconography

Just as print capitalism and colonial pedagogy transformed textual literacy, the visual culture of 19th-century India underwent a structural revolution driven by urban migration, colonial industrial-art pedagogy, and mechanical reproduction. This transformation unfolded across three distinct yet intersecting visual arenas: **(1) the subaltern Calcutta bazaar** (*Kalighat Patuas* and *Battala* woodcut printers), who adapted indigenous folk idioms to cheap industrial paper and urban social satire; **(2) the British Government Art Schools** (1850s), designed to train Indian artisans in technical draughtsmanship and European academic naturalism; and **(3) Raja Ravi Varma’s synthesis of European oil realism with Puranic mythology**, which was industrialized through his **1894 Lithographic Press** into mass-consumed calendar oleographs that standardized the visual vocabulary of modern Indian cultural nationalism.

#### Subaltern Calcutta Visualities: Kalighat Patuas and Battala Woodcuts

As Calcutta expanded rapidly into the administrative and commercial capital of British India, the traditional separation between the European "White Town" and the sprawling North Calcutta "Black Town" generated a vibrant, tumultuous urban bazaar economy around pilgrimage temples and printing alleys.

*   **Kalighat Paintings (c. 1830s–1930s)**: Originating around the **Kalighat Kali Temple** in South Calcutta, this school was created by rural scroll-painters (**Patuas** or *Chitrakars*) who migrated from the Bengal countryside (primarily Midnapore and 24-Parganas) into the colonial metropolis.
    *   *Material and Formal Shift for the Pilgrim Market*: In rural Bengal, *Patuas* painted long vertical narrative scrolls (*Jarano Pat*) on cloth stiffened with tamarind-seed paste and sang oral ballads from village to village. In urban Calcutta, to sell cheap, portable souvenirs rapidly to thousands of daily temple pilgrims and European visitors, the *Kalighat Patuas* abandoned multi-register cloth scrolls for **single-sheet, standardized (approx. 17 x 11 inch) machine-made mill paper** and **ready-made British watercolours**. To maximize daily output via family assembly-line production, they Eliminated complex landscape backgrounds entirely, executing bold, sweeping, calligraphic brush strokes enriched with colloidal **tin/silver pigment (*Ranga*)** for jewellery borders and pneumatic, tubular shading (*chiaroscuro* volume adapted from British prints) that gave figures dramatic three-dimensional rotundity.
    *   *Subaltern Social Satire and the Elokeshi-Mahanta Scandal (1873)*: Beyond religious icons of Kali, Durga, and Krishna, Kalighat painters functioned as sharp subaltern social commentators satirizing the moral hypocrisy of the Westernized Calcutta **Babu** and neo-rich *Bhadralok*. They painted foppish, hookah-smoking, Violin-playing *Babus* grovelling at the feet of domineering courtesans; wives beating henpecked husbands with brooms (inverting patriarchal order in the dreaded *Kali Yuga*); fake Vaishnava mendicants; and cats with shrimp in their mouths wearing Vaishnava *Tilaks* (satirizing hypocritical Brahmin ascetics who secretly ate fish and meat). Most famously, in **1873**, Kalighat painters produced dozens of visual series chronicling the sensational **Tarakeswar Murder Case (Elokeshi-Mahanta Scandal)**—in which **Madhab Chandra Giri**, the corrupt head priest (*Mahanta*) of the Tarakeswar Shiva temple, seduced a young housewife named **Elokeshi** with the complicity of her parents, leading her government-clerk husband **Nabin Chandra Banerjee** to decapitate her with a fish-knife (*Bonti*) in a fit of rage. Kalighat sheets depicted every stage of the affair, murder, courtroom trial, and the *Mahanta* turning the oil-mill in prison, functioning as visual journalism for the illiterate masses.
*   **Battala Woodcut Prints in Calcutta**: Named after a giant banyan tree (*Bat-tala*) in the **Shobhabazar–Chitpur Road** neighbourhood of North Calcutta, **Battala** emerged from the 1820s onward as the indigenous epicentre of cheap vernacular print capitalism and relief engraving.
    *   *Technical and Economic Democratization*: Concentrated around the **Kansaripara** lane, traditional Bengali metal-smiths, goldsmiths, and copper-engravers (**Karmakars**—such as **Panchanan Karmakar**, who had originally carved the first Bengali metal types for Charles Wilkins in 1778, followed by engravers like **Madhab Chandra Das**, **Nrityalal Datta**, and **Kartik Chandra Basak**) transitioned to carving **woodblock (xylographic) and metal-plate engravings** that could be locked directly into the letterpress frame alongside movable Bengali type.
    *   *Visual and Literary Repertoire*: Printed on the cheapest flimsy paper and sold by street hawkers for a few *pice*, Battala presses flooded Bengal with illustrated almanacs (**Panjikas**), mythological epics (*Ramayana*, *Mahabharata*, *Annadamangal*), Islamic *Dobhashi Punthis*, erotic romances (*Bidya-Sundar*), and sensational penny-dreadful plays and broadsheets on contemporary urban scandals (including over twenty plays on the 1873 Tarakeswar scandal alone). Visually, Battala woodcuts combined dense, decorative cross-hatching with hybrid urban details—depicting Hindu deities seated inside colonial shuttered mansions with Victorian cast-iron balconies, chandeliers, and British soldiers—until both Kalighat watercolours and Battala woodcuts were economically undercut after the 1880s by cheap German chromolithographs and native oleographs.

#### Colonial Art Schools, Raja Ravi Varma’s Oil Realism, and the 1894 Lithographic Press

*   **British Art Schools (1850s)**: Following the **1851 Great Exhibition at the Crystal Palace in London**, where British art critics (such as Owen Jones and George Birdwood) admired the flat ornamental geometry of Indian decorative crafts while dismissing Indian fine art (sculpture and painting) as anatomically distorted and monstrous, the colonial state established four **Schools of Art and Industry**: at **Madras (1850, founded by Dr. Alexander Hunter and taken over by govt in 1852)**, **Calcutta (1854, Government School of Industrial Art, later led by Henry Hover Locke)**, **Bombay (1857, Sir Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy School of Art, endowed by the Parsi baronet and guided later by John Griffiths)**, and **Lahore (1875, Mayo School of Arts under John Lockwood Kipling)**.
    *   *Ideological and Economic Function*: Modelled on the **South Kensington (Department of Science and Art) curriculum** in London, these schools served a dual colonial agenda: first, training working-class Indian artisans as cheap technical draughtsmen, surveyors, lithographers, wood-engravers, and architectural modellers for the **Public Works Department (PWD)**, **Archaeological Survey of India**, and **Indo-Saracenic** building projects; and second, disciplining upper-caste fine-art students in **European Academic Realism**—enforcing single-point Renaissance linear perspective, anatomical drawing from Greco-Roman plaster casts, *chiaroscuro* light-and-shade modelling, and oil painting on canvas—thereby displacing indigenous miniature guilds and establishing Victorian illusionism as the universal benchmark of "civilized" representation.
*   **Raja Ravi Varma’s Synthesis of European Oil Realism with Puranic Themes**: Born into the aristocratic royal household of **Kilimanoor in Travancore State (Kerala) in 1848**, **Raja Ravi Varma (1848–1906)** learned traditional *Thanjavur* painting from his uncle Raja Raja Varma before mastering European oil painting through watching the visiting Dutch-British portraitist **Theodore Jensen** at the Travancore court in 1868 and studying under palace painter Ramaswamy Naidu.
    *   *Visual Nationalization of the Sanskritic Past*: Sponsored initially by **Maharaja Ayilyam Thirunal of Travancore**, **Sir T. Madhava Rao** (Dewan of Travancore and later Baroda), and **Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda**, Ravi Varma achieved international acclaim at the **Madras Fine Arts Exhibitions (1873, 1874)**, the **Vienna Exhibition (1873)**, and the **1893 World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago**. While British academic painters claimed Indians could only copy static portraits and crafts, Ravi Varma appropriated the high-prestige European medium of **academic oil realism** to paint grand historical and mythological tableaux from the **Mahabharata, Ramayana, Puranas, and Kalidasa’s Sanskrit dramas**—including *Shakuntala Looking Back at Dushyanta (1870/1898)*, *Hamsa Damayanti (1899)*, *Ravana Carrying Away Sita and Slaying Jatayu (1895)*, *Arjuna and Subhadra*, *Draupadi Vastraharan*, and *Galaxy of Musicians*.
    *   *Theatrical Mise-en-Scène and the Pan-Indian Feminine Ideal*: Following his **1888–89 all-India tour** with Raja Raja Varma, Ravi Varma synthesized a unified "national" visual aesthetic: he rejected multi-armed grotesque forms whenever possible in favour of lifelike human anatomy, adopted the **nine-yard Maharashtrian (*Nauvari*) sari** as the pan-Indian sartorial standard for epic heroines and goddesses, and structured his compositions with velvet curtains, classical columns, and dramatic spot-lighting directly inspired by contemporary urban **Parsi and Marathi Sangeet Natak proscenium theatre**. By lending tangible, photographic flesh-and-blood materiality to *Shakuntala*, *Sita*, *Lakshmi*, and *Saraswati*, he provided the Indian nationalist bourgeoisie with a proud, "scientific" visual antiquity that could stand as an equal to European Renaissance masters.
*   **The 1894 Lithographic Press (Oleographs)**: Recognizing that original oil paintings remained confined to princely palaces and British governors' mansions, **Sir T. Madhava Rao** urged Ravi Varma in **1884** to send his paintings to Europe to be chromolithographed so that every Indian home could own them. In **1894**, partnering with the Bombay textile magnate **Govardhandas Khatau Makhanji**, Ravi Varma established the **Ravi Varma Fine Art Lithographic Press at Ghatkopar (Bombay)**—relocating it in **1898–99 to Malavli (near Lonavala, between Bombay and Poona)** following the Bombay plague outbreak and labour unrest—and imported steam-powered German lithographic machinery operated by German master printer **Fritz Schleicher** and chromolithographer Paul Gerhardt.
    *   *Mass Iconography, Caste Democratization, and Swadeshi Politics*: The press produced millions of cheap, glossy, multi-stone colour lithographs varnished to resemble oil paintings (**Oleographs**), priced at a few annas. These oleographs executed three profound structural transformations: **(1) National Standardization of Hindu Iconography**, permanently fixing the facial features, postures, and attributes of *Lakshmi*, *Saraswati*, *Ganapati*, and *Rama* in popular calendar art across linguistic boundaries; **(2) Subaltern Democratization of Darshan**, enabling lower-caste and untouchable (*Dalit*) households—who were physically barred from entering orthodox Brahmanical temples—to install framed oleographs of deities inside their own domestic prayer corners without priestly mediation; and **(3) Political Mobilization**, as the Malavli press printed mass portraits of **Chhatrapati Shivaji**, **Maharana Pratap**, **Rani Lakshmibai**, and **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** (who personally visited Ravi Varma at Malavli and used his Shivaji oleographs during the *Shivaji Utsav*), while the press’s debt-ridden sale to Fritz Schleicher in 1901 eventually employed a young photographer-engraver named **Dadasaheb Phalke**, who translated Ravi Varma’s Puranic visuality directly onto celluloid in India’s first feature film, **Raja Harishchandra (1913)**.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 8

While the bourgeois socio-religious reform and revivalist movements of the 19th century—from the **Brahmo Samaj** and **Prarthana Samaj** to the **Arya Samaj**, **Aligarh**, and **Ravi Varma’s** Sanskritic visuality—reconfigured upper-caste domesticity, standardized vernacular print spheres, and constructed a unified cultural antiquity to contest British racial hegemony, they remained structurally tethered to elite *Bhadralok*, *Chitpavan*, and mercantile class interests. Even when upper-caste reformers critiqued untouchability or enforced widowhood, they rarely sought to dismantle the material relations of agrarian labour exploitation and hereditary Brahmanical domination that underpinned the caste order. Beginning in the mid-19th century and accelerating through the early 20th century, a radically autonomous counter-hegemonic assertion erupted from below: led by **Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule**, **Sri Narayana Guru**, **Ayyankali**, **Iyothee Thass**, **Periyar E.V. Ramasamy**, and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, alongside pioneering feminist theorists like **Tarabai Shinde**, **Pandita Ramabai**, and **Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain**, these subaltern anti-caste, non-Brahmin, Dalit, and women's movements inverted the Aryan-Sanskritic mythos, weaponized print capitalism and temple/tank *Satyagrahas*, and forced the question of structural caste and gender annihilation onto the centre stage of Indian constitutional politics—a trajectory examined in **Part 8**.
