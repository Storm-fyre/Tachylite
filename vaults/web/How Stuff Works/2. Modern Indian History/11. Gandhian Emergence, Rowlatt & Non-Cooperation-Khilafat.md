# Part 11: Gandhian Emergence, the 1919 Constitutional-Coercive Rupture, and the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Mass Mobilization (1915–1922)

## I. The South African Crucible, Ideological Counter-Hegemony, and the Localized Satyagraha Triangulation (1893–1918)

### 1. The Political Economy of Settler-Colonial Labor and the South African Laboratory (1893–1914)

The political methodology that **Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** deployed across the Indian subcontinent after January 1915 was forged over twenty-one years (1893–1914) within the racialized settler-colonial economy of South Africa. Late-nineteenth-century Natal and the Transvaal operated on a bifurcated labor and mercantile extraction model: white plantation, mining, and agrarian capital depended heavily on the super-exploitation of unfree **Girmitiya** (indentured) laborers imported from Madras, Bihar, and the United Provinces since 1860, while simultaneously attempting to crush the autonomous commercial competition posed by "passenger Indians"—predominantly Gujarati Muslim (**Memon** and **Bohra**) and Hindu merchants who had migrated as free traders, along with **ex-indentured** workers who sought to settle as independent market gardeners and petty hawkers after their five-year contracts expired. Once the labor of the indentured workforce had built the sugar estates of Natal and the coal and gold infrastructure of the interior, the white settler regimes moved to strip Indians of municipal franchises, restrict their spatial mobility across provincial borders, impose punitive capitation taxes to force re-indenture or repatriation, and invalidate their familial and property inheritance rights.

Confronted with an adversary that combined British imperial jurisprudence with Boer-British racial supremacy, **M.K. Gandhi** transitioned from a Western-trained barrister drafting constitutional memorials into the architect of extra-constitutional mass resistance. This transformation required constructing durable institutional, print-capitalist, and commune-based infrastructures capable of uniting socially fractured linguistic, religious, and class strata—wealthy Muslim merchants, Tamil and Bhojpuri-speaking indentured coolies, and urban Christian clerks—into a disciplined political force:

*   **The Moderate Institutional Phase and the Natal Indian Congress (1894):** Arriving in Durban in 1893 to represent the firm of **Dada Abdulla & Co.**, Gandhi experienced the raw physical reality of colonial racial spatiality when he was forcibly ejected from a first-class railway compartment at **Pietermaritzburg**. Recognizing that isolated legal petitions could not halt the **Disenfranchisement Bill** before the Natal Legislative Assembly, he founded the **Natal Indian Congress (NIC)** on **August 22, 1894**. Modeled initially on the moderate constitutionalism of the early Indian National Congress, the NIC levied a steep membership fee (£3 annually) funded by Gujarati merchant capital, conducted systematic statistical surveys of settler discriminatory laws, and linked South African grievances directly to the metropolitan parliamentary conscience in London and nationalist opinion in Calcutta and Poona.
*   **Print Capitalism and the Commune Counter-Economy (*Indian Opinion*, Phoenix, and Tolstoy Farms):** To transcend elite mercantile petitioning and build ideological cohesion across polyglot laboring classes, Gandhi established the weekly newspaper ***Indian Opinion*** in **1903** (published in English, Gujarati, Hindi, and Tamil under the early editorship of **Mansukhlal Nazar**). Inspired by **John Ruskin’s** *Unto This Last*—which he read on a train journey in 1904 and later translated into Gujarati as ***Sarvodaya***—Gandhi relocated the printing press to **Phoenix Farm** near Durban in **December 1904**. Phoenix functioned as an egalitarian agricultural and artisanal commune where every resident, regardless of caste or professional status, performed manual labor and drew an equal subsistence stipend. When the epicenter of struggle shifted to Johannesburg against the Transvaal government, Gandhi—supported financially by his German-Jewish architect associate **Hermann Kallenbach**—established **Tolstoy Farm** (named after **Leo Tolstoy**, with whom he corresponded via *A Letter to a Hindu*) on a 1,100-acre estate at Lawley near Johannesburg in **May 1910**. Tolstoy Farm served as the self-sustaining logistical, pedagogical, and moral headquarters for the families of imprisoned **Satyagrahis**, eliminating dependence on external bourgeois charity during protracted strikes.
*   **The Satyagraha Campaigns against Biopolitical Surveillance and Fiscal Coercion (1906–1914):** The transition from "passive resistance" to **Satyagraha** occurred on **September 11, 1906**, at the Empire Theatre in Johannesburg, in response to the **Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act** (the "Black Act"), which mandated the compulsory registration and ten-finger biometric fingerprinting of all Indian men, women, and children—criminalizing an entire population's spatial existence. Over the next eight years, Gandhi escalated the struggle across four intersecting fronts:
    1.  *Public Burning of Registration Certificates (1908)* and deliberate crossing of the Natal–Transvaal border without permits to court mass imprisonment, overloading the colonial carceral apparatus.
    2.  *Opposition to the £3 Poll Tax*, an exorbitant annual fiscal levy (equivalent to six months' wages) imposed since 1895 on ex-indentured workers and their wives and children in Natal to coerce them into either perpetual indenture or deportation to India.
    3.  *Mobilization of Women and Working-Class Miners (1913)* following the **Cape Supreme Court Judgment (March 1913)** by Justice Searle, which invalidated all marriages not solemnized according to Christian rites and unregistered with the Registrar of Marriages, instantly reducing Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi wives to the legal status of concubines and disinheriting their children. Women including **Kasturba Gandhi** and the Tamil martyr **Valliamma R. Munuswami Mudaliar** crossed provincial borders to court arrest, galvanizing thousands of Tamil and North Indian coal miners in **Newcastle**.
    4.  *The Great March of November 1913*, in which Gandhi led over 2,000 striking miners and their families on foot across the border into the Transvaal, forcing Viceroy **Lord Hardinge** in India to publicly criticize the South African regime's brutality and compelling General **Jan Christiaan Smuts** and the **Solomon Commission** to enact the **Indian Relief Act (1914)**, which abolished the £3 tax and validated traditional Indian marriages.

#### The Epistemological Rupture of *Hind Swaraj* (1909) and the Mechanics of Satyagraha/Ahimsa

Written in Gujarati aboard the steamer *SS Kildonan Castle* between November 13 and 22, 1909, during his return voyage from London to South Africa—and promptly proscribed by the Bombay Presidency government under the **Indian Press Act of 1910**—**M.K. Gandhi’s** ***Hind Swaraj*** (*Indian Home Rule*) represented a foundational epistemological rupture with both colonial modernization theory and militant nationalist mimicry. Structured as a Socratic dialogue between the "Reader" (modeled on the expatriate revolutionary nationalists of **Shyamji Krishna Varma’s** **India House** in London, such as **V.D. Savarkar** and **Madan Lal Dhingra**, whom Gandhi had just debated) and the "Editor" (Gandhi), the text dismantled the premise that India could achieve genuine liberation (**Swaraj**) merely by expelling British personnel while retaining the institutional, industrial, and military hardware of British civilization ("English rule without the Englishman," or making India "Englistan").

*   **Structural Critique of Colonial Modernity and Institutional Networks:** In ***Hind Swaraj***, Gandhi argued that India was not conquered by the British sword, but had given itself to the British because Indian elites were seduced by modern commercial civilization (*Adhunik Sudharo*), which prioritized bodily comfort and capital accumulation over moral duty (**Dharma**). He identified three prime infrastructural pillars of colonial domination:
    *   *The Railway Network*, which did not civilize the country but accelerated the extraction of grain from rural hinterlands (turning localized scarcity into continent-wide famine), spread bubonic plague, and allowed the rapid deployment of colonial military forces.
    *   *The Colonial Legal Profession (Courts and Lawyers)*, which replaced indigenous communal arbitration with adversarial litigation, enriched an intermediary comprador class of vakils, and stabilized British sovereignty by making the colonial state the ultimate arbiter of native disputes.
    *   *Western Allopathic Medicine (Doctors)*, which severed physical health from ethical self-restraint, encouraging sensory indulgence while remedying only the symptoms of disease.
*   **The Dialectics of *Satyagraha* and *Ahimsa* vs. *Duragraha* (Passive Resistance):** Rejecting the English term "passive resistance"—which he associated with the Suffragettes and British Nonconformists—as a weapon of the weak (*Duragraha*) that harbored latent hatred and waited for an opportunity to deploy physical force, Gandhi coined **Satyagraha** (*Satya* = Truth + *Agraha* = Firmness/Soul-Force, suggested initially by **Maganlal Gandhi** as *Sadagraha*). Grounded in **Ahimsa** (non-injury in thought, word, and deed) and **Tapasya** (voluntary self-suffering), Satyagraha operated as a political-economic strategy of hegemonic delegitimation. Because a tiny European bureaucratic-military elite could govern three hundred million Indians only through the active or passive collaboration of native soldiers, policemen, tax collectors, lawyers, and consumers, the disciplined, non-violent withdrawal of consent exposed the moral bankruptcy of the colonial state. If the state responded to peaceful law-breaking with naked violence, it forfeited its ideological legitimacy ("Civilizing Mission" and "Rule of Law") before both domestic subjects and international public opinion; if it capitulated, the nationalists gained concrete political concessions while expanding mass consciousness.

### 2. The Triangulated Subcontinental Entry: Agrarian Rent, Wartime Inflation, and Industrial Arbitration (1917–1918)

Returning to Bombay on **January 9, 1915**, at the height of the First World War, **M.K. Gandhi** accepted the mentorship of the Moderate stalwart **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** and honored a pledge to abstain from public political pronouncements for one year while traveling across British India by third-class railway to observe the material conditions of the peasantry and working classes. Establishing his first subcontinental commune at the **Kochrab Ashram** in Ahmedabad in May 1915 (relocated to the banks of the Sabarmati River as the **Sabarmati Ashram** in June 1917), Gandhi deliberately bypassed the two dominant political currents of 1915–1916: the elite constitutionalism of the Moderates and the urban agitation of **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** and **Annie Besant’s** **Home Rule Leagues**, refusing to make British wartime vulnerability the basis for political bargaining.

Between early 1917 and mid-1918, however, the structural dislocations of the First World War—wartime price inflation, coercive war-loan extraction, supply-chain bottlenecks, and intensified agrarian and industrial surplus extraction—triggered localized socio-economic crises across Bihar and Gujarat. In three successive interventions at **Champaran**, **Ahmedabad**, and **Kheda**, Gandhi tested, calibrated, and institutionalized the three tactical pillars of his mass repertoire—**Civil Disobedience**, the **Hunger Strike**, and **Non-Cooperation**—while recruiting the core middle-class intelligentsia (**Rajendra Prasad**, **J.B. Kripalani**, **Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Mahadev Desai**, **Indulal Yagnik**, **Anasuya Sarabhai**) who would later command the national Congress apparatus.

#### Champaran Satyagraha (1917): Dismantling Coercive *Tinkathia* Planter Feudalism

In the north-western Bihar district of **Champaran** (Tirhut division), European indigo planters holding intermediary land tenures (*Thikadars* and *Mokarraridars*) leased primarily from the debt-ridden **Bettiah Raj**, **Ramnagar Raj**, and **Madhubani Raj** subjected the tenant peasantry to the coercive **Tinkathia** system. Under this statutory and customary regime, every peasant (**Ryot**) was legally compelled to cultivate indigo on **3 out of every 20 kathas** (15%) of their prime agricultural land holding (`1 Bigha = 20 Kathas`) and surrender the harvest to the European factory at fixed, below-market prices.

*   **The German Synthetic Dye Shock and Feudal Exactions (*Sharahbeshi*, *Tawan*, and *Abwabs*):** Beginning in the late 1890s, the commercial invention of cheap chemical synthetic indigo dye in Germany devastated the global market price of natural Bihar indigo. To offload their commercial losses onto the peasantry, the European planters refused to release the ryots from the statutory obligation of growing unprofitable indigo unless the peasants agreed to one of two extortionate financial alternatives:
    1.  ***Sharahbeshi***: A permanent, illegal enhancement of the annual cash rent by 50% to 75% on Occupancy Ryots in exchange for canceling the Tinkathia obligation.
    2.  ***Tawan***: A massive lump-sum cash compensation paid by non-occupancy tenants, to borrow which peasants were forced to mortgage their holdings to moneylenders (**Mahajans**) at usurious interest rates.
    3.  ***Abwabs***: Alongside *Sharahbeshi* and *Tawan*, planters levied dozens of arbitrary feudal cesses (*Bapahi-Putahi* inheritance tax, *Marwach* marriage tax, *Ghorahi* horse tax, *Motorahi* motor-car tax, and *Faguni* festival tax), enforced through private planter militias (*Lathiyals*) and systematic physical intimidation. Although the outbreak of World War I in 1914 temporarily halted German synthetic dye imports and caused a brief spike in natural indigo demand, the planters retained the enhanced rents while forcing peasants back into indigo cultivation.
*   **Subaltern Initiative and the First Civil Disobedience (April–November 1917):** At the **December 1916 Lucknow Congress Session**, a tenacious local peasant cultivator from Satwaria village, **Rajkumar Shukla**, pursued Gandhi and persuaded him to witness the agrarian servitude of Champaran firsthand. Arriving in **Muzaffarpur** and **Motihari** in **April 1917**, Gandhi was accompanied by a cadre of young Bihari lawyers and educators—**Rajendra Prasad**, **Brajkishore Prasad**, **Mazharul Haque**, **Anugrah Narayan Sinha**, **Ramnavami Prasad**, **Shambhusharan Varma**, and **J.B. Kripalani** (then a professor at Greer Bhumihar Brahman College in Muzaffarpur, joined shortly thereafter by **Mahadev Desai**). When the Commissioner of Tirhut and the District Magistrate of Champaran, W.B. Heycock, served Gandhi with an externment order under **Section 144 of the Criminal Procedure Code (CrPC)** to leave the district immediately, Gandhi executed his **First Civil Disobedience** in India on **April 18, 1917**: he refused to obey the order, pleaded guilty before the Motihari Magistrate to violating statutory law in obedience to the "higher law of our being, the voice of conscience," and demanded the maximum penalty without seeking bail.
*   **The Champaran Agrarian Enquiry Committee and Statutory Abolition:** Stunned by a defendant who refused to employ colonial legal technicalities and backed by Lieutenant-Governor **Sir Edward Gait**, the Bihar government withdrew the prosecution and appointed the official **Champaran Agrarian Enquiry Committee** (chaired by **F.G. Sly**, with **M.K. Gandhi** seated as a full member alongside planter and official representatives). Over several months, Gandhi and his legal associates recorded the verbatim, cross-examined depositions of over **8,000 peasants** across Motihari and Bettiah, transforming subaltern oral grievances into an irrefutable empirical archive. Based on the Committee's unanimous report, the **Champaran Agrarian Act (March 1918)** formally abolished the century-old **Tinkathia** system, reduced the *Sharahbeshi* rent enhancements by 20% to 26%, and compelled the European planters to refund **25% of the illegal *Tawan* recoveries**—a symbolic and material defeat that shattered the prestige of the European planter class and triggered their permanent exodus from Champaran within a decade.

#### Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918): Wartime Industrial Surplus, Plague Bonus Arbitration, and Class Trusteeship

In **February–March 1918**, Gandhi intervened in an industrial labor dispute in **Ahmedabad**, the premier indigenous textile manufacturing hub of western India. During 1916–1917, British military demand and the disruption of Lancashire imports had generated extraordinary wartime windfall profits for Ahmedabad's Indian mill-owners, while simultaneously driving up urban foodgrain and cloth prices by 100% to 200%.

*   **The Plague Bonus Withdrawal vs. Cost-of-Living Inflation:** When a virulent outbreak of bubonic plague struck Ahmedabad in late 1917, the textile magnates—fearing a mass exodus of weavers and spinners to their native villages that would halt wartime production—granted a **Plague Bonus** amounting to 75% to 80% of basic wages. Once the epidemic subsided in January 1918, the Ahmedabad Millowners' Association (led by **Ambalal Sarabhai**, a major financial benefactor of Gandhi's Sabarmati Ashram) moved to abolish the Plague Bonus entirely and replace it with a meager **20% dearness allowance**, despite the relentless rise in the wartime cost of living.
*   **Arbitration, the 35% Formula, and the First Hunger Strike (March 15, 1918):** Approached simultaneously by the Collector of Ahmedabad and by **Anasuya Sarabhai** (Ambalal Sarabhai's sister and a pioneering social and labor activist who championed the workers against her own brother), Gandhi investigated the comparative balance sheets of the mills in Bombay and Ahmedabad alongside working-class household budgets. Applying his doctrine of **Class Trusteeship** and non-antagonistic arbitration, he advised the workers to scale down their initial demand of a 50% wage increase to an economically defensible **35% wage hike** (representing the precise midpoint between the workers' 50% demand and the owners' 20% offer). When the mill-owners declared a lockout on February 22 and subsequently rejected independent arbitration, Gandhi led the workers in a disciplined daily strike pledge on the banks of the Sabarmati beneath the Babul tree, publishing daily instructional leaflets edited by **Shankarlal Banker** and **Anasuya Sarabhai**. After three weeks, as starvation began to erode worker morale and attendance dwindled, Gandhi announced his **First Hunger Strike** (fast unto death) in India on **March 15, 1918**, declaring that he would not touch food until the workers rallied to their pledge or secured the 35% settlement. To avoid the moral coercion of the mill-owners entirely, yet unable to bear the prospect of Gandhi's death, Ambalal Sarabhai and the mill-owners capitulated within four days (March 18), submitting the dispute to the arbitration of **Professor Anandshankar Dhruva**, who awarded the full **35% wage increase**.
*   **Institutional Legacy — The *Majoor Mahajan Sangh* (1920):** This strike directly catalyzed the founding of the **Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association (*Majoor Mahajan Sangh*)** in **1920** by **M.K. Gandhi**, **Anasuya Sarabhai**, and **Shankarlal Banker**. Explicitly rejecting the Marxist doctrine of irreconcilable class struggle that would soon dominate the Bombay **Girni Kamgar Union**, the *Majoor Mahajan Sangh* institutionalized a permanent mechanism of collective bargaining, joint arbitration tribunals, worker welfare clinics, and craft-based trade unions across Ahmedabad.

#### Kheda Satyagraha (1918): Revenue Code Rigidity, Patidar Peasant Mobilization, and First Non-Cooperation

Simultaneously with the Ahmedabad textile strike, a severe agrarian crisis unfolded in the fertile **Charotar** tract of **Kheda (Kaira) district** in Gujarat, inhabited predominantly by prosperous and middle-tier **Lewa and Kadva Patidar (Kanbi)** peasant proprietors holding land directly under the colonial **Ryotwari Settlement**, alongside poorer **Baria** and **Dharala** cultivators.

*   **The Four-Anna *Annewari* Threshold and Administrative Denial:** Following torrential unseasonal rains (`70 inches` instead of the normal `30 inches`) in late 1917, the autumn **Kharif** crops (*Bajri* and *Kudod*) were destroyed by rot, green mold, and swarms of rats, while the prices of basic agricultural inputs (kerosene, iron implements, salt, and draft cattle) soared due to wartime inflation. Under the **Bombay Land Revenue Code**, if the total crop yield of a village fell below **one-fourth (25%, or 4 annas in the rupee)** of the normal average outturn (**Annewari** valuation), the cultivators were statutorily entitled to a full suspension of land revenue collection for that revenue year. However, the British Collector and local revenue bureaucracy (**Talatis** and **Mamlatdars**) systematically manipulated the *Annewari* crop-cutting estimates, artificially inflating the official yield above 4 annas (often assessing it at 6 to 8 annas) to deny revenue suspension and extract full tax payments during wartime.
*   **Independent Agrarian Audit and the First Non-Cooperation Campaign (March–June 1918):** Initiated at the grassroots by local Kheda peasant leaders **Mohanlal Pandya** and **Shankarlal Parikh** from Kathlal and Nadiad, the agitation was subjected to rigorous empirical verification: inquiries conducted by **V.J. Patel** and **G.K. Parekh** of the Bombay Legislative Council, by **Amritlal Thakkar**, **N.M. Joshi**, and **G.K. Devdhar** of the **Servants of India Society**, and personally by **M.K. Gandhi**, **Vallabhbhai Patel** (who abandoned his lucrative Westernized barrister practice at the Ahmedabad bar to serve as Gandhi’s chief lieutenant), and **Indulal Yagnik** (alongside **Anasuya Sarabhai** and **Vithalbhai Patel**) across over 400 villages proved that the actual yield was well below 4 annas. When the Bombay Presidency government under **Lord Willingdon** rejected the Gujarat Sabha's appeals, Gandhi inaugurated his **First Non-Cooperation** movement from the Nadiad Anathashram on **March 22, 1918**.
*   **Patidar Solidarity, Property Confiscations, and the Secret Settlement:** Over 3,000 Patidar peasants signed the Satyagraha pledge, swearing that even those affluent farmers who could afford to pay the land tax would withhold payment entirely in solidarity with their impoverished neighbors, warning that if the rich paid, the destitute would be crushed into selling their cattle and trees or borrowing from usurers. When the colonial administration retaliated by attaching standing crops, confiscating buffaloes and household utensils, and forfeiting land (*Chouthai* penalties), Gandhi escalated the confrontation by advising **Mohanlal Pandya** (who earned the sobriquet ***Dungli Chor*** or "Onion Thief") and peasants in Navagam to openly harvest an attached onion crop from a wrongfully confiscated plot, courting arrest. Facing unbroken rural solidarity across Kheda and requiring Indian manpower for the war effort, the Collector issued a face-saving **secret administrative directive** in **June 1918**: revenue was to be recovered *only* from those affluent cultivators who could pay voluntarily, while all poor peasants were to be granted full suspension—fulfilling the exact substantive demand of the Satyagraha pledge and cementing the **Patidar peasantry** of Gujarat as the permanent social and financial backbone of Gandhian nationalism.

## II. The 1919 Constitutional-Coercive Binary: Dyarchy, the Rowlatt Exception, and the Jallianwala Rupture

### 3. The Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms): Fiscal Asymmetry and Divided Executive Control

By 1917, the British imperial state faced an acute crisis of political legitimacy and fiscal exhaustion in India. The subcontinent had contributed over **1.4 million soldiers** and upwards of **£146 million** in direct financial war gifts and military expenditure to the Allied war effort, resulting in severe domestic shortages, wartime price doubling, heavy taxation, and the political convergence of the Congress and the Muslim League via the **1916 Lucknow Pact** and the **Home Rule Leagues**. To fracture this nationalist coalition by detaching the Moderate constitutionalists from the Extremists and home-rulers while preserving ultimate British control over the military, fiscal, and police apparatus, Imperial policymakers deployed a classic "carrot-and-stick" binary: the constitutional devolution of the **Government of India Act 1919** paired with the draconian counter-insurgency regime of the **Rowlatt Act (1919)**.

On **August 20, 1917**, Secretary of State for India **Edwin Samuel Montagu** made his historic **August Declaration** in the British House of Commons, proclaiming that British policy aimed at the *"increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration and the gradual development of self-governing institutions with a view to the progressive realization of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire."* Crucially, the declaration stipulated that the timing and pace of each constitutional advance would be determined exclusively by the British Parliament and the Government of India. Following Montagu’s tour of India and the publication of the **Montagu-Chelmsford (Montford) Report (July 1918)**, the British Parliament enacted the **Government of India Act 1919** (which came into operational force in **1921**). While the Moderates (led by **Surendranath Banerjea**, **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, and **V.S. Srinivasa Sastri**) welcomed the Montford Report as a Magna Carta and seceded from the Congress in 1918 to form the **Indian National Liberal Federation**, the Congress Special Session at Bombay (August 1918, under **Hasan Imam**) condemned the proposals as *"disappointing and unsatisfactory."*

#### Provincial Dyarchy: The Structural Segregation of Fiscal Authority and Administrative Liability

The core structural innovation of the **Government of India Act 1919** was the introduction of **Dyarchy** (rule of two, a constitutional schema originally conceptualized by **Lionel Curtis** of the Round Table group) within the eight major Provincial Executives (Madras, Bombay, Bengal, United Provinces, Punjab, Bihar & Orissa, Central Provinces, and Assam; extended to Burma in 1923 and NWFP in 1932). Under the pretexts of administrative training, the Act first separated **Central** and **Provincial Subjects**, and then split the **Provincial Subjects** into two asymmetrically empowered categories:

*   **Reserved Subjects (The Coercive and Fiscal Core):** Comprising **Law and Order, Police, Justice, Prisons, Land Revenue, Finance, Famine Relief, Irrigation, Control of Press, and Factory/Labor Regulation**. These strategic levers of colonial extraction and coercion were administered directly by the British **Provincial Governor** through his nominated **Executive Councillors** (mostly British ICS bureaucrats), who were completely unaccountable and irresponsible to the Provincial Legislative Council.
*   **Transferred Subjects (Nation-Building Liabilities without Purse Strings):** Comprising **Education, Public Health, Sanitation, Local Self-Government, Agriculture, Cooperative Societies, Excise, and Public Works**. These welfare-intensive, expenditure-heavy departments were administered by the Governor acting with **Indian Ministers** nominated from among the elected members of the Provincial Legislative Council, who were constitutionally responsible to the Legislature and had to resign if they lost its confidence.
*   **The Fiscal-Administrative Trap of Dyarchy and the Meston Award (1920):** Dyarchy was engineered to insulate colonial sovereignty while transferring political blame for social underdevelopment onto Indian politicians. First, the **Finance Portfolio** was kept strictly as a **Reserved Subject** under a British Executive Councillor: thus, an Indian Minister of Education or Public Health could not sanction a single school, hospital, or irrigation canal without begging for budgetary allocations from a hostile British Finance Member whose primary priority was funding the **Police** and **General Administration**. Second, under the **Meston Financial Settlement (1920)**—which abolished the older system of "Divided Heads" of revenue, assigning Income Tax and Customs to the Center while giving Land Revenue, Excise, Irrigation, and Stamps to the Provinces—the provinces were required to pay fixed annual fiscal contributions (**Provincial Contributions**) to cover the central government's deficit, starving the Transferred nation-building departments of capital. Third, the Provincial Governor possessed overriding **powers of Veto and Certification**, enabling him to pass any rejected bill or restore any budget cut relating to Reserved subjects simply by certifying that it was essential for the discharge of his responsibility, while ICS secretaries had direct access to the Governor to overrule their own Indian Ministers.

#### Central Bicameralism, Communal Electorate Expansion, and the Muddiman Committee (1924)

At the **Central Government** level in New Delhi, the 1919 Act explicitly rejected any form of responsible government or Dyarchy: the **Viceroy (Governor-General in Council)** remained an autocrat responsible solely to the **Secretary of State for India** and the British Parliament, even though **3 out of the 8 members** of the Viceroy's Executive Council (excluding the Commander-in-Chief) were now required to be Indians (given minor portfolios like Law, Education, or Labor).

*   **Central Bicameral Legislature and Executive Override:** The unicameral Imperial Legislative Council was replaced by a **bicameral central legislature**:
    1.  The **Council of State** (Upper House), with a 5-year tenure, consisting of **60 members** (34 elected on an ultra-high property/title franchise and 26 nominated), serving as a conservative oligarchic check; women were completely barred from membership in the Council of State.
    2.  The **Central Legislative Assembly** (Lower House), with a 3-year tenure, consisting of **145 members** (104 elected and 41 nominated, of whom 26 were officials). While the Assembly could ask supplementary questions, move adjournment motions, and vote on roughly **25% of the Central Budget** (75% of the budget—including Defense, Debt Service, Political Relations, and ICS salaries—remained **non-votable**), the Viceroy retained absolute power to restore any rejected budget grant, veto any passed bill, certify rejected legislation into law, and promulgate emergency **Ordinances** valid for six months.
*   **Imperial Divide-and-Rule — Expansion of Separate Communal Electorates:** Building upon the **1909 Morley-Minto** precedent that had introduced separate electorates for Muslims, the 1919 Act aggressively fragmented the Indian body politic by extending **Separate Communal and Class Electorates** to **Sikhs** (in Punjab), **Indian Christians** (in Madras), **Anglo-Indians**, and **Europeans**, along with special constituencies for landlords, universities, and chambers of commerce, while instituting **Non-Brahmin reservations** in Madras and **Maratha reservations** in Bombay. Simultaneously, the property, tax, and educational qualifications for the franchise remained so restrictive that in 1920, out of a British Indian population of over 240 million, only **about 5.5 million** (roughly 2–3% of the total population) could vote for the Provincial Councils, around **1 million** for the Central Legislative Assembly, and barely **17,000** wealthy elites across all of India for the Council of State.
*   **Administrative Restructuring in London and the *Muddiman Committee* (1924):** To appease Indian fiscal critiques regarding the "Home Charges," the Act decreed that the salary of the **Secretary of State for India** and his Whitehall establishment—paid out of Indian revenues since 1793—would henceforth be paid out of the **British Exchequer**. Simultaneously, it created a new office of the **High Commissioner for India in London** (funded by India) to take over the agency and store-purchasing functions previously exercised by the Secretary of State. The Act also provided for the statutory establishment of a **Public Service Commission** in India (realized in 1926) and mandated a decennial **Statutory Commission** ten years after the Act's passage to review its working. However, the internal contradictions of Dyarchy proved so dysfunctional and faced such fierce obstruction from the **Swarajists** inside the councils that in **1924**, the government appointed the **Reforms Enquiry Committee** chaired by Home Member **Sir Alexander Muddiman** (the **Muddiman Committee**). The Committee split down racial-political lines: the **Majority Report** (comprising British officials and loyalists) claimed Dyarchy had not been given a fair trial and recommended only minor administrative tweaks, whereas the **Minority Report** (signed by non-official Indians: **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **P.S. Sivaswamy Aiyer**, **M.A. Jinnah**, and **R.P. Paranjpye**) delivered a devastating indictment, proving that Dyarchy had structurally failed and demanding immediate transition to provincial autonomy and unitary responsible government.

### 4. Wartime Counter-Insurgency Codified: The Rowlatt Act and the First All-India Urban-Rural Hartal (March–April 1919)

Throughout the First World War (1914–1918), the colonial state had suppressed the transnational **Ghadarite** uprisings in Punjab, the **Berlin Committee** networks, and the revolutionary underground in Bengal by deploying the emergency provisions of the **Defense of India Act (1915)**, which suspended normal judicial procedures and enabled mass preventive internments. As the war neared its end—meaning the temporary Defense of India Act would automatically expire six months after the official peace treaty—the British bureaucracy faced the prospect of releasing hundreds of militant nationalists at the exact moment when postwar demobilization, global influenza mortality (which killed 12–17 million Indians in 1918–19), severe price inflation, and the ideological tremors of the **1917 Russian Bolshevik Revolution** were destabilizing the subcontinent.

#### The *Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act* (March 1919): Permanentizing the Defense of India Act

In December 1917, the Government of India appointed the **Sedition Committee**, chaired by **Justice Sidney Rowlatt** of the King's Bench Division in England (alongside two British and two Indian jurists, **C.V. Kumaraswami Sastri** and **Prabhashankar Pattani**), to investigate "criminal conspiracies connected with the revolutionary movement in India" and recommend permanent statutory remedies. Submitting its report in April 1918—the very month the Montagu-Chelmsford constitutional report was finalized—the Rowlatt Committee recommended two draft bills designed to normalize wartime counter-insurgency law during peacetime.

*   **Draconian Statutory Mechanics of the Rowlatt Act (March 18, 1919):** Despite the unanimous opposition of every single elected Indian member of the Imperial Legislative Council, the colonial government rammed the first bill into law on **March 18, 1919**, officially titled the ***Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919*** (popularly known as the **Rowlatt Act** or the "Black Act"). The statute dismantled the foundational bourgeois-liberal guarantees of British jurisprudence:
    1.  *Special Three-Judge Tribunals Without Appeal:* Political offenses were to be tried summarily by a special court of three High Court judges sitting **in camera** (in secret), with **no juries**, **no commitment proceedings**, and **zero right of appeal** to any higher court. The tribunal could accept evidence—such as police-extracted confessions of dead or absconding witnesses—that was completely inadmissible under the **Indian Evidence Act (1872)**.
    2.  *Preventive Detention Without Trial and Suspension of Habeas Corpus:* Provincial governments were empowered to search any premises and arrest and detain any person suspected of "anarchical or revolutionary" intent for up to **two years without trial**, without warrant, and without legal representation, while demanding financial security bonds and restricting a suspect's residence and journalistic activity upon release.
    3.  *Criminalization of Possession of Seditious Print:* Mere physical possession of a document deemed seditious became punishable with two years' imprisonment unless the accused proved lawful intent, shifting the burden of proof onto the citizen.
*   **Constitutional Resignations and the Popular Epigram:** The enactment of this measure—summarized across North India by the devastating popular couplet *"Na Dalil, Na Vakil, Na Appeal"* (No Argument, No Lawyer, No Appeal)—triggered an immediate walkout by Indian constitutionalists. **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, **Mazharul Haque**, and **B.N. Sarma** resigned their seats from the Imperial Legislative Council in protest, with Jinnah writing a scathing letter to Viceroy **Lord Chelmsford** declaring that a government that enacted such laws in peacetime forfeited its claim to be called a civilized government.

#### The Rowlatt Satyagraha: Print Counter-Mobilization and the April 6 All-India *Hartal*

For **M.K. Gandhi**, who had campaigned loyally to recruit Indian soldiers for the British Empire in Kheda as late as mid-1918, the Rowlatt Act shattered the illusion of imperial moral reciprocity. Stepping outside the cautious framework of the official Congress leadership (where both the Liberal Moderates and veteran Home Rulers like **Annie Besant** opposed mass unconstitutional law-breaking), Gandhi founded the **Satyagraha Sabha** in Bombay on **February 24, 1919**, bypassing the formal INC machinery and drawing heavily on the network of the younger **Home Rule League** cadres, pan-Islamic Khilafatists (**Abdul Bari**, **Dr. M.A. Ansari**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan**), and urban labor Unions.

*   **The Mechanics of the First All-India Mass Agitation (April 6, 1919):** Gandhi called for a nationwide general strike (**Hartal**) accompanied by fasting, prayer, and public mass meetings—originally scheduled for March 30, 1919 (the day Delhi observed it, resulting in police firing at the railway station and **Swami Shraddhanand** addressing a historic Hindu-Muslim unity congregation from the pulpit of the **Jama Masjid**), and subsequently shifted to **April 6, 1919**. Because the Rowlatt Act itself required the government to formally invoke its emergency clauses in a specific province before a citizen could directly break it, Gandhi solved the tactical puzzle of civil disobedience by instructing Satyagrahis to violate the **1910 Press Act** and the **Salt Law**: volunteers openly printed and sold unregistered news-sheets (*Satyagraha*) and banned political texts—Specifically Gandhi's own proscribed ***Hind Swaraj*** and ***Sarvodaya*** (*Unto This Last*)—on the streets of Bombay.
*   **Urban-Rural Convergence and Colonial Preemptive Arrests:** The **April 6 Hartal** succeeded on a scale unprecedented in modern Indian history, paralyzing commercial markets, transport grids, and workshops across Bombay, Ahmedabad, Delhi, Lahore, and Amritsar. When Gandhi departed Bombay by train on April 8 to calm tensions in Delhi and Punjab, the colonial authorities intercepted him at **Palwal railway station** (Haryana) on **April 9**, served him with a prohibition order barring his entry into Punjab and Delhi, and forcibly escorted him back to Bombay. News of Gandhi's arrest triggered volcanic urban uprisings, strikes by textile workers in **Ahmedabad** and **Viramgam** (where telegraph lines were cut and government buildings burned), and a violent showdown across **Punjab**.

### 5. Spatial Terror in the Colonial Enclave: The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre and the Imperial Inquiry Whitewash (1919–1920)

Nowhere were the wartime structural contradictions of the colonial state more acute than in **Punjab**. Governed from 1913 to 1919 by the autocratic Lieutenant-Governor **Sir Michael O’Dwyer**, Punjab had functioned as the military barracks of the British Empire: under the post-1857 "Martial Races" doctrine, the province supplied over **60% of all combat recruits** raised in India during World War I. O’Dwyer achieved these staggering recruitment quotas and war-loan targets through ruthless bureaucratic coercion—imposing village quotas on **Zaildars** and **Lambardars**, threatening tax enhancements, and forcibly conscripting peasant youths—while simultaneously executing 42 Ghadar revolutionaries and sentencing hundreds to the Cellular Jail in the **Lahore Conspiracy Trials**, and banning nationalist newspapers (*The Tribune*, *Pratap*). By early 1919, Punjab was a tinderbox of demobilized soldiers, urban artisans crushed by grain hyperinflation, and middle-class professionals humiliated by a new urban income tax reassessment and the Rowlatt Act.

#### Martial Law in Punjab and the Enclosure Geometry of April 13, 1919

In the commercial and religious metropolis of **Amritsar**—spatially segregated between the dense, labyrinthine walled **Native City** surrounding the Golden Temple and the spacious, tree-lined European **Civil Lines** and **Cantonment** across the railway footbridges—the Rowlatt agitation was led by two charismatic local Congress and Khilafat leaders: **Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew** (a Cambridge-educated Muslim barrister) and **Dr. Satyapal** (a Hindu medical practitioner). After they organized peaceful, massive inter-communal *Hartals* on March 30 and April 6, and after Hindus and Muslims fraternized by drinking from the same vessels during the **Ram Navami** procession on April 9, **Michael O’Dwyer** ordered the Deputy Commissioner, Miles Irving, to secretly arrest and deport **Dr. Kitchlew** and **Dr. Satyapal** to Dharamsala on the morning of **April 10, 1919**.

*   **The Spatial Rupture of April 10 and Brigadier-General Dyer’s Occupation:** When an unarmed crowd marched toward the Deputy Commissioner’s bungalow in the Civil Lines to demand the whereabouts of Kitchlew and Satyapal, British pickets mounted on the **Railway Footbridge** opened fire, killing several demonstrators. The enraged crowd surged back into the walled city, attacking symbols of colonial extraction and communication: the **National Bank**, the **Chartered Bank**, the **Alliance Bank** (killing five European managers), the Town Hall, telegraph offices, and railway goods sheds, and brutally assaulting a British missionary schoolteacher, **Marcella Sherwood**, in the narrow alley of **Kucha Kurrichhan** (before she was rescued by local Hindu shopkeepers). On the night of **April 11**, **Brigadier-General Reginald Edward Harry Dyer** arrived from Jullundur Cantonment, assumed de facto military command of Amritsar, and on the morning of **April 13, 1919**—the auspicious harvest festival of **Baisakhi**—marched through a fraction of the city reading a proclamation banning all public assemblies and warning that any gathering would be dispersed by force of arms.
*   **The Tactical Geometry of the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919):** At 4:30 PM on Baisakhi afternoon, a crowd of **15,000 to 20,000 people** assembled inside **Jallianwala Bagh**—an irregular, sunken six-acre quadrangle near the Golden Temple, enclosed on all four sides by the high brick back-walls (`10 feet` or higher) of surrounding multi-story residential houses, containing an open masonry well and a *Samadhi*, and accessible primarily through a single, extraordinarily narrow, tunnel-like entrance passage (`roughly 7.5 feet wide`). Crucially, a vast proportion of the crowd consisted of rural Sikh and Hindu peasants and Jat villagers who had come into Amritsar from the surrounding districts for the annual **Baisakhi cattle fair** and Golden Temple pilgrimage, completely unaware of Dyer’s morning proclamation.
*   **1,650 Rounds and the Mechanics of "Moral Effect":** Arriving at the Bagh at 5:15 PM with **50 riflemen** (25 Gurkhas from the 1/9th Gurkha Rifles and 25 Baluchis/Pathans from the 54th Sikhs and 59th Scinde Rifles, armed with **.303 Lee-Enfield rifles**), **40 Gurkhas** armed only with *Kukris*, and two armored cars mounted with machine guns, **General Dyer** found that the armored cars were too wide to squeeze through the narrow entrance lane. Leaving the machine guns outside, Dyer marched his 50 riflemen onto the raised platform inside the entrance, deployed them left and right, and—**without issuing a single warning to disperse**—ordered continuous, directed rifle fire into the densest sections of the trapped, unarmed multitude. As thousands panicked, piling up against the locked back doors and leaping into the open well to escape the bullets, Dyer personally directed the fire toward the low perimeter walls where people were trying to climb out. The firing continued relentlessly for **10 to 15 minutes** until ammunition was nearly exhausted (**1,650 rounds** fired). Dyer then marched his troops away without providing a single drop of water or medical aid to the dying, declaring that looking after the wounded was "not my job." While the colonial government's belated official count acknowledged **379 dead** and **1,200 wounded**, the meticulous investigation conducted by the **Indian National Congress** and **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya’s** Sewa Samiti established that **over 1,000 people were killed** and more than 1,500 wounded.
*   **The Martial Law Regime of Racial Humiliation (April–June 1919) and Retributive Justice (1940):** Following the massacre, Viceroy **Chelmsford** and **Michael O’Dwyer** imposed formal **Martial Law** across five districts of Punjab (Amritsar, Lahore, Gujranwala, Lyallpur, and Sheikhupura, backdated to March 30) coupled with strict press censorship blocking news from leaving the province. Under Martial Law, the colonial state executed a systematic campaign of racial humiliation:
    1.  Dyer’s notorious **Crawling Order** in Amritsar's **Kucha Kurrichhan** lane (where Marcella Sherwood had been assaulted), forcing every Indian resident or passerby to crawl on their bellies (`150 yards`) along the filthy street at bayonet point, alongside erecting a public whipping triangle in the street to flog young boys.
    2.  In **Gujranwala**, Royal Air Force aircraft bombed and machine-gunned unarmed peasant protests and a boarding school from the air.
    3.  In **Lahore** and **Kasur**, **Colonel Frank Johnson** and Captain Doveton forced Indian students and lawyers to walk 16 miles daily in the scorching May sun for roll-calls, ordered all Indians to dismount from vehicles and *Salaam* every European officer, and forced suspects to touch the ground with their noses and skip.
    4.  Twenty-one years later, on **March 13, 1940**, the Punjabi revolutionary **Udham Singh** (a survivor of the Amritsar trauma who adopted the composite secular name **Ram Mohammad Singh Azad**) assassinated **Sir Michael O’Dwyer** at **Caxton Hall, London**, before being executed at Pentonville Prison in July 1940.

#### Epistemic and Moral Rupture: Tagore’s Renunciation and the Hunter Committee vs. Congress Inquiry Report

As news of the Punjab atrocities leaked past the martial law cordon in May 1919, it caused an irreversible moral rupture between the British Empire and Indian society. On **May 31, 1919**, **Rabindranath Tagore** wrote a blistering open letter to Viceroy **Lord Chelmsford** renouncing his British **Knighthood** (conferred in 1915), declaring that *"the time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation, and I for my part wish to stand, shorn of all special distinctions, by the side of those of my countrymen who, for their so-called insignificance, are liable to suffer degradation not fit for human beings."* Simultaneously, **Sir Sankaran Nair** resigned from the Viceroy's Executive Council in protest against the continuation of Martial Law.

*   **The Official *Disorders Inquiry Committee* (Hunter Committee, Oct 1919 – May 1920):** Pressured by nationalist outrage, Secretary of State Montagu ordered the formation of the **Disorders Inquiry Committee** on October 14, 1919, chaired by **Lord William Hunter** (former Solicitor-General of Scotland), comprising **4 British members** (Lord Hunter, Justice G.C. Rankin, W.F. Rice, Major-General Sir George Barrow) and **3 Indian members** (**Sir Chimanlal Harilal Setalvad**, **Pandit Jagat Narayan**, and **Sardar Sahibzada Sultan Ahmad Khan**). Prior to the Committee's hearings, the Government of India hurriedly passed an **Indemnity Act** ("Whitewashing Bill") that legally immunized all military and civil officers—including Dyer and O’Dwyer—from any criminal prosecution or financial liability for actions taken "in good faith" during the Punjab disturbances.
*   **Dyer’s Testimony and the Racial Split in the Hunter Report (May 1920):** Testifying before the Hunter Committee in November 1919, **General Dyer** brazenly admitted that he could have dispersed the Jallianwala Bagh crowd without firing, but stated he had decided before reaching the spot to fire immediately because his objective was not merely to disperse the meeting, but to *"produce a sufficient moral effect, from a military point of view, not only on those who were present, but more especially throughout the Punjab"*—to strike terror into the entire province. If the narrow passage had permitted the armored cars to enter, Dyer admitted, he would have opened fire with the machine guns. Despite this confession of premeditated state terrorism, the **Hunter Committee Report (March/May 1920)** split along racial lines:
    1.  The **Majority Report** (signed by the 4 British members) mildly censured Dyer's prolonged firing and the crawling order as a *"grave error of judgment"* and a *"mistaken conception of duty,"* while fully justifying the imposition of Martial Law in Punjab and exonerating **Michael O’Dwyer** and Viceroy **Chelmsford**.
    2.  The **Minority Report** (signed by the 3 Indian members—Setalvad, Jagat Narayan, and Sultan Ahmad) condemned the martial law administration as wholly unjustified, branded Dyer's actions as inhuman and un-British, and compared them to German wartime atrocities in Belgium.
    3.  Although the British Cabinet and Winston Churchill ("monstrous") forced Dyer to resign his commission on half-pay without facing a court-martial, the **British House of Lords** passed a resolution praising Dyer as the "Saviour of the Punjab," and the conservative London newspaper ***The Morning Post*** raised a public subscription of **£26,000** along with a jeweled "Sword of Honour" for him (to which **Rudyard Kipling** contributed, and the Managing Committee of the **Golden Temple** under the loyalist Sarbrah **Arur Singh** had earlier dishonored the Sikh faith by presenting Dyer with a *Siropa*).
*   **The Counter-Archive — The Congress Punjab Sub-Committee Inquiry Report (March 1920):** Having boycotted the Hunter Committee after the Punjab government refused to allow imprisoned Punjab leaders (**Dr. Kitchlew**, **Dr. Satyapal**, **Lala Harkishen Lal**) to attend the hearings freely, the Indian National Congress constituted its own independent **Punjab Sub-Committee** comprising **M.K. Gandhi**, **Motilal Nehru**, **Chittaranjan Das**, **Abbas Tyabji**, and **M.R. Jayakar** (with **Santanam** as secretary). Interviewing over **1,700 eyewitnesses** and publishing **650 verified depositions** (drafted entirely by **Gandhi** and published on **March 25, 1920**), the **Congress Inquiry Report** exposed the systemic collusion between **O’Dwyer**, **Chelmsford**, and **Dyer**, characterized the Jallianwala Bagh massacre as *"a calculated piece of inhumanity towards utterly innocent and unarmed men, including children, and unparalleled for its ferocity in the history of modern British administration,"* and demanded the immediate recall of Viceroy Chelmsford and the prosecution of O’Dwyer and Dyer.

## III. Pan-Islamic Geopolitics, the Nagpur Organizational Revolution, and the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Apparatus (1919–1922)

### 6. The Geopolitics of the Ottoman Dismemberment and the Khilafat Counter-Mobilization (1919–1920)

The immediate catalyst that fused the post-Jallianwala nationalist outrage with pan-Indian Muslim mass mobilization was the geopolitical dismemberment of the **Ottoman Empire** following the First World War. Throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Indian Muslims—deprived of sovereign political power since 1857—increasingly viewed the **Ottoman Sultan** in Constantinople as the **Khalifa (Caliph)**, the spiritual and temporal head of the global Sunni Islamic community (*Ummah*) and the custodian of the sacred sanctuaries of Islam. During World War I, when Ottoman Turkey allied with Germany against Britain, British Prime Minister **David Lloyd George** (on January 5, 1918) and Viceroy Hardinge had given solemn public assurances to secure the loyalty of Indian Muslim soldiers (who formed a massive contingent of the British Mesopotamian and Palestinian expeditionary forces) that the Allied powers had no intention of stripping Turkey of its Asia Minor and Thracian heartlands or violating the sovereignty of the Khalifa over the Islamic holy places.

#### The Treaty of Sèvres (August 1920) and the All-India Khilafat Committee

Once the November 1918 Armistice was signed, British and French imperialism betrayed every wartime pledge, occupying Constantinople, partitioning the Arab provinces into British and French League of Nations **Mandates** (Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon), and backing Greek territorial annexations in Smyrna and Thrace. This imperialist carving culminated in the humiliating **Treaty of Sèvres (August 10, 1920)**, which reduced Ottoman Turkey to a rump Anatolian state under Allied financial and military control, severed Arab lands, and reduced the **Khalifa** to a virtual prisoner of the Allied High Commissioners.

*   **The Three Core Khilafat Demands:** Indian Muslim opinion, galvanized earlier by the pan-Islamic journalism of **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** (*Al-Hilal* and *Al-Balagh* in Calcutta), **Maulana Mohammad Ali** (*Comrade* in English and *Hamdard* in Urdu), and **Zafar Ali Khan** (*Zamindar* in Lahore), crystallized around three non-negotiable religious-geopolitical demands:
    1.  The **Khalifa** must retain absolute control and guardianship over the Muslim sacred places (**Mecca**, **Medina**, and **Jerusalem**).
    2.  The **Jazirat-ul-Arab** (the sacred Islamic peninsula encompassing Arabia, Syria, Iraq, and Palestine, as defined by the Prophet's dying injunction that it must never fall under non-Muslim sovereignty) must remain under exclusive Muslim political sovereignty, free from British or French mandates.
    3.  The Khalifa must be left with sufficient territorial and military sovereignty to enable him to defend the Islamic faith effectively.
*   **Institutionalization of the All-India Khilafat Committee (1919–1920):** In early **1919**, a **Khilafat Committee** was formed in Bombay, followed by the constitution of the **All-India Khilafat Committee** in **September 1919**, uniting two distinct socio-political streams of Muslim leadership: the radical Western-educated journalist-activists and the traditional **Ulama** of **Firangi Mahal** (Lucknow, led by **Maulana Abdul Bari**) and **Deoband** (which founded the **Jamiat-ul-Ulama-i-Hind** in November 1919). The central leadership comprised the **Ali Brothers** (**Maulana Mohammad Ali** and **Maulana Shaukat Ali**, released from wartime internment in December 1919), **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan** (the revered Unani physician of Delhi who renounced his *Haziq-ul-Mulk* title), **Maulana Hasrat Mohani** (the radical Urdu poet-revolutionary of Aligarh), **Dr. Mukhtar Ahmad Ansari**, and **Seth Chotani** of Bombay.
*   **Gandhi’s Strategic Intervention and the June 1920 Allahabad Launch:** Observing **October 17, 1919**, as an all-India **Khilafat Day** with hartals and prayers, the movement convened the historic **All-India Khilafat Conference at Delhi (November 23–24, 1919)**, which elected **M.K. Gandhi** as its President. At this Delhi conference, while **Hasrat Mohani** demanded an immediate boycott of British goods, Gandhi first proposed the strategy of **Non-Violent Non-Cooperation** (withholding all cooperation from the government) if the Turkish peace terms violated the Khilafat demands, viewing the Khilafat crisis as *"an opportunity of uniting Hindus and Mohammedans as would not arise in a hundred years."* When the draft terms of the **Treaty of Sèvres** were published in May 1920 alongside the whitewashing **Hunter Committee Report** on the Punjab massacre, the **Central Khilafat Committee met at Allahabad (June 1–3, 1920)** and formally adopted Gandhi’s **four-stage Non-Cooperation program**:
    1.  *Stage I:* Renunciation of all British honorary titles, medals, and offices;
    2.  *Stage II:* Resignation from civil services under the government;
    3.  *Stage III:* Resignation from the police and military forces;
    4.  *Stage IV:* Non-payment of taxes (Civil Disobedience).
    Without waiting for the formal endorsement of the Indian National Congress, the Khilafat Committee and Gandhi officially launched the **Non-Cooperation Movement** on **August 1, 1920**—the exact morning **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** passed away in Bombay, transforming his funeral procession into the first massive national demonstration of the new era—with Gandhi returning his **Kaisar-i-Hind gold medal**, **Zulu War medal**, and **Boer War medal** to Viceroy Chelmsford, explicitly citing both the **Khilafat betrayal** and the **Punjab atrocities**.

### 7. The 1920 Constitutional Capture: From the Calcutta Special Session to the Nagpur Structural Overhaul

Because **M.K. Gandhi** had already launched the Non-Cooperation Movement on August 1, 1920, through the institutional machinery of the **Central Khilafat Committee** and his personal **Satyagraha** networks, the veteran leadership of the **Indian National Congress** was presented with a *fait accompli*. Between September and December 1920, an intense ideological and tactical struggle unfolded within the INC between the old-guard political establishment—who wished to contest the November 1920 elections under the **Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms** to obstruct the government from within the legislative councils—and the Gandhian-Khilafat coalition, which demanded an immediate, total boycott of the councils, courts, and schools.

#### The September 1920 Calcutta Special Session vs. the December 1920 Nagpur Session

*   **The Calcutta Special Session (September 4–9, 1920):** Convened under the presidency of **Lala Lajpat Rai** (who had just returned from exile in the United States and was personally skeptical of mixing pan-Islamic religious grievances with secular nationalism and pulling students out of schools), the Calcutta Special Session witnessed fierce opposition to Gandhi’s Non-Cooperation resolution. A formidable coalition of veteran nationalist stalwarts—including **Chittaranjan Das (C.R. Das)** and **Bipin Chandra Pal** from Bengal, **Annie Besant**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, and **G.S. Khaparde**—argued that boycotting the new Montagu-Chelmsford Legislative Councils would merely surrender institutional power to loyalist moderates and that mass extra-constitutional agitation would degenerate into chaos. However, backed by **Motilal Nehru** (the only senior frontline Congress leader who immediately crossed over to support Gandhi at Calcutta), the **Ali Brothers**, thousands of Khilafat delegates, and new provincial delegates from Gujarat, Bihar, and the United Provinces, Gandhi carried the Non-Cooperation resolution by **1,886 votes to 884**. The Calcutta resolution added **Swaraj** (Self-Government) alongside redressing the **Punjab** and **Khilafat** wrongs as the triple objective of the movement, promising **"Swaraj within One Year"** if the program was fully executed.
*   **The Total Council Boycott (November 1920) and the Nagpur Session (December 1920):** Following the Calcutta mandate, virtually every Congress and Khilafat candidate withdrew their nominations from the **November 1920 Legislative Council elections**, and vast swathes of the electorate boycotted the polls entirely (turnout dropped below 5–8% in major urban centers like Bombay and Allahabad). Recognizing the undeniable mass wave sweeping the countryside, **C.R. Das**—who had arrived at the **December 1920 Nagpur Congress Session** (presided over by **C. Vijayaraghavachariar**) with a large contingent of Bengal delegates intending to overturn the council-boycott clause—executed a historic strategic pivot: after intense negotiations with Gandhi, **C.R. Das himself moved the main Non-Cooperation Resolution**, seconded by **Lala Lajpat Rai**. Only **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, speaking dynamically against unconstitutional mass agitation and the mingling of religious fanaticism with politics, stood his ground in opposition before permanently leaving the Congress (alongside **Annie Besant**, **G.S. Khaparde**, and **Bipin Chandra Pal**).

#### The Nagpur Organizational Blueprint: Mass Party Financing, Linguistic Federalism, and Executive Centralization

Beyond endorsing Non-Cooperation unanimously, the **December 1920 Nagpur Session** executed a revolutionary constitutional overhaul of the **Indian National Congress** (drafted personally by **Gandhi** with **N.C. Kelkar** and **I.B. Sen**). Prior to December 1920, the INC had functioned for thirty-five years as a loose, seasonal Christmas-week deliberative assembly of Westernized urban lawyers and journalists without a permanent year-round executive, grassroots village branches, or steady mass financing. The **1920 Nagpur Constitution** transformed the Congress into a permanent, vertically integrated, mass-cadre political party:

1.  **Redefinition of the Congress Creed (Article I):** Under the old post-Surat (1908) Constitution, the Congress creed mandated the attainment of Colonial Self-Government within the Empire by strictly *"constitutional means."* At Nagpur, the creed was radically altered to: **"The attainment of Swarajya by the people of India by all peaceful and legitimate means."** This single semantic shift severed the mandatory link to British constitutional legality while locking in non-violence (**Ahimsa**), allowing both Dominion Status advocates and complete independence radicals to operate under one roof.
2.  **Creation of the 15-Member Congress Working Committee (CWC):** For the first time, the Congress established a compact, permanent, year-round national executive cabinet—the **15-member Congress Working Committee (CWC)**—empowered to direct daily political campaigns, enforce party discipline, allocate funds, and make emergency tactical decisions between sessions, while expanding the deliberative **All-India Congress Committee (AICC)** to **350 members** elected proportionally on a population basis.
3.  **Linguistic Reorganization of Provincial Congress Committees (PCCs):** Dismantling the arbitrary administrative boundaries of the colonial Presidencies (such as the sprawling multi-lingual Madras and Bombay Presidencies), the Nagpur Constitution reorganized the country into **21 Provincial Congress Committees (PCCs)** drawn strictly along **vernacular linguistic boundaries** (e.g., creating separate **Andhra**, **Tamil Nadu**, **Kerala**, **Karnataka**, **Maharashtra**, **Gujarat**, **Sindh**, and **Utkal** PCCs). Simultaneously, **Hindustani** was adopted as the common working language for all-India deliberations, and provincial committees were mandated to conduct all internal proceedings in the local vernacular—instantly breaking the monopoly of English-educated elites and enabling peasant and artisan participation.
4.  **Four-Tier Grassroots Hierarchy and the 4-Anna Membership Franchise:** Beneath the AICC and Linguistic PCCs, the party built a pyramidal network of **District**, **Taluka/Tahsil**, **Town**, and **Village/Mohalla Congress Committees**. To democratize entry for impoverished peasants and workers, the annual Congress membership fee was slashed to **4 annas (25 paise, or one-fourth of a rupee)** for anyone aged 21 or above who accepted the Congress creed—boosting paid Congress membership to over **5 million members** by mid-1921.
5.  **Mass Party Financing — The *Tilak Swaraj Fund* (1921):** At the March 1921 Bezwada (Vijayawada) AICC meeting, the Congress resolved to raise **Rs 1 Crore (10 million rupees)** for the **Tilak Swaraj Fund** (alongside enrolling 1 crore members and introducing 20 lakh **Charkhas** [spinning wheels]) before June 30, 1921. Through a combination of Gujarati/Marwari mercantile donations (**Jamnalal Bajaj**, **G.D. Birla**, **Ambalal Sarabhai**) and millions of small peasant and women’s contributions (including women donating their gold and silver wedding ornaments), the fund exceeded its target, collecting over **Rs 1.15 Crore** within three months, giving the Congress an autonomous fiscal treasury capable of paying full-time political cadres and sustaining national schools.

### 8. The Political Economy of Boycott and Swadeshi Institution-Building (1920–1922)

The operational architecture of the **Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Movement (1920–1922)** combined a dual strategy: a **Negative (Destructive) Boycott Program** aimed at paralyzing the fiscal, judicial, pedagogical, and political circuits of the colonial state, and a **Positive (Constructive) Swadeshi Program** designed to build autonomous parallel institutions inside civil society.

#### Destabilizing Colonial Revenue, Judicial, and Mercantile Circuits

*   **The Economic Boycott of Foreign Cloth and the *Charkha* Counter-Economy:** Recognizing that the import of Lancashire machine-manufactured cotton textiles was the primary commercial artery binding British industrial capitalism to the Indian colonial market—and the direct cause of rural de-industrialization and peasant underemployment—Gandhi made the **boycott and public burning of foreign cloth** paired with the universal adoption of hand-spun, hand-woven **Khadi (Khaddar)** via the **Charkha** the material centerpiece of the movement. Massive public bonfires of foreign textiles lit up Bombay (at Elphinstone Mill grounds on July 31, 1921), Calcutta, and Madras, which Gandhi defended against **Rabindranath Tagore’s** aesthetic and economic reservations by arguing that burning foreign cloth transferred the stigma of colonial dependence from the bodies of the poor onto the imported commodity itself. Picketing of foreign cloth shops—led heavily by urban women volunteers (**Basanti Devi**, wife of C.R. Das; **Urmila Devi**; **Suniti Devi**; **Sarojini Naidu**; **Bi Amman**, mother of the Ali Brothers)—combined with pledges by Marwari and Gujarati cloth merchants to halt foreign indent orders for a year, inflicted a devastating blow on British trade: **the value of foreign cloth imports into India plummeted by nearly 50%, falling from Rs 102 Crore in 1920–21 to Rs 57 Crore in 1921–22**, while the quantity imported dropped from `1,292 million yards` to `550 million yards`.
*   **Picketing of Liquor/Toddy Shops and Excise Revenue Collapse:** Simultaneously, Congress and Khilafat volunteers (though not part of the original four-stage blueprint) launched intensive picketing of government-licensed liquor, toddy, and opium shops. Because **Abkari (Excise) duties** constituted one of the largest revenue streams assigned to the **Transferred Departments** of the Provincial Governments under the 1919 Meston Settlement, the collapse of liquor sales created an immediate fiscal crisis for the provincial administrations in Punjab, Madras, Bihar, and Bombay, forcing British Governors to dip into reserves while exposing the hypocrisy of a regime that funded Indian education through working-class intoxication.
*   **Boycott of Colonial Law Courts, Titles, and the Prince of Wales Visit (Nov 1921):** Across India, prominent barristers and vakils abandoned their lucrative legal practices in colonial courts to become full-time political organizers: **Motilal Nehru**, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Chittaranjan Das** (whose practice yielded Rs 50,000 a month), **Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Vithalbhai Patel**, **Rajendra Prasad**, **C. Rajagopalachari**, **Saifuddin Kitchlew**, **Asaf Ali**, **T. Prakasam**, and **Lala Lajpat Rai**. In parallel, thousands of indigenous **Panchayats (Arbitration Courts)** were established in villages and towns across Bihar, Bengal, and UP to adjudicate civil and petty criminal disputes outside the colonial judiciary, depriving the state of stamp-duty revenue. While the **July 1921 Karachi All-India Khilafat Conference** passed a bold resolution (moved by **Maulana Mohammad Ali**) declaring it *Haram* (religiously unlawful) for any Muslim to continue serving in the **British Indian Army**—for which the Ali Brothers and five others were arrested and sentenced to two years' rigorous imprisonment in September 1921, prompting Gandhi and the CWC to replicate the exact seditious resolution at hundreds of public meetings across India on October 4—the movement reached its urban crescendo on **November 17, 1921**. When the **Prince of Wales (Edward)** landed in Bombay to inaugurate the new constitutional councils and rally imperial loyalty, he was greeted by an absolute all-India *Hartal* and deserted, ghostly streets across Bombay, Calcutta, and Allahabad, although four days of violent clashes broke out in Bombay against Parsi, Christian, and Anglo-Indian residents who had attended the royal welcome, causing Gandhi to undertake a five-day penitential fast.

#### Counter-Hegemonic Pedagogy: National Universities and Vernacular Print Networks

The educational boycott of government-owned and government-aided schools and colleges—spearheaded by **Lala Lajpat Rai** in Punjab, **C.R. Das** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** (who resigned from the prestigious Indian Civil Service in April 1921 to become Principal of the **National College** in Calcutta) in Bengal, and **Maulana Mohammad Ali** and **Maulana Azad**—saw over **90,000 students** walk out of colonial classrooms during 1920–1921 (with the highest withdrawals recorded in Bengal, Punjab, and Bombay).

*   **The Network of National Vidyapiths and *Jamia Millia Islamia* (1920–1921):** To absorb these boycotting students and construct an anti-colonial curriculum rooted in vernacular instruction, Indian history, vocational spinning, and civic service, nationalists founded a chain of autonomous **National Universities**:
    1.  ***Jamia Millia Islamia*** (National Muslim University), founded on **October 29, 1920**, at **Aligarh** (and relocated to **Karol Bagh, Delhi** in 1925) after **Maulana Mohammad Ali**, **Shaukat Ali**, **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan** (its first Chancellor), **Maulana Mahmud Hasan** (Shaykh-ul-Hind of Deoband), and young student-scholars like **Zakir Husain** broke away from the pro-British administration of the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College when its trustees refused to renounce colonial financial grants.
    2.  ***Gujarat Vidyapith*** at Ahmedabad (founded by **M.K. Gandhi** in October 1920).
    3.  ***Bihar Vidyapith*** at Patna (founded in February 1921 under **Mazharul Haque** as Chancellor and **Rajendra Prasad** as Principal, housed at the **Sadaqat Ashram**).
    4.  ***Kashi Vidyapith*** at Banaras (founded in February 1921 through the philanthropy of **Shiv Prasad Gupta** and the scholarship of **Bhagwan Das** and **Acharya Narendra Deva**).
    5.  ***Tilak Maharashtra Vidyapith*** at Poona, ***Bengal National University*** at Calcutta, and ***National Muslim University*** networks across UP and Punjab, alongside over **800 national schools**.
*   **Vernacular Print Capitalism and Nationalist Aesthetic Mobilization:** This institutional network was sustained by an explosion of nationalist print journalism that bypassed the colonial wire services: Gandhi assumed direct editorship of ***Young India*** (in English) and ***Navajivan*** (in Gujarati/Hindi, published from Ahmedabad without carrying commercial advertisements), **Motilal Nehru** founded ***The Independent*** in Allahabad (1919), **Ganesh Shankar Vidyarthi** edited the militant Hindi daily ***Pratap*** from Kanpur, **T. Prakasam** launched ***Swarajya*** in Madras (1921), and **C.R. Das** launched ***Banglar Katha*** in Calcutta. Simultaneously, nationalist poet-composers—such as **Subramania Bharati** in Tamil Nadu, **Garimella Satyanarayana** (*Maakoddee Telladoratanamu* — "We do not want this White Rule") in Andhra, and **Premchand** (who resigned his government school-inspectorship in Gorakhpur in February 1921 after hearing Gandhi speak and published novels like *Premashram* and stories in *Soz-e-Watan*)—infused subaltern oral culture with anti-imperial consciousness.

## IV. Subaltern Autonomies, Agrarian-Tribal Insurgencies, and the Chauri Chaura Strategic Retreat (1921–1924)

### 9. Peasant No-Tax Campaigns, Akali Agrarian-Religious Mobilization, and Working-Class Strikes

While the Congress High Command under **M.K. Gandhi** sought to manage the Non-Cooperation Movement as a tightly disciplined, cross-class moral confrontation—deliberately excluding no-rent campaigns against Indian **Zamindars** and **Taluqdars** so as not to fracture the anti-imperial united front—the movement at the grassroots rapidly acquired an autonomous subaltern momentum. Across the linguistic provinces, peasants, tribals, plantation laborers, and industrial workers translated the symbol of **"Gandhi Maharaj"** and **Swaraj** into their own regional moral economies, targeting the immediate triad of colonial revenue collectors (**Sarkar**), feudal landlords (**Zamindar/Jenmi**), and usurious moneylenders (**Sahukar/Diku**).

#### Andhra No-Tax Experiments: *Chirala-Perala* and *Pedanandipadu*

In the **Coastal Andhra** districts (Guntur, Krishna, and Godavari), local Congress committees pushed the movement directly into the fourth stage of Non-Cooperation—mass tax refusal and civil disobedience:

*   **The *Chirala-Perala* Municipal Boycott and Spatial Exodus (1921–1922):** In the twin weaving and commercial villages of **Chirala and Perala** (Guntur district, population ~15,000, consisting predominantly of **Devanga** and **Padmasali** handloom weavers and **Kamma/Telaga** cultivators), the Madras Government forcibly amalgamated the two villages into a Municipality in 1919, escalating the annual local tax burden from **Rs 4,000** (under the union panchayat) to **Rs 40,000** without providing sanitation or water facilities. Led by the charismatic Cambridge-educated nationalist **Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya** (styled *Andhra Ratna*), who organized a disciplined, red-shirted volunteer corps of over 1,000 youths named the ***Ramadandu*** (Rama's Army), the residents initially refused to pay the municipal tax and forced the municipal councillors to resign. Following a visit by **M.K. Gandhi** in April 1921—who suggested either non-violent tax refusal in place or collective spatial evacuation beyond the municipal boundary—**Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya** executed an extraordinary spatial operation: in **April–May 1921**, over **13,500 out of the 15,000 inhabitants** evacuated their ancestral homes inside Chirala-Perala entirely, abandoning the colonial municipality as an empty ghost town, and constructed a parallel, self-governing thatched-hut township named **Ramnagar** just outside the municipal limits. For **eleven full months**, resisting monsoon floods, scorching heat, and the imprisonment of Gopalakrishnayya (arrested at Berhampore in September 1921), the residents operated an autonomous parallel administration, court, and treasury in Ramnagar until the movement was called off in early 1922.
*   **The *Pedanandipadu* No-Tax Campaign in Guntur (January–February 1922):** Simultaneously, across nearly **100 villages** of the **Pedanandipadu Firka** (Bapatla taluka, Guntur district), the prosperous and middle-tier **Kamma** and **Reddi** peasant proprietors launched one of the most formidable rural no-revenue campaigns in British Indian history under the leadership of **Parvataneni Veeraiah Chowdary** (celebrated as the "Andhra Shivaji"), supported by his **Shanti Sena** volunteer corps. Crucially, the movement paralyzed the colonial revenue collection grid from within: the hereditary village revenue accountants (**Karanams**) and village headmen (**Munsiffs**) resigned their government posts en masse. When the British Collector deployed armored cars, punitive police, and the Malabar Special Police to attach crops and auction peasant cattle and lands, not a single bidder stepped forward to purchase the distrained property, nor would any villager supply food or carts to visiting colonial officials, holding Land Revenue collections down to **less than 5%** until Gandhi himself pressured the Andhra PCC (under **Konda Venkatappayya**) to suspend the no-tax campaign in February 1922.

#### United Provinces Kisans, Assam Tea Coolie Exodus, and Malabar/Punjab Intersections

*   **United Provinces (*Kisan Sabha* and *Eka* Movements) and Bengal (*Midnapore* Anti-Union Boards):** In the **Awadh** region of the United Provinces (Pratapgarh, Rae Bareli, Sultanpur, Faizabad), where arbitrary Taluqdari ejectments (*Bedakhli*), rack-renting, and illegal cesses (*Nazrana* and *Hari-Begari*) had sparked the **Oudh Kisan Sabha** (Oct 1920) under **Baba Ramchandra** and **Jawaharlal Nehru**, peasant militancy spilled over in January 1921 into bazaar lootings of grain merchants and Taluqdari granaries in Rae Bareli (Fursatganj and Munshiganj firing), and later re-emerged in late 1921 in Hardoi, Bahraich, and Sitapur as the **Eka (Unity) Movement** led by the low-caste leader **Madari Pasi** (where peasants took ritual oaths at river steps to pay only the recorded rent and reject forced labor). In **Bengal**, while urban jute-mill workers launched 137 strikes in 1921, peasants in the Contai and Tamluk subdivisions of **Midnapore district**, led by the dynamic barrister **Birendranath Sasmal**, waged a victorious no-tax campaign against the newly introduced **Village Self-Government Act (1919)** and its **Union Boards** (which increased village chaukidari taxes sevenfold), refusing to pay the tax, preventing the auction of attached utensils, and forcing the Bengal government to formally withdraw the Union Boards from Midnapore in December 1921.
*   **The Assam Tea Plantation Exodus and the *Assam-Bengal Railway Strike* (May 1921):** In the tea gardens of **Assam** (particularly the **Chargola Valley** in Sylhet/Cachar), where indentured Adivasi and UP/Bihar laborers worked under semi-slave conditions on starvation wages (3–4 annas a day) imposed by European tea companies, rumors spread in **May 1921** that "Gandhi Raj" had arrived and that Gandhi had ordered them to abandon the gardens and return to their native villages where land awaited them. Defying plantation guards, over **8,000 tea coolies** walked out of the Chargola gardens chanting *"Gandhi Maharaj Ki Jai."* When the District Magistrate and European planters stopped their repatriation at the **Chandpur** steamer ghat and unleashed Armed Gurkha Military Police in a brutal night assault on sleeping women and children at the Chandpur railway station on May 19–20, 1921, the outrage sparked a massive, two-month general sympathetic strike by the workers of the **Assam-Bengal Railway** and the **River Steam Navigation Company**, organized brilliantly by **Jatindra Mohan Sengupta** (assisted by **C.R. Das**), paralyzing the entire transport network of eastern Bengal and Assam.
*   **Intersection with the *Akali* (Gurdwara Reform) Movement in Punjab (1920–1925):** In central Punjab, the Non-Cooperation Movement intersected seamlessly with the **Akali Movement** (launched in late 1920 with the formation of the **Shiromani Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee [SGPC]** and the **Shiromani Akali Dal**). Aiming to liberate the historic Sikh Gurdwaras and their vast agricultural endowments from corrupt, hereditary **Udasi Mahants** who collaborated directly with the British administration (such as the Golden Temple manager who had honored General Dyer), the Akali *Jathas* adopted strict Gandhian **non-violent Satyagraha**. Even when the Mahant of **Nankana Sahib** (Narain Das) hired armed mercenaries to massacre **over 130 unarmed Akali Satyagrahis** inside the shrine on **February 20, 1921**, and when British police brutally beat non-violent Akali volunteers daily at the **Guru-ka-Bagh Morcha (1922)**, the Jat Sikh peasantry maintained absolute non-violent discipline. Moreover, during the **Keys Affair (*Chabian da Morcha*, Nov 1921 – Jan 1922)**—when the Deputy Commissioner of Amritsar seized the keys of the **Golden Temple Toshakhana** (treasury)—the Akalis and the Punjab Congress under **Baba Kharak Singh** launched a joint protest that forced the British government to surrender the keys unconditionally, prompting Gandhi to wire Baba Kharak Singh: ***"First decisive battle for India's freedom won. Congratulations."***

### 10. When Subaltern Moral Economies Ruptured Gandhian Restraint: Mappila, Rampa, and Tana Bhagat Movements

While regions like Kheda, Pedanandipadu, Midnapore, and the Akali shrines adhered to non-violent discipline, in other ecological and socio-economic zones—specifically the rack-rented agrarian tenancies of **South Malabar** and the exploited forest interiors of **Chotanagpur** and the **Eastern Ghats**—subaltern mobilization crossed the boundaries of Gandhian *Ahimsa*, erupting into armed peasant and tribal insurrections.

#### The Mappila (Moplah) Rebellion in Malabar (August 1921 – Early 1922)

In the **Ernad** and **Walluvanad** taluks of **South Malabar** (Madras Presidency), the agrarian structure was characterized by acute class and religious polarization. Under the colonial legal recognition of absolute landlord ownership, the land was monopolized by upper-caste Hindu **Jenmis** (predominantly **Namboodiri Brahmins** and **Nair** chieftains), who served as the loyalist local pillars of British administration and magistracy, while the actual cultivators, sub-tenants (**Verumpattamdars**), and agricultural laborers were predominantly impoverished Muslim **Mappilas (Moplahs)** alongside lower-caste Hindu **Tiyyas** and **Cherumans**. Throughout the nineteenth century, arbitrary evictions (*Melcharth*), exorbitant renewal fees (*Polichezhuthu*), and illegal feudal exactions had triggered over thirty localized Mappila agrarian outbreaks (*Hal Ilakkam*).

*   **From Khilafat-Tenancy Mobilization to Armed Insurrection (August 1921):** Between 1916 and early 1921, the establishment of **Kudiyan (Tenant) Sanghams** (led initially by **M.P. Narayana Menon** and **K.P. Kesava Menon**) merged with the **Khilafat** and **Non-Cooperation** committees across Malabar, reinforced by visits from **M.K. Gandhi** and **Shaukat Ali** in August 1920 and **Yakub Hasan** in February 1921. Alarmed by the massive mobilization of the Mappila peasantry, the British District Magistrate E.F. Thomas imposed prohibitions under Section 144 CrPC, arresting prominent secular Congress-Khilafat leaders who could have maintained non-violent restraint. On **August 20, 1921**, British troops and police surrounded the sacred **Tirurangadi Mosque** before dawn to arrest the revered Khilafat priest **Ali Musaliar**; rumors quickly spread across Ernad that the British army had desecrated and destroyed the historic **Mamburam Mosque**.
*   **The Parallel Khilafat Polities vs. Communal Degeneration and the *Podanur Wagon Tragedy*:** Within forty-eight hours, tens of thousands of Mappila peasants, armed with agricultural knives, swords, and homemade muskets, rose in open insurrection across Ernad and Walluvanad. In its initial phase, the rebellion targeted the infrastructural symbols of the colonial state and feudal landlordism: rebels tore up railway tracks, cut telegraph wires, felled trees to block military roads, burned down police stations, courts, and sub-registrar offices, and stormed the manor houses (*Illams* and *Kovilakams*) of oppressive **Jenmis** to burn debt bonds and land mortgage deeds, while executing **Khan Bahadur Chekkutty**, a retired Muslim police inspector who aided the British. Leaders such as **Variyamkunnath Kunjahammed Haji** (who proclaimed an independent Khilafat Kingdom, issued passports, abolished Jenmi rents for a year, and punished looters who harmed innocent Hindus), **Ali Musaliar** (proclaimed king at Tirurangadi), and **Seethi Koya Thangal** attempted to run parallel anti-imperial administrations. However, as the British deployed the **Dorset Regiment**, **Gurkha Rifles**, and the **Malabar Special Police** under full **Martial Law**—and as many Hindu **Jenmis** actively provided intelligence to the British troops—sections of the besieged Mappila insurgents turned their fury against the Hindu population, resulting in forced religious conversions, looting of Hindu homes, and communal killings that devastated Hindu-Muslim unity across India. British counter-insurgency was equally savage: by early 1922, colonial forces had killed **2,337 rebels**, wounded **1,652**, and taken **over 45,000 prisoners**. On **November 19, 1921**, in the infamous **Podanur Wagon Tragedy** (often termed the "Black Hole of Podanur"), British military authorities packed **100 Mappila prisoners** into an airless, hermetically sealed iron railway goods luggage van (`Goods Wagon No. MSM Lv 1711`) from Tirur to Coimbatore; when the doors were opened at **Podanur station**, **67 prisoners had died of horrific asphyxiation**, and several more died in hospital.

#### Adivasi Millenarianism and Forest Guerrilla Warfare: *Tana Bhagats* and Alluri Sitarama Raju’s *Rampa Rebellion* (1922–1924)

Across the forested Adivasi belts of Bihar/Jharkhand and the Eastern Ghats of Andhra, tribal communities synthesized Gandhian ideology with their own pre-existing traditions of anti-colonial forest resistance:

*   **The *Tana Bhagat* Movement in Chotanagpur (1914–1922):** Originating in **April 1914** among the **Oraon** Adivasis of the Ranchi district (Chotanagpur plateau) under the messianic leadership of a young Oraon prophet, **Jatra Bhagat** (and later **Sibhu Bhagat**, **Balram Bhagat**, and the woman leader **Devamaniya**), the **Tana Bhagat** movement ("Tana" meaning to "pull out" evil spirits and oppressive structures) began as a radical socio-religious purification and anti-feudal campaign. The Tana Bhagats rejected traditional animal sacrifices, ghost-worship, and liquor distillation, adopted strict vegetarianism and austerity, refused to perform **Beth Begari** (forced unpaid labor) for Hindu/Muslim **Zamindars (Dikus)**, and stopped paying land rent (*Chaukidari* and *Malguzari*), asserting that God (**Dharmesh**) alone owned the land which their ancestors had cleared. By **1920–1921**, finding a profound resonance between their own ascetic code and **M.K. Gandhi’s** teachings on **Ahimsa**, temperance, and spinning, the **Tana Bhagats** merged wholesale into the **Non-Cooperation Movement**. Adopting the **Charkha** and wearing only hand-spun **Khadi**, hundreds of Oraon Tana Bhagats walked miles to attend the **December 1920 Nagpur** and **December 1922 Gaya Congress Sessions**, courted arrest by picketing liquor shops, and allowed their ancestral lands to be auctioned by the colonial state rather than pay a single paisa of land tax—becoming the most committed non-violent Adivasi cohort within the Congress.
*   **Alluri Sitarama Raju and the *Rampa / Gudem Hills Rebellion* (August 1922 – May 1924):** In sharp tactical contrast to the non-violent Oraon Tana Bhagats, the **Koya** and **Konda Dora** Adivasis of the **Gudem and Rampa Hills** (Godavari Agency tract, Eastern Ghats of Andhra) waged a fierce two-year armed guerrilla war led by **Alluri Sitarama Raju**—a high-caste (**Kshatriya**) ascetic sanyasi, astrologer, and herbalist from West Godavari who had settled among the Koyas around 1918. The material triggers of the uprising were rooted in colonial ecological expropriation and bureaucratic coercion:
    1.  The enforcement of the **Madras Forest Act of 1882**, which reserved the forest commons, criminalized the Koyas' traditional **Podu** (slash-and-burn shifting cultivation), and barred them from collecting minor forest produce or grazing cattle without paying heavy fees to corrupt forest guards.
    2.  The tyrannical conduct of the British Special Tahsildar of Gudem, **Sebastian (Bastian)**, who forced starving Adivasis to construct forest roads (designed for military and timber extraction) via unpaid **Vetti** (forced labor) while withholding their wages and abusing village headmen (**Muttadars**), such as **Gam Mallu Dora** and **Gam Gantam Dora** (whose hereditary Muttadari lands had been stripped).
*   **Gandhian Rhetoric Fused with Armed Guerrilla Tactics:** Initially inspired by the Non-Cooperation Movement during 1921, **Alluri Sitarama Raju** preached total abstinence from liquor and toddy, urged the Koyas to wear **Khadi**, and established indigenous **Panchayat** courts to boycott colonial courts. However, deeply convinced that British imperialism could never be expelled from the forests without armed force, Raju launched the **Rampa Rebellion** on **August 22, 1922**, with a daring lightning raid on the **Chintapalli Police Station**, followed rapidly by raids on the **Krishnadevipeta** and **Rajavommangi** police stations. Operating with extraordinary chivalry and discipline alongside his Koya commanders—the brothers **Gam Mallu Dora** and **Gam Gantam Dora**, and **Aggiraju**—Raju’s guerrillas seized Lee-Enfield rifles, bayonets, and thousands of rounds of ammunition, left signed inventories of the exact weapons taken in the station registers, and released captured Indian policemen unharmed, declaring war exclusively against British officers. Mastering the dense jungle terrain through an intelligence network of sympathetic Adivasi villagers, Raju ambushed and routed British military columns at the **Peddagaddapalem** and **Damanapalli** mountain passes in September 1922 (killing British commanders Scott Coward and Hayter). Only after the Madras Government deployed the **Malabar Special Police** and the **Assam Rifles** under Special Commissioner **Rutherford** in 1924—burning villages, imposing collective fines, and cutting off food supplies—was **Alluri Sitarama Raju** captured and **summarily executed by firing squad (tied to a tree and shot by Major Goodall) at Koyyuru on May 7, 1924**, earning the immortal title ***Manyam Veerudu*** (Hero of the Jungles).

### 11. The Chauri Chaura Rupture, the Bardoli Withdrawal, and the Gramscian Mechanics of Retreat (February–March 1922)

By **December 1921** (the **Ahmedabad Congress Session**, presided over by **Hakim Ajmal Khan** in place of the imprisoned president-elect **C.R. Das**, where **Maulana Hasrat Mohani** unsuccessfully moved a resolution to define *Swaraj* as *Complete Independence* free from all foreign control), over **30,000 Congress and Khilafat volunteers**—including Motilal and Jawaharlal Nehru, C.R. Das, Lala Lajpat Rai, Maulana Azad, and the Ali Brothers—were behind bars. Granted sole dictatorial executive authority by the Ahmedabad Session to launch the final phase of the struggle—**Mass Civil Disobedience** (including universal non-payment of taxes)—**M.K. Gandhi** traveled to **Bardoli Taluka** in Surat district (Gujarat). On **February 1, 1922**, Gandhi sent an ultimatum to Viceroy **Lord Reading** (who had replaced Chelmsford in April 1921), announcing that unless the government released all non-violent political prisoners and lifted its repressive ban on press and assembly within seven days, he would launch mass Civil Disobedience and non-payment of land revenue from **Bardoli**.

#### The Material Anatomy of Chauri Chaura (February 5, 1922)

Four days after Gandhi's ultimatum, before the Bardoli no-tax campaign could be formally triggered, a violent confrontation erupted in the eastern United Provinces at **Chauri Chaura**, a railway market junction linking the neighboring villages of **Chaura** and **Chauri** in **Gorakhpur district**.

*   **Subaltern Mobilization, Market Picketing, and the Torching of the *Thana*:** Throughout 1921, the impoverished peasantry of Gorakhpur had been mobilized by local Congress and Khilafat committees (**Nazar Ali** and **Shikari**) around a potent blend of nationalist organization and popular belief in the miraculous powers (*Pratap*) of "Gandhi Maharaj." In Chauri Chaura, a local volunteer corps was trained by **Bhagwan Ahir**, a demobilized soldier (pensioner) of the British Indian Army who had returned from wartime service in Mesopotamia. On **February 1, 1922**, when Bhagwan Ahir and volunteers peacefully picketed the **Mundera Bazaar** (owned by a loyalist zamindar, Sant Baksh Singh) against exorbitant meat/fish prices and foreign cloth/liquor sales, the local Sub-Inspector of the Chauri Chaura police station (**Thana**), **Gupteshwar Singh**, severely beat Bhagwan Ahir and several volunteers.
*   **The Escalation of February 4–5, 1922:** In protest against this police brutality, on Saturday, **February 4, 1922** (historically recorded in several contemporary police and press dispatches as **February 4** and in the Bardoli/official political chronology as **February 5, 1922**, when news broke), a massive procession of **2,000 to 3,000 peasant volunteers and villagers** marched past the Chauri Chaura *Thana* toward the Mundera Bazaar chanting *"Mahatma Gandhi Ki Jai."* When the crowd clapped and jeered at the armed police drawn up outside the *Thana*, Sub-Inspector Gupteshwar Singh ordered his constables to fire warning shots into the air; when the crowd—believing through peasant rumors that Gandhi's grace had turned British bullets into water (*"Gandhi Baba ki goli pani ho gayi"*)—refused to disperse and began pelting brickbats, the police opened live lethal fire, killing **three peasants** on the spot. Overwhelmed after exhausting their ammunition, the policemen retreated and bolted themselves inside the brick *Thana*. The enraged peasant multitude soaked rags in kerosene from the bazaar, set the roof of the police station ablaze, hacked to death those constables who leaped out of the flames, and threw their bodies back into the fire. In total, **22 policemen** (including Sub-Inspector Gupteshwar Singh and young Chaukidars) were killed, while the crowd tore up the railway line and cut telegraph wires to prevent reinforcements from Gorakhpur. In the subsequent **Chauri Chaura Sessions Trial**, the colonial judge sentenced **172 peasants to death**; even after **Madan Mohan Malaviya** argued their appeal before the Allahabad High Court in 1923, **19 peasants (including Bhagwan Ahir and Nazar Ali) were executed on the gallows**, and 110 were sentenced to life transportation.

#### The Bardoli Resolution (February 12, 1922) and the "War of Position" (*Struggle–Truce–Struggle*)

Upon receiving news of the Chauri Chaura killings on February 8, **M.K. Gandhi** concluded that the country had not yet internalized the moral and tactical discipline of **Ahimsa** required for mass civil disobedience, and that continuing the movement would plunge India into an asymmetric bloodbath against the superior military firepower of the colonial state. Summoning an emergency meeting of the **Congress Working Committee at Bardoli on February 11–12, 1922**, Gandhi pushed through the historic **Bardoli Resolution**:

*   **Clauses of the Bardoli Withdrawal and Landlord Reassurance:** The **Bardoli Resolution (February 12, 1922)** ordered the immediate suspension of all offensive mass civil disobedience, tax-refusal campaigns, public processions, and picketing across India, marking the formal **end of the Non-Cooperation Movement**. Remarkably, Clauses 6 and 7 of the Bardoli Resolution addressed the agrarian class question directly: the CWC instructed Congress workers to inform peasants (ryots) that **"withholding of rent payment to the Zamindars is contrary to the Congress resolutions and injurious to the best interests of the country,"** assuring the **Zamindars** that the Congress movement *"is in no way intended to attack their legal rights"* and urging arbitration where peasant grievances existed. In place of political agitation, the Bardoli Resolution directed all Congressmen to retreat into the **Constructive Programme**: enrolling 1 crore Congress members, popularizing the **Charkha** and **Khadi**, organizing National Schools, uplifting the **Depressed Classes (Untouchables)**, promoting temperance, and establishing village Panchayats.
*   **Nationalist Shock, the "Great Trial" (March 1922), and the Secularization of Turkey (1922–1924):** The abrupt unilateral suspension of a nationwide mass movement over a localized violent clash in a remote UP village triggered fierce anger and bewildered disbelief among imprisoned nationalist leaders and young radicals. From prison, **Motilal Nehru** and **Lala Lajpat Rai** wrote angry letters asking why the entire nation—or a disciplined district in Andhra or Punjab—should be penalized for the error of a single village in Gorakhpur; **Subhas Chandra Bose** termed the withdrawal just when public enthusiasm was reaching the boiling point *"nothing short of a national calamity"*; **Jawaharlal Nehru** admitted grieving over the sudden collapse of civil resistance; and Marxist theorists like **M.N. Roy** and **Rajani Palme Dutt** argued that the bourgeois Congress leadership had called off the movement at Bardoli out of class fear because peasant no-rent and no-tax militancy was threatening to sweep away indigenous landlordism and capitalism alongside British rule.
    *   *The Gramscian Counter-Analysis (Struggle–Truce–Struggle):* From a structural political-economy perspective, however, Gandhi's Bardoli retreat exemplified a **Gramscian "War of Position"** operating through a **Struggle–Truce–Struggle (S-T-S)** rhythm. Because a mass movement cannot sustain peak emotional and material sacrifice indefinitely—and because by February 1922 urban participation was waning, the Khilafat alliance was fraying after Malabar, and Chauri Chaura provided Viceroy Reading with the exact moral pretext needed to unleash massive military terror—a controlled tactical retreat preserved the organizational fabric of the Congress, claimed the high moral ground of uncompromised *Ahimsa*, and shifted cadres into rural constructive work to rebuild hegemony for the next mass offensive (1930).
    *   *The Great Trial at Ahmedabad (March 18, 1922) and the End of the Khilafat Plank:* Sensing that Gandhi was temporarily isolated from his own resentful party lieutenants after the AICC ratified the Bardoli withdrawal at Delhi on February 24 (where **Dr. Moonje** moved a censure motion against Gandhi), the British government arrested **M.K. Gandhi** at Sabarmati Ashram on **March 10, 1922**, charging him with sedition under **Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code** for three articles published in ***Young India*** (*"Tampering with Loyalty,"* *"The Puzzle and Its Solution,"* and *"Shaking the Manes"*). At the **Great Trial** held at the Circuit House in **Ahmedabad on March 18, 1922**, before Sessions Judge **C.N. Broomfield**, Gandhi pleaded guilty, read out a devastating indictment of colonial economic drain and the Punjab/Khilafat crimes, and invited the Judge either to resign his post to dissociate himself from evil or to inflict the severest penalty known to law. Judge Broomfield, bowing respectfully to Gandhi as a man in a wholly different category from any person he had ever tried, sentenced him to **six years' simple imprisonment** (he was released early in February 1924 following emergency surgery for appendicitis). Shortly thereafter, the international basis of the **Khilafat Movement** evaporated completely from within the Islamic world itself: in **November 1922**, the Turkish nationalist revolution led by **Mustafa Kemal Pasha (Atatürk)** deposed the Ottoman Sultan Mehmed VI and abolished the Sultanate; followed by the **Treaty of Lausanne (July 1923)** securing sovereign Turkish borders; and on **March 3, 1924**, Kemal Pasha formally **abolished the Caliphate (Khilafat)** altogether, exiling the last Caliph and transforming Turkey into a secular republic.

### 12. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From the Bardoli Retreat to the Swarajist Council Entry and Revolutionary Regrouping (Into Part 12)

The **February 1922 Bardoli withdrawal** and the imprisonment of **M.K. Gandhi** in March 1922 closed the first pan-Indian mass mobilization of the twentieth century, leaving behind a permanently transformed political landscape. Although the promise of *"Swaraj within One Year"* remained unfulfilled and the fragile Hindu-Muslim unity cemented by the Khilafat alliance fractured rapidly into communal riots across North India following the Malabar trauma and the abolition of the Caliphate in 1924, the **Non-Cooperation Movement** had irreversibly shattered the hegemonic mystique of the British Raj. Through the **1920 Nagpur organizational overhaul**, the Indian National Congress had ceased to be an anglophone elite petitioning club and had emerged as a permanent, linguistically federated, mass-financed political apparatus anchored deep within the Indian peasantry and urban working classes. Simultaneously, the sudden suspension of mass street agitation after Chauri Chaura created an immediate strategic vacuum within the anti-colonial movement, fracturing nationalist ranks along three intersecting trajectories that define the next chronological horizon (**Part 12** and **Part 10/13**): first, disillusioned young radicals who had abandoned schools in 1920 rejected non-violence to forge the socialist revolutionary underground (**HRA/HSRA** under **Ramprasad Bismil**, **Chandrashekhar Azad**, and **Bhagat Singh**); second, a powerful constitutional faction within the Congress led by **Chittaranjan Das** and **Motilal Nehru** (the **Pro-Changers** or **Swarajists**) challenged the orthodox Gandhian **No-Changers** (**Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Rajendra Prasad**, **C. Rajagopalachari**) at the **December 1922 Gaya Congress Session**, forming the **Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party (January 1923)** to carry the non-cooperation struggle directly inside the Montagu-Chelmsford Legislative Councils; and third, this seven-year phase of constructive grass-roots consolidation and parliamentary obstruction prepared the institutional launchpad for the **1927 Simon Commission Boycott**, the **1928 Nehru Report**, the **1929 Lahore Purna Swaraj Resolution**, and the **1930–1934 Civil Disobedience Movement**.
