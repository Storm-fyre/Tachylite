# Part 4: Subaltern, Peasant, and Tribal Insurrections against Colonial and Feudal Extraction (1760s–1900)

## I. The Political Economy of Subaltern Insurgency & Dispossessed Military-Feudal Resistances (1763–1848)

### 1. Structural Mechanics of Autonomous Subaltern Insurgency: The Sarkar–Sahukar–Zamindar/Diku Triad

The establishment of Company statehood following the **1765 Diwani of Bengal** and the subsequent military-fiscal annexations across peninsular and continental India did not encounter a passive agrarian landscape. Instead, the century preceding the **1857 Revolt** witnessed what **Kathleen Gough** categorized as at least **77 major peasant uprisings**, ranging from restorative feudal rebellions and religious-millenarian wars to autonomous tribal (*Adivasi*) and agrarian insurrections. Unlike pre-colonial Mughal or regional state revenue systems—which operated on custom-bound, harvest-sensitive share-cropping (*Batai*) and left internal village and forest tenures largely intact—the colonial regime introduced alien bourgeois property concepts, rigid cash-rent deadlines (**Sunset Laws**), civil courts (*Adalats*) that enforced usurious debt contracts through land alienation, and ecological enclosures that monetized the forest commons.

As theorized by **Ranajit Guha** in *Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India* (**1983**), subaltern insurgency was neither a pre-political, spontaneous reflex to biological starvation ("belly politics") nor a passive appendage of elite leadership. Rather, it constituted a conscious, politically autonomous domain structured against an interlocking triad of extraction: **Sarkar** (the colonial bureaucratic, police, and judicial state), **Sahukar / Mahajan** (the merchant-usurer utilizing colonial contract law to expropriate peasant land and labor), and **Zamindar / Planter / Diku** (the rent-extracting landlord, European indigo/tea planter, or non-tribal outsider encroaching upon customary communal lands). When every institutional petition failed, the subaltern peasant or Adivasi inverted the semiotic, spatial, and legal codes of this triad through six structural modalities of insurgency:

*   **Negation (Inversion of Deference Codes):** The insurgent peasant defined his political identity not through abstract ideological treatises, but by negating the material and symbolic insignia of superordinate authority. Customary deference (*Salam*, stepping off roads, lowering umbrellas before **Zamindars** or **Brahmans**) was deliberately inverted: **Santhal** and **Munda** rebels appropriated the turbans, horses, and palanquins of landlords; **Pabna** and **Moplah** tenants defied seigneurial etiquette; and insurgents systematically targeted the physical instruments of legal and fiscal subjugation—burning **Mahajani Khatas** (account books), **Kachahris** (landlord estate offices), **Thanas** (police stations), and civil court stamp papers, rather than engaging in indiscriminate looting.
*   **Ambiguity (Crime vs. Insurgency):** Because the colonial state criminalized collective rural protest under statutes such as **Warren Hastings’ 1772 Dacoity Regulations** and later the **Indian Penal Code (1860)**, elite colonial archives recorded peasant rebellions through the lexicon of pathology and banditry ("dacoity," "fanaticism," "outrage"). In reality, what the **Sarkar** coded as solitary, secretive crime was executed by peasants as public, collective, and morally sanctioned corporate action (*Hool*, *Ulgulan*, *Bidroha*, *Dhawa*).
*   **Modality (Public, Corporate, and Ritualized Action):** Subaltern violence rejected the clandestine individuality of criminal theft in favor of open, daylight mass assemblies (**Raij-Mels** in Assam, **Kootams** in South India, **Bhagnadihi** assemblies in the **Santhal Hool**). Plunder (*Loot*) wastransformed into ritualized redistribution or public destruction of unholy wealth—such as the **Deccan** peasants publicly burning debt bonds in village squares in **1875** or **Indigo** ryots destroying factory vats in **1859–60**—accompanied by sacralized oaths before local deities (*Thakur*, *Singbonga*, or Quranic injunctions).
*   **Solidarity (Horizontal Class/Ethnic Cohesion and Coercive Sanction):** Insurgency relied on pre-existing kinship, clan (*Khunt*), caste, or religious networks to enforce total horizontal solidarity against the vertical patronage of the **Sarkar–Sahukar–Zamindar** nexus. Traitors, informers, and compliant debtors were disciplined through complete social boycott (**Nai-Dhobi Bandh**—denying barber, washerman, and ritual services), social excommunication (*Hukka-Pani Bandh*), or physical reprisals against collaborators.
*   **Transmission (Non-Literate Sonic and Symbolic Communication):** Operating largely outside the sphere of elite print capitalism, pre-1857 subaltern insurgencies transmitted mobilization directives across hundreds of square miles at remarkable speed through non-verbal, iconic, and sonic media: the circulation of the **Sal** tree branch (*Girah*) among the **Santhals** and **Kols**, arrow-passing among the **Ho** and **Mundas**, the blowing of buffalo horns (*Singa*) and beating of *Nagada* kettle-drums in **Pabna**, and rumour (*Afrieg*), which functioned not as falsehood, but as an autonomous subaltern medium translating structural grievances into prophecies of imminent British collapse.
*   **Territoriality (Intersection of Ethnic Space and Geographical Domain):** Subaltern uprisings were anchored in a deep moral geography—such as the **Damin-i-Koh** of the **Santhals** or the ancestral **Khuntkatti** hills of the **Mundas**—attempting to purge a sacred homeland of alien **Dikus**. While this intense localism generated fierce defensive cohesion, the incongruity between the rebels' ethnic space (*Des*) and the vast administrative-military grid of the colonial state ultimately enabled the **Company** to isolate and crush these uprisings sequentially.

The first wave of anti-colonial resistance between the **1760s** and the **1840s** was spearheaded by deposed regional rulers, demobilized military retainers, autonomous hill chieftains (**Poligars**, **Paiks**, **Ramosis**), and mendicant orders whose rent-free service tenures (*Inam*, *Jagir*, *Paikan*) were systematically liquidated by the Company’s drive to maximize net land revenue and enforce a colonial monopoly over coercion.

### 2. Eastern & Deccan Dispossessed Military-Feudal Insurrections: Sanyasi-Fakir, Chuar, and Paika Rebellions

#### The Sanyasi-Fakir Rebellion (1763–1800) and Ascetic-Peasant Mobilization

The earliest sustained armed challenge to Company rule in Bengal and Bihar was the **Sanyasi-Fakir Rebellion (1763–1800)**, which erupted immediately after the **East India Company** acquired revenue administration and worsened dramatically during the **Great Bengal Famine of 1770** (which wiped out one-third of Bengal's population while Company collections increased). For centuries, armed ascetic orders—specifically the Muslim **Madariya** Sufi Fakirs and the Hindu **Dasnami Naga** Sanyasis (primarily *Giri* and *Puri* sects)—had seasonally traversed northern and eastern Bengal on pilgrimage to shrines and temples, acting as trader-bankers, mercenary soldiers for regional rulers, and recipients of customary alms (*Britti* and *Mathote*) and rent-free land grants from local **Zamindars**.

*   **Fiscal and Mobility Triggers:** Following the **1765 Diwani**, the Company viewed mobile armed mendicants as "gypsy-like" marauders, imposed taxes on pilgrimage routes, prohibited the carrying of arms, and bankrupted the traditional **Zamindars** through **Warren Hastings’ Izaredari** farming system, thereby drying up the customary charity that sustained both orders. Joined by starving peasants, evicted small proprietors, and disbanded soldiers of the Nawab’s army, the Sanyasis and Fakirs launched a thirty-year guerrilla war targeting Company **Kuthis** (factories), revenue treasuries, and grain stores across **Rangpur**, **Dinajpur**, **Bogra**, **Mymensingh**, **Purnea**, and **Cooch Behar**.
*   **Leadership and Cross-Confessional Solidarity:** The insurgency demonstrated remarkable Hindu-Muslim operational coordination. The **Madariya** Fakirs were commanded by **Majnu Shah** (who orchestrated mobile raids from **1771** until his death in **1787**) and subsequently by his lieutenants **Musa Shah**, **Chirag Ali**, and **Paragal Shah**. They operated in strategic alliance with Hindu **Dasnami** leaders **Bhawani Pathak** and the female strategist **Devi Chaudhurani**, who commanded a fleet of riverine boats along the Brahmaputra and Teesta channels, intercepting British revenue convoys and redistributing plunder to famine-stricken villagers.
*   **Military Suppression and Literary Appropriation:** The insurgents repeatedly defeated Company detachments—killing **Captain Thomas** (1772) and **Captain Edwards** (1773)—before **Warren Hastings** deployed regular infantry battalions and enacted draconian anti-dacoity regulations that executed ascetics on sight, gradually containing the movement by **1800**. In the late nineteenth century, **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** immortalized this insurgency in his landmark Bengali novels **Anandamath (1882)**—which gave the nationalist movement the hymn *Vande Mataram*—and **Devi Chaudhurani (1884)**; however, Bankim selectively recast a syncretic Hindu-Muslim subaltern rebellion against Company famine extraction into an exclusively Hindu ascetic crusade (*Santans*) against Muslim Nawab rule.

#### The Chuar Uprising (1766–1816) and Jungle Mahal Paik Dispossession

Simultaneously, along the forested western frontier of Bengal in **Midnapore**, **Bankura**, and **Manbhum** (the **Jungle Mahals**), the Company’s revenue maximization triggered the multi-phase **Chuar Uprising (1766–1816)**. The indigenous **Bhumij** tribals and local peasantry—derogatorily termed *Chuars* ("outlandish/wild pigs") by plains scribes—served as hereditary **Paiks** (local militia-policemen) under frontier chieftains (*Tarafdars* and *Zemindars*), holding rent-free **Paikan** lands in exchange for policing the forest passes rather than paying cash taxes.

*   **First Phase (1766–1772):** When the Company demanded exorbitant cash assessments from the Jungle Mahal Zamindars and dispatched military expeditions to demolish mud forts in **1767**, **Jagannath Singh**, the Zamindar of **Ghatshila** (Dhalbhum), alongside the chiefs of **Barabhum** and **Manbhum**, mobilized 5,000 armed Bhumij Paiks in **1768**, forcing the British to temporarily restore his estate.
*   **Second & Peak Phase (1798–1799):** Following the imposition of the **Permanent Settlement (1793)** and the **Cornwallis Police Regulations**—which replaced indigenous **Paiks** with salaried outsider **Darogas** and resumed their hereditary **Paikan** lands for full revenue assessment—a massive insurrection erupted in **1798–99**. Led by **Durjan Singh**, the dispossessed Zamindar of **Raipur** (who mobilized 1,500 Chuars to burn 30 villages and attack Company auction-purchasers), **Madhab Singh** (brother of the Raja of Barabhum), and **Rani Shiromani** of **Karnagarh** (Midnapore), the rebels paralysed British administration until **Wellesley’s** administration suspended the auction of **Paikan** lands and created the special administrative unit of the **Jungle Mahals District (Regulation XVIII of 1805)**. Later Bhoomij discontent erupted again in **1832–33** under **Ganga Narain Singh** in Barabhum and Manbhum ("Ganga Narain's Hangama").

#### Bakshi Jagabandhu and the Paika Rebellion in Odisha (1817)

Following the British conquest of **Odisha** from the **Bhonsle Marathas** in **1803**, the colonial dismantling of the indigenous military-agrarian order provoked the **Paika Rebellion of 1817**. For centuries under the Gajapati kings of **Khurda**, the **Paiks** (literally "foot soldiers," drawn from all castes including *Chasa* cultivators, *Pana* untouchables, and *Khandayats*) formed a landed peasant militia holding hereditary rent-free lands (**Niskar Jagirs**) in exchange for military and policing service.

*   **The Four-Fold Fiscal Rupture (1804–1817):** After suppressing an initial anti-British rising in **1804** led by **Jayi Rajaguru** (the royal preceptor, who was brutally executed by having his body tied to the branches of two banyan trees and torn apart in **1806**), the British annexed the **Khurda** kingdom, deposed young **Raja Mukunda Deva II**, and instituted four devastating fiscal measures under Commissioner **Major Fletcher**:
    1.  **Resumption of Paikan Lands:** Hereditary rent-free **Paik** service holdings were confiscated and subjected to eleven short-term, speculative revenue settlements within twelve years, dispossessing original Odia proprietors in favor of Bengali absentee auction-purchasers from Calcutta.
    2.  **Abolition of Kauri Currency:** The colonial state demonetized the indigenous **Kauri** (cowrie shell) currency—which crashed in value from 4,000 to 7,000 Kauris per Sicca Rupee—and demanded all land revenue exclusively in scarce **Silver Sicca Rupees**, driving peasants into the hands of Mahajans.
    3.  **Colonial Salt Monopoly (Regulation XXII of 1814):** The British monopolized salt manufacture along the Odisha coast, barring villagers from boiling brackish water and causing the price of essential dietary salt to skyrocket six-fold.
*   **Bakshi Jagabandhu’s Insurgency:** The uprising was led by **Bakshi Jagabandhu Bidyadhar Mahapatra Bhramarabar Rai**, the hereditary military commander (*Bakshi*) of the Raja of Khurda, whose ancestral estate of **Rorang** had been fraudulently auctioned off by Bengali officials, reducing him to surviving on voluntary alms from the people of Khurda. In **March 1817**, a body of **400 Kondh** tribals from **Ghumsur** marched into **Banpur** and joined **Bakshi Jagabandhu’s** Paiks.
*   **Trajectory and Settlement:** The rebels burned the police stations and government buildings at **Banpur** and **Khurda**, defeated a British detachment at **Gangapada**, and marched into the sacred temple city of **Puri** in **April 1817**, forcing the British forces to flee to Cuttack and proclaiming **Raja Mukunda Deva II** as sovereign. Supported by **Gopabandhu** priests, salt workers (*Malangis*), and feudal chiefs of **Kujang** and **Kanika**, the Paiks waged guerrilla warfare from the jungles for eight years. While British martial law and **Judge Walter Ewer’s Commission (1818)** crushed the open revolt—leading to the death of Mukunda Deva II in Cuttack fort jail—**Bakshi Jagabandhu** evaded capture until **May 1825**, when he surrendered under a negotiated amnesty granting him a pension at Cuttack, where he died in **1829**. The British were compelled to reduce revenue assessments, moderate salt prices, and suspend arbitrary estate sales in Khurda.

### 3. Southern, Western, and North-Eastern Anti-Company Resistances

#### The Poligar (Palaiyakkarar) Wars (1799–1805) and Velu Thampi Dalawa’s Revolt (1808–1809)

In the Tamil country (Madras Presidency), political authority below the **Nawab of Arcot** was exercised by **72 Poligars (Palaiyakkarars)**—sovereign warrior-chieftains initially instituted under the **Vijayanagara Nayaka** system who maintained forts, independent artillery, and armed retainers, collecting customary **Kaval** (watch/protection) fees and sovereign taxes (*Kudimai*). When the heavily indebted **Nawab of Arcot (Muhammad Ali Khan Wallajah)** assigned the revenue management (*Kavali* and *Peshkash*) of **Tirunelveli** and the Carnatic to the East India Company in **1781** and **1792**, the British moved to strip the Poligars of their sovereign military status and reduce them to mere tax-paying landlords.

*   **First Poligar War (1799) & Veerapandiya Kattabomman:** After early resistance by **Puli Thevar** of Nerkattumseval (**1755–1767**) and **Rani Velu Nachiyar** of Sivaganga (**1780**), the **First Poligar War** erupted in **1799**, centered on **Veerapandiya Kattabomman**, the 30-year-old Poligar of **Panchalankurichi**. Humiliated by British Collector **Colin Jackson** at Ramanathapuram in **1798** (where Kattabomman’s minister **Sivasubramania Pillai** was arrested), Kattabomman formed a confederacy of southern Poligars (including **Nagelpuram** and **Elayirampannai**) and refused to submit to **Major Bannerman’s** ultimatum in **September 1799**. After repulsing an initial British assault on Panchalankurichi fort, Kattabomman evacuated into the jungles, was betrayed by **Vijaya Raghunatha Tondaiman** (Raja of Pudukkottai), and was defiantly hanged by the British at **Kayathar** on **October 16, 1799**, while his fort was razed and his wealth confiscated.
*   **Second Poligar War (1800–1801) & The Marudu Brothers:** Far more expansive was the **Second Poligar War (also termed the South Indian Confederacy)**, triggered in **February 1801** when Kattabomman’s deaf-mute brother **Oomaithurai** escaped from **Palayamkottai Prison** and rebuilt the Panchalankurichi fort within days. He was joined by the **Marudu Brothers**—**Periya Marudu** (Vella Marudu) and **Chinna Marudu**—who governed **Sivaganga**, alongside **Gopala Nayak** of Dindigul, **Kerala Varma Pazhassi Raja** of Malabar, and **Dheeran Chinnamalai** of Kongu Nadu. In **June 1801**, **Chinna Marudu** issued the historic **Tiruchirappalli Proclamation (Jambudweepa Proclamation)**, pasted on the walls of the **Tiruchirappalli Nawab’s Palace** and the **Srirangam Vaishnava Temple**, calling upon all castes, Hindus, and Muslims across the peninsula to unite and drive the Europeans into the sea. British forces under **Colonel Agnew** deployed overwhelming artillery to storm **Kalaiyar Kovil**, executed the **Marudu Brothers** at **Tirupathur Fort** on **October 24, 1801**, beheaded **Oomaithurai** in **November 1801**, and hanged **Dheeran Chinnamalai** at **Sankagiri Fort** in **1805**. By the **Carnatic Treaty of July 31, 1801**, the British liquidated the Nawab of Arcot's sovereignty, disarmed all Poligars, demolished their forts, replaced the *Kaval* system with a colonial Zamindari/Ryotwari settlement, and renamed Tirunelveli district to pacify the region.
*   **Velu Thampi Dalawa’s Revolt in Travancore (1808–1809):** In neighboring **Travancore**, **Lord Wellesley** had forced **Raja Bala Rama Varma** into an extortionate revised **Subsidiary Alliance Treaty in 1805**, doubling the annual military subsidy to Rs 8 lakhs and empowering the British Resident, **Colonel Colin Macaulay**, to interfere directly in internal administration. When the state treasury fell into arrears, Macaulay demanded the dismissal of the Prime Minister (**Dalawa**), **Velu Thampi**, and the disbanding of the royal **Nair** brigades. Forging a secret alliance with **Paliath Achan** (the Prime Minister of **Cochin**) and seeking French assistance from Mauritius, **Velu Thampi Dalawa** launched an armed insurrection in **December 1808**, attacking the British Residency at **Quilon (Kollam)**. On **January 11, 1809**, from the **Bhagavathy Temple at Kundara**, Velu Thampi issued the famous **Kundara Proclamation**, a devastating indictment of British imperialism warning that unchecked Company control would replace traditional Nair governance with Christian missionaries, salt monopolies, and universal land taxes. When massive British reinforcements under **Colonel St. Leger** stormed the **Aramboly Lines** and marched on Trivandrum, **Velu Thampi** sought refuge at the **Mannadi Bhagavathy Temple**; surrounded by Company troops in **March 1809**, he committed suicide to prevent capture, though the British vindictively gibbeted his dead body at **Kannammoola** in Trivandrum.

#### Deccan & Western Ghats Feudal-Militia Revolts: Kittur, Rayalaseema, Ramosi, Gadkari, and Sawantwadi

Across the **Bombay Presidency**, **Madras Ceded Districts**, and the **Deccan**, the liquidation of the **Maratha Confederacy (1818)** and the resumption of hereditary military-service tenures provoked a chain of fierce resistances:

*   **Kittur Chennamma (1824) and Sangolli Rayanna (1829–1830) in Karnataka:** When **Shivalinga Rudra Sarja**, the Desai ruler of the prosperous princely state of **Kittur** (Belgaum district), died in **September 1824** shortly after adopting a son (**Shivalingappa**), the British Collector and Political Agent at Dharwad, **St. John Thackeray** (backed by Bombay Governor **Mountstuart Elphinstone** and Commissioner **William Chaplin**), refused to recognize the adoption—anticipating **Dalhousie’s Doctrine of Lapse** by twenty-four years—and moved to annex the state’s Rs 15 lakh treasury. **Rani Chennamma** (the senior queen of the late Mallasarja), assisted by her commanders **Sardar Gurusiddappa** and **Sangolli Rayanna**, mobilized the Kittur militia. In **October 1824**, when Thackeray brought artillery to blow open the gates of **Kittur Fort**, Chennamma’s forces counter-attacked, killing **St. John Thackeray** on the battlefield and capturing two British officers (**Stevenson** and **Elliot**), whom she treated with chivalrous restraint and released upon Chaplin’s promise to halt hostilities. Betraying this pledge, the British surrounded Kittur in **December 1824** with massive regiments from Mysore and Sholapur; sabotaged by traitors (**Mallappa Shetty** and **Venkata Rao**) who mixed cow-dung into Kittur’s gunpowder reserves, **Rani Chennamma** was captured and imprisoned at **Bailhongal Fort**, where she died on **February 21, 1829**. Her loyal lieutenant, **Sangolli Rayanna** (a warrior from the *Kuruba* shepherd community), immediately launched a relentless guerrilla insurgency (**1829–1830**), burning British *Taluka* offices and records across Dharwad to install the adopted heir **Shivalingappa**, before he was betrayed by local Deshmukhs and publicly hanged by the British from a banyan tree at **Nandagad** on **January 26, 1831**.
*   **Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy in Rayalaseema (1846–1847):** In the **Ceded Districts (Rayalaseema)** of Andhra Pradesh—transferred by the Nizam of Hyderabad to the British in **1800**—**Thomas Munro** had ruthlessly suppressed the **Poligars**, confiscating their estates and commuting their sovereign rights into meager hereditary pensions, while resuming the rent-free **Kattubadi** lands of village militia retainers. In **June 1846**, **Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy**, a dispossessed Poligar scion from **Koilkuntla** (Kurnool/Kadapa region) who received a paltry monthly pension of Rs 11 and 10.5 annas and whose lands were threatened with resumption, mobilized **5,000 Kattubadi** peasant-retainers, **Boya** and **Chenchu** tribals. He stormed and looted the **Koilkuntla Treasury**, killed the *Tahsildar*, defeated a Company force at **Giddalur**, and waged a mobile guerrilla campaign across the **Nallamala Hills** (**Rudravaram**, **Ahobilam**). Captured near **Jagannathakonda** in **October 1846** after the British held his family hostage, **Narasimha Reddy** was publicly hanged at **Koilkuntla** on **February 22, 1847**, and his severed head was suspended inside an iron cage on the **Koilkuntla Fort** wall for thirty years (**until 1877**) as an instrument of colonial terror.
*   **The Ramosi Uprisings in Maharashtra (1822–1829 & 1879):** The **Ramosis**, an indigenous martial hill community of the Western Ghats who had served as night-watchmen and lower-rank fort garrisons under the **Maratha administration** in exchange for rent-free **Inam** lands and **Naikwadi** village fees, were reduced to destitution after **1818** when the British disbanded the Maratha armies and imposed heavy cash assessments.
    *   *Early Phase (1822–1829):* In **1822**, **Chittur Singh** of **Satara** led the Ramosis in plundering British territory around Satara and attacking forts. Between **1825 and 1829**, driven by acute famine and administrative dispossession in the **Poona** region, **Umaji Naik** and his lieutenant **Bapu Trimbakji Sawant** organized the Ramosis into a disciplined parallel government in the **Purandhar hills**, issuing proclamations demanding that villagers pay land revenue to him rather than the British, until the Bombay government granted an amnesty, employed Ramosis as hill police, and later captured and hanged **Umaji Naik** at Poona in **1832**.
    *   *Late Proto-Nationalist Phase (1879):* Half a century later, following the catastrophic **Deccan Famine of 1876–78**, **Vasudev Balwant Phadke** (1845–1883)—an educated Chitpavan Brahman clerk in the Military Finance Department at Poona influenced by **M.G. Ranade’s** lectures on the **Drain of Wealth**—forged a revolutionary alliance with **Ramosi** (led by **Daulatrao Naik**), **Bhil**, **Koli**, and **Dhangar** youths. Organizing a 300-strong armed guerrilla force in **February 1879** to establish a **Hindu Republic**, Phadke conducted *Dacoities* on rich **Marwari Sahukars** and colonial communications around **Dhamari**, **Valhe**, and **Palaspe** to finance an anti-imperial army. Captured in the **Kaladgi** district (Hyderabad border) in **July 1879** and defended by **Ganesh Vasudev Joshi (Sarvajanik Kaka)**, Phadke was sentenced to transportation for life and died on hunger strike in the British cellular prison at **Aden** on **February 17, 1883**.
*   **The Gadkari Revolt (1844) and Sawantwadi Revolts (1830–1858):** In the Maratha princely state of **Kolhapur**, the **Gadkaris**—a hereditary martial class who manned the hill forts (*Gads*) in return for rent-free land (*Inam*) and grain allowances—faced direct unemployment when the British appointed a Brahman regent, **Daji Krishna Pandit**, in **1844** to reorganize revenue, supervise fort garrisons, and resume their rent-free estates. In **September–December 1844**, the Gadkaris shut the gates of the **Samangad** and **Bhudargad** forts, overthrew the regent, and set up a parallel government at **Kolhapur** and **Panhala**, requiring **General Delamotte’s** artillery brigades to batter down the fort walls. Simultaneously, in the neighboring coastal principality of **Sawantwadi** (Ratnagiri border)—which had already witnessed noble rebellions in **1830**, **1836**, and **1838** after the British deposed the ruler **Khem Sawant**—the Sardars led by **Phond Sawant** and the heir-apparent **Anna Sahib** allied with the Kolhapur **Gadkaris** in **1844** to expel the British Political Superintendent. Although suppressed by **1845** via martial law and the extradition of rebel leaders who had fled into **Portuguese Goa**, **Phond Sawant’s** sons rose again during the **1857 Revolt**.

#### Gomdhar Konwar and the Ahom Revolt (1828–1833)

In the **North-East**, during the **First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826)**, British Commissioner **David Scott** had marched into the **Brahmaputra Valley** promising the **Ahom** nobility that once the Burmese invaders were expelled, the East India Company would restore the traditional Ahom monarchy. Instead, following the **Treaty of Yandabo (February 1826)**, the British permanently annexed Lower Assam and began dismantling the indigenous **Paik / Khel** administrative system—under which the Ahom aristocracy (*Gohains*, *Phukans*, and *Baruas*) received the compulsory labor service of adult male subjects (*Paiks* organized into *Gots*) instead of cash salaries—replacing it with a monetized colonial bureaucracy staffed by Bengali clerks (*Amlahs*) and imposing poll/land taxes on the nobility.

*   **First Uprising under Gomdhar Konwar (1828):** In **April 1828**, **Gomdhar Konwar**, a prince of the Ahom royal family, supported by **Dhanjoy Borgohain** and **Jairam Khargharia Phukan**, was formally enthroned as *Swargadeo* (King) near **Jorhat** according to Ahom bailung rituals. Recruiting disbanded **Paiks**, Gomdhar Konwar advanced toward the British military station at **Rangpur (Sibsagar)** in **November 1828**, but was intercepted and defeated by **Lieutenant Rutherford** at **Mariani**. Captured in the Naga hills, Gomdhar Konwar’s death sentence was commuted to seven years’ imprisonment in the **Rangpur (Bengal)** jail.
*   **Second Uprising & Concession (1829–1833):** Undeterred, **Dhanjoy Borgohain** (who escaped custody), his sons **Harakanta** and **Harनाथ**, his son-in-law **Jeoram Dulia Barua**, and **Peali Barphukan** (son of Badanchandra Barphukan) organized a wider pan-tribal conspiracy in **1829–1830**, dispatching emissaries to the **Khasis**, **Singphos**, **Khamtis**, and **Garo** chiefs. In **March 1830**, the rebels attacked the British magazine and guard post at **Rangpur**, but were repulsed; **Peali Barphukan** and **Jeoram Dulia Barua** were publicly hanged by the British at **Sibsagar** in **August 1830**. Faced with endemic Ahom hostility and the simultaneous **Khasi** and **Singpho** wars, the Company retreated tactically in **March 1833**, restoring Upper Assam (east of the Dhansiri river) to the Ahom prince **Purandar Singh** in exchange for an annual tribute of Rs 50,000, before finally annexing his territory permanently in **1838** on charges of financial default.

## II. Socio-Religious Peasant Insurrections & Millenarian Counter-Hegemony (1820s–1870s)

### 4. Islamic Revivalist-Agrarian Mobilizations in Bengal and North India

When the **Permanent Settlement of 1793** created a class of predominantly high-caste Hindu **Zamindars** in Bengal and European **Indigo Planters** across the Gangetic plains, the structural contradiction between landlord rent-maximization and Muslim peasant subsistence was frequently articulated through Islamic revivalist theology. Drawing inspiration from **Shah Waliullah of Delhi (1703–1762)** and his son **Shah Abdul Aziz**—whose **1803 Fatwa** declared British-occupied India to be **Dar-ul-Harb** ("Abode of War") requiring conversion into **Dar-ul-Islam** ("Abode of Peace")—these movements fused puritanical religious reform (purging syncretic Pir-worship and un-Islamic rituals) with militant agrarian class struggle and anti-imperial warfare.

#### Syed Ahmad Barelvi and the Wahabi (Tariqah-i-Muhammadiya) Movement (1820s–1870s)

Founded by **Syed Ahmad of Rae Bareli (1786–1831)**—a former cavalry trooper in the army of **Amir Khan Pindari** of Tonk who studied under **Shah Abdul Aziz** in Delhi and was influenced by eighteenth-century Arabian **Wahhabi** puritanism during his **1821–23 Hajj** to Mecca—the **Tariqah-i-Muhammadiya** (called the **Wahabi Movement** by British officials) became the most tightly organized, pan-Indian clandestine anti-colonial network of the nineteenth century.

*   **Organizational Architecture (Patna Headquarters & Sithana Base):** Recognizing that direct insurrection inside Company-held Gangetic plains was logistically vulnerable, **Syed Ahmad Barelvi** established an operational military sanctuary (**Dar-ul-Islam**) among the Pathan tribes at **Sithana** in the independent North-Western Frontier hills (**1826**), while constructing an underground recruitment, courier, and fiscal-remittance apparatus headquartered at **Sadiqpur in Patna** (Bihar). Led by his four **Khalifas**—most notably the Patna brothers **Wilayat Ali** and **Inayat Ali**, alongside **Shah Muhammad Husain** and **Farhat Husain**—the Patna center collected mandatory religious tithes (*Zakat* and *Muthi* handfuls of grain from peasant households), converted funds into gold **Mohurs** and **Hundis** via Delhi bankers, and smuggled thousands of armed **Mujahideen** recruits and cryptic cipher messages along the Grand Trunk Road to the frontier.
*   **Transition from Anti-Sikh to Anti-British War (1831–1870):** Initially, **Syed Ahmad Barelvi** directed his *Jihad* against **Maharaja Ranjit Singh’s** Sikh Empire in Punjab, capturing **Peshawar** in **1830** and striking coins in his name before he and his intellectual lieutenant **Shah Ismail Dehlavi** were killed by Sikh forces under **Sher Singh** at the **Battle of Balakot (May 1831)**. However, following the British annexation of Punjab in **1849**, the **Wahabi** network turned its entire military apparatus directly against the **British Raj**. Under **Wilayat Ali** and **Inayat Ali**, the Sithana camp fought over twenty frontier engagements against British expeditions between **1850 and 1863**, culminating in the bloody **Ambela Campaign (1863)** where British forces under **General Neville Chamberlain** suffered over 900 casualties before destroying the **Malka** settlement. Subsequently, the British dismantled the **Patna** underground through a series of high-profile **Sedition Trials at Ambala (1864), Patna (1865), Malda (1870), and Rajmahal (1870)**, transporting leaders like **Yahya Ali** and **Ahmadullah** to the **Andaman Cellular Jail** (where a Wahabi convict, **Sher Ali Afridi**, assassinated Viceroy **Lord Mayo** at Port Blair in **1872**).

#### Haji Shariatullah, Dudu Miyan, and the Faraizi Movement in Eastern Bengal (1818–1860s)

In the riverine districts of **Eastern Bengal** (**Faridpur**, **Bakarganj**, **Dacca**, **Pabna**, **Mymensingh**, and **Tippera**), the overwhelmingly Muslim peasantry (*Ryots*), handloom weavers (*Jolahas*), and oil-pressers (*Kulus*) were subjected to intense rack-renting by Hindu **Zamindars** and forced cultivation by European **Indigo Planters**. In **1818**, **Haji Shariatullah (1781–1840)** of **Shamodail** village (Faridpur)—returning after twenty years of theological study in Mecca—founded the **Faraizi Movement**, derived from **Faraiz** (the obligatory religious duties enjoined by God and the Prophet).

*   **Theological Premise as Agrarian Subversion:** **Haji Shariatullah** preached strict monotheism (*Tawhid*), demanded repentance (*Taubah*) from polytheistic customs, abolished Pir-Muridi hierarchy in favor of an egalitarian brotherhood of *Ustad* (teacher) and *Shagird* (pupil), and declared that because Bengal under British rule was **Dar-ul-Harb**, the Friday congregational prayers (*Juma*) and **Eid** prayers were suspended. Crucially, he prohibited Muslim tenants from paying illegal, humiliating religious cesses (**Abwabs**) levied by Hindu Zamindars—such as **Kali Puja**, **Durga Puja**, and **Sraddha** taxes—alongside bans on cow slaughter.
*   **Dudu Miyan’s Parallel State (The Khalifat System, 1840–1862):** Upon Shariatullah’s death in **1840**, his son **Muhsinuddin Ahmad**, popularly known as **Dudu Miyan (1819–1862)**, transformed the Faraizis from a religious reform sect into a militant agrarian trade union and parallel state headquartered at **Bahadurpur**. **Dudu Miyan** proclaimed the radical egalitarian maxim that **"All land belongs to God (*Allah*), and no man has the right to inherit it by birth or levy taxes upon it,"** urging peasants to settle ONLY on state-owned **Khas Mahal** lands and pay solely the fixed legal government revenue while boycotting Zamindars and Indigo Planters.
*   **The Two-Winged Administrative Hierarchy:** **Dudu Miyan** organized Eastern Bengal into a cellular administrative grid under himself as **Ustad / Pir**:
    1.  **The Siyasi (Political/Military) Wing:** Maintained an armed force of expert club-men (**Lathiyals** and *Affrids*) to defend Faraizi peasants against Zamindari eviction squads and raid oppressive Indigo factories (such as **Dunlop’s** Panchchar factory in **1846**).
    2.  **The Dini (Judicial/Administrative) Wing:** Divided the countryside into provinces (*Halqas*) of 300 to 500 families, each governed by a **Gaon Khalifa** (village commissioner), supervised by a **Superintendent Khalifa** over a *Thana*, and an **Uparath Khalifa** at the district level. These **Faraizi Panchayat Courts** adjudicated all civil, agrarian, and criminal disputes among peasants (Regardless of whether they were Muslim or low-caste Hindu weavers), strictly forbidding any ryot from approaching British **Adalats** without the Khalifa's permission on pain of social boycott. Repeatedly arrested on fabricated murder and arson charges (and acquitted in **1847** when no witness dared testify against him), **Dudu Miyan** was preventively detained in **Alipore Jail** during the **1857 Revolt** and died in **1862**, after which his son **Naya Miyan** continued the agrarian struggle into the **Pabna** era.

#### Titu Mir (Mir Nithar Ali) and the Bansher Kella (Bamboo Fort) Uprising (1831)

In **Western Bengal** (**24 Parganas**, **Nadia**, and **Faridpur** districts), a parallel confluence of **Wahabi** egalitarianism and fierce anti-Zamindar/anti-Planter peasant resistance erupted under **Mir Nithar Ali (1782–1831)**, famously known as **Titu Mir**. Born a peasant in **Chandpur (24 Parganas)** who had worked as a professional *Lathiyal* (wrestler-clubman) for Nadia Zamindars before meeting **Syed Ahmad Barelvi** in Mecca in **1822**, **Titu Mir** returned in **1827** to organize the Muslim peasantry and Hindu low-caste weavers around **Haiderpur** and **Barasat**.

*   **The Beard Tax and Landlord Retaliation:** To visually distinguish his reformed peasant followers from orthodox elites, **Titu Mir** instructed them to wear their *Dhotis* plain (unfolded between the legs) and grow beards. Alarmed by their growing refusal to pay illegal cesses or perform forced indigo labor, the local Hindu Zamindars—led by **Krishna Deva Rai** of **Pura** (alongside the Zamindars of **Taragunia**, **Nagarpur**, and **Gobardanga**)—colluded with British Indigo Planters to impose a humiliating **Beard Tax of Rs 2.50 per beard** on every peasant follower, sending armed retainers to pluck beards, burn mosques, and fabricate debt suits in Company police stations.
*   **The Bansher Kella at Narkelbaria (1831):** When the British Magistrate of Barasat (**Alexander**) dismissed the peasants' legal complaints in favor of the Zamindars, **Titu Mir** assembled an army of **4,000 to 5,000 armed peasants** at **Narkelbaria** (near Barasat) in **October 1831** and constructed the legendary **Bansher Kella (Bamboo Fort)**—a heavily reinforced palisade built of double rows of bamboo stakes filled with mud walling that absorbed musket fire. Proclaiming the end of British rule, nominating **Muizzuddin** as *Wazir* (Prime Minister) and his bold nephew **Ghulam Masum** as *Senapati* (Commander-in-Chief), Titu Mir’s forces sacked **Krishna Deva Rai’s** estate at Pura, destroyed the **Bargachia Indigo Factory**, and defeated two successive Company forces led by Magistrate **Alexander** and **Collector Scott** (riding elephants with Calcutta militia).
*   **Bentinck’s Artillery Assault:** Recognising the strategic threat just thirty miles from Calcutta, Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck** dispatched a full military expedition under **Lieutenant-Colonel Stewart** comprising the **11th Native Infantry**, **Bodyguard Cavalry**, and **Horse Artillery**. On **November 19, 1831**, refusing to use musket fire where bamboo held firm, British artillery pounded the **Bansher Kella** with solid iron cannonballs; **Titu Mir** and 50 peasants died fighting in the ruins, the bamboo fort was burned to ashes, and **Ghulam Masum** was publicly hanged in front of the fort while 350 peasants were sentenced to hard labor.

### 5. Syncretic and Sectarian Anti-Imperial Movements: Pagal Panthis and Kukas

#### Karam Shah, Tipu Shah, and the Pagal Panthi Uprising in Mymensingh (1825–1833)

Along the northern frontier of **Mymensingh** district (**Sherpur Pargana**, bordering the **Garo Hills** in Eastern Bengal), the semi-tribal **Hajong** and **Garo** peasantry faced dual extraction: arbitrary illegal cesses (*Abwabs*) imposed by the local Zamindars to recoup high **Permanent Settlement** assessments, and forced labor/supplies demanded by the British military during the **First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826)**. Here, resistance coalesced around the **Pagal Panthis** ("Followers of the Mystic/Mad Path"), a syncretic religious order founded in the late eighteenth century by **Karam Shah** (a *Darvesh* influenced by Sufi fakirism and Hindu Vaishnava-Shaiva egalitarianism).

*   **Ideological Egalitarianism:** **Karam Shah** preached a doctrine of universal equality, fraternity, and truthfulness, rejecting caste hierarchy and Brahmanical/Mullawi orthodoxy by addressing all initiates—Garo, Hajong, Muslim, or Hindu—simply as *Bhai-Sahib* (brother).
*   **Tipu Shah’s Peasant State (1825–1833):** Following Karam Shah’s death in **1813**, his son **Tipu Shah** assumed leadership and politicized the sect against Zamindari extortion. In **1825**, **Tipu Shah** decreed that no Pagal Panthi peasant should pay rent exceeding a fixed minimum ceiling (**4 annas per rupee**), led armed peasant bands to plunder the houses of oppressive Zamindars at **Sherpur**, and established an independent parallel administration at **Garjaripa** (an ancient mud fort), appointing his own judge, magistrate, and revenue collector. Although the British arrested **Tipu Shah** twice, peasant insurgency under his lieutenants (**Janku Pathar** and **Dobraj Pathar**) persisted until **1833**, when the colonial government was forced to intervene, reduce the rent burden, and survey the frontier holdings.

#### Baba Ram Singh and the Kuka (Namdhari) Movement in Punjab (1840–1872)

Following the British annexation of Punjab in **1849**, the dismantling of the **Khalsa** army, the introduction of the cash-based **Mahalwari** settlement, and the opening of municipal cattle-slaughterhouses (*Kamelas*) in sacred Sikh cities provoked the **Kuka (Namdhari) Movement**—a movement that began as a puritanical socio-religious purification of Sikhism and evolved into a pioneering proto-**Swadeshi** and non-cooperation campaign against the British Raj.

*   **Genesis (Bhagat Jawahar Mal & Baba Balak Singh):** Founded around **1840** in Western Punjab by **Bhagat Jawahar Mal** (popularly called **Sian Saheb**) and his disciple **Baba Balak Singh** at **Hazro** (Rawalpindi), the sect was called **Namdhari** because it emphasized the constant recitation of the Divine Name (*Nam*), and **Kuka** ("Crier/Shouter") because its followers emitted high-pitched ecstatic cries (*Kuks*) during devotional congregational singing (*Kirtan*).
*   **Baba Ram Singh and the Sant Khalsa (1857):** After Baba Balak Singh’s death, leadership passed to **Baba Ram Singh (1816–1885)**, a carpenter (*Ramgarhia*) by caste and former soldier in **Prince Nau Nihal Singh’s** regiment of the Khalsa Army, who established his headquarters at **Bhaini Sahib** (Ludhiana district) on **Baisakhi day, April 1857**, founding the **Sant Khalsa**.
*   **Socio-Religious Reform & Gender Egalitarianism:** **Baba Ram Singh** sought to purge Sikhism of Brahmanical ritualism, idol worship, caste distinctions, and the corrupt hereditary priesthood (*Mahants* and *Sodhis/Bedis*). He strictly prohibited female infanticide, child marriage, dowry, and the sale of daughters; instituted the inexpensive **Anand Karaj** marriage ceremony (costing just Rs 1.25); allowed widow remarriage; and initiated women equally into the Khalsa with the *Amrit* baptism, dressing followers exclusively in home-spun white khadi garments and white turbans tied straight across the forehead (*Sidhi Pag*), carrying a bamboo staff (*Lathi*) instead of a sword, and wearing a woollen rosary (*Mala*).
*   **The Proto-Swadeshi & Parallel Government Apparatus:** Anticipating **Mahatma Gandhi’s Non-Cooperation Movement** by sixty years, **Baba Ram Singh** instituted a comprehensive boycott of British colonial institutions across Punjab:
    1.  **Institutional Boycott:** Kukas boycotted British law courts (settling disputes in village *Kuka Panchayats*), government schools, foreign mill-made cloth, and British railway and postal networks.
    2.  **Parallel Postal & Administrative System (Subas):** **Baba Ram Singh** divided Punjab into **22 provinces**, each administered by a **Suba** (Governor, including a female Suba, **Hukmi**), supported by *Naib Subas* and *Jathedars*, and operated an extraordinarily efficient clandestine **Kuka Postal Service** utilizing runners who carried coded messages across Punjab faster than the Imperial Post.
    3.  **Diplomatic Outreach:** Kuka emissaries were dispatched to the **Maharaja of Kashmir** (where a Kuka regiment was briefly formed), the **King of Nepal**, and **Russian authorities in Central Asia** to seek military alliances against the British.
*   **The Cow-Slaughter Clashes and Malerkotla Execution (1871–1872):** Tensions boiled over when militant Kukas attacked colonial slaughterhouses and killed Muslim butchers at **Amritsar (June 1871)** and **Raikot (July 1871)**. In **January 1872**, following the annual *Maghi Mela* at **Bhaini Sahib**, a band of **150 militant Kukas** led by **Hira Singh** and **Lehna Singh**—defying Baba Ram Singh’s counsel of restraint—attacked **Malodh Fort** and then marched on the princely town of **Malerkotla** on **January 15, 1872** to seize weapons and avenge cow-slaughter. After a fierce battle in which 8 Kukas and 10 state soldiers died, 68 Kukas (including 2 women) surrendered in neighbouring Patiala territory and were handed over to **L. Cowan**, the British Deputy Commissioner of **Ludhiana**. Without any judicial trial or authorization from his superior (Commissioner **T.D. Forsyth**), **Cowan** had **49 Kukas tied to the mouths of British cannons and blown to pieces** on the **Malerkotla** parade ground on **January 17, 1872** (one thirteen-year-old boy, **Bishan Singh**, who lunged at Cowan’s beard after being called a child, was hacked to pieces with swords), while Forsyth arrived the next day and executed the remaining **16 Kukas**. **Baba Ram Singh** was arrested under **Regulation III of 1818** and deported to **Rangoon** and **Mergui (Burma)**, where he died in captivity in **1885**.

## III. Adivasi (Tribal) Insurrections against Ecological Expropriation, Diku Intrusion, and Forced Labor (1770s–1900)

### 6. The Chotanagpur & Rajmahal Crucible: Pahariya, Tilka Manjhi, Kol, Ho, and Khond Uprisings

Whereas plains peasantries fought over the quantum of rent and occupancy security within an established agrarian order, the **Adivasi (Tribal)** insurrections of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were existential, totalizing wars waged to defend an entire communal mode of production and ecological lifeworld. Across the forested plateaus of **Chotanagpur**, **Santhal Parganas**, **Odisha**, the **Eastern and Western Ghats**, and the **North-East**, indigenous communities practiced either shifting cultivation (**Jhum**, **Podu**, **Bewar**, **Kurwa**) mixed with hunting-gathering or communal lineage-based plough agriculture (**Khuntkatti** and **Bhuinhari** tenures), where land belonged collectively to the clan that first cleared the primeval forest and village headmen (**Munda**, **Manjhi**, **Manki**, **Patel**) functioned as primus inter pares rather than rent-extracting landlords.

Colonial penetration shattered this equilibrium through a four-stage structural assault:
1.  **Tenurial Privatization & Diku Intrusion:** The British recognized tribal chieftains or Hinduized rajas as private **Zamindars**, who imported non-tribal outsiders (**Dikus**—Hindu, Sikh, and Muslim *Thikadars* [revenue farmers], *Jagirdars*, and *Mahajans* [usurers]) to clear lands and extract cash rents.
2.  **Debt-Bondage & Forced Labor (Beth Begari & Kamioti):** Unable to pay cash taxes, Adivasis fell into intergenerational debt traps (**Kamioti**) and were forced by **Thikadars** and landlords to perform unpaid agricultural labor (**Beth Begari**) on their own alienated ancestral lands.
3.  **Excise & Judicial Traps:** The colonial state abolished customary home-brewing of sacred rice beer (*Pachwai / Handia*) in favor of licensed **Abkari** liquor contractors (*Kalals*), while distant British **Adalats** and corrupt **Darogas** enforced fraudulent debt deeds written in alien scripts.
4.  **Ecological Expropriation:** Culminating in the **Indian Forest Acts of 1865 and 1878**, the colonial state enclosed forests as state monopolies for railway timber sleepers, criminalizing Adivasi **Jhum** cultivation, grazing, and minor forest produce collection.

#### The Pahariya Rebellion (1772–1780s) and Tilka Manjhi’s Insurgency (1784–1785)

The earliest recorded Adivasi resistance against the Company occurred in the **Rajmahal Hills (Jungleterry)** of Bihar/Bengal, inhabited by the **Pahariyas** (*Maler*), who subsisted on **Jhum** cultivation (using the digging stick/hoe) and forest produce while levying customary transit tolls on plains merchants.

*   **The Pahariya Wars (1772–1780s):** Following the **1770 Famine**, as the Company expanded settled plough cultivation into the Rajmahal foothills to boost **Diwani** revenue, the Pahariyas launched devastating raids on plains Zamindaris in **1772**, **1778**, and **1779**. After military campaigns under **Captain Brooke** and **Captain Browne** failed to subdue the hill fastnesses, **Augustus Cleveland** (Collector of Bhagalpur, **1779–1784**) instituted a paternalist "Pacification Policy": he granted monthly cash stipends to Pahariya chiefs (*Sardars* and *Naibs*), exempted the hills from regular court jurisdiction via a tribal assembly (**Hill Assembly**), and raised an auxiliary corps of **Pahariya Hill Rangers** to police the frontier.
*   **Tilka Manjhi (Jabra Pahariya, 1784–1785):** Rejecting Cleveland’s stipendiary divide-and-rule policy, **Tilka Manjhi** (born **Jabra Pahariya** in **1750**), a charismatic Adivasi leader of the **Santhal/Pahariya** frontier around **Sultanganj** and **Bhagalpur**, organized an armed guerrilla insurgency against both British revenue collectors and compliant hill chiefs. Operating from the **Tilakpur** forests and circulating **Sal** leaves to mobilize villagers, **Tilka Manjhi** raided the Company treasury at **Bhagalpur** and, on **January 13, 1784**, ambushed **Augustus Cleveland**, fatally shooting him with a poisoned arrow from a palm tree (Cleveland died shortly after on a ship to Cape Town). Surrounded by British forces under **General Eyre Coote** and **Bura Sahib** in the **Sultanganj Hills**, **Tilka Manjhi** was captured in **1785**, dragged behind four horses all the way to **Bhagalpur**, and publicly hanged from a **Banyan tree** (making him one of the very first Adivasi martyrs of anti-colonial resistance).

#### The Ho (1820–1837) and Kol Uprising (1831–1832) under Buddho Bhagat

In the **Chotanagpur** plateau and **Singhbhum**, the **Ho** (*Larka Kols* or "Fighting Kols"), **Mundas**, and **Oraons** lived in autonomous village confederacies (**Parhas** or **Pirs**) under village headmen (**Mundas**) and confederacy chiefs (**Mankis**).

*   **The Ho Uprisings in Singhbhum (1820–1821 & 1831–1837):** In **1820**, the **Raja of Porahat (Ghanashyam Singh)** signed a subordinate alliance with the British purely to obtain Company troops to subjugate the fiercely independent **Ho** tribals of **Kolhan** (Singhbhum), who refused to pay him tax. When **Major Roughsedge** marched into **Kolhan** with artillery in **1820**, the **Hos** armed only with bows and battle-axes (*Tangi*) fought him furiously at **Chaibasa** before being forced into temporary submission in **1821**. The Hos rose again during the **1831–32 Kol Uprising** and continued resisting until **1837**, when the British detached **Kolhan** from the local Rajas and made it a directly administered **Government Estate (Kolhan Estate)** under **Wilkinson’s Rules (1837)**, preserving the traditional **Munda-Manki** administrative system.
*   **The Great Kol Uprising (1831–1832):** In **Chotanagpur proper** (**Ranchi**, **Hazaribagh**, **Palamau**, and **Manbhum**), the **Maharaja of Chotanagpur** and his brothers began paying off debts by leasing blocks of tribal villages (**Pattis**) to **Diku Thikadars**—Sikh horse-dealers, Muslim cloth merchants, and Hindu moneylenders—who systematically dispossessed traditional **Munda** and **Manki** headmen, imposed **Beth Begari**, levied taxes on **Handia** (rice beer) and opium poppy cultivation, and dishonored Adivasi women.
    *   *The Immediate Spark (Lankah Assembly, Dec 1831):* When **Kunwar Harnath Sahi** (brother of the Maharaja) dispossessed **Singrai Manki** of twelve villages in **Sonepur Pargana**, handed them to Sikh **Thikadars** who abducted and dishonored two of Singrai’s sisters, and when a Muslim farmer (**Jafar Ali**) seized the villages and abducted the wife of **Surga Munda** of **Bandgaon**, **700 Mundas, Oraons, and Hos** assembled at **Lankah** in **Tamar** in **December 1831**.
    *   *Trajectory & Buddho Bhagat’s Heroism:* Circulating the war arrow (*Dali*), the insurgency swept across **Ranchi**, **Singhbhum**, **Hazaribagh**, **Palamau**, and **Manbhum** within weeks, joined by the **Cheros** and **Kharwars**. Sparing local tribal artisans (blacksmiths, carpenters, and potters), the Kols systematically burned over 1,000 **Diku** houses, killed oppressive **Thikadars**, and wiped out British police posts. The most formidable resistance in the **Choria / Sillagaon** sector (Ranchi) was led by **Buddho Bhagat** of **Sillagaon** ( alongside **Joao Bhagat**, **Madara Mahato**, **Bindrai Manki**, and **Surga Munda**). On **February 13–14, 1832**, when **Captain Impey** attacked **Sillagaon** with cavalry and infantry, **300 Kols** formed a human shield around **Buddho Bhagat**, refusing to retreat as British musketry cut them down; **Buddho Bhagat**, his brother, and his two sons **Halधर** and **Giridhar** perished on the battlefield.
    *   *Statutory Restructuring (Regulation XIII of 1833):* Recognizing that regular Bengal civil laws were the root cause of the rebellion, the Company enacted **Regulation XIII of 1833**, abolishing regular courts in the region and constituting a special **Non-Regulation Province** called the **South-West Frontier Agency (SWFA)**, headquartered at **Kishunpur (Ranchi)** under the paternalist administration of **Captain Thomas Wilkinson** (who drafted **Wilkinson’s Rules** to restrict land sales and utilize tribal *Panchayats*).

#### Chakra Bisoi and the Khond Uprisings in Odisha (1837–1856)

Across the mountainous Eastern Ghats extending from **Ghumsur** and **Baudh** in Odisha to **Kalahandi** and **Visakhapatnam**, the **Khonds (Kandhas)** lived under clan patriarchs (**Malliks** and **Bisois**) owing nominal allegiance to hill Rajas. In **1835**, when the British invaded **Ghumsur** to depose **Raja Dhananjaya Bhanja** for revenue arrears, the Khonds—led initially by **Dora Bisoi** (*Kamala Lochan Dora Bisoi*, the Commander-in-Chief of Ghumsur)—waged a fierce guerrilla war (**1835–1837**) until Dora Bisoi was captured and died in **Gooty Jail (1846)**.

*   **The Meriah Agency and Chakra Bisoi (1846–1856):** In **1845**, the British established the **Meriah Agency** under **Captain Samuel Charters Macpherson** to forcibly stamp out the Khonds’ customary fertility ritual of **Meriah** (human sacrifice to the Earth Goddess *Tari Pennu*) and **female infanticide**, while simultaneously introducing colonial land taxes and **Beth Begari** road-building. Viewing Macpherson’s seizure of *Meriah* victims and humiliation of Baudh chiefs as a prelude to taxing their ancestral hills and enslaving them, the Khonds of **Ghumsur**, **Baudh**, **Daspalla**, and **Kalahandi** rose in a massive ten-year insurgency (**1846–1856**) led by Dora Bisoi’s nephew, **Chakra Bisoi**. Allied with **Nabaghan Konhoro** and supported in **Parlakhemundi (1856–57)** by **Radhakrishna Dandasena** of the **Savara** tribe (who was captured and hanged in **1857**), **Chakra Bisoi** forced the temporary recall of Macpherson in **1847**, evaded every British military column across the Odisha-Central Provinces forests, and vanished undefeated from colonial records around **1856**.

### 7. The Santhal Hool (1855–1856) and the Politics of Damin-i-Koh

#### The Damin-i-Koh Enclosure, Mahajani Bondage (Kamioti), and the Thakur’s Mandate

The most massive, best-organized Adivasi war against the colonial-usurer nexus before **1857** was the **Santhal Hool** (*Hool* meaning "Sacred Rebellion/Liberation Movement" in Santhali) of **1855–1856**. Driven from their earlier settlements in **Birbhum**, **Bankura**, and **Midnapore** by the **1793 Permanent Settlement**, the **Santhals**—skilled clearers of dense forest and industrious plough cultivators—migrated northward into the forested valleys surrounding the **Rajmahal Hills**. Because the indigenous hill **Pahariyas** refused to abandon shifting cultivation (**Jhum**) to come down to the valleys and pay cash revenue, the East India Company demarcated a **1,366-square-mile** forested territory in **1832–1833** called **Damin-i-Koh** ("Skirts of the Hills," surveyed by **John Petty Ward** and administered by Superintendent **James Pontet**), explicitly inviting the **Santhals** to clear the jungle and settle as plough agriculturists.

*   **The Demographic & Revenue Boom (1832–1851):** Between **1838 and 1851**, the number of Santhal villages inside **Damin-i-Koh** exploded from **40 to 1,473**, the Santhal population surged from **3,000 to over 82,000**, and the Company’s annual land revenue from the tract jumped more than **twenty-two-fold** (from Rs 2,000 in 1838 to Rs 43,918 in 1851).
*   **The Diku–Mahajan–Daroga Trap:** However, once the Santhals had transformed primeval forest into fertile paddy fields, the colonial state raised cash assessments and opened **Damin-i-Koh** to rapacious plains **Dikus**—specifically Bengali and Bhojpuri **Mahajans** and **Sahukars** (from Burdwan and Shahabad)—who trapped non-literate Santhals in catastrophic debt-bondage:
    1.  **Fraudulent Weights (*Kenaram* and *Becharam*):** Moneylenders lent grain or cash at **50% to 500% compound interest**, using a light false weight called **Becharam** ("the seller") when advancing grain to Santhals, and a heavy false weight called **Kenaram** ("the buyer") when weighing the Santhal’s harvest at repayment, confiscating the entire crop along with cattle and ploughs while the principal remained unpaid.
    2.  **Kamioti / Harwahi Bondage:** Indebted Santhals were forced to sign hereditary **Kamioti** bonds, reducing entire families to generational serfs working without wages on lands they had originally cleared.
    3.  **Daroga & Naib Suzawal Corruption:** When Santhals petitioned the British Superintendent (**Pontet**) or local courts at **Bhagalpur** and **Birbhum**, the **Naib Suzawals** (native revenue collectors) and **Darogas** (such as the notoriously corrupt **Mahesh Lal Datta** of Dighee Thana), who were on the payroll of the **Mahajans**, arrested the Santhal complainants instead. Simultaneously, European contractors constructing the **East Indian Railway** line through Rajmahal abducted Santhal women, seized goats and poultry without payment, and coerced unpaid labor.

#### Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand, and Bhairav Murmu: Military Trajectory and the Santhal Parganas Act

In **early 1855**, four landless Santhal brothers of **Bhagnadihi** village (near Barhait in the heart of **Damin-i-Koh**)—**Sidhu Murmu**, **Kanhu Murmu**, **Chand Murmu**, and **Bhairav Murmu** (alongside their sisters **Phulo** and **Jhano Murmu**, who actively participated in night raids on British camps)—proclaimed that the Supreme Deity, **Thakur (Chando Bonga)**, had appeared to **Sidhu** and **Kanhu** in seven forms (including a luminous cartwheel, a flame, and a book of paper falling from heaven) commanding the Santhals to annihilate the **Dikus** and establish **Abua Raj** ("Our Own Rule"), where land tax would be fixed at a nominal **2 annas per buffalo plough** and **half an anna per bullock plough**.

*   **The Bhagnadihi Assembly (June 30, 1855):** Circulating the **Sal** branch across hundreds of villages, the four brothers assembled **10,000 Santhals** at **Bhagnadihi** on **June 30, 1855** (*Hool Dibas*). Remarkably, before resorting to arms, **Sidhu** and **Kanhu** followed subaltern legal protocol: they dispatched formal written ultimatums (*Parwanas*) to the **Governor-General at Calcutta**, the **Commissioner of Bhagalpur**, and the **Collectors and Darogas** of Bhagalpur and Birbhum, explaining that the **Mahajans** and **Darogas** had committed unpardonable sins and ordering all **Dikus** to vacate **Damin-i-Koh** immediately.
*   **Outbreak and Sweep (July–November 1855):** On **July 7, 1855**, when **Daroga Mahesh Lal Datta** arrived at **Panchkethia** funded by Mahajans with bamboo handcuffs to arrest **Sidhu** and **Kanhu**, **Sidhu** personally executed him and the notorious usurer **Kenaram Bhagat**. Within weeks, **60,000 Santhals** armed with bows, poisoned arrows, and battle-axes—supported by low-caste non-tribal village artisans (*Kumhars* [potters], *Telis* [oilmen], *Kamars* [blacksmiths], *Momins* [weavers], and *Gwalas* [milkmen], whom the Santhals exempted from attack as productive allies)—paralyzed British rule across a 300-mile corridor from **Bhagalpur** and **Rajmahal** to **Birbhum** and **Murshidabad**. They routed **Major Burroughs’** troops at **Pirpainti (July 16, 1855)**, burned **Pakur** and **Maheshpur**, and cut the Calcutta-Delhi telegraph and postal lines.
*   **Martial Law and Suppression (November 1855 – February 1856):** Panicked, **Lord Dalhousie’s** government deployed **14,000 troops** under **Major-General Lloyd** and **Brigadier-General Bird**, mounted elephants to trample bamboo huts, and proclaimed **Martial Law on November 10, 1855** (offering a Rs 10,000 reward for the leaders). In epic uneven battles—such as at **Sangrampur** and **Naghore**, where Santhals stood in open ranks firing arrows into musket volleys refusing to surrender—over **15,000 to 20,000 Santhals** were slaughtered. **Sidhu** was betrayed and executed in **August 1855** (or early 1856), **Chand** and **Bhairav** were killed in action near **Barhait**, and **Kanhu** was captured in **February 1856** and publicly hanged at **Bhagnadihi**.
*   **Statutory Concessions (Santhal Parganas Act, 1855):** To prevent another Hool, the Company passed **Act XXXVII of 1855**, carving out **5,470 square miles** from Bhagalpur and Birbhum into a special **Non-Regulation District** called the **Santhal Parganas** (exempted from general civil laws and placed under a Deputy Commissioner and four Assistant Commissioners). Furthermore, the **Santhal Parganas Police Rules (Yule’s Rules, 1856)** abolished the corrupt **Daroga** system altogether, restoring police and judicial authority directly to traditional Santhal village headmen (**Manjhis**) and pargana chiefs (**Parganites**), while the **Santhal Parganas Settlement Regulation (III of 1872)** later capped interest rates and prohibited land alienation.

### 8. Western, Southern, and North-Eastern Frontier Adivasi Resistances

#### Western & Central Ghats: Bhil Uprisings (1817–1913) and Koli Insurrections (1829–1848)

Across the hill ranges of **Khandesh**, **Malwa**, **Mewar**, and southern **Rajasthan/Gujarat**, the **Bhils**—who controlled the mountain passes (*Ghats*) and collected customary transit tolls (*Bhom* and *Rakhwali*)—resisted British penetration throughout the nineteenth century:

*   **Khandesh Bhil Uprisings (1817–1846):** Immediately after the British annexed **Khandesh** from the Peshwa in **1818**, the Bhils rose in arms (**1817–19**), followed by a major insurrection in **1825** led by **Sewaram** (a blacksmith-chief of Baglana), and subsequent outbreaks in **1831** and **1846**. While **Mountstuart Elphinstone** and **Lieutenant James Outram** eventually pacified sections of the Khandesh Bhils by raising the **Khandesh Bhil Corps (1825)** and granting agricultural taccavi loans, late-nineteenth-century forest laws triggered the Robin Hood-style insurgency of **Tantya Bhil (Tantya Mama, 1878–1889)** across Nimar and Central Provinces until his execution at Jabalpur in **1889**.
*   **Govind Guru and the Mangarh Bhagat Movement (1883–1913):** In the princely states of southern Rajasthan and Gujarat (**Dungarpur**, **Banswara**, **Sunth**, and **Idar**), where Bhils were subjected to crushing feudal **Begar** (forced labor) and liquor monopolies, **Govind Guru (1858–1931)**—a nomadic **Banjara** social reformer influenced by **Dayananda Saraswati’s Arya Samaj**—founded the **Samp Sabha (1883)** ("Assembly of Unity/Fraternity") and the **Bhagat Movement**. Preaching monotheism, vegetarianism, total abstinence from liquor (which devastated colonial and princely *Abkari* revenues), refusal of **Begar**, and setting up village *Dhunis* (sacred fire altars) and Bhil schools, Govind Guru assembled tens of thousands of Bhils at **Mangarh Hill** (on the Banswara-Sunth border) in **October–November 1913** to establish a moral **Bhil Raj**. On **November 17, 1913**, a combined force of the **Mewar Bhil Corps**, **104th Wellesley’s Rifles**, and princely troops surrounded **Mangarh Hill** and opened fire with machine guns and artillery, massacring **over 1,500 Bhils** (an atrocity six years prior to Jallianwala Bagh) and sentencing **Govind Guru** to life imprisonment at **Hyderabad Jail** (later released in 1919).
*   **The Koli Uprisings in the Sahyadris (1829–1848):** Neighbouring the Bhils and Ramosis in the **Western Ghats** (**Poona**, **Ahmednagar**, **Nasik**, and **Gujarat**), the **Mahadeo Kolis**—traditional fort guards who lost their livelihoods when the British dismantled Maratha hill forts—launched three major uprisings: in **1829** (under **Ramji Bhangre** and **Rama Kirva**), in **1839** (storming the **Ghodnadi** treasury at Poona proclaiming the restoration of the Peshwa), and most fiercely between **1844 and 1848** under **Raghoji Bhangre**, who targeted oppressive **Marwari Sahukars** across Ahmednagar and Nasik (cutting off the noses of usurers and burning their account books) before he was captured at Pandharpur and hanged at **Thane** in **May 1848**.

#### Eastern Ghats & Godavari Agency: The Koya and Rampa Rebellions (1879–1880)

In the forested **Chodavaram (Rampa)** hill tract of the **Godavari Agency** (Andhra Pradesh), the **Koya** and **Konda Dora** Adivasis were subjected to ruthless extortion by hereditary estate-holders (**Mansabdars** and **Muttadars**) backed by the British police, who imposed new taxes on **Podu** (shifting cultivation), seized forest grazing rights under the **Madras Forest Rules**, and enacted an **Abkari Act** banning the Koyas from tapping their own **Toddy** palm trees (*Jeelugu*) while leasing toddy rights to plains merchants.

*   **Tomma Sora (Tamma Dora) and the 1879–1880 Rampa Rebellion:** Following smaller Koya outbreaks in **1840**, **1845**, **1858**, and **1862**, a full-scale rebellion erupted in **March 1879** in **Rampa** when the Mansabdar of Chodavaram tripled the **Toddy Tax**. Led by **Tomma Sora (Tamma Dora)** of **Koyyuru**—alongside **Chendrayya**, **Ambul Reddi**, and **Rajanna**—the Koyas captured six policemen for ritual sacrifice at **Boduluru**, burned **Chodavaram** and **Rajahmundry** police stations, and waged a mobile jungle war across **5,000 square miles** of the Godavari and Visakhapatnam hills. Proclaimed *Raja of Malkangiri* by his followers, **Tomma Sora** required the deployment of six regiments of the **Madras Infantry** before he was shot dead by the police in **July 1880** (and Chendrayya killed in **October 1880**). A subsequent Koya rising under **Raja Anantayyar** in **1886** sought support from the **Maharaja of Jeypore** as a religious struggle (*Rama Dandu*), laying the foundation for **Alluri Sitarama Raju’s** famous **1922–24 Rampa Uprising**.

#### The North-Eastern Frontier: Khasi (1829–1833), Singpho (1830–1843), and Naga Resistances

Following the **1826 Treaty of Yandabo**, British military-commercial penetration into the hills surrounding the **Brahmaputra** and **Surma** valleys ignited fierce frontier wars:

*   **Tirot Sing and the Khasi Uprising (1829–1833):** Occupying the strategic **Jaintia and Khasi Hills** between the **Brahmaputra Valley** (Assam) and the **Surma Valley** (Sylhet), the **Khasis** were governed by a democratic council (*Dorbar*) of thirty chiefs (**Syiems**). In **1826**, British Agent **David Scott** persuaded **U Tirot Sing** (the **Syiem of Nongkhlaw**) to allow the construction of a military road linking **Guwahati** to **Sylhet** through the Khasi Hills. However, when British soldiers, Bengali clerks, and convicts swarmed into Nongkhlaw, abused Khasi villagers, and threatened to impose lowland house taxes on the hills, **Tirot Sing** convened a **Khasi Dorbar** (supported by **Bar Manik**, Syiem of Mylliem, **Bormanik**, **Zabor Singh**, and **Monbhut**) and ordered the British to vacate. On **April 4, 1829**, **Tirot Sing’s** warriors—joined by the **Garos** and **Khamtis**—attacked the **Nongkhlaw** garrison, killing **Lieutenants Bedingfield** and **Burlton** along with 60 sepoys and burning the British sanitarium. For four years, **Tirot Sing** waged an extraordinary guerrilla campaign from hill caves using only bows, swords, and shields against British artillery and economic blockades before surrendering on **January 13, 1833** (under a British pledge to spare his life); deported as a state prisoner to **Dacca**, **Tirot Sing** died in captivity on **July 17, 1835**.
*   **The Singpho Rebellions (1830–1843):** In the extreme eastern frontier of **Upper Assam** (**Sadiya** and **Tirap**, bordering Burma), where the British discovered indigenous **Assam Tea** plants and began encroaching on tribal lands while freeing **Singpho** agricultural slaves (*Bandhas*), the **Singphos** launched a coordinated series of rebellions:
    1.  In **1830**, a Singpho chief named **Wakum Kunjob**, coordinating with **Gomdhar Konwar’s** Ahom rebels and the **Khamtis**, marched with **3,000 warriors** on **Sadiya**, before being repulsed by **Captain Neufville**.
    2.  In **1839**, the neighboring **Khamtis** under **Ranua Gohain** stormed the **Sadiya** garrison and killed the British Political Agent, **Colonel Adam White**, along with 80 soldiers.
    3.  In **January 1843**, the Singphos rose in a massive rebellion led by **Nirang Phidu** and **Beesa Gaum**, attacking the British military outpost at **Beesa** and **Ningroo**, holding out for months until British artillery forced their surrender.
*   **The Naga Resistances (1832–1880):** Beginning in **1832**, when **Captains Jenkins and Pemberton** marched through the **Angami Naga** hills to open a route between **Manipur** and **Assam**, the **Nagas** resisted every British punitive expedition (over ten military expeditions between **1835 and 1851**). In **October 1879**, when British Political Officer **G.H. Damant** attempted to seize weapons at the fortified Angami village of **Khonoma**, the Nagas shot **Damant** dead and laid a twelve-day siege to the British garrison at **Kohima**, before British reinforcements stormed **Khonoma** in **November 1879**, finally subduing the region in **1880**.

### 9. Birsa Munda’s Ulgulan (1899–1900) and the Statutory Defense of Khuntkatti

#### Erosion of Khuntkatti Communal Tenure, Beth Begari, and the Birsait Millenarian Vision

The culmination of nineteenth-century Adivasi insurgency—exhibiting the highest synthesis of anti-feudal agrarian resistance, socio-religious revitalization, and explicit anti-colonial sovereignty—was **Birsa Munda’s Ulgulan** ("The Great Tumult") of **1899–1900** in the **Ranchi** and **Singhbhum** districts of **Chotanagpur**. For centuries, the **Mundas** had held their villages under the **Khuntkatti** system: the descendants of the original founders (*Khuntkattidars*) who cleared the primeval forest owned the village land, upland (*Tanr*), lowland (*Don*), and surrounding forest (**Sarna** sacred groves) collectively as a lineage brotherhood, paying only a nominal collective subscription (**Chanda**) to the **Munda** (village headman) and **Manki** (head of a *Patti* of 10–12 villages), with zero individual rent or landlordism.

*   **The Destruction of Khuntkatti & The Sardari Larai (1858–1895):** Throughout the nineteenth century, the Hinduized **Nagvanshi Maharaja of Chotanagpur** granted villages to Hindu, Muslim, and Sikh **Jagirdars** and **Thikadars** (**Dikus**), who seized **Khuntkatti** lands as their private **Rajhas** lands, reduced the founding Mundas to rack-rented tenants, imposed compulsory unpaid labor (**Beth Begari**) and illegal cesses (**Rakumats**), and enclosed village forests under the **Indian Forest Act of 1878**. For nearly forty years (**1858–1895**), educated Munda headmen (**Sardars**) waged a peaceful legal, petitioning, and no-rent campaign known as the **Sardari Larai (or Mulki Larai)**—converting in large numbers to **German Lutheran** and **Roman Catholic** missions on the promise that European missionaries would help recover their ancestral lands in British courts. When both the colonial courts (even after the partial **Chotanagpur Tenures Act of 1869**, which registered only *Bhuinhari* lands while ignoring **Khuntkatti**) and the missionaries betrayed their land claims, the **Sardars** turned from legal petitions to revolutionary millenarianism under **Birsa Munda**.
*   **Birsa Munda (1875–1900) as Dharti Aba:** Born on **November 15, 1875** at **Ulihatu** (Ranchi district) to a poor Munda sharecropper (**Sugana Munda**), **Birsa Munda** was educated at the **German Mission School at Chaibasa** (where he left after a missionary insulted Munda land rights) and subsequently studied Vaishnava traditions for three years under **Anand Panre** at **Bandgaon**. In **1894–1895**, at **Chalkad** village, **Birsa** experienced a prophetic vision and proclaimed himself the messenger of **Singbonga** (the Supreme Sun-God), revered by Mundas and Oraons as **Dharti Aba** ("Father of the Earth") and **Birsa Bhagwan**.
    *   *The Birsait Socio-Religious Reform:* Birsa initially launched a puritanical reform movement (**Birsait**) to morally regenerate Adivasi society: he abolished polytheistic blood sacrifices to malevolent spirits (*Bongas*), prohibited the drinking of intoxicants (**Handia** and **Abkari** liquor) which muddled tribal minds for **Sahukar** exploitation, commanded followers to wear the sacred thread (*Janeu*), observe Thursday as a sacred day of rest when no cattle could be yoked to the plough, and maintain strict purity and truth.
    *   *First Arrest (August 1895):* When **Birsa** went beyond religious reform in **1895** to declare that the reign of **Queen Victoria** had ended, that **Munda Raj** had begun, and that no Munda should pay rent to **Zamindars** or **Sarkar**, Deputy Commissioner **Meares** had him arrested at **Chalkad** in **August 1895** and sentenced to two years’ rigorous imprisonment in **Hazaribagh Central Jail**.

#### The Ulgulan (Great Tumult, 1899–1900), Sail Rakab Hill, and the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908)

Released from prison in **November 1897** on the occasion of Queen Victoria’s Diamond Jubilee, **Birsa Munda** found Chotanagpur devastated by the **Famine of 1896–97**, smallpox/cholera epidemics (during which Birsa organized tireless medical nursing brigades), and intensified **Thikadar** evictions. Between **February 1898 and December 1899**, **Birsa** and his chief lieutenant **Gaya Munda** of **Etkedih** (alongside **Soma Munda** and **Donka Munda**) held clandestine night meetings on **Dombari Hill** and **Simbua Hill**, organizing a three-tiered revolutionary army: one tier for religious propagation (*Pracharak*), a second for political organization, and a third military wing tasked with armed guerrilla action.

*   **Ideological Song of the Ulgulan:** Rejecting the earlier **Sardari** illusion that one could fight **Dikus** while remaining loyal to the British Queen, **Birsa** identified the **British Raj**, **Christian Missionaries**, and **Jagirdars/Thikadars** as a single indivisible structure of oppression ("*Saheb Saheb Ek Topi Hai*"—"The British official and the White Missionary wear the same hat"). His followers chanted the famous anti-imperial war song:
    > *"Katong Baba Katong, Saheb Katong Katong, Rari Katong Katong..."* ("Cut down, O Father, cut down the European Sahibs, cut down the Diku outsiders! **Abua Raj Ete Jana, Maharani Raj Tundu Jana**—Let the Kingdom of the Queen be ended, and our Kingdom be established!")
*   **Outbreak and Sail Rakab (Dombari) Hill Massacre (Dec 1899 – Jan 1900):** On **Christmas Eve, December 24, 1899**, the **Ulgulan** erupted simultaneously across **six police station areas** of **Ranchi** (**Khunti**, **Tamar**, **Basia**, **Karra**) and **Singhbhum** (**Chakradharpur**). Armed with bows, arrows, and battle-axes, Munda squads burned **Diku** houses, missionary stations (*Sarwada*, *Murhu*, *Burju*), and attacked the **Khunti** and **Etkedih** police forces (where **Gaya Munda** and his heroic wife **Maki Munda** fought off the police before being captured). On **January 7, 1900**, **300 Mundas** stormed the **Khunti Police Station**, killing Constable Jayram and setting the buildings ablaze.
*   **The Stand at Sail Rakab Hill (January 9, 1900):** The main Munda force—including women and children—entrenched themselves behind stone breastworks atop **Sail Rakab (Dombari) Hill**. On **January 9, 1900**, Commissioner **A. Forbes** and Deputy Commissioner **H.C. Streatfeild** surrounded the hill with the **6th Jats Infantry** and Ranchi Police. When the Mundas refused to surrender, declaring the land was theirs, British troops opened fire with rifle volleys and stormed the ridge, slaughtering between **200 and 400 Mundas** (though official records admitted only 20 deaths) and turning the hillside stream red with blood.
*   **Martyrdom and the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908):** **Birsa Munda** evaded capture in the **Singhbhum** forests until **February 3, 1900**, when seven local traitors (enticed by a Rs 500 British reward) spotted the smoke of his cooking fire in the **Jamkopai Forest (Chakradharpur)** and betrayed him while he slept. Imprisoned in **Ranchi Jail** alongside 482 Munda rebels (**Gaya Munda** and **Sukhram Munda** were hanged), the 24-year-old **Birsa Munda** died in his cell on **June 9, 1900** (officially attributed to cholera, though widely suspected of slow poisoning).
*   **The Statutory Legacy (CNT Act, 1908):** Shaken to its foundations by the **Ulgulan**, the colonial government ordered a comprehensive **Survey and Settlement Operation (1902–1910)** under **John Reid** and **Lister**, heavily assisted by the German Jesuit scholar **Father John Baptist Hoffmann** (who meticulously documented the customary Munda land system), culminating in the landmark **Chotanagpur Tenancy (CNT) Act of 1908**:
    1.  **Statutory Recognition of Khuntkatti:** It gave full legal recognition and protection to **Mundari Khuntkatti** communal tenures.
    2.  **Ban on Land Alienation (Section 46):** It strictly prohibited the transfer or sale of Adivasi (*Raiyati* and *Khuntkatti*) land to non-tribals (**Dikus**), invalidating usurious mortgage foreclosures.
    3.  **Abolition of Beth Begari:** It outlawed compulsory unpaid labor (**Beth Begari**) and restored summary jurisdiction for tribal tenancy disputes to the Deputy Commissioner’s court rather than regular civil courts.

## IV. Post-1857 Late-19th-Century Peasant Movements, Legal-Print Mobilization, and Tenancy Codification (1859–1900)

### 10. Structural Shift in Post-1857 Peasant Resistance: Legalism, Print Capitalism, and Anti-Planter/Anti-Usurer Focus

Following the suppression of the **1857 Revolt** and the **1858 Queen’s Proclamation**—which forged a conservative imperial alliance with princes, **Taluqdars**, and **Zamindars** while disarming the countryside under the **Arms Acts** and expanding railway-telegraph military deployment—the structural character of peasant resistance underwent a decisive paradigm shift during the second half of the nineteenth century (**1859–1900**).

Unlike the pre-1857 insurgencies, which were frequently led by dispossessed feudal chiefs (**Poligars**, **Paik Bakshis**, **Talukdars**) or millenarian prophets seeking to restore pre-colonial kingdoms or establish a sacred **Thakur/Munda Raj**, late-nineteenth-century peasant movements in settled agrarian regions (**Bengal**, **Maharashtra**, **Assam**, **Malabar**) displayed five distinct modernizing characteristics:
1.  **Autonomous Peasant Leadership & Class Combinations:** Princes and feudal chieftains disappeared from the leadership; instead, movements were organized directly by substantial occupancy peasants (*Jotedars*, *Kunbis*, *Head Ryots*), retired minor officials, and village headmen through horizontal **Agrarian Leagues**, **Ryot Sabhas**, and **Raij-Mels**.
2.  **Specific Economic & Tenurial Targets (Non-Sovereign Goals):** Rather than seeking to overthrow the **British Raj**—which peasants recognized possessed overwhelming military superiority—post-1857 movements targeted specific intermediate extractors: European **Indigo Planters** (**1859–60**), rent-enhancing **Zamindars** (**Pabna 1873–85**), and land-grabbing **Sahukars** (**Deccan 1875**), frequently proclaiming tactical loyalty to the Crown ("*We want to be Ryots of Her Majesty the Queen alone*") to pit the colonial state against landlords and planters.
3.  **Weaponization of Colonial Law & Litigation:** Peasants exhibited sophisticated mastery of colonial statutes (such as **Act X of 1859**), pooling collective subscriptions (*Chanda*) to fight prolonged test cases in District and High Courts, depositing contested rents in court registries, and demanding official **Commissions of Inquiry**.
4.  **Syndicalist Tactics (Rent Strikes, Crop Boycotts, & Nai-Dhobi Bandh):** Armed rebellion was largely replaced by non-violent or low-casualty syndicalist coercion: universal cultivation strikes (refusing to sow indigo), coordinated no-rent strikes, and total social boycott (**Nai-Dhobi Bandh**) of landlords, moneylenders, and non-striking peasants.
5.  **Symbiosis with Urban Print Capitalism & Intelligentsia:** For the first time, rural peasant struggles forged direct institutional links with the emerging urban middle-class intelligentsia, vernacular newspapers (*The Hindu Patriot*, *Amrita Bazar Patrika*, *Somprakash*), political associations (*Indian Association*, *Poona Sarvajanik Sabha*), and protest theatre (*Nil Darpan*), compelling the colonial state to enact landmark **Tenancy and Agrarian Relief Acts**.

### 11. The Indigo Revolt (Nil Bidroha, 1859–1860), Cultural Counter-Hegemony, and the Indigo Commission

#### Political Economy of Nij and Ryoti Cultivation: Dadon Advances and Planter Coercion

Following the collapse of West Indian slave-grown indigo in the late eighteenth century, **Bengal and Bihar** became the world’s premier supplier of natural blue dye for the British Lancashire textile revolution. European **Indigo Planters** (often former slave-drivers from Jamaica or Company officials) operated across **Nadia**, **Jessore**, **Pabna**, **Faridpur**, **Murshidabad**, **Rajshahi**, and **Malda** under two distinct production systems:
1.  **Nij-abad (Direct Cultivation):** Accounting for less than **25%** of production, the planter cultivated indigo directly on land he owned or leased (*Zirat*) using hired wage labor and factory-owned bullocks and ploughs.
2.  **Ryoti (Contract/Coerced Peasant Cultivation):** Accounting for over **75%** of Bengal’s indigo, the planter forced local peasants (**Ryots**) to sign stamped contracts (*Sattas*) to cultivate indigo on the most fertile **Bhadai** (autumn rice) lands of their own holdings.

*   **The Dadon Debt-Slavery Mechanism:** Under the **Ryoti** system, the planter induced or coerced the peasant to accept a small cash advance (**Dadon** of Rs 2 per bigha) at the start of the season. Once a peasant touched the **Dadon**, he never escaped:
    *   The planter supplied the seed, deducted charges for the stamp paper, seed, cartage, and factory bribes (*Dasturi* to factory *Gomashtas* and *Amins*), and measured the peasant's field with a fraudulent, elongated measuring rod (**Planter’s Bigha** being up to 50% larger than the standard government Bigha).
    *   At harvest, the cut indigo plant was weighed at the factory using rigged scales at fixed, heavily depressed prices (**4 to 6 bundles per Rupee**), leaving the peasant with a net loss on every bigha while food-grain (*Aus* rice) prices doubled in the **1850s**.
    *   The unpaid balance of the **Dadon** was carried forward in the factory ledgers (*Hathchitha*) from father to son for generations; as Lieutenant-Governor **John Peter Grant** later testified, growing indigo yielded the ryot a net loss of **Rs 7 per bigha** compared to rice.
*   **Planter Violence & Magisterial Collusion:** To prevent peasants from sowing lucrative rice, European planters purchased **Zamindari** or **Patni** rights (accelerated by the **Charter Act of 1833**, which allowed Europeans to buy land in India) and maintained private armies of armed club-men (**Lathiyals**) and spear-men (*Spearmen/Peadas*). Recalcitrant ryots were kidnapped and locked inside dark factory godowns (*Kuthis*), flogged with leather whips (*Shamal* or "Shamchand"), had their cattle impounded and houses burned, while British District Magistrates—who dined and hunted with the planters—enforced the draconian **Regulation V of 1830** and later **Act XI of 1860**, which made breach of an indigo civil contract a **criminal offense** punishable by immediate imprisonment.

#### Digambar and Bishnu Charan Biswas, Nil Darpan, and the Indigo Commission (1860)

In **early 1859**, a sympathetic Magistrate of Barasat/Nadia, **Ashley Eden** (and previously **Hem Chandra Kar**, Deputy Magistrate of Kalaroa), issued a vernacular proclamation (**Rubakari**) stating that under British law the police were bound to protect ryots on their own lands and that **no peasant could be forced to accept indigo advances against his will**. Word spread like wildfire across the Bengal countryside that the Queen had abolished compulsory indigo cultivation.

*   **The Govindpur Spark (September 1859):** In **September 1859**, in **Chaugacha** and **Govindpur** village (**Nadia district**), two brothers from the middle-class *Kayastha* caste who had formerly served as *Dewans* in an indigo factory—**Digambar Biswas** and **Bishnu Charan Biswas**—organized the peasants of a hundred surrounding villages into a disciplined union, declaring a total refusal to accept **Dadon** or sow a single seed of indigo. When the notorious planter **William White** of the **Bansberia Factory** sent hundreds of armed **Lathiyals** to attack Govindpur on **September 13, 1859**, **Digambar** and **Bishnu Biswas** mobilized a trained peasant defense force armed with spears, shields, and slingshots (*Bhemul*), routing the planter’s mercenaries. When planters sued the peasants for breach of contract under **Act XI of 1860**, the Biswas brothers spent **Rs 17,000 of their personal fortune** to pay off peasant decrees and hire Calcutta lawyers.
*   **Pan-Bengal Spread & Syndicalist Tactics (1859–1860):** By **Spring 1860**, the **Nil Bidroha** had engulfed **Nadia**, **Jessore**, **Pabna**, **Faridpur**, **Rajshahi**, **Malda**, and **Murshidabad**, led by local peasant commanders including **Rafique Mondal** in Malda, **Kader Molla** in Pabna, and **Ramratan Mullick** (the "Nanak of Bengal") in Jessore. Peasants demonstrated extraordinary cross-communal unity (Hindu *Kayasthas/Sadgops* and Muslim *Faraizis* fighting side-by-side):
    1.  **Universal Cultivation Strike & Social Boycott:** Ryots took sacred oaths on the *Ganga water* and the *Quran* never to touch indigo seed; any villager who worked for a factory faced instant **Nai-Dhobi Bandh** (social excommunication).
    2.  **Rent Strikes against Planter-Zamindars:** When planters tried to use their **Zamindari** powers under **Act X of 1859** to enhance rents or evict striking ryots, peasants launched collective **No-Rent Strikes** and contested every eviction suit in court.
*   **Urban Print Capitalism & Cultural Counter-Hegemony (Nil Darpan, 1860):** Crucially, the Indigo Revolt galvanized the Calcutta intelligentsia:
    *   **Harish Chandra Mukherjee**, the fearless editor of **The Hindu Patriot**, turned his home into a legal aid clinic for Indigo ryots and published devastating daily exposes detailing planter atrocities, aided by field reports from **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** (future founder of the *Amrita Bazar Patrika*, who wrote under the pseudonym *M.L.L.* from Jessore) and **Manmohan Ghosh**.
    *   In **September 1860**, **Dinabandhu Mitra**—an Inspector in the Post Office Department who had witnessed planter terror firsthand across Nadia and Jessore—published at Dacca his monumental Bengali play **Nil Darpan** (*The Indigo Planting Mirror*). Depicting the tragic destruction of the prosperous peasant family of **Goluk Chandra Basu** of **Swaruppur**, the brutal torture of the heroic ryot **Torap**, and the rape and murder of the pregnant peasant woman **Kshetramani** by the sadistic British planters **J.J. Wood** and **P.P. Rose**, **Nil Darpan** electrified urban Bengal and became the foundational text of Indian protest theatre (inaugurating the ** Calcutta National Theatre in December 1872**).
    *   To expose the scandal to British Parliamentarians in London, the play was translated into English by the celebrated poet **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** and published under the supervision of the Christian missionary **Reverend James Long** (with the official sanction of Bengal Secretary **W.S. Seton-Karr**). Outraged, the **Landholders and Commercial Association** (the planters' lobby) prosecuted **Rev. James Long** for criminal libel in the Calcutta Supreme Court in **July 1861**; Judge **Mordaunt Wells** fined Long **Rs 1,000** (paid on the spot in court by the literary patron **Kaliprasanna Singha**) and sentenced him to **one month’s imprisonment**, turning Long into a popular hero celebrated in folk songs: *"Fine of a thousand rupees and prison for a month—Rev. Long’s prison became his crown of glory!"*
*   **The Indigo Commission (1860) and Collapse of Bengal Indigo:** Confronted with total rural paralysis, the Government appointed the **five-member Indigo Commission in March 1860**, chaired by **W.S. Seton-Karr** (with **Richard Temple**, **W.F. Fergusson** representing planters, **Rev. J. Sale** representing missionaries, and **Chandra Mohan Chatterjee** representing the British Indian Association). Testifying before the Commission, **Faridpur Magistrate E.W.L. Tower** delivered the immortal indictment: **"Not a chest of Indigo reached England without being stained with human blood."** Following the Commission's report (**August 1860**), the government refused to renew the coercive **Act XI of 1860** and issued the historic **Notification of November 1860**, proclaiming that **no ryot could be compelled to cultivate indigo against his will** and that all disputes must be settled strictly through civil law. By **1863**, indigo factories across Bengal had shut down permanently, forcing European planters to relocate their operations to **Bihar (Champaran and Darbhanga)**.

### 12. The Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873–1885) and the Bengal Tenancy Act (1885)

#### Rent Enhancement, Illegal Abwabs, and Act X of 1859 Occupancy Struggles

While the **Indigo Revolt** targeted European planters, the **Pabna Agrarian Uprising (1873–1885)** in **Eastern Bengal** marked a watershed confrontation between substantial and middle peasants (**Jotedars** and **Occupancy Ryots**) and indigenous **Zamindars**. Under the **Bengal Rent Act (Act X of 1859)**—the first statutory modification of the **1793 Permanent Settlement**—any peasant (**Ryot**) who had cultivated the same plot of land continuously for **twelve years** was recognized as an **Occupancy Ryot**, protected against arbitrary eviction and entitled to pay a "fair and equitable" rent that could only be enhanced on specific legal grounds (such as a general rise in agricultural prices not due to the ryot's own labor).

*   **The Yusufshahi Pargana Conflict:** In **Yusufshahi Pargana** of **Pabna district** (specifically the **Sirajganj** subdivision, a booming center of commercial **Jute** cultivation), the old **Natore Raj** estate had been partitioned and bought at auction by five aggressive new **Zamindar** families—the **Tagores** (of Calcutta), the **Banerjees**, the **Sanyals**, the **Pakrashis**, and the **Bhaduris**. To capture the windfall profits of the **Jute** boom and prevent peasants from claiming **Act X Occupancy Rights**, these Zamindars launched a systematic offensive:
    1.  **Consolidation of Illegal Abwabs into Base Rent (Jama):** Between **1793 and 1872**, the Zamindars had multiplied rents seven-fold and imposed a plethora of illegal feudal cesses (**Abwabs**—marriage taxes, *Tahuri* clerk fees, *Khoraki* diet money, elephant/horse purchase levies). In **1872–73**, the Zamindars attempted to force peasants to sign fraudulent written agreements (**Kabuliyats**) that permanently merged these illegal **Abwabs** into the legal consolidated rent (**Asal Jama**).
    2.  **Shortening the Measuring Pole (Nul) & Eviction:** Simultaneously, the Zamindars fraudulently shortened the standard land measuring pole (**Hath / Nul**) from 18 inches to 14.5 inches (artificially expanding the taxable area of every peasant plot) and shifted tenants from plot to plot before the completion of twelve years to destroy their **Occupancy** claims.

#### Legal Resistance ("Ryots of Her Majesty the Queen") and the Bengal Tenancy Act (1885)

In **early 1873**, the peasants of **Urkandee** village refused to sign the fraudulent **Kabuliyats**, deposited their old legal rents with the **Munsif’s Court**, and won a landmark civil appeal in **April 1873** when the District Judge of Rajshahi nullified the Zamindars' fabricated documents.

*   **Formation of the Pabna Agrarian League (May 1873):** Galvanized by this courtroom victory, the peasants of **269 villages** across **Yusufshahi Pargana** assembled in **May 1873** to establish the **Pabna Ryots’ League (Agrarian League)**. The League was led by a remarkable triumvirate representing the rural class coalition:
    1.  **Ishan Chandra Roy** of **Dulatpur**—a prosperous small taluqdar and merchant revered by the peasantry as **Ishan Raja** ("King Ishan").
    2.  **Shambhunath Pal**—a former Hindu village surveyor (*Amin*) previously employed in a Zamindar’s estate office, who possessed intimate knowledge of land measurement fraud and court procedures.
    3.  **Khoodi Mollah (Khodi Mulla)**—a charismatic Muslim **Jotedar** (rich peasant) of **Jugirgoalkandi**, who commanded the mass Muslim peasantry.
*   **Operational Mechanics & The Queen’s Ryot Proclamation:** Sounding buffalo horns (*Singas*), musical instruments (*Sankhas*), and beating *Nagada* drums at night to summon thousands of peasants, the **Pabna League** operated with strict legal discipline:
    *   It collected a graduated litigation fund (*Chanda*) to finance hundreds of civil suits against the Zamindars, completely paralyzing rent collection without resorting to murder or arson (even Lieutenant-Governor **Sir George Campbell** acknowledged the remarkable lawfulness of the Pabna combinations and issued a **July 1873 Proclamation** upholding the right of peasants to form peaceful unions).
    *   To neutralize accusations of sedition and exploit the fissure between the **Colonial State** and the **Zamindars**, the Pabna rebels famously declared that they were **rebelling not against the British Crown, but to become the "Ryots of Her Majesty the Queen and of her only"** (*Maharanir Raiyat*).
*   **Spread Across Eastern Bengal & Intelligentsia Split (1873–1885):** Within two years, similar **Agrarian Leagues** spread across **Dacca**, **Mymensingh**, **Tripura**, **Bakarganj**, **Faridpur**, **Bogra**, and **Rajshahi**. In Calcutta, the movement exposed a sharp class cleavage: while the landlord-dominated **British Indian Association** and its newspaper **Hindoo Patriot** (now edited by **Kristo Das Pal**) hysterically denounced the Pabna peasants as "communists" and "dacoits," progressive intellectuals—including **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** (in his explosive treatise *Bangadesher Krishak* in *Bangadarshan*), civil servant **Romesh Chunder Dutt** (*The Peasantry of Bengal*, **1874**), and **Surendranath Banerjea’s Indian Association (1876)**—championed the tenants, organizing giant **Ryot Sabhas** across Bengal in **1880–85** to demand statutory reform.
*   **The Bengal Tenancy Act (Act VIII of 1885):** Fearing a general agrarian convulsion (especially after the **1873–74 Bihar Famine**), the colonial government appointed the **Rent Law Commission (1879)** and enacted the landmark **Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885** under Viceroy **Lord Dufferin**:
    1.  **Settled Ryot Provision:** It closed the 12-year plot-shifting loophole by stipulating that a peasant only needed to have cultivated *any* land within the **same village** (not necessarily the exact same plot) for **12 years** to become a **Settled Ryot** with full **Occupancy Rights** over all lands held by him in that village.
    2.  **Cap on Rent Enhancement:** It prohibited Zamindars from raising the rent of an Occupancy Ryot by more than **2 annas in the rupee (12.5%)** at one time, and barred any further enhancement for a period of **15 years**, while protecting sub-tenants (*Korfa* ryots) to a limited extent (though leaving *Bargadar* sharecroppers outside its ambit).

### 13. The Deccan Riots (1875), Ryotwari-Usury Nexus, and the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879)

#### American Civil War Cotton Collapse, Ryotwari Upward Revision, and Marwari/Gujarati Sahukar Bondage

In the **Bombay Deccan** (**Poona**, **Ahmednagar**, **Solapur**, and **Satara** districts), where the British had instituted the **Ryotwari Settlement** directly with individual **Kunbi** (Maratha peasant-caste) cultivators, the structural abolition of the pre-colonial village collective (*Mirasi/Gotsabha*) and the introduction of rigid cash revenue deadlines created an acute dependency on outside moneylenders (**Sahukars**—predominantly immigrant **Marwari** and **Gujarati** *Vanis*).

*   **The Three-Stage Fiscal-Commercial Trap (1859–1874):**
    1.  **The Limitation Act of 1859 & Judicial Bias:** Under pre-colonial Maratha rule (**Elphinstone’s Report**), moneylenders had no legal power to seize a peasant’s hereditary *Miras* land or agricultural implements, and customary law (**Damdupat**) dictated that cumulative interest could never exceed the original principal. Under British civil courts (*Adalats*), land became an alienable commodity attachable for debt. Worse, the **Limitation Act of 1859** reduced the validity of debt bonds from twelve years to just **three years**; to prevent bonds from lapsing, **Marwari Sahukars** forced non-literate **Kunbi** ryots to sign fresh, compound-interest bonds every three years (packing principal, 36–72% interest, and court costs into a new principal) or obtained *ex-parte* foreclosure decrees within days.
    2.  **The American Civil War Cotton Boom & Crash (1861–1870):** During the **American Civil War (1861–1865)**, when the Union blockade cut off Southern US cotton to Lancashire mills, Deccan cotton prices quadrupled, creating a brief illusion of rural prosperity and unlimited credit. When the war ended in **1865**, American cotton flooded back into Europe, and Deccan cotton prices crashed by over **50%**.
    3.  **The 1867 Upward Revenue Revision (50% to 100% Hike):** Precisely as cotton prices collapsed and drought struck, the Bombay Revenue Department (operating on the thirty-year revision cycle of **Goldsmid and Wingate’s 1836 Pringle/Wingate Survey**) executed a new **Revision Settlement in 1867** (starting in **Indapur Taluka**), raising the cash land revenue assessment by **50% to 100%** based on the artificial wartime boom prices! When peasants turned to **Marwari Sahukars** for cash to pay the **1874** tax installment, the Sahukars—realizing cotton prices were dead and land values were falling—refused further credit unless peasants surrendered outright title to their ancestral fields, reducing original **Kunbi** proprietors to tenant-serfs on their own land.

#### Social Boycott, Burning of Khatas (Debt Bonds) at Supa, and the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879)

Unlike a blindjacquerie, the **Deccan Uprising of 1874–1875** began with a calculated, non-violent social boycott organized through traditional village councils.

*   **Phase I: The Kardeh Social Boycott (December 1874):** In **December 1874**, in **Kardeh** village (**Sirur Taluka**, Poona district), when a notorious Marwari moneylender, **Kaluram**, obtained a court decree to pull down the house of a prominent peasant (**Baba Saheb Deshmukh**) for a Rs 150 debt, the villagers organized a total **Social Boycott** against all Marwari and Gujarati Sahukars. Village barbers (*Nhavis*), washermen (*Parits*), carpenters (*Sutars*), ironsmiths (*Lohars*), water-carriers, and domestic servants were forbidden to serve the moneylenders; nobody would cultivate their fields, buy from their shops, or drive their carts, forcing the Marwaris of Kardeh to flee to the police station. This boycott rapidly spread across **Sirur**, **Bhimthadi**, **Purandhar**, and **Indapur** talukas.
*   **Phase II: The Supa Eruption & Targeted Burning of Khatas (May–June 1875):** On **May 12, 1875**—stimulated by rumours that the British government quietly sympathized with the debtors after a British judge (*Wedderburn*) criticized usury—hundreds of **Kunbi** peasants from surrounding villages gathered at the market town of **Supa** (in **Bhimthadi Taluka**, Poona district) on bazaar day. Over the next three weeks (**May 12 to June 1875**), the uprising swept across **33 villages in Poona** (**Supa**, **Kedgaon**, **Pimpalgaon**, **Damare**) and **Ahmednagar** (**Parner**, **Shrigonda**, **Nagar**, **Karjat**), with isolated incidents in **Satara** and **Solapur**.
*   **Surgical Modality of the Riots:** Demonstrating **Ranajit Guha’s** modality of *Negation*, the Deccan peasants exhibited extraordinary discipline and surgical precision: their sole objective was not murder or general plunder, but the **confiscation and public burning of the Sahukars' Bahi-Khatas (account books), debt bonds, mortgage deeds, and civil court decrees** held inside the moneylenders' houses. Once the *Khatas* were thrown into the village bonfire—legally erasing the evidence of their bondage—the peasants dispersed without harming the Sahukars’ families (zero fatalities occurred among moneylenders across the entire uprising, though a few Sahukars had their noses slit in symbolic humiliation).
*   **Suppression, Poona Sarvajanik Sabha Intervention, and the 1879 Act:** Bombay authorities swiftly deployed the **Poona Horse**, infantry, and police, arresting nearly **1,000 peasants** and imposing punitive police taxes on rebel villages. However, the urban nationalist intelligentsia of Maharashtra—led by **Justice Mahadev Govind Ranade** and **Ganesh Vasudev Joshi’s Poona Sarvajanik Sabha** (which had already published its own independent inquiry into rural indebtedness in **1873** and sent volunteer lecturers into the villages)—campaigned vigorously to prove that the root cause was the **1867 Ryotwari revenue hike** combined with rigid colonial contract law.
*   **The Deccan Riots Commission (1875–78) & Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879):** Following the report of the **Deccan Riots Commission (1878)**, the colonial legislature enacted the landmark **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (Act XVII of 1879)**:
    1.  **Power to Go Behind the Bond:** It empowered civil courts to go behind the written letter of the contract, audit the entire history of the debt transaction from its origin, reduce unconscionable interest rates, and enforce the Hindu rule of **Damdupat** (interest cannot exceed principal).
    2.  **Protection Against Land Alienation & Arrest:** It prohibited the arrest and imprisonment of a Deccan peasant for failure to repay a civil debt, exempted agricultural land from attachment and sale unless it had been specifically and voluntarily mortgaged in a registered deed witnessed by the **Village Registrar**, and created **Conciliators** and **Village Munsifs** to settle small debts outside formal courts.

### 14. Malabar Moplah Outbreaks (1836–1900) and Assam Peasant Raij-Mels (1861–1894)

#### The Malabar Moplah (Mappila) Agrarian Outbreaks (1836–1896)

In **South Malabar** (**Ernad** and **Walluvanad** talukas of Madras Presidency, modern Kerala), the intersection of a colonial tenurial revolution with sharp religious-caste stratification produced an endemic cycle of rural insurgency known in colonial archives as the **Moplah (Mappila) Outbreaks** (**22 major outbreaks between 1836 and 1854**, and over **30 in total before 1900**).

*   **The Pre-Colonial Customary Equilibrium (*Janmam–Kanam–Verumpattam*):** Prior to British annexation in **1792**, agricultural land in Malabar was not privately owned by a single individual, but shared cooperatively under custom (*Maryada*) among three hereditary tiers who split the net produce in roughly equal thirds:
    1.  The **Jenmi** (predominantly high-caste **Nambudiri Brahmans** and **Nair** chieftains), who held ritual birthright (*Janmam*) without the power to evict tenants at will.
    2.  The **Kanamdar** (customary twelve-year tenure-holder who advanced a security deposit called *Kanam*), and
    3.  The **Verumpattamdar** (actual cultivating sub-tenant) and **Cherumar** (untouchable *Pulaya* agricultural laborers)—both overwhelmingly composed of **Moplahs (Mappilas)**, descendants of Arab traders and lower-caste Hindu converts to Islam who had briefly enjoyed land ownership when **Tipu Sultan** expelled the Hindu Jenmis during the **1780s**.
*   **Colonial Tenurial Subversion:** Following the **1792 Treaty of Seringapatam**, the British restored the exiled **Nambudiri** and **Nair Jenmis** and—applying English notions of absolute freehold property—declared the **Jenmi** to be the **absolute Roman-style Lord of the Soil**, reducing **Kanamdars** to mere mortgagees/leaseholders and **Verumpattamdars** to precarious tenants-at-will. Armed with British civil courts, police, and Hindu *Adhikaris* (village headmen), the **Jenmis** imposed exorbitant renewal fees (**Polichezhuthu**), raised rents arbitrarily, and executed ruthless court-ordered evictions (**Melcharth**—granting land over the head of the sitting Moplah tenant to a higher bidder).
*   **Tangal Spiritual Legitimation and Shahid (Martyr) Modality (1836–1896):** Deprived of any legal redress, evicted Moplah peasants and landless *Cherumar* converts turned to the spiritual leadership of the Arab **Tangals** (Sayyids claiming descent from the Prophet) headquartered at **Mambram (Tirurangadi)**—specifically **Sayyid Alawi Tangal (died 1844)** and his son **Sayyid Fazl Pookoya Tangal** (alongside rural preachers like **Veliyankode Umar Qazi**). The outbreaks followed a highly ritualized, suicidal religious-agrarian modality:
    *   A small band of evicted Moplah youths (typically 5 to 30 men) would take the vow of **Shahid** (martyrdom), divorce their wives, dress in white burial shrouds, seek the blessing of the **Mambram Tangal**, assassinate the oppressive **Jenmi** (such as **Kaprat Krishna Panikkar** at **Manjeri in 1849** or **Kallatil Kesavan Tangal in 1851**), burn his mortgage deeds and grain stores, barricade themselves inside the Jenmi’s stone house or a local temple, and fight advancing British infantry regiments with war-knives (*Pishamkatti*) until every single insurgent was shot or bayoneted to death, celebrated in Moplah folk ballads (*Malappuram Padappattu*).
*   **Assassination of Collector Conolly (1855) & William Logan’s Commission (1881):** In **1852**, British Collector **H.V. Conolly** forced **Sayyid Fazl Pookoya Tangal** into exile in Arabia; in retaliation, in **September 1855**, four escaped Moplah prisoners hacked **Collector Conolly** to death in front of his wife at his bungalow in **Calicut**. The British responded with the draconian **Moplah Outrages Acts (Act XXIII of 1854 and Act XX of 1859)**, which authorized summary execution without trial, the burning of martyrs' corpses (to deny them Islamic burial in mosques), collective fines wiping out entire Moplah families, and the deportation of suspects. It was not until **1881** that **William Logan**, appointed as Special Commissioner of Malabar, proved in his magisterial **Malabar Land Tenure Report** that the "fanatical outrages" were directly caused by British judicial misinterpretation of **Jenmi** property rights and ruthless **Melcharth** evictions—Leading cautiously to the **Malabar Compensation for Tenants' Improvements Act (1887 and 1900)**, though the refusal to grant permanent occupancy rights ultimately detonated the **1921 Moplah Rebellion**.

#### The Assam Peasant Raij-Mels: Phulaguri Dhawa (1861) and Patharughat (1894)

In the **Brahmaputra Valley of Assam**—where the British instituted a direct **Ryotwari** settlement with individual peasant households while transferring vast tracts of wasteland entirely tax-free to European **Tea Planters** (under the **Wasteland Rules of 1838 and 1854**)—the colonial state repeatedly hiked peasant land revenue to force self-sufficient Assamese cultivators into wage-labor on British tea gardens. To resist this fiscal offensive, the multi-caste and multi-tribal peasantry of Assam revived a remarkable indigenous institution of popular democracy: the **Raij-Mel** ("Assembly of the People"). Transcending caste, tribe (**Bodo-Kachari**, **Tiwa/Lalung**, **Koch**, **Kalita**), and religion (Hindu and Muslim), the **Raij-Mel** operated under the moral authority of local *Gossains* (Vaishnava Satra heads), *Dolois*, and *Gaonburas* (village headmen) as a sovereign peasant parliament whose collective verdict was considered higher than that of the British **Maharani**.

*   **The Phulaguri Dhawa (October 1861):** The first major Raij-Mel confrontation erupted at **Phulaguri** (near **Nowgong** in Central Assam), inhabited largely by **Tiwa (Lalung)** and **Kachari** tribal peasants.
    *   *Fiscal Triggers:* To compel peasants to buy expensive government-monopoly **Abkari Opium** imported from Bihar (and simultaneously drive cash-starved peasants onto tea plantations), the British **banned private cultivation of opium poppy in 1860**, doubled land taxes, introduced an **Income Tax (1861)**, and floated proposals to levy new taxes on **Tamul-Pan** (betel-nut and betel-leaf, the universal dietary and ritual staple of every Assamese household) and forest timber.
    *   *The October 1861 Assembly & Death of Lt. Singer:* In **September–October 1861**, over **3,000 to 4,000 peasants** armed with traditional bamboo staves (*Jathis/Tangon*) convened a continuous five-day **Raij-Mel at Phulaguri** to protest to the tyrannical Deputy Commissioner of Nowgong, **Lieutenant Herbert Sconce** (who mocked petitioners and detained them in jail). On **October 18, 1861**, when Sconce dispatched his Assistant Commissioner, **Lieutenant B.H. Singer**, with a police squad to disperse the assembly and forcibly confiscate the peasants' bamboo staves, a scuffle broke out; a peasant named **Bahua Kaibarta** struck **Lt. Singer** down with a bamboo lathi, and the peasants threw Singer’s body into the **Kalang River**. British military reinforcements under **Major Raban** and **General Henry Hopkinson** subsequently fired upon the unarmed Phulaguri assembly, killing **39 to 54 peasants**, publicly hanging key leaders (**Lakshman Deka**, **Songbor Lalung**, and **Rongbor Deka**) at Nowgong, and transporting others (*Koliapani*) to the Andamans.
*   **The 1893–1894 Assam Uprisings: Rangia, Lachima, and Patharughat (Dalgoma):** Three decades later, in **1892–1893**, the Chief Commissioner of Assam, **Sir William Ward**, ordered a draconian **Re-settlement of Land Revenue across the Brahmaputra Valley**, arbitrarily reclassifying village lands to increase cash assessments by **70% to 100%** at a single stroke.
    *   *Rangia and Lachima Raij-Mels (Dec 1893 – Jan 1894):* Massive **Raij-Mels** sprang up across **Kamrup** (**Rangia**, **Nalbari**, **Barama**, and **Lachima** in **Sarukhetri Mouza**) and **Darrang**, enforcing a strict **No-Tax Campaign** and decreeing social excommunication (*Dandi*) against any peasant who paid the enhanced rate or bid for attached property. On **December 24, 1893**, **3,000 Kachari and Assamese peasants** looted the **Rangia Bazaar** (targeting Marwari usurers who financed tax auctions) and besieged the Tehsil office; at **Lachima** on **January 21, 1894**, peasants led by **Pushparam Kalita** assaulted the revenue *Mouzadars* and Circle Officer **Madhab Chandra Bordoloi** (who died days later), prompting the British Deputy Commissioner **McCabe** to arrest 75 leaders and humiliate respected *Gossains* and Brahmans by yoking them like bullocks to ploughs across the ground.
    *   *The Patharughat (Patharughatar Ran) Massacre (January 28, 1894):* The bloodiest climax occurred at **Patharughat** in **Mangaldai subdivision (Darrang district)**. On **January 26–28, 1894**, despite a ban on assemblies, an estimated **2,000 to 3,000 unarmed Hindu and Muslim peasants** converged on the **Patharughat** rest-house—where Deputy Commissioner **J.D. Anderson** and Police Superintendent **J.R. Berington** were camped with Military Police—carrying only bamboo sticks and demanding a reduction of the impossible tax hike. When Anderson flatly refused to lower the assessment and ordered **Berington** to clear the ground with bayonets, the peasants pressed forward throwing earthclods (*Dali*) and bamboo sticks. On **January 28, 1894**, **Berington’s** troops opened sustained rifle volley fire into the dense crowd at point-blank range: while official colonial dispatches claimed **15 dead and 37 wounded**, popular records and local memorials (**Patharughatar Ran**) document that **140 unarmed Hindu and Muslim peasants were martyred on the spot**, forcing the Government of India to eventually intervene and moderate the Assam assessment enhancements to 37–53%.

### 15. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Fragmented Subaltern Insurrections to the 1857 Great Watershed

Across the entire arc from the **1763 Sanyasi-Fakir Rebellion** to the **1855–56 Santhal Hool**, the early localized civil, feudal, socio-religious, and Adivasi insurrections had exposed the profound structural fragility of the **East India Company’s** military-fiscal extraction machine. Every instrument of Company statehood—the resumption of rent-free **Inam** and **Paikan** service tenures, the extortionate cash assessments of the **Zamindari**, **Ryotwari**, and **Mahalwari** settlements, the judicialization of usury via **Cornwallis’s Adalats**, the colonial salt and opium monopolies, and the ecological enclosure of tribal forests—generated fierce, recurring explosions of rural resistance. Yet, prior to **1857**, the Company survived and crushed each of these **77+ uprisings** in isolation because of three structural firewalls: first, the insurgencies remained geographically fragmented within regional ethnic or feudal boundaries (*Des*), lacking a synchronized pan-Indian operational command; second, the rebels fought with pre-modern weapons (bows, arrows, bamboo staves, and matchlocks) against disciplined Company artillery; and third, and most decisively, the Company relied upon the loyalty of its native mercenary army—specifically the high-caste **Bengal Army Sepoy**, the "peasant in uniform" recruited overwhelmingly from **Awadh** and **Bihar**—to shoot down rebellious **Santhals**, **Kols**, **Poligars**, **Paiks**, and **Faraizis**.

Between **1848 and 1856**, Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie’s** aggressive annexationist offensive—wielding the **Doctrine of Lapse** against **Satara**, **Jhansi**, and **Nagpur**, abolishing royal titles and pensions, and culminating in the **February 1856 annexation of Awadh** and the **1856 Summary Settlement** that simultaneously dispossessed the **Taluqdars** and over-assessed the peasant family holdings of **75,000 Bengal Army Sepoys**—shattered this final firewall. When the structural grievances of the dispossessed feudal nobility, the rack-rented peasantry, and the alienated tribal communities (such as **Gonoo’s Kols** in Singhbhum and **Shah Mal’s Jats** in Baghpat) fused directly with a full-scale mutiny inside the Company’s own military apparatus over the **1856 General Service Enlistment Act** and the **Enfield Rifle greased cartridges**, localized subaltern insurgency transformed into the continental conflagration of the **1857 Watershed**, which we systematically deconstruct in **Part 5**.
