# Part 12: The Swarajist Interlude, Constitutional Boycotts, the Civil Disobedience Movement, and the Round Table Conferences (1922–1934)

## I. The Post-Chauri Chaura Strategic Recalibration and the Swarajist Parliamentary Offensive (1922–1926)

### 1. The Strategic Mechanics of the Truce Phase and the 1922 Gaya Rupture

The abrupt termination of the Non-Cooperation Movement following the **Chauri Chaura incident** (**February 5, 1922**) and the **Bardoli withdrawal resolution** (**February 12, 1922**) coupled with the arrest of **Mahatma Gandhi** in **March 1922** (sentenced to six years' imprisonment by Judge **C.N. Broomfield** under **Section 124A** of the Indian Penal Code) precipitated a profound strategic crisis within the anti-colonial movement. Measured from the first principles of mass mobilization, no subaltern or bourgeois coalition can sustain indefinite, high-intensity extra-constitutional confrontation against a modern military-fiscal state without experiencing organizational exhaustion, economic attrition, and ideological demoralization. The withdrawal initiated a necessary structural transition from an extra-parliamentary "war of movement" to a prolonged ideological and institutional "war of position." In **June 1922**, the All-India Congress Committee (AICC) appointed the **Civil Disobedience Enquiry Committee** (headed by **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, with **Motilal Nehru**, **C. Rajagopalachari**, **M.A. Ansari**, **Vithalbhai Patel**, and **Kasturiranga Iyengar**) to tour the provinces and audit the material readiness of the peasantry and urban cadres. The Committee concluded that the country was not immediately prepared for a renewed mass civil disobedience offensive, opening a sharp strategic cleavage over how to occupy the political space during the intervening non-combative phase.

This strategic divergence crystallized at the **1922 Gaya Session** of the Indian National Congress in **December 1922**, presided over by **Chittaranjan Das** (**C.R. Das**). The delegates fractured into two distinct ideological and organizational camps over the question of contesting the upcoming **November 1923** elections to the legislative councils established under the **Government of India Act 1919** (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms):

*   **The Pro-Changers (Swarajists):** Led by **C.R. Das**, **Motilal Nehru**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, **Vithalbhai Patel**, and **N.C. Kelkar**, this faction argued that in a period of mass political vacuum, maintaining a rigid boycott of the legislative councils merely surrendered the statutory bodies to colonial collaborators, loyalist landlords, and moderate liberals. They proposed entering the Central Legislative Assembly and Provincial Legislative Councils not to cooperate with the **Dyarchy** machinery, but to open a second front of anti-imperial struggle: practicing "uniform, continuous, and consistent obstruction" from within the legislature to wreck the **1919 Reforms**, block colonial budgets, and expose the autocratic reality behind the façade of representative institutions.
*   **The No-Changers:** Led by **Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Rajendra Prasad**, **C. Rajagopalachari**, and **M.A. Ansari**, this faction contended that council entry would inevitably lead to parliamentary co-optation, constitutionalist parliamentary cretinism, and the dilution of revolutionary mass ethics. They insisted on strict adherence to the **1920 Nagpur** boycott programme, arguing that the inter-movement hiatus must be dedicated entirely to quiet, grass-roots **Constructive Work**—promoting the *Charkha* (spinning wheel) and *Khadi*, eradicating untouchability, fostering Hindu-Muslim unity, enforcing prohibition of liquor/opium, and operating national schools (*Vidyapiths*) to forge the social and organizational infrastructure for the next round of mass Satyagraha.
*   **The Gaya Vote and Institutional Split:** At the **1922 Gaya Session**, the **No-Changers** mobilized the provincial rural delegates and defeated **C.R. Das**’s council-entry motion by 1,740 votes to 890. Refusing to subordinate their parliamentary strategy, **C.R. Das** resigned from the Congress presidency, and on **January 1, 1923**, **C.R. Das** (as President) and **Motilal Nehru** (as General Secretary) formally announced the formation of the **Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party** (popularly known as the **Swaraj Party**) at Allahabad.

Both camps, however, had absorbed the devastating structural lesson of the **1907 Surat Split**, where an open organizational fracture between Moderates and Extremists had allowed the colonial state to isolate and crush the radical wing. Consequently, both **Pro-Changers** and **No-Changers** maneuvered to preserve the overarching institutional unity of the Indian National Congress.

### 2. Institutionalization of the Swaraj Party, Constructive Work, and the 1923–1924 Accommodation

The **Swaraj Party** defined itself as an integral wing of the Congress rather than a rival political organization, explicitly accepting the creed of attaining *Swaraj* by peaceful and legitimate means while claiming operational autonomy within the legislative sphere. Throughout early **1923**, as communal riots erupted in several northern urban centers following the collapse of the Khilafat alliance, the imperative to prevent a permanent Congress rupture became paramount.

*   **The 1923 Delhi Special Session Compromise:** In **September 1923**, a Special Session of the Congress held in Delhi under the presidency of **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** engineered a pragmatic compromise. The Congress suspended all propaganda against council entry and permitted Congressmen who had no religious or conscientious objections to stand as candidates and vote in the forthcoming **November 1923** legislative elections. This compromise was reaffirmed at the regular **December 1923 Kakinada Session** presided over by **Maulana Mohammad Ali**.
*   **The 1924 Gandhi-Das-Nehru Pact and the Belgaum Session:** Following an emergency appendectomy, **Mahatma Gandhi** was unconditionally released from Yerawada Prison on health grounds in **February 1924**. Initially skeptical of council entry—viewing it as incompatible with the spiritual and ethical discipline of non-violent non-cooperation—Gandhi moved toward accommodation when the colonial regime launched a ferocious repressive assault on the Bengal Congress in **October 1924** (arresting **Subhas Chandra Bose**, then Chief Executive Officer of the Calcutta Corporation, along with numerous Swarajist workers under the draconian **Bengal Criminal Law Amendment Ordinance**). In **November 1924**, Gandhi signed a joint statement with **C.R. Das** and **Motilal Nehru**, which was ratified at the **December 1924 Belgaum Congress Session** (the only session ever presided over by **Mahatma Gandhi**). Under this agreement, the Congress formally endorsed the **Swaraj Party** to carry out legislative work on behalf of and as an integral organ of the Indian National Congress, while making the spinning of 2,000 yards of yarn per month the mandatory franchise qualification for Congress membership.
*   **The Political Economy of Constructive Work:** While the Swarajists occupied the legislative arena, the **No-Changers** (**Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Rajendra Prasad**, **C. Rajagopalachari**) established the **All-India Spinners’ Association** (**1925**) and a network of rural *Ashrams* across Gujarat, Bihar, Andhra, and Tamil Nadu. Far from being apolitical philanthropy, **Constructive Work** operated as a material and spatial counter-hegemony: *Khadi* networks provided supplemental subsistence income to underemployed peasant households during the agricultural off-season, tribal welfare programs countered Christian missionary and colonial forest-department penetration among Adivasis (such as the *Kaliparaj* / *Raniparaj* in South Gujarat), and rural *Ashrams* served as permanent command posts that trained disciplined peasant cadres—Directly enabling the **1928 Bardoli Satyagraha** and the **1930 Dandi Salt March**.

### 3. Legislative Obstruction, Fiscal Audits, and the Responsivist Fragmentation

In the **November 1923** elections, despite having barely eight weeks to organize an electoral machinery for a restricted property-qualified electorate of only 6.2 million voters, the **Swaraj Party** secured 42 out of 101 elected seats in the **Central Legislative Assembly**, achieved an absolute majority in the **Central Provinces**, emerged as the single largest party in **Bengal**, and won substantial representation in **Bombay** and the **United Provinces**. In the **Central Legislative Assembly**, **Motilal Nehru** forged a parliamentary coalition—the **Nationalist Party**—comprising the 42 Swarajists and 28 Moderates/Independents led by **Madan Mohan Malaviya** and **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, commanding a working majority against the official government bloc.

#### The Mechanics of Fiscal and Constitutional Obstruction
Under the **Government of India Act 1919**, the colonial executive remained completely irremovable by the legislature, and roughly 80% of the central budget (military expenditure, debt servicing, Home Charges, and covenanted civil service salaries) was designated as "non-votable." However, the remaining 20% of revenue grants and the annual **Finance Bill** required legislative assent unless the Viceroy invoked his extraordinary certification power under **Section 67B** of the Act. The Swarajists systematically targeted this constitutional vulnerability:

*   **Wrecking Provincial Dyarchy:** In the **Central Provinces**, where Swarajists held an absolute majority, they passed a motion of no-confidence against the Indian ministers and rejected the entire provincial budget, Including ministerial salaries, forcing the Governor to suspend **Dyarchy** and assume direct administration of the "Transferred" subjects. In **Bengal**, **C.R. Das** forged the historic **Bengal Pact** (**December 1923**) with Muslim leaders (promising 55% representation in administrative services and 60% in local bodies to the Muslim majority upon attaining self-government), enabling the Swarajists to repeatedly vote down the salaries of Bengal ministers in **1924** and **1925** and compel the Governor to suspend the **Dyarchy** constitution.
*   **Central Fiscal Vetoes and Commission Rejections:** Between **1924** and **1926**, **Motilal Nehru** led the Central Assembly in throwing out the colonial **Finance Bill** and rejecting budgetary demands for the Salt Tax, Railways, and Customs, forcing the Viceroy (**Lord Reading**) to repeatedly invoke **Section 67B** to certify taxes by executive fiat—thereby stripping the regime of any pretension of governing by consent. In **February 1924**, Motilal Nehru successfully moved a landmark amendment demanding the convening of a representative **Round Table Conference** to frame a constitution for full responsible government. When the government responded by appointing the bureaucratic **Muddiman Committee** (**Reforms Enquiry Committee, 1924**), the Swarajists rejected its majority report. Similarly, they rejected the recommendations of the **Lee Commission (1924)** on the Superior Civil Services, which sought to perpetuate British recruitment and financial privileges in the ICS and IP.
*   **Election of Vitthalbhai Patel as Speaker (1925):** In **August 1925**, the Swarajists scored a major institutional triumph by electing **Vitthalbhai Patel** as the first Indian President (Speaker) of the **Central Legislative Assembly**, defeating the official-backed candidate. Wearing a homespun *Khadi* robe and Gandhi cap in the Speaker's chair, **Vitthalbhai Patel** strictly enforced parliamentary autonomy against the colonial executive, asserted control over the Assembly Secretariat and security precinct, and later played a decisive role in blocking repressive colonial statutes.
*   **Defeat of the Public Safety Bill (1928):** As working-class strikes (spearheaded by the **Girni Kamgar Union** in Bombay) and socialist/communist networks expanded across India, the colonial government introduced the draconian **Public Safety Bill** in **September 1928**, seeking summary executive power to deport non-Indian communist organizers (specifically targeting British communists **Philip Spratt** and **Ben Bradley**) and confiscate funds remitted from abroad. Uniting across nationalist factions, **Motilal Nehru** (who condemned the Bill as the "Slavery of India Bill, No. 1"), **Lala Lajpat Rai**, and **Madan Mohan Malaviya** attacked the measure on the floor of the House as an assault on civil liberties and the labour movement. When the vote ended in an exact tie (61 to 61), Speaker **Vitthalbhai Patel** delivered a historic constitutional ruling: casting his deciding vote against the government on the principle that an extraordinary coercive statute could not be enacted without an explicit parliamentary majority, thereby defeating the **Public Safety Bill** (and later ruling the re-introduced Bill out of order in **1929** pending the **Meerut Conspiracy Case** trial).

#### The Responsivist vs. Non-Responsivist Split (1925–1926)
Despite its initial parliamentary brilliance, a purely obstructionist strategy inside powerless legislatures possessed an inherent structural ceiling: once the **1919 Montford Reforms** had been exposed as hollow, repeated walkouts and budget rejections yielded diminishing political returns while the allure of executive patronage and communal anxieties corroded party cohesion. The death of **C.R. Das** in **June 1925** removed the Swaraj Party's most agile coalition-builder. By late **1925**, a severe ideological cleavage emerged within the Swarajist ranks, culminating in the **Responsivist vs. Non-Responsivist split**:

*   **The Responsivists:** Led by **Lala Lajpat Rai**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, **N.C. Kelkar**, **M.R. Jayakar**, and **B.S. Moonje**, the Responsivists broke away from **Motilal Nehru**’s policy of blanket non-cooperation within the councils. Adopting the late B.G. Tilak’s doctrine of "**Responsive Cooperation**," they argued that Swarajists should accept ministerial offices and sit on executive committees whenever the colonial government made concessions, claiming that refusing office in provinces like Bombay and the Central Provinces harmed regional patronage networks and left "Hindu interests" vulnerable against Muslim ministers who cooperated with the British.
*   **The Non-Responsivists:** Led by **Motilal Nehru**, the mainstream Swarajists insisted that accepting ministerial office under the **1919 Dyarchy** framework without fundamental constitutional reform was an act of political surrender. In **March 1926**, executing the **1925 Kanpur Congress** directive, **Motilal Nehru** and the **Non-Responsivist** Swarajists staged a dramatic walkout from the Central Legislative Assembly and Provincial Councils, declaring the legislatures deadlocks of humiliation.
*   **The 1926 Electoral Setback:** Ahead of the **November 1926** elections, **Madan Mohan Malaviya** and **Lala Lajpat Rai** joined forces with the Maharashtra Responsivists to form the **Independent Congress Party** (aligned closely with the **Hindu Mahasabha**). Fighting on a platform combining "Responsive Cooperation" with Hindu communal consolidation against the mainstream Swaraj Party, the fracture—compounded by rising Hindu-Muslim riots—badly damaged the **Swaraj Party** in the **1926** polls, severely reducing its strength in the **United Provinces**, **Punjab**, and the **Central Provinces**, though it retained command in **Madras** and **Bengal**.

## II. Imperial Statutory Counter-Offensives and the Nationalist Constitutional Synthesis (1927–1929)

### 4. The Simon Commission Boycott and the Butler Committee on Princely Paramountcy (1927)

By **1927**, the demoralization of the post-1922 truce phase was abruptly shattered by two simultaneous statutory initiatives from Whitehall designed to lock India into permanent constitutional subordination: the appointment of the **Simon Commission** for British India and the **Butler Committee** for the Princely States.

#### The All-White Simon Commission (November 1927)
Under **Section 84A** of the **Government of India Act 1919**, a statutory royal commission was mandated to be appointed ten years after the Act's passage (i.e., in 1929) to inquire into the working of the system of government and report whether the degree of responsible government should be extended, modified, or restricted. However, the Conservative government in Britain under Prime Minister **Stanley Baldwin** and the staunchly imperialist Secretary of State for India, **Lord Birkenhead**, feared that the British general election due in 1929 might bring the Labour Party to power, which might appoint a sympathetic, pro-Indian commission. To pre-empt this, **Birkenhead** accelerated the schedule by two years and announced on **November 8, 1927**, the appointment of a seven-member **Indian Statutory Commission** chaired by the Liberal jurist **Sir John Simon** (including **Clement Attlee** as a Labour member).

*   **The Racial and Constitutional Insult:** Every single member of the seven-man **Simon Commission** was a white British Member of Parliament; not a single Indian was included. **Lord Birkenhead** justified this exclusion on the racist premise that Indians were irreconcilably fractured along religious and caste lines and therefore constitutionally incapable of framing an agreed governance structure, reducing three hundred million Indians to passive objects of an imperial parliamentary audit.
*   **The Pan-Indian Boycott Coalition:** The announcement triggered immediate, near-universal outrage across the Indian political spectrum. At the **December 1927 Madras Congress Session** (presided over by **Dr. M.A. Ansari**), the Congress passed a resolution to boycott the **Simon Commission** "at every stage and in every form." Crucially, the Congress was joined in this boycott by the **Liberal Federation** (led by **Tej Bahadur Sapru**), the **Hindu Mahasabha**, the **Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FICCI)**, and the majority faction of the **All-India Muslim League** led by **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** (while a breakaway Punjab loyalist faction of the Muslim League led by **Muhammad Shafi**, along with the **Justice Party** in Madras and the **Unionist Party** in Punjab, chose to cooperate with the Commission).
*   **Mass Mobilization and Police Repression:** When the **Simon Commission** landed in Bombay on **February 3, 1928**, it was greeted by an all-India *hartal* (strike), mass processions waving black flags, and the thunderous slogan "**Simon Go Back**" (coined by socialist leader **Yusuf Meherally**). As the Commission traveled across India between **1928** and **1929**, it was met by militant student and youth demonstrations in Calcutta (led by **Subhas Chandra Bose**), Lucknow (where **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Govind Ballabh Pant** were severely beaten by mounted police lathi charges), Madras (where **T. Prakasam** bared his chest before armed police after firing killed a protester), and Lahore. On **October 30, 1928**, during a massive anti-Simon demonstration in Lahore, Superintendent of Police **James A. Scott** ordered a brutal lathi assault on **Lala Lajpat Rai**, the *Sher-e-Punjab*, who succumbed to his injuries on **November 17, 1928**, prophetic in his dying declaration that "every blow aimed at me is a nail in the coffin of British imperialism"—an event that directly catalyzed the **HSRA**’s revolutionary retaliation by **Bhagat Singh**, **Chandrashekhar Azad**, and **Rajguru** in December 1928.

#### The Butler Committee on Princely Paramountcy (1927–1929)
Simultaneously with the Simon Commission, **Lord Birkenhead** appointed the three-member **Indian States Committee** in **December 1927**, chaired by **Sir Harcourt Butler** (alongside **Prof. W.S. Holdsworth** and **S.C. Peel**), known as the **Butler Committee**, to investigate the constitutional and financial relationship between the Paramount Power (the British Crown) and the 560+ **Princely States**, and to recommend adjustments for their economic and financial relations with British India.

The appointment of the **Butler Committee** was a sophisticated exercise in imperial geo-constitutional partitioning. As nationalist pressure for responsible central government in British India intensified, both colonial strategists and autocratic Indian Princes (represented by the **Chamber of Princes** and their costly legal counsel, **Sir Leslie Scott**) feared that a future Indian nationalist cabinet in New Delhi might inherit the Crown’s Paramountcy powers over the Princely States. The **Butler Committee Report** (submitted in **1929**) codified two foundational colonial doctrines:
*   **"Paramountcy must remain paramount":** Rejecting Leslie Scott’s contractual theory that Paramountcy was strictly limited by original treaties, the Committee ruled that Paramountcy was an undefined, living, and elastic prerogative that must adapt to changing imperial imperatives, preserving the British resident's right to intervene at will in any state to suppress popular **Praja Mandal** democratic movements.
*   **The Constitutional Firewall against Nationalist India:** Crucially, the **Butler Committee** decreed that the Princes' relationship was exclusively with the **British Crown** (acting through the **Viceroy** in his distinct capacity as Crown Representative, rather than the **Governor-General-in-Council**), and solemnly assured the Princes that **Paramountcy would never be transferred** to a newly constituted Indian government in British India responsible to an Indian legislature **without the express consent of the Princes**. This legally partitioned India into two disconnected constitutional spheres, weaponizing the Princely States as a permanent conservative bulwark against unified Indian sovereignty.

### 5. Constitutional Bargaining: From the Delhi Proposals to the Motilal Nehru Report and Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1927–1929)

In the House of Lords in **November 1927**, **Lord Birkenhead** threw down a provocative gauntlet to Indian nationalists, challenging them to abandon negative criticism and produce a constitution on which all major Indian political parties and religious communities could agree. Accepting this challenge as a test of anti-colonial statesmanship, the Congress initiated an intensive series of inter-party constitutional negotiations between **December 1927** and **March 1929**.

#### The Delhi Proposals (December 1927)
To bridge the communal divide that had widened since the collapse of the **1916 Lucknow Pact** and the post-1922 riots, a conference of prominent Muslim leaders led by **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** met in Delhi on **December 20, 1927**, and formulated four constitutional conditions—known as the **Delhi Proposals**—under which the Muslim League offered to surrender its cherished demand for **separate electorates** (originally entrenched by the **1909 Morley-Minto Reforms**) in favor of **joint electorates**:
1.  **Joint Electorates with Reserved Seats** across all legislatures.
2.  **Creation of Three New Muslim-Majority Provinces:** **Sindh** to be separated from the Bombay Presidency as an autonomous province, and full provincial constitutional reforms to be introduced in the **North-West Frontier Province (NWFP)** and **Baluchistan** on the same footing as other provinces.
3.  **Representation on Population Basis in Punjab and Bengal:** In the Muslim-majority provinces of **Punjab** and **Bengal**, legislative seats to be reserved strictly in proportion to population (guaranteeing statutory Muslim majorities).
4.  **One-Third Central Representation:** In the Central Legislature, Muslim representation to be fixed at **not less than one-third (33.3%)** of the total seats.

The **December 1927 Madras Congress Session** welcomed the **Delhi Proposals** as a breakthrough toward joint electorates. However, when the **All-Parties Conference** convened across **February**, **May**, and **August 1928** (bringing together the Congress, the Liberal Federation, the Muslim League, the Hindu Mahasabha, the Central Sikh League, and labour organizations), fierce opposition erupted from the **Hindu Mahasabha** (led by **M.R. Jayakar** and **Madan Mohan Malaviya**) and Sikh delegates, who adamantly opposed statutory Muslim majorities in Punjab and Bengal as well as the separation of Sindh.

#### The Motilal Nehru Report (August 1928)
To break the deadlock, the **All-Parties Conference** in Bombay (**May 1928**) appointed a sub-committee chaired by **Motilal Nehru** (with **Jawaharlal Nehru** as Secretary, and members including **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Subhas Chandra Bose**, **M.S. Aney**, **Ali Imam**, **Shuaib Qureshi**, **Mangal Singh**, and **G.R. Pradhan**) to draft the principles of an Indian constitution. Submitted in **August 1928**, the **Motilal Nehru Report** was the first comprehensive constitutional blueprint drafted entirely by Indians, demonstrating high juristic mastery while attempting to balance secular nationalism against competing communal pressures:

*   **Dominion Status:** The Report defined India’s immediate constitutional status as a self-governing **Dominion** (designated the "Commonwealth of India") on the exact constitutional model of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the Union of South Africa, and the Irish Free State, with a parliamentary executive collectively responsible to a bicameral legislature (a 200-member Senate and a 500-member House of Representatives).
*   **Nineteen Fundamental Rights and Secularism:** It enshrined a justiciable declaration of **Nineteen Fundamental Rights**, including universal adult suffrage for every person aged 21 and above, equal civic and legal rights for women, freedom of conscience, speech, press, and association, free elementary education, protection of minority cultural and religious rights, and the complete separation of the state from any established religion.
*   **Linguistic Reorganization of Provinces:** Recognizing that colonial provincial boundaries were arbitrary administrative accidents of military conquest, the **Nehru Report** mandated the redistribution of provinces on a **linguistic basis** (explicitly endorsing the creation of **Karnataka**, **Andhra**, **Utkal/Orissa**, and **Sindh**).
*   **Joint Electorates and the Communal Compromise:** Under intense pressure from the **Hindu Mahasabha**, the **Nehru Report** completely rejected **separate electorates** and introduced a modified communal formula:
    *   **Joint electorates** throughout India.
    *   **Reservation of seats for Muslims ONLY at the Centre and in Muslim-minority provinces** strictly in proportion to their population, with the explicit right to contest additional unreserved seats.
    *   **No reservation of seats for Muslims in Punjab and Bengal** (where Muslims were a demographic majority).
    *   **Separation of Sindh from Bombay** (contingent upon establishing financial self-sufficiency) and full provincial status for the **NWFP** and **Baluchistan**.
*   **Unitary Bias in Federalism:** While establishing a federal structure, the **Nehru Report** vested **residuary powers** in the **Central Government** rather than the provinces.
*   **The Princely States Clause:** Countering the **Butler Committee**, the Report explicitly declared that the future Commonwealth of India would seamlessly inherit all rights, treaties, and **Paramountcy** powers currently exercised by the British Crown over the Indian States.

#### The Calcutta All-Parties Convention (December 1928) and Jinnah’s Fourteen Points (March 1929)
When the **All-Parties National Convention** met in Calcutta in **December 1928** to ratify the **Motilal Nehru Report**, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**—representing the nationalist wing of the Muslim League—pleaded for three principal amendments to reconcile Muslim opinion: (1) **one-third (33.3%) representation** for Muslims in the Central Legislature, (2) reservation of seats for Muslims in **Punjab** and **Bengal** on a population basis for ten years until adult suffrage was established, and (3) vesting **residuary powers** in the **Provinces** rather than the Centre. However, **M.R. Jayakar** of the **Hindu Mahasabha** fiercely opposed any concession, threatening to pull the Mahasabha out of the consensus if a single comma of the communal settlement was altered, and Jinnah’s amendments were voted down.

Calling this rejection the "parting of the ways," **Jinnah** reunited with the conservative **Shafi** faction of the Muslim League and, at the Delhi session of the All-India Muslim League in **March 1929**, promulgated **Jinnah’s Fourteen Points**—a consolidated charter of Muslim elite constitutional safeguards that structured Muslim League bargaining for the next decade:

### 6. The Radical Youth Upsurge, Irwin's Diwali Declaration, and the 1929 Lahore Purna Swaraj Rupture

While the older constitutionalist leadership debated communal weightages in the **Nehru Report**, a militant generational and ideological shift was transforming the Indian political landscape. Between **1927** and **1929**, youth leagues, student unions, peasant associations, and socialist trade unions surged across the country. At the **December 1927 Madras Congress**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** (fresh from the **Brussels Congress of Oppressed Nationalities** in February 1927 and a visit to the Soviet Union) successfully moved a snap resolution declaring **Complete National Independence** (*Purna Swaraj*) as the goal of the Indian people.

#### The Independence for India League (August 1928) and the Calcutta Ultimatum
When the **Motilal Nehru Report** was published in **August 1928** recommending **Dominion Status** rather than complete severance from the British Empire, the younger left-wing nationalists rebelled against their elders' moderation. **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** argued that accepting Dominion Status reduced the anti-imperial struggle to a domestic dispute inside a British capitalist empire, betraying India's global solidarity with anti-colonial struggles. In **August 1928**, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Subhas Chandra Bose**, and **S. Srinivasa Iyengar** founded the **Independence for India League** as a pressure group within the Congress to campaign for two objectives: (1) complete severance of the British connection (**Purna Swaraj**), and (2) the reconstruction of Indian society on a **socialist economic basis** to abolish feudalism and capitalist exploitation.

At the **December 1928 Calcutta Congress Session**—presided over by **Motilal Nehru** himself—a dramatic confrontation took place between the father and son. **Subhas Chandra Bose** moved an amendment backed by **Jawaharlal Nehru** rejecting Dominion Status in favor of Complete Independence. To prevent an open fracture, **Mahatma Gandhi** intervened with a historic compromise resolution: the Congress would adopt the **Motilal Nehru Report** in its entirety *provided* the British Parliament accepted it intact within **one year** (originally proposed by Gandhi as two years, cut to one year under youth pressure—expiring on **December 31, 1929**). If the British Government failed to grant Dominion Status by that midnight deadline, the Congress would abandon the Nehru Report, declare **Purna Swaraj**, and launch a nationwide **Civil Disobedience Movement**.

#### Irwin’s Diwali Declaration (October 31, 1929) and the Delhi Manifesto
In **May 1929**, a Labour Government led by Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** with **Wedgwood Benn** as Secretary of State for India took office in London. Recognizing the gathering revolutionary storm in India ahead of the December 31 ultimatum, Viceroy **Lord Irwin** traveled to London for consultations and, upon his return on **October 31, 1929**, issued a formal policy statement known as **Irwin’s Diwali Declaration**:
*   **The Substance of the Declaration:** Reaffirming the preamble to the **1919 Montagu Declaration**, **Lord Irwin** stated on behalf of His Majesty’s Government that "the natural issue of India’s constitutional progress, as there contemplated, is the attainment of **Dominion Status**" (at an unspecified future date), and promised that once the **Simon Commission** submitted its report, a **Round Table Conference** of British and Indian representatives (including the Princely States) would be convened in London to discuss the next constitutional steps.
*   **The Delhi Manifesto (November 2, 1929):** Meeting in Delhi two days later, top Congress and Liberal leaders (**Mahatma Gandhi**, **Motilal Nehru**, **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Vithalbhai Patel**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**) issued the **Delhi Manifesto**, stipulating four non-negotiable conditions for attending the proposed Round Table Conference: (1) all discussions at the Conference must proceed on the basis of granting immediate **Dominion Status**—acting as a Constituent Assembly to draft a Dominion constitution rather than debating *whether* or *when* it should be granted; (2) the **Indian National Congress** must have majority representation at the Conference; (3) a general amnesty for all political prisoners; and (4) the immediate adoption of a conciliatory policy by the Government of India.
*   **The December 23 Breakdown:** Faced with a fierce parliamentary backlash in London from Conservatives (**Stanley Baldwin**, **Winston Churchill**) and Liberals (**Lloyd George**, **Lord Reading**) who denounced Irwin’s promise of Dominion Status, **Lord Irwin** met **Gandhi**, **Motilal Nehru**, **Sapru**, **Jinnah**, and **Vithalbhai Patel** at Viceroy’s House in New Delhi on **December 23, 1929** (hours after surviving a bomb attack on his viceregal train orchestrated by **Bhagwati Charan Vohra** and **Yashpal**). Irwin bluntly informed Gandhi that he could give no assurance that the Round Table Conference would draft a constitution for Dominion Status, rendering the **Calcutta Congress** ultimatum operative.

#### The December 1929 Lahore Congress Session and the January 26, 1930 Independence Pledge
In the freezing winter of late **December 1929**, the Indian National Congress convened its annual session on the banks of the **Ravi River** in **Lahore**. Although 10 out of 18 Provincial Congress Committees had nominated **Mahatma Gandhi** for the presidency (and 5 had nominated **Vallabhbhai Patel**), Gandhi stepped aside and engineered the election of the 40-year-old **Jawaharlal Nehru** as Congress President—symbolically passing the torch to the radical youth vanguard while binding them to Gandhian discipline.

*   **Nehru’s Socialist-Republican Presidential Address:** In his landmark presidential address, **Jawaharlal Nehru** declared himself "a socialist and a republican, and am no believer in kings and princes, or in the order which produces the modern kings of industry," Situating India's struggle within the global anti-imperialist movement while acknowledging that the Congress as a multiclass national front could not immediately adopt a full socialist programme.
*   **The Purna Swaraj Resolution (Midnight, December 31, 1929):** Key provisions enacted by the **Lahore Congress** included:
    1.  Declaring the **Motilal Nehru Report** to have officially lapsed and redefining the word *Swaraj* in Article 1 of the Congress Constitution to mean **Purna Swaraj** (**Complete Independence**).
    2.  Completely boycotting the proposed **Round Table Conference**.
    3.  Directing all Congress legislators in the Central and Provincial Legislatures to resign their seats immediately and boycott future elections.
    4.  Authorizing the All-India Congress Committee (AICC) to launch a programme of **Civil Disobedience**, including the **non-payment of taxes**, whenever it deemed the country ready, under the supreme tactical leadership of **Mahatma Gandhi**.
    5.  Repudiating the unjust **imperial public debt** accumulated by the colonial regime (such as military wars of conquest in Burma, Afghanistan, and the 1857 suppression), subject to an audit by an impartial tribunal.
*   **The Spatial and Symbolic Semiology:** At the stroke of midnight on **December 31, 1929**, as the one-year Calcutta ultimatum expired, **Jawaharlal Nehru** unfurled the newly adopted **Tricolour Flag** of Indian Independence on the banks of the **Ravi River** amidst chants of *Inquilab Zindabad* and *Bande Mataram*.
*   **The January 26, 1930 Independence Pledge:** To translate the Lahore resolution from an elite convention into a mass village-level ritual, the Congress Working Committee designated **January 26, 1930**, as the first nationwide **Purna Swaraj Day** (**Independence Day**). Drafted by **Mahatma Gandhi** and **Jawaharlal Nehru**, the **Independence Pledge** was read out simultaneously in thousands of towns and villages in local vernaculars by millions of citizens standing under the Tricolour. The Pledge indicted British rule on a fourfold structural charge—**economic**, **political**, **cultural**, and **spiritual** ruin—declaring it "a crime against man and God to submit any longer to a rule that has caused this fourfold disaster to our country," and affirming the inalienable right of the Indian people to alter or abolish a government that deprived them of freedom and exploited the masses.

## III. The Political Economy of the Great Depression, the Salt Monopoly, and the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1931)

### 7. Agrarian Price Collapse, Monetary Orthodoxy, and Gandhi's Eleven Demands (January 1930)

The eruption of the **Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934)** cannot be understood solely through constitutional deadlocks; its explosive social energy was generated by the intersection of nationalist mobilization with the catastrophic impact of the **Great Depression (1929–1933)** on the colonial political economy. Following the Wall Street Crash of **October 1929**, global commodity markets collapsed. Because colonial India had been structurally integrated into the world market as an exporter of raw agricultural commodities (cotton, jute, wheat, oilseeds, groundnuts) and an importer of British manufactured goods, the price crash devastated the Indian countryside while the colonial state enforced a deflationary fiscal and monetary stranglehold.

#### The Anatomy of Agrarian and Monetary Distress
*   **The Scissors Crisis of Fixed Revenue vs. Falling Prices:** Between **1929** and **1932**, the wholesale prices of Indian agricultural produce plunged by over **50%** (for instance, Bengal jute fell from Rs 9 per maund to Rs 3; Punjab wheat fell from Rs 4-12-0 to Rs 1-9-0). However, colonial **land revenue** assessments (in both **Ryotwari** and **Mahalwari** tracts), **Zamindari** rents, canal water rates, and moneylender (*Sahukar*) debt obligations remained fixed in nominal cash terms—meaning the real burden of state taxation and rural debt effectively **doubled overnight**.
*   **The Overvalued 1s 6d Rupee-Sterling Ratio and Distress Gold Exports:** Following the **Hilton Young Commission (1926)** and the **Currency Act of 1927**, the colonial government artificially pegged the Indian Rupee at **1 shilling 6 pence (1s 6d)** instead of the pre-war historical rate of **1s 4d** demanded by Indian industrialists (such as **G.D. Birla** and **Purshottamdas Thakurdas**). An overvalued **1s 6d** Rupee acted as a hidden **12.5% bounty on British imports** entering India while penalizing Indian agricultural exporters and domestic textile mills. To defend this overvalued exchange rate and continue remitting **Home Charges** (~£30–35 million annually) to London during the Depression, the colonial government contracted domestic currency circulation and forced Indian peasant households to liquidate their last savings—their wedding jewelry and family gold—triggering a massive wave of **"distress gold" exports** totaling over **Rs 300 crore** (£240 million) shipped to London between **1931** and **1937**.

#### Gandhi’s Eleven Demands (January 31, 1930)
Assigned by the **Lahore Congress** to initiate the struggle, **Mahatma Gandhi** initially perplexed radical nationalists like **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** when, on **January 31, 1930**, he published an ultimatum to Viceroy **Lord Irwin** in his journal ***Young India*** listing **Eleven Demands** (giving the government until **March 11, 1930**, to accept them). Rather than speaking in abstract constitutional vocabulary about **Purna Swaraj**, Gandhi defined these **Eleven Demands** as the concrete material "**substance of independence**," masterfully welding every aggrieved class of Indian society into a single anti-colonial coalition:

1.  **Three Specific Bourgeois / Capitalist Demands:**
    *   Reduction of the **Rupee-Sterling exchange ratio** from **1s 6d** to **1s 4d** (1 shilling 4 pence).
    *   Imposition of a **protective tariff** on foreign cloth and restriction of foreign textile imports.
    *   Passage of the **Coastal Traffic Reservation Bill** (reserving Indian coastal shipping exclusively for Indian-owned steamship companies, long championed by **Walchand Hirachand** and **S.N. Haji** against the British BI Steam Navigation monopoly).
2.  **One Specific Peasant Demand:**
    *   A **50% reduction in Land Revenue** and subjecting land revenue assessment to direct legislative control.
3.  **Seven Universal / Common National Demands:**
    *   **Abolition of the Salt Tax** and the Government’s **Salt Monopoly**.
    *   **Total prohibition** of intoxicating liquor and drugs (eliminating colonial *Abkari* excise revenue while appealing to women and social reformers).
    *   A **50% reduction in military expenditure** (which consumed nearly half of the central budget to maintain an imperial army of occupation).
    *   A **50% reduction in the salaries** and emoluments of top-grade covenanted civil servants.
    *   **Release of all political prisoners** (except those convicted of Murder or attempted murder), withdrawal of all political prosecutions, abrogation of **Section 124A (Sedition)** and the **1818 Regulation III**, and permission for all Indian political exiles to return.
    *   **Abolition of the Criminal Investigation Department (CID)** or its placement under popular legislative control.
    *   Issuance of **licenses to use firearms** for self-defense under popular control (reversing the racial disarmament of the **Indian Arms Act of 1878**).

### 8. The Dandi Salt March and the Spatial-Ideological Mechanics of Satyagraha (March–April 1930)

When **Lord Irwin** ignored the **Eleven Demands** and declined to meet Gandhi, Gandhi wrote his famous **March 2, 1930**, letter to the Viceroy ("On bended knees I asked for bread and I have received stone instead") announcing that he would inaugurate Civil Disobedience by violating the **Salt Laws**.

#### The Fiscal and Symbolic Genius of Targeting the Salt Monopoly
Under the **Indian Salt Act of 1882**, the colonial state enforced a strict monopoly over the manufacture, collection, and sale of salt across India, criminalizing coastal villagers who evaporated seawater or inland peasants who scraped natural salt-earth, and levying a heavy duty that amounted to roughly **2,400% of the actual cost of production** (fetching the state approximately **Rs 6 to 7 crore**—roughly 4% to 5% of net central revenue). While initially viewed with skepticism by urban intellectuals and dismissed by colonial officials (the *Statesman* mocked that it was difficult to refrain from laughing at the idea of overthrowing the King-Emperor by boiling seawater in a kettle), the **Salt Tax** was the single most brilliant mobilizational target in modern political history:
*   **Universal Subaltern Resonance:** Next to air and water, salt was the most vital biological necessity for human and cattle survival in a tropical climate where manual labourers sweated profusely. Because a starving agricultural labourer consumed at least as much salt as a millionaire, the **Salt Tax** was the most regressive consumption tax in the empire—amounting to nearly **three to five days’ wages per year** for a rural family.
*   **Transcending Internal Class and Communal Cleavages:** Unlike a no-rent campaign (which immediately pitted tenants against Indian *Zamindars*) or a temple-entry drive (which divided orthodox and reformist Hindus), salt had zero divisive sectarian or class connotations. It linked the intimate domestic kitchen economy (immediately drawing millions of **Indian women** into the street movement) directly to the abstract concept of **Purna Swaraj**.

#### The Execution of the Dandi March (March 12 – April 6, 1930)
On **March 12, 1930**, **Mahatma Gandhi** (aged 61), accompanied by **78 handpicked members** of the **Sabarmati Ashram** (carefully selected across regional, linguistic, caste, and religious lines—including scholars, Dalits, Muslims, Christians, and youths from Kerala, Punjab, Bengal, Maharashtra, UP, and Nepal), set out on foot from Sabarmati Ashram in Ahmedabad toward the coastal village of **Dandi** in Navsari (Surat district), **241 miles (388 km)** away on the Arabian Sea.

*   **Spatial Routing through the Gujarat Peasant Heartland:** The 24-day route through **Ahmedabad**, **Kheda**, **Broach**, and **Surat** districts was deliberately chosen because **Vallabhbhai Patel** and Gandhian constructive workers had spent the previous decade building an impenetrable network of rural *Ashrams* and peasant unions there during the **1918 Kheda** and **1928 Bardoli** Satyagrahas. When **Vallabhbhai Patel** was arrested on **March 7, 1930**, at the village of **Ras** while preparing the route, 75,000 people gathered at Sabarmati swearing not to rest until India was free.
*   **Administrative Hollowing and Mass Theatre:** Walking 10 to 12 miles a day and addressing village assemblies every evening, Gandhi turned the slow physical progression into a 24-day live national drama tracked daily by domestic newspapers, underground cyclostyled bulletins (*Congress Patrikas*), and international wire services. Along the route, villagers watered the dusty roads, festooned arches with mango leaves and *Khadi* banners, and over **300 village headmen (*Patels* and *Talatis*)** resigned their government posts on the spot—crippling the base of the colonial revenue administration in central and south Gujarat.
*   **The Ritual Breach at Dandi (April 6, 1930):** Reaching **Dandi** on **April 5**, Gandhi and his marchers waded into the sea for prayers at dawn on **April 6, 1930** (the symbolic anniversary of the 1919 Rowlatt Satyagraha *hartal*). At 6:30 AM, Gandhi bent down and picked up a fistful of natural salt-mud left by the receding tide, technically violating the **Indian Salt Act of 1882** without paying duty. Declaring, "With this, I am shaking the foundations of the British Empire," Gandhi issued the signal for simultaneous, nationwide defiance of the Salt Laws.

The breach of the Salt Law at Dandi acted as a detonator across the subcontinent. #### Coastal Salt Satyagrahas: Vedaranyam, Payyanur, and the Dharasana Salt Works Raid
*   **C. Rajagopalachari’s Vedaranyam March (Tamil Nadu):** Mirroring the Dandi prototype in the Madras Presidency, **C. Rajagopalachari** (*Rajaji*), recently elected President of the Tamil Nadu Congress Committee, led a disciplined column of 100 volunteers on a **150-mile** foot march from **Tiruchirappalli** to the coastal village of **Vedaranyam** in the Tanjore (Thanjavur) delta. Setting out on **April 13, 1930** (Tamil New Year's Day), the marchers sang the nationalist anthem *"Kathiindri Rathamindri Yuddham Ondru Varugudhu"* ("A war is coming without sword and without blood," composed by poet **Namakkal Ramalingam Pillai**). Despite severe warning notifications issued by Tanjore Collector **J.A. Thorne** threatening five years' rigorous imprisonment for anyone feeding or sheltering the marchers, villagers across the Kaveri delta defied the ban. Reaching **Vedaranyam** on **April 28**, **C. Rajagopalachari** broke the salt law at the **Agasthiyampalli** salt pans on **April 30, 1930**, and was immediately arrested alongside **Sardar Vedaratnam Pillai** and **K. Santhanam**, igniting salt protests at Marina Beach in Madras (led by **T. Prakasam** and **K. Nageswara Rao**) and Tuticorin.
*   **K. Kelappan’s Payyanur March (Malabar / Kerala):** In Malabar, **K. Kelappan** (*Kerala Gandhi*)—a veteran of the **1924–25 Vaikom Satyagraha** and founder of the *Mathrubhumi* nationalist daily—led 33 Satyagrahis on a **128-mile** march from **Calicut (Kozhikode)** on **April 13, 1930**, to the beaches of **Payyanur** in North Malabar, chanting **P. Krishna Pillai** and **A.K. Gopalan**’s patriotic songs. After breaking the salt law at **Payyanur** on **April 21, 1930**, the camp was subjected to brutal police raids, and when **Kelappan** was arrested, **Moyarath Sankaran**, **P. Krishna Pillai**, and **K. Madhavan Nair** sustained the agitation across Calicut beach.
*   **The Dharasana Salt Works Raid (May 21, 1930):** On **May 4, 1930**, **Mahatma Gandhi** wrote to **Lord Irwin** announcing his intention to lead a non-violent raid to take possession of the government-owned **Dharasana Salt Works** in Surat district (150 miles north of Bombay). Before midnight on **May 4–5**, colonial police arrested Gandhi at his camp in **Karadi** under the archaic **Bombay Regulation XXV of 1827** (permitting indefinite detention without trial). The leadership of the march immediately passed to Gandhi’s chosen successor, the 76-year-old retired Baroda Chief Justice **Abbas Tyabji**, who was arrested on **May 12**. Command then passed to **Sarojini Naidu**—the first Indian woman President of the Congress (**1925 Kanpur Session**)—assisted by **Imam Saheb** (**Abdul Qadir Bawazeer**, Gandhi’s 62-year-old comrade from South Africa) and Gandhi’s son **Manilal Gandhi**. On **May 21, 1930**, **Sarojini Naidu**, **Imam Saheb**, and **Manilal Gandhi** led 2,500 unarmed Satyagrahis toward the barbed-wire stockades and water-filled ditches surrounding the **Dharasana Salt Works**. Under the command of British officers, 400 Indian police armed with five-foot *lathis* tipped with steel ferrules charged the silent columns. As documented to the entire world by the American United Press correspondent **Webb Miller** (whose dispatch was flashed to 1,350 newspapers globally and read into the official record of the United States Senate), wave after wave of Satyagrahis walked forward in disciplined ranks without raising an arm to fend off the blows as their skulls were fractured and bodies carried away on stretchers—crystallizing the supreme moral asymmetry of non-violent **Satyagraha** against raw colonial coercion. Simultaneously, on the exact same day in Bombay, 28,000 volunteers raided the **Wadala Salt Depot** (where **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** and **Lilavati Munshi** played vanguard roles), and similar raids struck **Sanikatta** in Karnataka.

#### Frontier, Tribal, and Urban Militancy: Peshawar, Rani Gaidinliu, and Sholapur
*   **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan’s Khudai Khidmatgars and Chandra Singh Garhwali at Peshawar (April 1930):** In the **North-West Frontier Province (NWFP)**—the empire’s most heavily militarized strategic buffer where the colonial regime had denied basic constitutional reforms—**Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** (*Badshah Khan* / *Frontier Gandhi*) had forged a remarkable Pathan socio-political renaissance. Building upon his *Anjuman-i-Islah-ul-Afghania* (1921) and the Pashto journal ***Pakhtun*** (**1928**), he founded the **Khudai Khidmatgars** ("Servants of God") in **1929**, popularly known as the **Red Shirts** (*Surkh Posh*) due to their brick-dust-dyed tunics. Combining Islamic ethical egalitarianism with strict Gandhian non-violence, **Badshah Khan** mobilized over 50,000 Pathan villagers against tribal blood-feuds and British rule. On **April 23, 1930**, the arrest of **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** and local Congress leaders triggered a massive, peaceful general strike in **Peshawar**. When colonial authorities dispatched armored cars into the packed **Qissa Khwani Bazaar**, running down protesters and opening machine-gun fire that killed over 200 unarmed civilians, the crowd stood its ground collecting the dead. At this critical juncture, two platoons of the **2/18th Royal Garhwal Rifles**—a Hindu regiment from the Himalayan hills deployed to suppress a Muslim Pathan crowd—led by Havildar Major **Chandra Singh Garhwali**, lowered their rifles and **refused the British officer’s order to fire** on unarmed country-men, declaring: *"Garhwali cease fire! We will not shoot our unarmed brethren."* For ten days (**April 25 to May 4, 1930**), British forces were forced to evacuate **Peshawar**, leaving the city entirely administered by the **Khudai Khidmatgars** until British infantry backed by Royal Air Force squadrons reoccupied it. **Chandra Singh Garhwali** and his men were court-martialed and sentenced to life imprisonment (serving 11 years before release in 1941), marking a terrifying crack in the post-1857 "Martial Races" military architecture.
*   **Rani Gaidinliu’s Heraka Movement in Manipur and the Naga Hills (1931–1932):** In the northeastern frontier hills of **Manipur**, the **Naga Hills**, and the **Cachar** tracts, anti-colonial resistance fused with an indigenous Zeliangrong Naga socio-religious revitalization movement known as **Heraka** ("Pure"). Initiated in the late 1920s by the charismatic spiritual leader **Haipou Jadonang** (1905–1931), the **Heraka** movement sought to reform traditional Naga religion by abolishing irrational taboos and animal sacrifices, resisting both Christian missionary proselytization and Kukis' encroachment, and establishing an independent **"Naga Raj"** by expelling the British and refusing to pay the colonial **house tax** (*Khajana*) and perform forced **porterage labour** (*Pothang* / *Begar*). After the British arrested and executed **Jadonang** by hanging at Imphal on **August 29, 1931**, his 16-year-old spiritual and political successor, **Gaidinliu** (born 1915 at Nungkao village), assumed supreme leadership. Linking the **Heraka** prophecy directly with the **Civil Disobedience Movement**—proclaiming to the Zeliangrong Nagas that "Gandhi Raja" was coming to end British rule—**Gaidinliu** organized armed guerrilla fortifications, tax boycotts, and forest defiance across Manipur, Assam, and Nagaland. Following a massive manhunt by the **Assam Rifles**, **Gaidinliu** was captured at **Pulomi** village in **October 1932** while building a wooden fortress, and sentenced to life imprisonment (serving 14 years in Guwahati, Shillong, Aizawl, and Tura jails until India's independence in 1947). After meeting her in Shillong Jail in **1937**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** hailed her indomitable spirit and bestowed upon her the title "**Rani**" (**Rani Gaidinliu**).
*   **The Sholapur Textile Workers' Strike and Parallel Administration (May 1930):** The sharpest urban working-class intervention of the Civil Disobedience Movement occurred in the industrial town of **Sholapur** in southern Maharashtra. Immediately following **Mahatma Gandhi**’s arrest on **May 5, 1930**, over **50,000 textile mill workers** in Sholapur launched a militant general strike. When police opened fire on a procession felling palm trees (the source of taxed *toddy* liquor) on **May 8**, workers retaliated by burning liquor shops, railway stations, municipal buildings, law courts, and police outposts. The British District Magistrate and police fled the town, and for several days (**May 8–15, 1930**), the local Congress committee and volunteer corps established a virtual **parallel administration** in **Sholapur**, hoisting the National Flag over the municipal hall and directing city traffic and civic order. Alarmed by this proletarian takeover, the colonial regime declared **Martial Law** in Sholapur on **May 16, 1930**, deploying British troops who imposed shoot-at-sight curfews against anyone wearing a Gandhi cap and subsequently executed four prominent local leaders—**Mallappa Dhanshetti**, **Abdul Rasul Qurban Hussain**, **Jagannath Shinde**, and **Kisan Sarda**—by hanging on **January 12, 1931**.

#### Inland Fiscal, Ecological, and Student Defiance: Chaukidari Tax, Forest Satyagrahas, and the Cunningham Circular
*   **Chaukidari Tax Refusal in Bihar and Bengal:** In landlocked provinces like **Bihar** and interior **Bengal**, where geographical absence of a coastline precluded large-scale marine salt manufacture (after initial symbolic boiling of salt-earth *khari*), the movement pivoted to the non-payment of the **Chaukidari Tax**—a deeply hated rural tax levied on every village household to pay the salaries of the village watchmen (*Chaukidars*). Because *Chaukidars* functioned as the grass-roots eyes, ears, and informers of the colonial police station (*Thana*) and local *Zamindars*, refusing the **Chaukidari Tax** struck simultaneously at colonial fiscal revenue and rural surveillance. Spearheaded by **Rajendra Prasad** and **Abdul Bari** in Bihar (across **Saran**, **Champaran**, **Muzaffarpur**, **Bhagalpur**, and **Munger** districts) and in **Midnapore** in Bengal, peasants refused to pay the tax even when police seized their bullocks, grain stores, brass utensils, and ploughs worth ten times the tax amount, while hundreds of *Chaukidars* and *Dafadars* resigned their badges.
*   **Forest Satyagrahas in Central Provinces, Maharashtra, and Karnataka:** In the forested highlands of the **Central Provinces and Berar** (Betul, Seoni, Chhindwara), **Maharashtra** (Nashik, Satara, Akola, Kolaba), and **Karnataka** (Uttara Kannada), the **Indian Forest Acts of 1878 and 1927** had enclosed vast tracts as "Reserved Forests," criminalizing Adivasi shifting cultivation, timber gathering, and cattle grazing. During the summer and monsoon of **1930**, Congress leaders (such as **D.P. Mishra**, **Brijlal Biyani**, and **M.S. Aney**—who was arrested in **July 1930** while cutting a handful of grass with a sickle at Pusad in Yavatmal) converged with autonomous **Gond** and **Korku** Adivasi movements led by tribal leaders like **Ganjan Korku** in **Betul** and **Turia** in **Seoni**. Tens of thousands of tribals and peasants marched into Reserved Forests waving the Tricolour, cutting timber, and grazing cattle without paying colonial grazing fees (*pullari*), enduring lethal police firings at **Banjari Dhal** and **Turia**.
*   **No-Revenue and Hijrat in Gujarat, UP, and Karnataka:** In **Kheda**, **Bardoli**, and **Jambusar** in Gujarat, peasant proprietors launched a total refusal of **land revenue**. When colonial authorities responded by confiscating lands, crops, and buffaloes and beating villagers, over **80,000 peasants** executed a mass ***Hijrat*** (collective protest migration)—abandoning their ancestral villages, homes, and standing crops in British territory to live in makeshift tent camps across the border in the adjoining Princely State of **Baroda** until the signing of the Gandhi-Irwin Pact. In the **United Provinces**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** initiated a militant **No-Tax / No-Rent campaign** in **October 1930** (calling upon *Zamindars* to withhold revenue from the state and tenants to withhold rent from loyalist *Zamindars* in Agra and Rae Bareli), while in **Siddapur** and **Ankola** talukas of **North Kanara (Karnataka)**, peasant women and *Havik* cultivators sustained a heroic no-revenue struggle.
*   **The Assam Cunningham Circular Student Agitation (May 1930):** To crush the militant participation of school and college students in **Assam**, **J.R. Cunningham**, the British Director of Public Instruction in Assam, issued a draconian administrative directive in **May 1930** known as the **Cunningham Circular**. The Circular banned students from participating in any political activity, picketing, or hartals, and mandated that every student and their parents or guardians sign a written bond guaranteeing good political behavior and immediate obedience, on pain of summary expulsion, denial of scholarships, and blacklisting. Instead of intimidating the youth, the **Cunningham Circular** ignited a fierce province-wide student revolt across the Brahmaputra and Surma valleys: thousands of students boycotted government schools and colleges, picketed educational institutions, and established independent national schools (such as the *Kamrup Academy* in Guwahati).

## IV. Imperial Constitutional Diplomacy, the Karachi Socio-Economic Charter, and the Round Table Impasse (1930–1934)

### 10. The First Round Table Conference and the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1930–1931)

By the autumn of **1930**, over **90,000 Satyagrahis** were packed into colonial prisons, British cloth imports had fallen by more than half (from £26 million in 1929 to £13.7 million in 1930, with 16 British Lancashire mills shutting down permanently), and colonial customs, excise, and forest revenues had suffered a severe fiscal contraction. Amid this confrontation, the **Simon Commission Report** was finally published in **May 1930** (proposing the abolition of provincial Dyarchy in favor of provincial autonomy subject to sweeping gubernatorial overriding powers, while completely rejecting any responsible government at the Centre). Recognizing that the **Simon Report** was dead on arrival, the British Government proceeded with the **First Round Table Conference** in London.

#### The First Round Table Conference (November 12, 1930 – January 19, 1931)
Inaugurated by **King George V** at St. James’s Palace in London and chaired by Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald**, the **First Round Table Conference** was the first constitutional assembly in which British and Indian delegates sat officially as peers rather than ruler and subject. However, because the **Indian National Congress**—the single largest political force in the country—was boycotting the Conference and waging mass Satyagraha from prison, the gathering resembled, in the contemporary phrase, "a staging of *Hamlet* without the Prince of Denmark."

*   **Composition of the 89 Delegates:** The Conference comprised 16 British parliamentary delegates (from the Labour, Conservative, and Liberal parties), 16 representatives of the **Indian Princely States** (including the rulers of **Bikaner**, **Bhopal**, **Patiala**, **Alwar**, and **Baroda**, alongside Dewans **Mirza Ismail** of Mysore and **Akbar Hydari** of Hyderabad), and 57 nominated delegates from British India selected by the Viceroy to represent diverse sectional interests—including the **Liberal Federation** (**Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **V.S. Srinivasa Sastri**, **C.Y. Chintamani**, **M.R. Jayakar**), the **Muslim League** (**Aga Khan III**, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, **Maulana Mohammad Ali**, **Muhammad Shafi**, **Fazlul Huq**), the **Hindu Mahasabha** (**B.S. Moonje**), the **Depressed Classes** (**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and **Rettamalai Srinivasan**), the **Sikhs** (**Sampuran Singh**, **Ujjal Singh**), **Indian Christians** (**K.T. Paul**), **Women** (**Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz**, **Radhabai Subbarayan**), **Labour** (**N.M. Joshi**, **B. Shiva Rao**), and Anglo-Indians/Europeans.
*   **The All-India Federation Counter-Strategy:** At the opening plenary, **Tej Bahadur Sapru** invited the Indian Princes to join British India in an immediate **All-India Federation** with a responsible central executive. To the surprise of many observers, the Maharaja of **Bikaner** and the Nawab of **Bhopal** immediately endorsed the federation proposal. For British imperial strategists (like **Lord Reading** and **Sir Samuel Hoare**) and the Princes alike, this **All-India Federation** was a calculated masterstroke:
    1.  For the **Princes**, joining a federal centre allowed them to bypass the **Butler Committee**’s unpopular fiscal wall and secure direct leverage over central customs tariffs and railway policy while insulating their internal autocracies from **Praja Mandal** democratization.
    2.  For **Whitehall**, conceding "responsible government at the Centre" became safe only if a conservative bloc of **nominees appointed by autocratic Princes** (occupying 33% to 40% of the federal legislature) was combined with communally divided British Indian representatives to neutralize the **Indian National Congress**, while "safeguards" kept Defense, External Affairs, and 80% of the federal budget firmly in the Viceroy’s hands.
*   **The Imperative of Congress Inclusion:** Realizing that no federal constitution could function in India while the **Congress** remained at war with the Raj, **Ramsay MacDonald** adjourned the **First Round Table Conference** on **January 19, 1931**, with an appeal to bring the Congress into the second session.

#### The Gandhi-Irwin Pact (March 5, 1931)
On **January 25, 1931**, **Lord Irwin** unconditionally released **Mahatma Gandhi** and all members of the **Congress Working Committee** and lifted the ban on the CWC to create an atmosphere for talks. Moreover, Indian business magnates (**Purshottamdas Thakurdas**, **G.D. Birla**, **Walchand Hirachand**, **Homi Mody**), whose commercial credit and working capital were strained by prolonged hartals and boycotts, together with Liberal mediators **Tej Bahadur Sapru** and **M.R. Jayakar**, urged Gandhi to seek an honourable settlement. Between **February 17** and **March 4, 1931**, **Mahatma Gandhi** and **Lord Irwin** held eight protracted face-to-face meetings at Viceroy’s House in New Delhi—negotiating as plenipotentiaries of two equal powers (provoking **Winston Churchill**’s infamous racist tirade against the "nude fakir" striding half-naked up the steps of the Viceregal Palace to parley on equal terms with the representative of the King-Emperor).

On **March 5, 1931**, the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** (also known as the **Delhi Pact**) was signed:

*   **Concessions Agreed by the Colonial Government (Irwin):**
    1.  Immediate withdrawal of all emergency repressive ordinances and termination of pending political prosecutions.
    2.  **Immediate release of all political prisoners** not convicted of direct violence.
    3.  **Remission of all uncollected fines** imposed on Satyagrahis during the movement.
    4.  **Return of all forfeited movable and immovable properties** (including agricultural lands in **Kheda** and **Bardoli**) **provided they had not already been sold to third parties**.
    5.  Lenient treatment toward **resigned government servants** (such as village *Patels* and *Talatis*), re-employing them wherever their posts had not been permanently filled.
    6.  **Right to Make Salt for Personal Consumption:** Villagers residing immediately along the sea coasts were granted the legal right to collect or manufacture **salt for domestic consumption** or local village sale on foot (though not for commercial transport beyond the village), effectively breaching the psychological absolutism of the **1882 Salt Act**.
    7.  **Right to Peaceful Picketing:** Recognition of the legal right to conduct peaceful, unaggressive picketing of **foreign cloth**, **liquor**, and **opium** shops.
*   **Commitments Agreed by the Congress (Gandhi):**
    1.  Immediate **suspension (discontinuance) of the Civil Disobedience Movement** (including the lifting of no-tax campaigns, commercial boycotts used as political weapons against British goods *qua* British, and salt raids).
    2.  Agreement that the **Indian National Congress** would participate in the **Second Round Table Conference** in London on the constitutional frame of Federation, Indian Responsibility, and "reservations or safeguards in the interests of India" (for defense, external affairs, minorities, and financial credit).
    3.  Withdrawal of the Congress demand for an official public inquiry into **police excesses** during the movement.
*   **Colonial Refusals and Radical Disappointment:** **Lord Irwin** remained immovable on two critical points: (1) he refused to force loyalist purchasers to return auctioned peasant lands in **Gujarat** that had already been sold to **third parties** (though Gandhi and **Vallabhbhai Patel** pledged that a future Swaraj government would restore every inch of land), and (2) despite Gandhi’s repeated personal appeals during their meetings and a final letter on the morning of March 23, Irwin refused to commute the death sentences of **Bhagat Singh**, **Sukhdev**, and **Rajguru** in the Lahore Conspiracy Case, executing them on **March 23, 1931**—six days before the **Karachi Congress Session**.

### 11. The 1931 Karachi Congress Session: Constitutional Rights and Economic Dirigisme

Convening at **Karachi** from **March 26 to 31, 1931**, under the presidency of **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**, the **1931 Karachi Congress Session** met under an atmosphere of intense grief and youth anger over the execution of **Bhagat Singh**, **Sukhdev**, and **Rajguru** (members of the **Naujawan Bharat Sabha** greeted Gandhi with black cloth flowers at Karachi railway station). The session navigated this tension by passing a resolution drafted by Gandhi and moved by **Jawaharlal Nehru** that placed on record its profound admiration for the bravery and self-sacrifice of **Bhagat Singh** and his comrades while dissociating the Congress from political violence.

The session unanimously endorsed the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact**, explicitly reiterating that the goal of **Purna Swaraj** remained intact and appointing **Mahatma Gandhi** as the **sole representative** of the Congress to the **Second Round Table Conference**. However, the supreme historical monument of the **1931 Karachi Congress Session** was the adoption of the **Resolution on Fundamental Rights and National Economic Programme**—originally drafted by **Jawaharlal Nehru**, modified in consultation with **Mahatma Gandhi** (who added eight clauses) and **M.N. Roy** (then attending incognito under the alias Dr. Mahmud), and moved from the chair. For the first time in its history, the Indian National Congress defined what *Swaraj* would concretely mean for the starving peasant and industrial worker, establishing the normative blueprint for **Part III (Fundamental Rights)** and **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)** of the **1950 Constitution of India**:

*   **Civil Liberties and Democratic Egalitarianism:** Guaranteed freedom of expression, press, and association; freedom of conscience and **religious neutrality of the state**; equality before the law without distinction of caste, creed, or sex; disability removal regarding public wells, tanks, roads, and schools; **universal adult franchise**; **free and compulsory primary education**; abolition of capital punishment; and freedom of movement across India.
*   **Agrarian Relief and Peasant Protection:** Committed the future Swaraj state to a rigorous reform of land tenure and revenue systems, an immediate and substantial **reduction in agricultural rent and land revenue**, total **exemption from rent on uneconomic holdings** for a specified period, relief of agricultural indebtedness, and state control of usury.
*   **Industrial Labour Welfare:** Mandated a **living wage** and healthy conditions of work for industrial workers, limited hours of labour, suitable machinery for settling labour disputes, old-age/sickness/unemployment insurance, liberation of bonded serfs, **protection of women workers** with adequate **maternity leave benefits**, absolute **prohibition of boy and girl child labour** of school-going age in factories and mines, and the **right of workers and peasants to form unions** to protect their interests.
*   **State Dirigisme and Fiscal Restructuring:** Enshrined the socialist principle that **"the State shall own or control key industries and services, mineral resources, railways, waterways, shipping and other means of public transport."** Furthermore, it pledged to protect indigenous cloth and industries against foreign competition, control currency and exchange in the national interest, slash military expenditure by at least one-half, cap the maximum salary of any civil servant at **Rs 500 per month** (except for specialized experts), levy a graduated tax on agricultural income from large estates and a **death duty (inheritance tax)** on property above a fixed limit, and abolish the **Salt Tax** completely.

### 12. The Second and Third Round Table Conferences, Coercive Martial Rule, and the 1934 Withdrawal

Between the signing of the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** in **March 1931** and the opening of the **Second Round Table Conference** in **September 1931**, the political landscape in both Britain and India underwent a drastic rightward shift that doomed the negotiations before they began.

#### The Second Round Table Conference (September 7 – December 1, 1931)
In **April 1931**, **Lord Irwin** retired as Viceroy and was replaced by **Lord Willingdon**—a hardline Tory imperialist who openly despised the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** for elevating Gandhi to the status of a co-equal sovereign. Simultaneously, in **August 1931**, Britain's deepening financial crisis caused the collapse of the Labour Government; **Ramsay MacDonald** formed a Conservative-dominated **"National Government"** in which the arch-conservative **Sir Samuel Hoare** replaced **Wedgwood Benn** as Secretary of State for India. Sailing from Bombay on the *SS Rajputana*, **Mahatma Gandhi** arrived in London as the **sole official representative of the Indian National Congress** (while **Sarojini Naidu**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, and **G.D. Birla** attended as individual nominees of the Viceroy).

*   **Gandhi’s Hegemonic Claim vs. Sectional Fragmentation:** At the **Second Round Table Conference**, Gandhi asserted that the **Indian National Congress** alone was a truly national, all-inclusive organization representing 85% of India's rural peasantry, women, and all communities—including the **Depressed Classes**—and demanded that full responsible self-government be established immediately at the Centre and in the Provinces, with complete Indian control over Defense, External Affairs, and Finance.
*   **The Minorities Committee Deadlock:** The British Cabinet systematically exploited the presence of handpicked communal and sectional delegates to refute Gandhi’s claim that the Congress spoke for all of India. Inside the **Minorities Committee**, talks broke down over the question of **separate electorates** and weightage. Encouraged by British Tory backbenchers, representatives of the Muslims (**Aga Khan III**), Depressed Classes (**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**), Anglo-Indians (**Henry Gidney**), Indian Christians (**Pannirselvam**), and the European commercial community (**Hubert Carr**) signed a joint **Minorities Pact** demanding separate electorates for each of their groups. While Gandhi had offered a "blank cheque" to Muslims and Sikhs if they supported the unified demand for national independence, he fiercely opposed **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**’s demand for **separate electorates for the Depressed Classes**, arguing that constitutionally segregating Dalits from the Hindu fold would permanently institutionalize untouchability and fracture every village in India.
*   **Collapse of the Conference:** With the **Princes** cooling toward Federation once they realized the Congress demanded democratic representation from the States' people, and the **Minorities Committee** deadlocked, **Ramsay MacDonald** closed the **Second Round Table Conference** on **December 1, 1931**, announcing the unilateral creation of three British-controlled Muslim-majority provinces (**NWFP** and **Sindh**) and warning that if Indian leaders failed to reach an internal communal agreement, the British Government would unilaterally impose its own **Communal Award**.

#### The Second Phase of Civil Disobedience (1932–1934) and Willingdon's Martial Blitzkrieg
Even before **Mahatma Gandhi** landed back in Bombay on **December 28, 1931**, **Lord Willingdon** had dismantled the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact** and launched a pre-planned preemptive counter-insurgency offensive across India. Determined never again to negotiate with the Congress or allow a slow buildup like the 1930 Dandi March, the colonial bureaucracy had spent the 1931 truce drafting a comprehensive battery of **draconian emergency ordinances**—establishing virtual **"Civil Martial Law"**:
*   **Preemptive Arrests in UP, NWFP, and Bengal:** In the **United Provinces**, where the Congress had launched a no-rent campaign amid continuing agrarian distress, **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Purushottam Das Tandon** were arrested on **December 26, 1931**. In the **NWFP**, the **Khudai Khidmatgars** were declared illegal and **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** and his brother **Dr. Khan Sahib** were arrested on **December 24**. In **Bengal**, draconian ordinances turned Chittagong and Midnapore into military zones.
*   **Resumption of CDM (January 4, 1932):** When **Lord Willingdon** refused even to grant Gandhi an interview to discuss these repressive measures, the **Congress Working Committee** resumed the **Civil Disobedience Movement** on **January 1, 1932**. On **January 4, 1932**, **Mahatma Gandhi** and **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** were arrested and imprisoned together in Yerawada Jail, and the colonial state unleashed four sweeping ordinances (**Emergency Powers**, **Unlawful Instigation**, **Unlawful Associations**, and **Prevention of Molestation and Boycotting Ordinances**):
    *   Every level of the **Indian National Congress**—from the AICC down to village committees, youth leagues, *Ashrams*, national schools, and volunteer corps—was outlawed as an illegal organization.
    *   All Congress bank accounts, buildings (*Swaraj Bhawan* in Allahabad), printing presses, motor vehicles, and funds were confiscated.
    *   The **Indian Press (Emergency Powers) Act** imposed absolute censorship, prohibiting newspapers from even printing the names or photographs of Satyagrahis.
*   **Popular Resistance and Structural Exhaustion (1932–1933):** Despite the decapitation of its entire leadership in the first week, the movement displayed immense initial resilience: over **80,000 Satyagrahis** courted arrest in the first four months of **1932** alone (and over **120,000** by early 1933). Urban women picketed foreign cloth shops, rural peasants in UP, Bihar, and Karnataka defied tax attachments, underground Congress radio transmitters operated briefly in Bombay, and delegates held defied, clandestine annual Congress sessions under police lathi charges at **Delhi (April 1932)** and **Calcutta (March 1933, presided over by Nellie Sengupta)**. Simultaneously, Britain enforced the **Ottawa Imperial Preference Agreement (August 1932)**, binding Indian tariffs to British imperial trade interests over the protests of the Indian Legislative Assembly.
*   **The Third Round Table Conference (November 17 – December 24, 1932):** Held in London without the **Indian National Congress** and boycotted even by the **British Labour Party**, the **Third Round Table Conference** was a truncated gathering of only **46 delegates** that rubber-stamped **Sir Samuel Hoare**’s constitutional proposals, published as the **White Paper of March 1933** (which formed the basis of the **Government of India Act 1935**).
*   **The 1933–1934 Withdrawal:** Following **Ramsay MacDonald’s Communal Award (August 1932)** and the **Poona Pact (September 1932)** (analyzed in full in Part 8), **Mahatma Gandhi** shifted his immediate focus toward the anti-untouchability (*Harijan*) campaign. Recognizing that two waves of intense repression and property confiscations had exhausted the peasantry and middle classes, Gandhi undertook a 21-day self-purification fast in **May 1933**, upon which the government released him. Gandhi immediately suspended mass Civil Disobedience, replacing it in **July 1933** with **Individual Civil Disobedience** by selected Satyagrahis. Finally, in **April 1934**, Gandhi called off Civil Disobedience altogether, and in **May 1934**, the **All-India Congress Committee (AICC)** meeting at **Patna** officially ratified the **1934 withdrawal** of the Civil Disobedience Movement and authorized Congressmen to contest the upcoming **November 1934 Central Legislative Assembly elections**.

### 13. Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Mass Satyagraha Suspension to Left-Wing Radicalization and the 1935 Constitutional Arena

The **1934 withdrawal** of the Civil Disobedience Movement did not signify a defeat of Indian nationalism; rather, the **1930–1934** crucible had permanently transformed the balance of forces on the subcontinent. By forcing the Viceroy of India to negotiate the **1931 Gandhi-Irwin Pact** on equal terms, mobilizing millions of peasant proprietors, Adivasis, industrial workers, and women into active statutory defiance, and adopting the **1931 Karachi Resolution**, the Indian National Congress had established itself as the parallel hegemonic state in waiting. However, the suspension of mass Satyagraha in **1934** without the immediate attainment of **Purna Swaraj**, combined with the devastating socio-economic fallout of the **Great Depression** on rural tenants and urban factory workers, generated a profound ideological ferment within the nationalist ranks. Disillusioned with the cycle of Gandhian compromise and truce, a powerful new cohort of young radicals inside and outside the Congress—anchored by **Jawaharlal Nehru**, **Subhas Chandra Bose**, **Jayaprakash Narayan**, **Acharya Narendra Deva**, **Swami Sahajanand Saraswati**, and the underground **Communist Party of India (CPI)**—concluded that political independence could only be achieved by organizing the peasantry and industrial proletariat along independent class lines. This structural pivot directly generated the **1934 formation of the Congress Socialist Party (CSP)** in Patna, the **1936 founding of the All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)**, the militant resurgence of the **All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC)**, the integration of the Princely **Praja Mandal** movements, and the rise of **Socialist Realism** across literature and theatre (**PWA** and **IPTA**)—the exact structural forces examined in **Part 13**.
