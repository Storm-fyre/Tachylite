# Part 8: Anti-Caste Radicalism, Non-Brahmin Mobilizations, Dalit Emancipation, and Women’s Social Assertion (1829–1947)

## I. Subaltern Anti-Caste Radicalism and Non-Brahmin Assertion in Western India (1848–1920)

### 1. The Political Economy of Peshwa-Colonial Continuity and the Satyashodhak Rupture

#### The Brahmanical-Colonial Administrative Nexus under the Bombay Ryotwari Regime
The British conquest of the Maratha Confederacy in **1818** did not dismantle the social hierarchy of the erstwhile Peshwa state; rather, the colonial **Ryotwari Settlement** in the Bombay Presidency restructured and intensified upper-caste dominance through a new bureaucratic and legal apparatus. Under the Peshwa regime, Chitpavan and Deshastha Brahmins had monopolized ritual authority, military command, and revenue administration (*Kulkarni* village accountancy and *Mamlatdar* district collection). When the East India Company instituted direct Ricardian rent extraction from the cultivator (*Ryot*), it relied overwhelmingly on this literate Brahmin gentry to staff the subordinate revenue, judicial, and police bureaucracies, while Marwari and Gujarati Bania merchants alongside Brahmin usurers (*Sahukars*) financed the rigid cash-revenue demands of the colonial state. 

This structural convergence produced what **Jyotirao Phule** diagnosed as the **Shetji-Bhatji** (moneylender-priest) nexus. While the *Bhatji* (Brahmin clerk, judge, and ritual specialist) monopolized colonial civil courts, district collectorates, and ritual life-cycle taxation, the *Shetji* (usurer) utilized British contract laws and civil courts to expropriate the land of indebted **Kunbi-Maratha**, **Mali**, and **Dhangar** peasants (*Shudras*) and perpetuate the hereditary agrarian servitude (*Balutedari* and *Vethbegari*) of the untouchable **Mahar** and **Mang** communities (*Ati-Shudras*). Upper-caste socio-religious reform organizations in Western India—such as the **Prarthana Samaj** and the **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha**—sought to modernize domestic norms within the bourgeois upper-caste household without challenging the material foundations of varna-jati surplus extraction or the bureaucratic monopoly of the literati.

#### Jyotirao Phule, Savitribai Phule, and the Institutional Counter-Grid (1848–1873)
Recognizing that the colonial education policy—anchored in **Macaulay’s Downward Filtration Theory**—funneled public revenues derived from peasant taxation exclusively into the education of the Brahmin elite, **Jyotirao Phule** (1827–1890) and **Savitribai Phule** (1831–1897) initiated a direct institutional assault on the monopoly of knowledge and ritual mediation. Born into the agrarian **Mali** (gardener) caste and influenced by **Thomas Paine’s *Rights of Man***, republican egalitarianism, and anti-Brahmanical Bhakti/Buddhist traditions, Phule identified the denial of literacy to women and lower castes as the primary instrument maintaining Peshwa-era social hegemony under British rule.

*   **The 1848 Pune Pedagogical Rupture**: In **August 1848**, **Jyotirao Phule** and **Savitribai Phule** established India’s first school for lower-caste and untouchable girls at **Bhide Wada** in **Pune**. When orthodox pressure forced Phule’s family to evict the couple, **Usman Sheikh** and his sister **Fatima Sheikh**—who became the first Muslim woman teacher in modern India alongside Savitribai—provided their residence in Pune to house the school and co-taught the subaltern curriculum. Between **1848 and 1852**, the Phules opened **18 schools** across Pune and its environs (including schools at **Rasta Peth** and **Vetal Peth** specifically for **Mahar** and **Mang** children), replacing rote Sanskritic learning with practical agronomy, grammar, mathematics, and historical critique.
*   **Subaltern Welfare and Bodily Protection**: Confronting the brutal intersection of Brahmanical patriarchy and caste endogamy—which punished upper-caste widowhood with social death while driving pregnant widows to suicide or infanticide—the Phules opened a **Home for the Prevention of Infanticide (Balhatya Pratibandhak Griha)** in their own residence in **1863**, offering safe maternity care to ostracized Brahmin widows and adopting **Yashwant**, the son of a Brahmin widow, as their legal heir. In **1868**, they threw open their private domestic water cistern to untouchables, directly violating the spatial pollution codes of Pune.
*   **The Satyashodhak Samaj (1873)**: On **September 24, 1873**, Phule founded the **Satyashodhak Samaj** (Truth-Seekers' Society) in Pune. Designed as a horizontal, non-Brahmanical mass organization uniting *Shudras* (cultivating, pastoral, and artisanal castes) and *Ati-Shudras* (untouchables), the Samaj rejected the authority of the *Vedas*, *Smritis*, and *Puranas*, abolished the need for a Brahmin priest (*Bhat*) in weddings and funerary rites, and instituted secular, egalitarian **Satyashodhak marriage vows (*Mangalashtakas*)** recited by the bride and groom in Marathi.
*   **Print Capitalism and Working-Class Convergence**: Supported by **Krishnarao Bhalekar** (who launched the Samaj’s chief organ, ***Deenbandhu***, in **1877**) and **Narayan Meghaji Lokhande** (who later took over ***Deenbandhu*** in Bombay and established the **Bombay Millhands Association** in **1890**), the Satyashodhak movement linked rural anti-caste peasant mobilization directly with the nascent industrial proletariat of the Bombay textile mills.

#### Ideological Deconstruction: Gulamgiri, Shetkaryacha Asud, and the King Bali Counter-Myth
To shatter the internalized ideological legitimacy of the varna order, **Jyotirao Phule** executed a radical historiographical inversion across a series of foundational texts: the play ***Tritiya Ratna*** (**1855**, exposing how Brahmin priests manipulated an illiterate peasant couple through astrological superstition), the ballad ***Chatrapati Shivaji Raje Bhosale Yancha Powada*** (**1869**, reinterpreting **Shivaji** not as a protector of cows and Brahmins, but as a *Kulwadi-Bhushan*—the champion of the toiling Shudra peasantry), ***Gulamgiri*** (**1873**), and ***Shetkaryacha Asud*** (**1883**).

*   ***Gulamgiri* (Slavery, 1873) and the King Bali Counter-Myth**: Dedicated to the *"Good People of the United States"* for their abolition of Black chattel slavery during the American Civil War, ***Gulamgiri*** inverted the colonial Orientalist "Aryan Theory of Race." While upper-caste nationalists used Max Müller’s Aryan thesis to claim racial kinship with British rulers, Phule argued that the Aryans (*Irani Bhats*) were alien conquerors who invaded an indigenous, prosperous, and egalitarian agrarian society ruled by the benevolent autochthonous monarch **King Bali (*Baliraja*)**. In Phule’s historical materialist reading of Puranic mythology, the **Dashavatara** tales (such as **Vamana** trampling King Bali and **Parashurama** exterminating the *Kshatriyas* twenty-one times) were distorted historical records of violent military campaigns waged against the indigenous **Kshetriyas** (people of the land/field). Those indigenous warriors who offered the fiercest military resistance to Aryan subjugation—the **Mahars** (*Maha-Ari*, or "Great Enemy") and **Mangs**—were socially ostracized, stripped of land rights, and reduced to hereditary untouchability once defeated. By invoking the popular agrarian blessing *"Ida Pida Javo, Baliche Rajya Yevo"* (Let sorrow and misery depart, let the kingdom of Bali come), Phule transformed **King Bali** into a potent utopian symbol of an anti-caste peasant democracy, contrasting him with the Brahmanical cult of Rama and Vamana.
*   ***Shetkaryacha Asud* (The Cultivator’s Whipcord, 1883)**: Read aloud while dressed as an impoverished peasant before the Governor of Bombay and Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III, ***Shetkaryacha Asud*** provided a forensic political economy of agrarian pauperization. Phule demonstrated that the Deccan cultivator was crushed between two blades of extraction: the **Bhatji-Shetji** apparatus (which extracted surplus at every biological stage from conception to post-mortem *Shraddha* rites, alongside usurious debt) and the **Colonial Bureaucratic State** (which raised land assessments every 30 years, enclosed village grazing pastures through the **Imperial Forest Department**, imposed heavy irrigation cesses, and transferred peasant tax revenues to fund high salaries and pensions for European civil servants and English-educated Brahmin clerks).
*   ***Sarvajanik Satyadharma Pustak* (1891)**: Published posthumously, this text articulated a universalist ethical code (*Satyadharma*) grounded in human rights, gender equality within the household, and the complete rejection of caste hierarchy and sacred texts.

### 2. Pre-Ambedkarite Dalit Vanguard, Non-Brahmin Princely Statecraft, and Liberal Mediation

#### Military-Fiscal Demobilization and the Mahar Awakening: Walangkar, Kamble, and Bansode
During the 18th and early 19th centuries, the East India Company’s **Bombay Army** had recruited heavily from the untouchable **Mahar** community. Military service provided Mahar soldiers with cash wages, pensions, compulsory regimental literacy (via army schools), and institutional leverage outside the village *Balutedari* system. However, following the **Peel Commission (1859)** and the late-19th-century codification of the colonial **"Martial Races" ideology** under Lord Roberts, the British Indian Army progressively halted Mahar recruitment in the **1890s** to appease high-caste Maratha and northern "martial" recruits who refused to serve alongside untouchables. This sudden military-fiscal exclusion triggered the first autonomous political mobilization of the Mahar vanguard in Maharashtra.

*   **Gopal Baba Walangkar (c. 1840–1900)**: A retired *Havildar* of the Bombay Army and a direct disciple of **Jyotirao Phule**, **Gopal Baba Walangkar** became the pioneer of autonomous Dalit political assertion and anti-caste journalism. In **1888**, he began publishing monthly articles in ***Deenbandhu*** and authored ***Vital-Vidhvansak*** (*Destroyer of Brahmanical Ceremonial Pollution*, **1888**), the first published book specifically attacking untouchability from a Dalit perspective, followed by ***Hindu Dharma Darpan*** (**1894**). In **1890**, Walangkar founded the **Anarya Dosh-Parihar Mandali** (*Society for the Removal of Evils Among the Non-Aryans*) at **Dapoli** in Ratnagiri district. In **1894**, he drafted a historic **Petition of Grievances** demanding the restoration of Mahar recruitment into the British Indian Army, police, and civil administration, highlighting how Mahar troops had fought for the Company from Koregaon (1818) to the Second Anglo-Afghan War, only to be cast back into civil untouchability during peacetime.
*   **Shivram Janba Kamble (1875–1940)**: Operating from the **Pune Cantonment**—where employed working as a butler in the Masonic Hall gave him access to English newspapers and print infrastructure—**Shivram Janba Kamble** institutionalized urban Dalit activism in Western Maharashtra. In **July 1904**, Kamble launched ***Somvanshiya Mitra*** in Pune, the first periodical dedicated exclusively to the emancipation of the untouchable castes. He convened a massive conference of Mahars from **51 villages at Saswad (1903)** to sign and forward Walangkar’s military recruitment petition to the British Parliament, founded the **Shri Shankar Prasadik Somvanshiya Hitchintak Mitra Samaj (1904)** to open reading rooms and night schools, and led early direct-action protests at the **Parvati Temple** in Pune alongside struggles against the ritual dedication of Dalit girls as *Murlis* (Devadasis).
*   **Kisan Faguji Bansode (1879–1946)**: In the **Nagpur-Vidarbha** region (Central Provinces), where Mahars constituted nearly afifth of the population and formed the backbone of the emerging **Empress Mills** textile workforce and manganese mining labor, **Kisan Faguji Bansode** synthesized educational reform, labor unionism, and Dalit print culture. In **1907**, Bansode founded the **Sanmarg Bodhak Nirashrit Samaj** and established his own printing press in Nagpur, launching a series of militant vernacular journals: ***Nirashrit Hind Nagarik*** (**1910**), ***Vital Vidhvansak*** (**1913**), ***Majur Patrika*** (**1918**, articulating the grievances of textile millworkers), and ***Chokhamela*** (**1931**). Bansode established girls' schools in Mohpa and Nagpur, campaigned against humiliating municipal caste restrictions in Vidarbha, and used devotional Dalit poetry alongside trade union organization to build political consciousness prior to B.R. Ambedkar’s rise.

#### Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj of Kolhapur and the 1902 Reservation Decree
While subaltern activists organized from below, **Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj of Kolhapur** (r. **1894–1922**), a direct descendant of Shivaji’s dynasty, deployed the sovereign apparatus of a native Princely State to execute a structural counter-revolution against Brahmanical bureaucratic hegemony. The immediate catalyst was the **Vedokta Controversy (1899–1905)**, in which the royal Brahmin priest, **Narayan Bhat Sevekari**, backed by the orthodox **Lokmanya Tilak** faction in Pune, refused to chant Vedic mantras (*Vedokta* rites) for Shahu Maharaj, insisting that the Maratha rulers had degenerated into *Shudras* and were only entitled to inferior *Puranokta* (Puranic) rituals. Recognizing that his ritual degradation was underpinned by the fact that Brahmins (constituting less than 4% of Kolhapur’s population) held **60 out of 71 general administrative posts** and **46 out of 53 private state posts**, Shahu Maharaj launched a comprehensive administrative, educational, and legal restructuring of Kolhapur State:

*   **The Historic July 26, 1902 Reservation Decree**: Issuing an extraordinary state notification in the *Kolhapur State Gazette* on **July 26, 1902**, **Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj** reserved **50% of all vacancies** in the state administrative, revenue, and judicial services for **"Backward Classes"**—explicitly defined as all non-Brahmin, Shudra, and untouchable communities. This decree constitutes the foundational statutory origin of affirmative action and caste-based public sector reservations in modern India.
*   **Dismantling Feudal-Clerical Monopolies (1918)**: In **1918**, Shahu Maharaj abolished the hereditary **Kulkarni Watan** (village land-record monopoly held by Brahmins), replacing hereditary Kulkarnis with salaried, state-appointed **Talathis** recruited from all castes, including Mahars. Simultaneously, he abolished the **Mahar Watan** forced labor system (*Vethbegari*) and ended the humiliating **Hajeri** (daily compulsory police roll-call) imposed on communities stigmatized under the colonial **Criminal Tribes Act**.
*   **Institutionalizing Non-Brahmin Education and Dalit Autonomy**: Shahu Maharaj enacted **free and compulsory primary education (1917)** in Kolhapur and constructed a network of **21 caste-specific boarding hostels** (such as the **Victoria Maratha Boarding House**, **Miss Clarke Hostel** for untouchables in **1908**, and hostels for Jains, Lingayats, Muslims, and Chambhars) to enable rural subaltern youth to pursue higher education in Kolhapur city. He established the **Kshatriya Jagadguru Peeth** at Patgaon (appointing a Maratha scholar as religious head to break the Shankaracharya's monopoly), founded a Vedic school (*Patil-Talathi* and *Satyashodhak* priest training academies) open to non-Brahmins, legalized inter-caste marriages in **1919**, and provided critical financial patronage to **B.R. Ambedkar** (funding ***Mooknayak*** in **1920** and attending the **Mangaon Depressed Classes Conference**, where he publicly declared Ambedkar the true leader of India's untouchables).

#### Vitthal Ramji Shinde and the Depressed Classes Mission (1906)
Operating at the intersection of the liberal **Prarthana Samaj** and the non-Brahmin movement, **Maharshi Vitthal Ramji Shinde** (1873–1944)—an educated Maratha reformer trained in comparative religion at Manchester College, Oxford—sought to sensitize upper-caste society while creating dedicated institutional infrastructure for Dalit upliftment. On **October 18, 1906**, Shinde founded the **Depressed Classes Mission of India (*Bharatiya Asprishyata Nivaran Mandal*)** in Bombay under the presidency of **Justice Narayan Ganesh Chandavarkar**.

*   **Institutional Footprint and Scholarship**: The **Depressed Classes Mission** established over **30 day and night schools**, boarding hostels, technical workshops (bookbinding, carpentry, shoe-making), and free dispensaries across Bombay, Pune, Hubli, Akola, Amravati, and Madras. In **1917**, Shinde successfully lobbied the **Indian National Congress** at its Calcutta Session (presided over by Annie Besant) to pass its first official resolution urging the removal of untouchability.
*   **Historiographical and Agrarian Interventions**: In **1933**, Shinde published ***Bharatiya Asprishyatecha Prashna*** (*The Problem of India's Untouchability*), one of the earliest sociological and historical studies arguing that untouchables were ancient Buddhist rulers degraded after the Brahmanical counter-revolution. Shinde also actively led the **1928–30 Bombay Small Holdings Bill agitation** alongside peasants and participated in the **Vaikom Satyagraha** (**1924**). However, by the **1920s**, a sharp structural divergence emerged between Shinde’s paternalistic, upper-caste-mediated philanthropic model and **B.R. Ambedkar’s** demand for autonomous Dalit political representation, culminating in Ambedkar’s critique before the **Southborough Franchise Committee (1919)** that the Depressed Classes Mission represented high-caste trusteeship rather than genuine subaltern self-determination.

## II. Southern Social Revolutions: Caste-Feudal Dismantling in Kerala and Dravidian Counter-Hegemony in Madras (1888–1944)

### 3. Agrarian Servitude, Spatial Segregation, and the Kerala Social Revolutions

#### The Jenmi-Kanamdars-Verumpattamdar Hierarchy and Distance Pollution (Theendal)
In pre-modern and colonial Kerala (comprising the Princely States of **Travancore** and **Cochin** and the British district of **Malabar**), the agrarian political economy was fused directly with the most rigid ritual-spatial hierarchy in the Indian subcontinent. Under British colonial courts in Malabar, the customary *Janmam* right of the **Nambudiri Brahmin** and aristocratic **Nair** chieftains was legally re-codified as absolute freehold private property (**Jenmi** landlordism). Beneath the **Jenmis** stood the **Kanamdars** (predominantly dominant **Nair** superior-tenure holders who controlled village policing and militia power), below whom toiled the **Verumpattamdars** (rack-rented tenants-at-will, largely drawn from the backward **Ezhava/Tiyya** and Muslim **Mappila** communities) and the landless **Pulaya**, **Paraya**, and **Cheruma** agrestic serfs, who had historically been bought, sold, and mortgaged along with the soil.

To enforce this four-tier agrarian extraction grid without constant military expenditure, the Kerala caste order operated not merely through touch pollution (*Thottukoodayma*), but through mathematically calibrated **distance or atmospheric pollution (*Theendal*)** and **visual pollution (*Kandukoodayma*)**. While an avarna **Ezhava** (traditionally associated with coconut cultivation and toddy-tapping) had to maintain a physical distance of **24 to 36 feet** from a **Nambudiri Brahmin**, an untouchable **Pulaya** or **Paraya** agrarian laborer was forced to remain **64 to 96 feet** away, forbidden from walking on public roads surrounding temples, banned from wearing upper-body garments or gold ornaments, prohibited from carrying umbrellas or using tiled roofs, and compelled to shout self-degrading warning cries (*Payahu*) when approaching public thoroughfares so that upper-caste *Savarnas* could avoid ritual contamination.

#### Sri Narayana Guru, Aravippuram (1888), SNDP Yogam (1903), and Sahodaran Ayyappan’s Radical Atheism
By the late 19th century, the **Ezhava** community (known as **Tiyyas** in Malabar), constituting nearly 26% of Kerala’s population, experienced a profound structural contradiction: while colonial commercialization of coconut oil, coir manufacturing, cashew processing, and plantation agriculture had created a prosperous new class of Ezhava entrepreneurs, merchants, Ayurvedic physicians, and English-educated graduates, the royal administration of **Travancore** continued to bar educated Ezhavas from all government civil service posts and public schools on grounds of ritual pollution.

*   **Sri Narayana Guru (1856–1928) and the Aravippuram Movement (1888)**: A deeply learned Ezhava ascetic, Sanskrit scholar, and Advaita Vedanta philosopher, **Sri Narayana Guru** recognized that the spiritual and social subjugation of the *Avarnas* rested on the Brahmanical monopoly over the consecration of high-god idols (*Agama* temples), which confined lower castes to blood-sacrifice and spirit-worship cults. On the night of **Sivaratri in February 1888**, at **Aravippuram** on the banks of the Neyyar River, Narayana Guru dove into the river, retrieved a stone, and consecrated it as a **Shiva Linga**. When orthodox Brahmins challenged his ritual right to install a Vedic deity, he inscribed on the temple wall his celebrated counter-epigram: ***"Devoid of dividing walls of caste or race, or hatred of rival faith, we all live here in brotherhood; such, know this place to be! This model foundation!"***—retorting verbally that he had consecrated an **"Ezhava Shiva,"** not a Brahmin Shiva. In later temples (such as at **Murukkumpuzha** and **Kalavankode**), he abandoned anthropomorphic idols altogether, consecrating an oil lamp and a **mirror** inscribed with *Om, Satyam, Daya, Dharma* to signify the Advaitic truth that the divine resides within the self.
*   **The SNDP Yogam (1903), Dr. Palpu, and Kumaran Asan**: On **May 15, 1903**, **Sri Narayana Guru’s** spiritual revolution was merged with modern organizational and print capitalism through the founding of the **Sri Narayana Dharma Paripalana (SNDP) Yogam**, registered as a joint-stock company with **Sri Narayana Guru** as lifelong President. Its institutional architect was **Dr. Padmanabhan Palpu (Dr. Palpu)**—an Ezhava medical graduate trained at Madras and Cambridge who had been denied employment in the Travancore Medical Service due to his caste and instead served as chief medical officer in Mysore. **Dr. Palpu** had previously orchestrated the **Malayali Memorial (1891)** and the **Ezhava Memorial (1896, signed by 13,176 Ezhavas)** demanding civil service entry. The SNDP Yogam’s first General Secretary was the great Malayalam Romantic poet **Kumaran Asan** (author of the anti-caste poetic masterpieces ***Duravastha*** and ***Chandalabhikshuki***), who edited the Yogam’s mouthpiece, ***Vivekodayam*** (**1904**).
*   **Ideological Maxim and Socio-Economic Restructuring**: Propagating the universalist dictum ***"Oru Jati, Oru Matham, Oru Daivam Manushyanu"* ("One Caste, One Religion, One God for Mankind")**, the SNDP Yogam simultaneously campaigned for Ezhava admission to public schools and administrative services while executing internal social reforms—abolishing expensive puberty/marriage rituals (*Thalikettu Kalyanam* and *Tirandukuli*), promoting modern industry, and building an independent network of colleges and temples open to all castes, including Pulayas.
*   **Sahodaran Ayyappan (1889–1968) and the Cherai Inter-Dining Rupture**: As the Ezhava bourgeoisie consolidated its socio-economic position, a radical rationalist and socialist wing emerged within the movement led by **K. Ayyappan**, known as **Sahodaran Ayyappan**. Observing that upwardly mobile Ezhavas frequently practiced untouchability against the lower **Pulaya** and **Paraya** castes, Ayyappan organized the historic **Misrabhojanam** (inter-caste dining) at **Cherai** in **May 1917**, where Ezhavas and Pulayas cooked and ate together, earning the wrath of conservative SNDP elites. Founding the **Sahodara Sangham** and the journal ***Sahodaran***, Ayyappan radicalized Sri Narayana Guru’s spiritual maxim into a materialist, atheist battle-cry: ***"Jati Venda, Matham Venda, Daivam Venda Manushyanu"* ("No Caste, No Religion, No God for Mankind")**, introducing socialist thought, the Russian Revolution, and rationalism into Kerala’s political discourse.

#### Ayyankali’s Sadhu Jana Paripalana Sangham (1907) and the Pulaya Agrarian General Strikes
At the bottom of the Travancore agrarian pyramid, the landless **Pulaya** agricultural laborers launched a militant, direct-action struggle under **Mahatma Ayyankali** (1863–1941), born at **Venganoor** near Thiruvananthapuram. Unlike the Ezhavas, who possessed an emerging commercial bourgeoisie, the Pulayas possessed only their physical labor power in the wetland paddy fields of the upper-caste **Nair** and **Syrian Christian** landlords.

*   **The 1893 Villuvandi (Bullock-Cart) Reclamation of Public Space**: Defying the ban on untouchables walking on public royal roads (*Rajapatha*) or riding wheeled vehicles, **Ayyankali** purchased a decorated, white-bullock-drawn cart (**Villuvandi**) with brass bells in **1893**, dressed in a white *dhoti*, *angavastram*, and turban, and drove it directly through the market roads of **Venganoor** and **Balaramapuram**. When upper-caste Nair musclemen attempted to block the cart, Ayyankali drew a long dagger and physically fought his way through alongside a trained band of Pulaya youth (**Ayyankali Pada**), winning the de facto right of untouchables to traverse public highways in southern Travancore.
*   **The Sadhu Jana Paripalana Sangham (SJPS, 1907) and the First Agrarian Strike**: In **1907**, **Ayyankali** founded the **Sadhu Jana Paripalana Sangham** (*Association for the Protection of the Poor*, consciously using the economic category *Sadhu Jana* rather than a single jati label to unite all depressed classes), publishing the journal ***Sadhu Jana Paripalini*** edited by **Chempazhanthi Kaliambi**. When the Travancore government issued a royal order in **1907** permitting Pulaya children into government schools, local Nair landlords violently obstructed its implementation: when Ayyankali attempted to enroll an 8-year-old Pulaya girl named **Panchami** into the **Ooruttambalam School** in **1910**, upper-caste mobs burned down the schoolhouse and unleashed anti-Pulaya riots across Neyyattinkara.
*   **Weaponizing Agricultural Labor Withdrawal (1907–1910)**: In response, **Ayyankali** organized the **first recorded agricultural workers’ general strike in Indian history**. Declaring that *"if our children are not allowed to enter your schools, weeds will grow in your paddy fields,"* Pulaya laborers across southern Travancore refused to plow or harvest the wetlands of Nair *Jenmis*. To sustain the striking landless laborers against starvation, Ayyankali negotiated an extraordinary economic alliance with the coastal **Mukkuvar** (fishermen) of **Vizhinjam**, who shared their daily catch with the striking Pulayas. Faced with rotting fields and looming revenue collapse, the Nair landlords capitulated, agreeing to Pulaya school admission, wage increases, and regulated working hours.
*   **The Kallumala (Stone-Necklace) Agitation (1915)**: Nominated in **1912** to the **Sree Moolam Popular Assembly** (making him the first Dalit legislator in India), Ayyankali led the **Kallumala Agitation** at **Perinad (Quilon)** in **December 1915**, where thousands of Pulaya women publicly ripped off and cast away the heavy chains of glass beads and stone necklaces (*Kallumala*) that they were legally forced to wear as badges of servitude and prohibited from covering their upper bodies.

#### Civic Space and Temple Entry Struggles: Vaikom (1924–25) and Guruvayur (1931) Satyagrahas
During the 1920s and 1930s, the anti-caste mobilization of the **SNDP Yogam** and **SJPS** converged with the **Indian National Congress** and the emerging **Communist left**, transforming temple-road access and temple entry into mass civil disobedience campaigns that restructured Kerala politics.

*   **The Vaikom Satyagraha (March 30, 1924 – November 23, 1925)**: At the **Vaikom Mahadeva Temple** in Kottayam district (Travancore), four public municipal roads surrounding the outer temple walls were open to Christians, Muslims, and even dogs, but strictly barred by notice-boards (*Theendal Palaka*) to Ezhavas, Pulayas, and other *Avarnas*. Having secured a resolution on untouchability at the **1923 Kakinada INC Session**, the Ezhava leader and editor of ***Deshabhimani***, **T.K. Madhavan**, joined hands with the Kerala Pradesh Congress Committee President **K.P. Kesava Menon** and the Syrian Christian nationalist **George Joseph** to launch a non-violent Satyagraha on **March 30, 1924**.
    *   Every morning, batches of three volunteers—one **Savarna** (such as **K. Kelappan** or **K.P. Kesava Menon**), one **Ezhava**, and one **Pulaya**—walked past the prohibitive signboards and were arrested by Travancore police or beaten by orthodox Nambudiri/Nair hirelings.
    *   When the entire Kerala leadership was imprisoned, the President of the Tamil Nadu Congress Committee, **E.V. Ramasamy 'Periyar'**, arrived at Vaikom in **April 1924**, revitalizing the movement through fiery mass rallies and suffering two terms of rigorous imprisonment—earning him the enduring title **Vaikom Virar** (*Hero of Vaikom*).
    *   Simultaneously, the reformist Nair leader **Mannathu Padmanabhan** organized the historic **Savarna Jatha** in **November 1924**, leading an upper-caste march from Vaikom to Trivandrum to submit a 25,000-signature petition to the Regent Maharani **Sethu Lakshmi Bayi**, while **Akali Sikhs** from Punjab set up a free *Langar* kitchen at Vaikom before **M.K. Gandhi** ordered it closed to keep the struggle strictly intra-Hindu. Following Gandhi’s mediation with the Regent Maharani and orthodox Nambudiri head **Indanthuruthil Devan Neelakandan Nambudiripad** in **March 1925**, a compromise was reached in **November 1925** opening three of the four surrounding roads to all castes.
*   **The Guruvayur Satyagraha (1931–1932)**: Moving from peripheral temple roads to the inner sanctum itself, the Kerala Congress launched the **Guruvayur Satyagraha** on **November 1, 1931**, demanding the opening of the famous **Guruvayur Sri Krishna Temple** in Ponnani taluk (British Malabar), controlled by the orthodox **Zamorin of Calicut**. Led by **K. Kelappan** (*Kerala Gandhi*) as Campaign Dictator and **A.K. Gopalan (AKG)** as Captain of the Volunteer Corps—alongside **P. Krishna Pillai** (who was brutally beaten inside the temple for ringing the sanctum bell) and **Mannathu Padmanabhan**—the movement galvanized rural Malabar. When the Zamorin remained intransigent, **K. Kelappan** began a **fast-unto-death on September 21, 1932**, which he suspended on **October 2, 1932**, at Gandhi’s urgent request. A subsequent referendum organized among Caste Hindus in Ponnani taluk showed over **70% in favor of temple entry**.
*   **The Travancore Temple Entry Proclamation (November 12, 1936)**: Faced with the imminent threat of mass Ezhava conversion to Christianity, Islam, or Buddhism following the **1932 Abstention (Nivarthana) Movement** (where Ezhavas, Christians, and Muslims united to boycott the Travancore legislature), **Maharaja Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma** and his Diwan **Sir C.P. Ramaswami Iyer** issued the historic **Temple Entry Proclamation on November 12, 1936**, throwing open all state-controlled Hindu temples in Travancore to all *Avarnas* and untouchables. Furthermore, the **Guruvayur Satyagraha** radicalized its youth leadership—specifically **A.K. Gopalan**, **P. Krishna Pillai**, and **E.M.S. Namboodiripad**—directly catalyzing the formation of the **Congress Socialist Party (1934)** and the **Communist Party in Kerala (1937/1939)**.

### 4. Subaltern Buddhism, Non-Brahmin Constitutionalism, and the Dravidian Self-Respect Rupture in Tamil Nadu

#### Pandit Iyothee Thass, the Sakya Buddhist Society, and Oru Paisa Tamizhan
Long before the elite non-Brahmin **Justice Party** or the pan-Indian rise of **B.R. Ambedkar**, a profound subaltern ideological revolution occurred among the untouchable **Paraiyar** community of the Madras Presidency, led by **Pandit Iyothee Thass** (born Kathavaraya, 1845–1914), a Siddha physician, classical Tamil philologist, and polyglot scholar from Madras and the Nilgiris. Along with **Rettaimalai Srinivasan** (who founded the **Paraiyar Mahajana Sabha** in **1891** and the newspaper ***Paraiyan*** in **1893**, and later accompanied Ambedkar to the **Round Table Conferences**), Iyothee Thass pioneered Dalit print culture and anti-caste historiography in South India.

*   **The 1891 Census Intervention and the *Adi-Dravida* Identity**: Ahead of the **1891 Colonial Census**, **Iyothee Thass**founded the **Dravida Mahajana Sabha** in the Nilgiris and submitted a memorial demanding that untouchables be recorded not as "Hindus" or by the derogatory caste label "Paraiyar," but as **Casteless Dravidians (*Sathi Bhedamullatha Dravidar*)** or **Adi-Dravidas** (Original Dravidians).
*   **The Sakya Buddhist Society (1898) and the Buddhist Counter-History**: Recognizing that the Hindu fold offered only perpetual degradation, **Iyothee Thass** traveled to **Colombo (Ceylon)** in **1898** with the support of Theosophist **Colonel H.S. Olcott** and received the Buddhist *Panchasheela* from the venerable Sinhalese monk **Sumangala Nayake**. Returning to Madras, he founded the **Sakya Buddhist Society** (also known as the **South Indian Buddhist Association**) at **Royapettah, Madras**, establishing branches across the **Kolar Gold Fields (KGF)**—where thousands of Paraiyar miners formed his militant working-class base—as well as in Bangalore, Rangoon, and Natal (South Africa).
*   ***Oru Paisa Tamizhan* (1907–1914)**: In **June 1907**, Iyothee Thass launched his weekly newspaper ***Oru Paisa Tamizhan*** (*One-Paisa Tamilian*, later renamed ***Tamizhan***), which became the intellectual crucible of subaltern Dravidian Buddhism. Through rigorous philological analyses of classical Sangam and post-Sangam Tamil texts (such as ***Manimekalai*** and ***Tirukkural***), Thass argued that the untouchables of Tamilakam were the original **Sakya Buddhists** of ancient India who were reduced to outcastes (*Paraiyars*) when invading Aryan-Brahmins usurped their monasteries, falsified palm-leaf manuscripts (*Vesha Brahmanas*), and rebranded Buddhist ethical festivals (like Pongal and Deepavali) as Puranic Hindu myths. He also delivered a devastating critique of the **Swadeshi Movement**, arguing that high-caste Congress nationalists who practiced domestic untouchability against their own countrymen had no moral legitimacy to demand political *Swaraj* from the British.

#### The Justice Party (South Indian Liberal Federation, 1916) and Dyarchy Statecraft
At the elite socio-economic level of the Madras Presidency, colonial higher education and administrative recruitment exhibited an acute structural asymmetry. Although **Tamil and Telugu Brahmins** comprised barely **3.2% of the Madras Presidency’s population**, between **1870 and 1918** they captured over **70% of all Madras University degrees** and monopolized the overwhelming majority of posts in the provincial civil services (*Deputy Collectors*, *Sub-Judges*, and *District Munsifs*), as well as the leadership of the Madras Indian National Congress and **Annie Besant’s Home Rule League**. Resenting this administrative and political monopoly, an upwardly mobile coalition of non-Brahmin landed magnates (**Vellalas**, **Reddys**, **Kammas**, **Velamas**, **Nairs**) and urban commercial/banking elites (**Chettiars**, **Mudaliars**, **Naidus**) organized to challenge Brahmin dominance.

*   **Institutional Genesis (1912–1916)**: In **1912**, **Dr. C. Natesa Mudaliar** founded the **Madras United League** (renamed the **Madras Dravidian Association**) and opened the **Dravidian Home** hostel in **1916** for non-Brahmin university students, who had been denied admission or subjected to segregated dining in Brahmin-run hostels. Bridging the rivalry between two titan non-Brahmin leaders—**Dr. T.M. Nair** (an outspoken Ezhava/Nair medical practitioner and municipal reformer) and **Sir P. Theagaraya Chetty** (a wealthy Devanga weaver-merchant and President of the Southern India Chamber of Commerce)—**C. Natesa Mudaliar** facilitated a historic meeting of 30 non-Brahmin leaders at Victoria Public Hall on **November 20, 1916**, founding the **South Indian People’s Association** and its political wing, the **South Indian Liberal Federation (SILF)**.
*   **The Non-Brahmin Manifesto (December 1916) and Print Organs**: In **December 1916**, **P. Theagaraya Chetty** issued the **Non-Brahmin Manifesto**, armed with empirical colonial census and employment statistics demonstrating how a tiny Brahmin minority had captured the state machinery, and warning that immediate *Home Rule* without prior caste-based administrative equalization would merely replace British colonial rule with an unchecked **"Brahmin Oligarchy."** The SILF launched three newspapers—***Justice*** in English (edited by **Dr. T.M. Nair**, from which the party acquired its popular name, the **Justice Party**), ***Dravidan*** in Tamil (edited by **Bhaktavatsalam Pillai**), and ***Andhra Prakasika*** in Telugu (edited by **A.C. Parthasarathi Naidu**).
*   **Legislative Capture and Structural Reforms under Dyarchy (1920–1937)**: When the **Indian National Congress** boycotted the **November 1920** elections held under the **Montagu-Chelmsford (1919) Dyarchy Reforms**, the **Justice Party** swept the polls in the Madras Presidency, winning 63 out of 98 elected seats and forming ministries under **A. Subbarayalu Reddiar** (**1920–21**), the **Raja of Panagal** (**1921–26**), and later the **Raja of Bobbili** (**1932–37**). Utilizing control over the "Transferred Subjects" of provincial Dyarchy, the Justice Party enacted transformative statutory interventions:
    1.  **The First and Second Communal Government Orders (G.O. No. 613 of September 16, 1921, and G.O. No. 658 of August 15, 1922)**: Introduced proportional caste-based quotas for all public service appointments across the Madras Presidency, allocating specific shares to Non-Brahmin Hindus, Brahmins, Muslims, Anglo-Indians/Christians, and the Depressed Classes.
    2.  **The Staff Selection Board (1924)**: Established to enforce the Communal G.O.s and strip individual Brahmin department heads of discretionary hiring patronage; upgraded in **1929** into the **Madras Public Service Commission**, the first provincial public service commission in India.
    3.  **Women’s Suffrage (1921) and Abolition of the Devadasi System**: In **April 1921**, the Justice Party-controlled Madras Legislative Council became the **first legislature in British India to grant voting rights to women**. In **1926**, **Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy** was nominated to the Council (becoming India’s first female legislator and Deputy President), where, backed by the Justice Party and **Periyar**, she successfully enacted legislation abolishing the feudal *Pottukattu* (Devadasi dedication) land-grant system (**1929/1930**).
    4.  **The Madras Hindu Religious Endowments Act (1926)**: Enacted under the **Raja of Panagal**, this statute brought the vast, tax-exempt land revenues and cash treasuries of major South Indian temples—hitherto monopolized by hereditary Brahmin trustees and *Mathadhipatis*—under the direct financial oversight and auditing of a state-appointed **Endowments Board**.
    5.  **Educational Democratization**: Established **Andhra University (1926)** and **Annamalai University (1929)**, introduced free mid-day meals and scholarships in Thousand Lights corporation schools in Madras, and deleted the mandatory requirement of **Sanskrit proficiency** for admission to medical degrees, opening modern medicine to non-Brahmin students.

 However, because the early **Justice Party** leadership remained dominated by wealthy *Zamin* landlords and urban plutocrats, its initial patronage largely benefited elite high-caste non-Brahmins (Vellalas, Reddys, Mudaliars), generating acute friction with Dalit leaders like **M.C. Rajah**—who walked out of the Justice Party in **1923** after accusing it of neglecting the *Adi-Dravidas* and refusing to aggressively abolish untouchability.

#### E.V. Ramasamy 'Periyar', the Self-Respect Movement (1925), and Dravidar Kazhagam (1944)
The transformation of the Tamil non-Brahmin movement from an elite constitutional lobby for civil service jobs into a militant, anti-caste, atheist, and feminist mass movement was executed by **E.V. Ramasamy ('Periyar')** (1879–1973). Born into a wealthy **Balija Naidu** merchant family in **Erode**, Periyar joined the **Indian National Congress** in **1919**, threw himself into the **Non-Cooperation Movement** (courting arrest, promoting Khadi, and cutting down 500 coconut palms on his own estate to enforce prohibition), and served as President of the Tamil Nadu Congress Committee alongside his triumph at the **Vaikom Satyagraha (1924–25)**.

*   **The Gurukulam Controversy and the 1925 Kanchipuram Rupture**: Periyar’s break with the Congress was triggered by two structural collisions within the Tamil Nadu Congress: first, the **Cheranmahadevi Gurukulam Controversy (1925)**, where it was revealed that a Congress-funded national school run by **V.V.S. Aiyar** enforced segregated dining and separate water pots for two Brahmin boys away from the majority non-Brahmin students; second, the refusal of the Brahmin-dominated Tamil Nadu Congress executive (led by **C. Rajagopalachari** and **S. Srinivasa Iyengar**) at the **November 1925 Kanchipuram Provincial Conference** to pass Periyar’s resolution demanding communal representation (proportional reservations) for non-Brahmins in legislatures and public bodies. Declaring that the destruction of the Congress, Hinduism, and Brahmin domination had become his life’s mission, Periyar walked out of the Congress in **November 1925**.
*   **The Self-Respect Movement (*Suyamariyadhai Iyakkam*, 1925) and *Kudi Arasu***: Launching the **Self-Respect Movement** in **1925** and founding his radical Tamil weekly ***Kudi Arasu*** (*Republic*, **May 1925**), followed by ***Revolt*** (in English, **1928**), ***Puratchi*** (*Revolution*, **1933**), ***Pagutharivu*** (*Rationalism*, **1934**), and ***Viduthalai*** (*Liberation*, **1935**), Periyar argued that political freedom without social emancipation (*Suyamariyadhai* or human dignity/self-respect) was a sham designed to perpetuate upper-caste hegemony.
    *   **Atheism and Epistemological Iconoclasm**: Unlike reformists who sought to purify Hinduism by returning to the Vedas or Advaita, Periyar rejected all religion, god, fate (*Karma*), and sacred texts (burning the ***Manusmriti*** in **1927** alongside **J.S. Kannappar** and staging public critiques of the ***Ramayana*** through his tract ***Irമായana Kurippugal***, where he inverted the epic as an Aryan conquest narrative celebrating **Ravana** as a noble Dravidian hero).
    *   **Radical Gender Liberation and Self-Respect Marriages (*Suyamariyadhai Thirumanam*)**: Identifying the subjugation of women as the bedrock of caste endogamy, Periyar’s **1929 Chingleput First Provincial Self-Respect Conference** demanded equal inheritance and property rights for women, the right to divorce, widow remarriage, access to contraception/birth control (arguing in his masterpiece ***Pen Yen Adimaiyanal?*** / *Why Did Woman Become Enslaved?* that forced motherhood was used by patriarchy to chain women to domestic servitude), and women’s entry into the police and army. From **1928**, the movement institutionalized **Self-Respect Marriages**—conducted without Brahmin priests, Sanskrit mantras, auspicious timings (*Rahu Kalam*), or the *Thali* (mangalsutra) symbol of female subordination, encouraging inter-caste and widow marriages.
    *   **The Samadharma (Socialist) Turn (1931–1932)**: Following a nearly year-long tour of the **Soviet Union** and Europe in **1931–32**, Periyar collaborated with India’s pioneer communist **M. Singaravelu Chettiar** to draft the **Erode Plan (December 1932)**, integrating Marxian class struggle, abolition of landlordism, and nationalization of key industries with the annihilation of caste—until colonial sedition arrests forced a tactical retreat in **1935**.
*   **The First Anti-Hindi Agitation (1937–1940) and the Dravidar Kazhagam (1944)**: When the newly elected Congress ministry in Madras under Premier **C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji)** introduced **compulsory Hindi instruction** in secondary schools in **1937**, Periyar viewed it as a twin cultural-linguistic offensive of **"Aryan-Sanskritic and North Indian Brahmanical imperialism"** aimed at subordinating the Tamil language and Dravidian culture. Leading a massive three-year agitation in which over **1,200 protesters** (including huge contingents of women led by **Moovalur Ramamirtham** and **Neelavathi**, who conferred the title **"Periyar"**—*The Great One*—upon him at the **1938 Madras Women’s Conference**) were imprisoned and two young activists (**Thalamuthu** and **Natarajan**) died in prison, Periyar forced the British Governor to withdraw compulsory Hindi in **February 1940** after the Congress ministry resigned. Elected President of the declining **Justice Party** while still in Bellary Jail in **December 1938**, Periyar convened the historic **Salem Conference on August 27, 1944**, where, on a resolution moved by his brilliant young lieutenant **C.N. Annadurai**, the elite Justice Party and the mass-based Self-Respect Movement were merged and renamed the **Dravidar Kazhagam (DK)**—requiring all members to renounce British colonial titles (*Rao Bahadur*, *Diwan Bahadur*), drop caste surnames, refrain from contesting colonial elections, wear **black shirts** as a symbol of mourning the social degradation of Dravidians, and fight for an egalitarian, casteless **Dravida Nadu**.

## III. B.R. Ambedkar, Dalit Political Emancipation, and the Constitutional Bargaining Grid (1919–1947)

### 5. From Civic Satyagraha and Print Mobilization to Epistemological Rupture

#### Print Capitalism and Institutional Genesis: Mooknayak (1920), Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha (1924), and Bahishkrit Bharat
The emergence of **Dr. Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar** (1891–1956)—born at **Mhow** Cantonment into a Mahar military family from **Ambavade** in Ratnagiri district, and armed with doctorates in economics from **Columbia University** (where he absorbed **John Dewey’s** pragmatist democracy) and the **London School of Economics** alongside qualification as a Barrister-at-Law from **Gray’s Inn**—transformed the fragmented regional struggles of the untouchable castes into a coherent, pan-Indian political, legal, and constitutional movement. Unlike upper-caste nationalists who treated untouchability as a secondary moral blemish to be addressed after winning political independence, Ambedkar established from first principles that a society structured on hereditary civil, economic, and spatial exclusion could never constitute a democratic nation unless the social and material foundations of caste were dismantled simultaneously with colonial rule.

*   ***Mooknayak* (Leader of the Voiceless, 1920)**: Recognizing that the mainstream nationalist press (which he termed the *"Drum-beaters of the Tilakite and Congress elite"*) systematically blacked out the atrocities suffered by the **60 million untouchables** of British India, Ambedkar launched his fortnightly Marathi newspaper ***Mooknayak*** on **January 31, 1920**, from Bombay, with a financial grant of **Rs. 2,500** from **Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj of Kolhapur** and edited initially by **Pandurang Nandram Bhatkar**. In its inaugural editorial, Ambedkar conceptualized Hindu society as a **"multi-storied tower without a staircase or an entrance,"** in which one was condemned to die in the exact story where one was born, and declared that the emancipation of the Depressed Classes required autonomous political power rather than high-caste charity.
*   **Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha (1924)**: Upon returning from London in **1923** after completing his monumental monetary treatise ***The Problem of the Rupee***, Ambedkar founded the **Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha** (*Society for the Welfare of the Ostracized*) in Bombay on **July 20, 1924**, with **Sir Chimanlal Harilal Setalvad** as President and Ambedkar as Chairman of the Managing Committee. Adopting the famous foundational triad—***"Educate, Agitate, Organize!"***—the Sabha established free hostels for Dalit schoolboys at **Solapur**, **Belgaum**, **Dharwad**, and **Jalgaon**, opened libraries and Mahar hockey clubs, founded the **Saraswati Vilas** monthly magazine, and represented the economic grievances of Mahar *Watandars* and industrial textile laborers before the colonial administration.
*   ***Bahishkrit Bharat* (1927), *Samata* (1928), and *Janata* (1930)**: In **April 1927**, amid the storm of the Mahad agitation, Ambedkar launched his second fortnightly newspaper, ***Bahishkrit Bharat*** (*Ostracized India*), which he wrote almost single-handedly to articulate the legal philosophy of civil rights, followed by ***Samata*** (**1928**, organ of the **Samaj Samata Sangh**, which promoted inter-caste marriages and social equality alongside progressive upper-caste allies) and the long-running weekly ***Janata*** (**1930**).

#### Civic Commons and Sacred Space: Mahad Chavdar Tank Satyagraha (1927), Manusmriti Burning, and Kalaram Temple Entry (1930–35)
During the late 1920s and early 1930s, **B.R. Ambedkar** deployed the weapon of mass non-violent direct action (*Satyagraha*) not to seek spiritual assimilation into orthodox Hinduism, but as a juridical and psychological test of whether Hindu society was willing to recognize the basic human personhood and civic citizenship of Dalits over public resources.

*   **The Mahad Chavdar Tank Satyagraha (March 19–20, 1927)**: In **August 1923**, the Bombay Legislative Council had passed a resolution moved by the non-Brahmin leader **S.K. Bole** declaring that untouchables must be permitted to use all public watering places, wells, dharmashalas, and schools built or maintained out of public funds. Pursuant to the **Bole Resolution**, the **Mahad Municipality** in Kolaba district had formally thrown open the **Chavdar Tank** (*Chavdar Tale*) to untouchables in **1924**, yet local orthodox caste Hindus violently prevented Dalits from drawing a single drop of water.
    *   Convening the **Colaba District Depressed Classes Conference** at **Mahad** on **March 19–20, 1927**, attended by nearly **3,000 delegates** (organized locally by **Surendranath Tipnis**, **Anant Vinayak Chitre**, and **Subhedar Vishram Sawant**), **Ambedkar** led a disciplined march on **March 20, 1927**, to the **Chavdar Tank**, knelt down, and drank water from the tank followed by thousands of Dalits.
    *   Hours later, after a rumor spread that Dalits were planning to enter the nearby **Vireshwar Temple**, armed orthodox caste-Hindu mobs brutally assaulted the unarmed delegates in their dining pavilions and subsequently performed a humiliating ritual purification (*Panchagavya* ceremony—pouring 108 pots of cow dung, cow urine, milk, curd, and ghee into the tank) to cleanse the water of "untouchable pollution."
*   **The Second Mahad Conference and the Burning of the *Manusmriti* (December 25, 1927)**: Determined to avenge this civic humiliation, Ambedkar reconvened over **10,000 Satyagrahis** at **Mahad** on **December 25–27, 1927**, organizing the **Samata Sainik Dal** (Social Equality Corps) to protect the gathering. Comparing the Conference to the **1789 French National Assembly**, Ambedkar proclaimed a Charter of Human Rights demanding the complete abolition of birth-based inequality. On the night of **December 25, 1927**, on a resolution moved by **Gangadhar Neelkanth Sahasrabuddhe** (a progressive Chitpavan Brahmin lieutenant of Ambedkar) and seconded by Dalit leaders **P.N. Rajbhoj** and **Thorat**, copies of the ancient Brahmanical law code ***Manusmriti***—which prescribed pouring molten lead into the ears of Shudras who heard the Vedas and codified the civil degradation of untouchables and women—were ceremonially burned in a sacrificial pit on the conference grounds. Although the District Collector issued a court injunction halting a second march to the Chavdar Tank pending litigation, Ambedkar fought the case through the colonial courts for ten years, finally winning a landmark judgment from the **Bombay High Court on March 17, 1937**, establishing the unrestricted legal right of Dalits to use the Chavdar Tank.
*   **The Kalaram Temple Entry Satyagraha at Nasik (1930–1935)**: Following the **Parvati Temple Satyagraha** in Pune (**October 1929**, led by **S.J. Kamble** and **P.N. Rajbhoj**), Ambedkar and **Dadasaheb (Bhaurao) Gaikwad** launched the massive **Kalaram Temple Entry Satyagraha** at **Nasik** on **March 2, 1930**. Over **15,000 Dalit volunteers** marched in a mile-long procession to the four gates of the **Kalaram Temple**, which the orthodox Brahmin trustees immediately bolted shut. For over five years (**1930–1935**), thousands of Dalit men and women courted arrest, faced police lathi-charges during the annual *Rama Navami* chariot procession, and suffered economic boycotts in surrounding villages.
*   **The Yeola Conversion Declaration (October 13, 1935)**: Concluding that five years of peaceful Satyagraha at Nasik had failed to soften the heart of caste-Hindu orthodoxy, Ambedkar formally terminated the temple entry movement. On **October 13, 1935**, speaking before **10,000 followers** at the **Yeola Conversion Conference** in Nasik district, Ambedkar urged Dalits to stop wasting their energies and resources on temple entry and delivered his thunderous historical verdict: ***"Unfortunately for me, I was born a Hindu Untouchable; it was beyond my power to prevent that, but I solemnly assure you that I will not die a Hindu!"***

#### Theoretical Culmination: Annihilation of Caste (1936) and the Critique of Graded Inequality
In **December 1935**, the **Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal** of **Lahore**—an anti-caste reformist organization affiliated with the **Arya Samaj**—invited **B.R. Ambedkar** to deliver the Presidential Address at its annual conference in early **1936**. However, upon reviewing the printed draft of Ambedkar’s speech, the Mandal’s executive committee panicked: Ambedkar had not merely criticized sub-caste endogamy, but had called for the complete destruction of the theological authority of the **Vedas** and **Shastras** and reaffirmed that this would be his final speech as a Hindu. When Ambedkar refused to delete a single comma to appease the Arya Samajists, the Mandal cancelled the conference. In **May 1936**, Ambedkar self-published the undelivered address at his own expense as ***Annihilation of Caste***, which immediately became the canonical theoretical manifesto of anti-caste radicalism in modern India.

*   **Critique of Political Congressites and Orthodox Socialists**: Ambedkar dismantled two dominant dogmas of the 1930s nationalist movement:
    1.  Against the **Congress political nationalists** (who argued that political reform must precede social reform), Ambedkar cited historical precedents—ranging from the Roman Patrician-Plebeian struggle to the **1919 Montagu-Chelmsford** transfer of power—to prove that political constitutions are worthless unless supported by a democratized social structure.
    2.  Against the emerging **Indian Socialists and Communists** (who mechanically applied the European Marxist model of an economic base and ideological superstructure, arguing that proletarian economic revolution would automatically dissolve caste), Ambedkar demonstrated that in India, **religion and caste are not mere superstructural illusions, but direct material forces regulating the division of labor and property**. He warned that Indian workers would never unite across class lines to overthrow capitalism until they first annihilated the **"monster of caste"** that stood directly across the path of proletarian solidarity.
*   **Division of Labor vs. Division of Laborers and "Graded Inequality"**: Ambedkar formulated his famous sociological distinction: the caste system is not merely a **division of labor** (which exists in all civilized societies), it is an unnatural **division of laborers** into watertight compartments graded one above the other, based on pre-destined birth (*predestination*) rather than individual aptitude, condemning millions to occupations they view as degrading (*compulsory unemployment* or *forced labor*). Crucially, he explained why no general revolution had ever overthrown the caste system from within: because caste is structured on **Graded Inequality**, every intermediate caste (from Kshatriya down to Shudra and even higher sub-castes among Untouchables) possesses both an **ascending scale of reverence** and a **descending scale of contempt**. Because every intermediate caste has another caste below it to oppress and look down upon, the oppressed cannot easily unite into a single revolutionary class against the pinnacle of the pyramid unless the entire doctrinal edifice of *Chaturvarnya* and scriptural sanctity (*Shastras*) is dynamite-blasted through **inter-caste marriage** (the physical fusion of blood) and the complete rejection of sacred texts.

### 6. Imperial Constitutional Bargaining, the Poona Pact, and Autonomous Class-Caste Party Formation

#### Franchise Commissions and the All India Depressed Classes Congress (1930): Ambedkar vs. M.C. Rajah
From the inauguration of the **1919 Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms**, **B.R. Ambedkar** recognized that as the British Empire devolved legislative, executive, and fiscal powers to Indian hands, the critical question was how the **Depressed Classes** would secure independent political leverage within the emerging constitutional machinery so as not to become permanent political serfs of the caste-Hindu majority.

*   **The Southborough Franchise Committee Testimony (January 1919)**: In his first major constitutional intervention before Lord Southborough’s Franchise Committee in **1919**, Ambedkar argued that because untouchability created a wall of social isolation far deeper than the religious divide between Hindus and Muslims, the Depressed Classes constituted a distinct sociological minority requiring **universal adult suffrage** (or a drastically lowered property qualification for Dalits) coupled with either **Separate Electorates** or reserved seats with weighted representation. Instead, the **Government of India Act 1919** granted the Depressed Classes only a handful of **nominated seats** chosen by British Governors.
*   **The Simon Commission Testimony (October 1928) and the Bombay Provincial Committee**: Appointed by the Bombay Legislative Council (where he served as a nominated member from **1926 to 1936**) to work alongside the all-white **Indian Statutory (Simon) Commission**, Ambedkar submitted a rigorous dissenting report in **1928–29**. Before the Simon Commission, Ambedkar actually proposed **Joint Electorates with Reserved Seats** for the Depressed Classes * strictly conditional* on the immediate introduction of **Universal Adult Franchise**. However, when the Simon Commission rejected universal adult franchise (restricting the vote to roughly 10% of the population based on property and tax qualifications, which excluded the vast majority of landless Dalits), Ambedkar shifted strategically to demanding **Separate Electorates** as the only guarantee that Dalit legislators would not be handpicked puppets of wealthy caste-Hindu voters.
*   **The All India Depressed Classes Congress at Nagpur (August 1930) and the M.C. Rajah Divergence**: In **August 1930**, Ambedkar convened the landmark **All India Depressed Classes Congress** at **Nagpur**, establishing an autonomous pan-Indian political platform that endorsed **Dominion Status / Self-Government** while demanding constitutional safeguards, anti-discrimination fundamental rights, and civil service quotas for Dalits prior to any transfer of British power. At this juncture, a rival current within Dalit politics was led by **Rao Bahadur M.C. Rajah** (1883–1943)—a prominent **Adi-Dravida** educator from Madras, President of the **All India Depressed Classes Association (founded 1926)**, and the **first Dalit nominated to the Central Legislative Assembly (1927–1937)**. While M.C. Rajah initially supported separate electorates alongside Ambedkar, in **February 1932** he signed the **Rajah-Moonje Pact** with the Hindu Mahasabha President **B.S. Moonje**, agreeing to **Joint Electorates with Reserved Seats** in proportion to the Dalit population. Ambedkar mobilized Dalit associations across India to repudiate the Rajah-Moonje Pact, proving to the British government at the Round Table Conferences that the overwhelming majority of organized Dalit opinion backed his demand for political autonomy.

#### The Three Round Table Conferences, the Communal Award (August 1932), and the Poona Pact (September 1932) vs. Harijan Sevak Sangh
Selected by the British Crown along with **Rettaimalai Srinivasan** to represent the Depressed Classes at all **Three Round Table Conferences (RTCs)** in London (**1930–1932**), **B.R. Ambedkar** emerged as the foremost constitutional jurist of the untouchable movement.

*   **First and Second Round Table Conferences (1930–1931)**: At the **First RTC (Nov 1930 – Jan 1931)**, which the Congress boycotted during the Civil Disobedience Movement, Ambedkar presented his **"Scheme of Political Safeguards for the Protection of the Depressed Classes in the Future Constitution of a Self-Governing India,"** demanding equal citizenship, statutory criminalization of social boycotts, adequate representation in cabinet and civil services, and **Separate Electorates** for the first ten years. At the **Second RTC (Sept–Dec 1931)**, attended by **M.K. Gandhi** as the sole representative of the Indian National Congress following the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact**, a historic ideological collision occurred in the Minorities Committee:
    *   **Gandhi’s Position**: Claiming that the Indian National Congress alone represented all of India, including the untouchables, Gandhi vehemently opposed Separate Electorates for the Depressed Classes. While willing to concede separate electorates to Muslims and Sikhs, Gandhi argued that untouchables were an integral part of the Hindu religious fold and that separate electorates would permanently institutionalize their stigma, divide Hindu villages into warring camps, and prevent upper-caste Hindus from performing moral *penance* to eradicate untouchability. He declared before the Minorities Committee: ***"I would far rather that Hinduism died than that Untouchability lived... I will resist [separate electorates for the Untouchables] with my life."***
    *   **Ambedkar’s Rebuttal**: Challenging Gandhi’s claim to represent Dalits, Ambedkar demonstrated that untouchability was a civic, material, and hereditary tyranny maintained by the caste-Hindu majority, and joined other minorities to sign the **Minorities Pact (November 1931)**.
*   **Ramsay MacDonald’s Communal Award (August 16, 1932)**: Following the deadlock of the Second RTC, British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** announced the **Communal Award** on **August 16, 1932**. Recognizing the Depressed Classes as an official minority, the Award granted Dalits a unique **"Double Vote"** system for a period of **20 years**:
    1.  **One Vote in Separate Electorates**: Exclusively Dalit voters would elect **71 (later revised to 78) Dalit representatives** across provincial legislatures in designated Depressed Class constituencies.
    2.  **A Second Vote in the General (Hindu) Electorate**: Dalit voters would *also* possess a second vote in the general constituencies, compelling caste-Hindu candidates to court Dalit support without allowing caste-Hindu majorities to dictate who won the dedicated Dalit seats.
*   **Gandhi’s Epic Fast at Yerwada Jail and the Poona Pact (September 24, 1932)**: Imprisoned in **Yerwada Central Jail (Pune)**, **M.K. Gandhi** launched a **fast-unto-death on September 20, 1932**, demanding the immediate revocation of separate electorates for the Depressed Classes. As Gandhi’s health rapidly deteriorated, immense moral and physical coercion descended upon **B.R. Ambedkar**, who declared that while he would not be blackmailed into sacrificing the political survival of his people, he was forced to negotiate to prevent a nationwide retaliatory pogrom against vulnerable rural Dalits should Gandhi die in prison. After intense four-day negotiations involving **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, **C. Rajagopalachari**, **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **M.R. Jayakar**, **M.C. Rajah**, and **P.N. Rajbhoj**, the **Poona Pact** was signed on **September 24, 1932**, by **Ambedkar** (on behalf of the Depressed Classes) and **Madan Mohan Malaviya** (on behalf of Caste Hindus), and immediately incorporated by the British government as an amendment to the Communal Award and subsequently into the **Government of India Act 1935**.

*   **Diagnostic Clauses of the Poona Pact (September 1932)**:
    *   **Abolition of Separate Electorates and Expansion of Reserved Seats**: Separate electorates and the "Double Vote" for the Depressed Classes were abolished. In exchange, the number of seats reserved for the Depressed Classes in the Provincial Legislatures was nearly doubled from **71/78 to 148 seats** (distributed across Madras: 30; Bengal: 30; Central Provinces: 20; United Provinces: 20; Bihar & Orissa: 18; Bombay with Sindh: 15; Punjab: 8; Assam: 7), while **18% of the general seats in the Central Legislature** were reserved for the Depressed Classes.
    *   **Two-Tier Primary and General Election Mechanism**: For every reserved seat, registered Depressed Class voters would first meet separately in a **Primary Election** to elect a **panel of four Depressed Class candidates**. In the **Final Election**, the general **Joint Electorate** (comprising both Caste Hindus and Dalits) would vote to elect one legislator from among those four panel candidates. While the Primary Panel system was slated to expire after 10 years unless ended earlier by mutual consent, reserved seats in joint electorates would continue until abolished by mutual agreement.
    *   **Educational Grants and Public Service Non-Discrimination**: Every province was mandated to earmark an adequate sum out of its educational grant specifically for providing educational facilities to members of the Depressed Classes, and no person was to be barred from local bodies or public services due to untouchability.
*   **Gandhi’s Harijan Sevak Sangh (September 1932) and the Divergence**: Immediately following the Poona Pact, on **September 30, 1932**, **M.K. Gandhi** founded the **All India Anti-Untouchability League** (renamed the **Harijan Sevak Sangh**), with the prominent industrialist **Ghanshyam Das Birla** as President and **Amritlal Vithaldas Thakkar (Thakkar Bapa)** as General Secretary, and launched his weekly journal ***Harijan*** in **February 1933** alongside a **9-month, 20,000-kilometer nationwide Harijan Tour (Nov 1933 – Aug 1934)** collecting Rs. 8 lakhs for the Harijan Fund.
    *   Although **B.R. Ambedkar**, **M.C. Rajah**, and **Rettaimalai Srinivasan** were initially placed on the Central Board of the **Harijan Sevak Sangh**, Ambedkar resigned after submitting a comprehensive proposal demanding that the Sangh wage a militant civil rights campaign against rural social boycotts and hand control of the organization over to Dalits.
    *   **G.D. Birla** and **Gandhi** explicitly rejected this demand, stating that the **Harijan Sevak Sangh** was an organization of **penitent Savarna (Caste) Hindus** paying back a moral debt of sin (*Prayaschitta*) to their oppressed brethren, not a political rights organization of Dalits, and therefore its executive control must remain exclusively in upper-caste Hindu hands. Moreover, Gandhi defended the idealized four-fold **Varna system (*Varnashrama Dharma*)** as an egalitarian ancestral division of labor without hierarchy, whereas Ambedkar insisted that Untouchability was the direct, inseparable byproduct of the Varna system and could never be eradicated without the total annihilation of Varna and caste itself.

#### Class-Caste Convergence and Electoral Autonomy: Independent Labour Party (1936) and All India Scheduled Castes Federation (1942)
Preparing for the **1937 Provincial Elections** under the **Government of India Act 1935**, **B.R. Ambedkar** executed a bold political experiment to unite the Dalit movement with the broader peasant and industrial working-class struggle.

*   **The Independent Labour Party (ILP, August 15, 1936)**: Founded by **Ambedkar** in **August 1936**, the **Independent Labour Party (ILP)** was consciously designed not as an exclusively Dalit communal party, but as a radical, class-caste **working-class and peasant party** open to progressive non-Brahmins and caste Hindus. Its comprehensive 1936 Manifesto demanded state ownership and management of key industries, technical education, minimum wages, paid leave, housing for industrial workers, rehabilitation of fragmented agricultural holdings, and the complete abolition of the feudal **Khoti** landlord system in the Konkan and the **Mahar Watan** forced labor system.
    *   In the **February 1937 Bombay Provincial Assembly Elections**, the ILP achieved a stunning victory, winning **14 out of the 17 seats it contested** (11 of the 15 reserved Scheduled Caste seats plus 3 general seats).
    *   Inside the Bombay Assembly (**1937–1939**), Ambedkar and the ILP functioned as a militant opposition to the B.G. Kher Congress Ministry: in **September 1937**, Ambedkar introduced the historic **Khoti Abolition Bill** (the first legislative bill in British India seeking to abolish landlordism) and organized a massive **20,000-strong peasant march to the Bombay Council Hall on January 10, 1938**, uniting Kunbi and Mahar tenants alongside Communist leaders (**S.A. Dange** and **Indulal Yagnik**).
    *   When the Congress Ministry introduced the repressive **Bombay Industrial Disputes Bill (1938)**—which severely curtailed the workers' right to strike by mandating compulsory four-month conciliation delays—Ambedkar’s **ILP** joined hands with the **Communist Party of India (CPI)** to organize a historic **One-Day General Strike on November 7, 1938**, shutting down Bombay’s textile mills as Ambedkar and S.A. Dange addressed over 100,000 workers from the same platform at Kamgar Maidan.
*   **The All India Scheduled Castes Federation (AISCF, July 1942)**: Following the outbreak of World War II and the **March 1942 Cripps Mission**—which ignored Dalit political demands on the grounds that Dalits were not organized into a pan-Indian communal party comparable to the Muslim League—Ambedkar realized that a regional labor party in Bombay was insufficient to secure constitutional safeguards during the final transfer of power negotiations.
    *   Convening the **All India Depressed Classes Conference at Nagpur on July 18–20, 1942**, attended by **70,000 delegates** (including a massive 25,000-strong Women’s Conference presided over by **Sulochanabai Dongre**), Ambedkar dissolved the ILP into the **All India Scheduled Castes Federation (AISCF)**, with the veteran Madras Dalit leader **Rao Bahadur N. Sivaraj** as President and **P.N. Rajbhoj** as General Secretary.
    *   The **AISCF** serves as the first unified pan-Indian political party exclusively representing the **Scheduled Castes**, demanding the abrogation of the Poona Pact, restoration of Separate Electorates, proportional budget allocations for Dalit education, and the creation of separate, state-funded Dalit settlements (*Separate Villages*) armed with independent arable land outside the economic and physical tyranny of caste-Hindu villages.
*   **Viceroy’s Executive Council (1942–1946) and Entry into the Constituent Assembly**: Appointed in **July 1942** as **Labour Member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council** (**1942–1946**), Ambedkar utilized imperial executive power to construct the foundational architecture of modern Indian labor and infrastructure policy:
    *   Enacted the **8-hour working day**, statutory **Dearness Allowance (DA)**, paid maternity leave, equal pay for equal work regardless of sex, the **Coal and Mica Mines Labour Welfare Funds**, the **Employment Exchange** network, and compulsory recognition of trade unions.
    *   Established the **Central Waterways, Irrigation and Navigation Commission (CWINC)** and the **Central Technical Power Board**, initiating India's first multipurpose river-valley projects—the **Damodar Valley Corporation (DVC)**, **Hirakud**, and **Sone** river basin plans.
    *   Secured an official **8.33% reservation for Scheduled Castes in Central Government services (1943)** (raised to **12.5% in June 1946**), alongside overseas state scholarships that sent batches of brilliant Dalit students to universities in London and New York.
    *   Although the **Poona Pact’s** Joint Electorate mechanism enabled the **Indian National Congress** to defeat the **AISCF** in the **1946 Provincial Elections** (demonstrating the exact structural flaw Ambedkar had exposed in his **1945** classic ***What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables***), Ambedkar was initially elected to the **Constituent Assembly in July 1946 from Bengal** with the support of the **Namasudra** leader **Jogendra Nath Mandal** (and later re-elected from Bombay after the 1947 Partition), ascending on **August 29, 1947**, to the Chairmanship of the **Drafting Committee of the Indian Constitution**.

## IV. Statutory Gender Interventions, Brahmanical Patriarchy, and Organized Women’s Assertion (1829–1947)

### 7. Colonial Statutory Codification, Property Regimes, and the Politics of the Domestic Sphere

#### Early Coercive Interventions: Abolition of Sati (Regulation XVII of 1829) and the Female Infanticide Acts (1795, 1804, 1870)
In Indian social structure, the subordination of women was not an isolated cultural custom; it operated as the indispensable reproductive and economic engine of **Brahmanical Patriarchy** and caste endogamy. Because the purity of a caste group (*Jati*) could only be preserved by strictly regulating female sexuality, upper-caste lineages enforced three interlocking institutions to prevent surplus or unattached women (*"the gateways of caste,"* as theorized by both classical *Dharmashastras* and **B.R. Ambedkar**) from marrying outside their endogamous circle: **pre-pubertal child marriage**, **enforced celibate widowhood** (involving tonsure, coarse white garments, and domestic servitude), and **Sati** (*Sahamarana* or widow immolation). Furthermore, this ritual mechanism had a direct material base in property law. When the East India Company codified **Anglo-Hindu Law** (via **Warren Hastings’ 1772 Judicial Plan**, which privileged textual Sanskritic *Shastras* interpreted by Brahmin court *Pandits* over flexible local lower-caste custom), the **Dayabhaga** school of inheritance prevailing in Bengal granted childless Hindu widows a life-interest share in their deceased husband’s joint-family property—creating a powerful material incentive for greedy male coparceners (*bhadralok* kinsmen) in early-19th-century Bengal to pressure widows onto the funeral pyre.

*   **Abolition of Sati (Bengal Regulation XVII of December 4, 1829)**: Between **1815 and 1828**, official colonial police records in the **Bengal Presidency** alone documented over **8,134 instances of Sati**, heavily concentrated in the wealthy districts surrounding **Calcutta** where the **Dayabhaga** property regime intersected with competitive upward caste mobility among new *Zamindari* and commercial elites. Initial Company regulations (**1812–1817**) had perversely increased Sati rates by drawing an administrative distinction between "legal/voluntary" Sati (performed with a Brahmin magistrate's supervision) and "illegal/coerced" Sati, inadvertently lending colonial state sanction to the rite.
    *   **Raja Rammohan Roy’s Textual and Public Campaign**: Mobilizing both print capitalism (via ***Sambad Kaumudi***) and his **Atmiya Sabha** / **Brahmo Samaj**, **Rammohan Roy** published two devastating tracts in **1818 and 1820** (***A Conference Between an Advocate for, and an Opponent of, the Practice of Burning Widows Alive***). Roy bypassed Puranic texts to demonstrate from the older, authoritative ***Manusmriti*** and ***Upanishads*** that *Sahamarana* (dying with the husband) was an inferior ritual seeking transient heaven, whereas ascetic life was the true scriptural path, while simultaneously exposing how male relatives physically tied widows down with bamboo poles and drugged them with opium to seize their **Dayabhaga** property shares.
    *   **Statutory Enactment**: Backed by Roy’s scriptural arguments and assured by military commanders that the Bengal Army sepoys (recruited from Awadh/Bihar where Sati was far rarer than in Calcutta) would not mutiny, Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck** enacted **Bengal Regulation XVII on December 4, 1829**. The statute declared the practice of *Sati* (burning or burying alive of Hindu widows) illegal and punishable by the criminal courts as **culpable homicide**, regardless of whether the act was claimed to be voluntary, and was extended with modifications to the **Madras and Bombay Presidencies in 1830**. When conservative Calcutta *bhadralok* led by **Radhakanta Deb’s Dharma Sabha** appealed to London claiming interference in religious freedom, Roy traveled to England and submitted a counter-petition, securing the **Privy Council’s dismissal of the Dharma Sabha appeal in 1832**.
*   **The Female Infanticide Acts (1795, 1804, and 1870)**: Among high-status landowning martial and agrarian clans in Northern and Western India—specifically **Rajputs** in Benares, Rajputana, and Kathiawar, **Jats**, and **Lewa Patidars** in Gujarat—strict hypergamous marriage rules (*Kulinism* / marrying daughters only into superior clans) combined with exorbitant dowry demands made the birth of a daughter a source of acute status anxiety and fiscal ruin, resulting in systematic female infanticide (by drowning newborn girls in milk or administering opium).
    *   **Bengal Regulation XXI of 1795** (enacted under **Sir John Shore** after British Resident **Jonathan Duncan** uncovered widespread female infanticide among the **Rajkumar Rajputs** of **Jaunpur** in Benares province) and **Regulation III of 1804** formally declared female infanticide equivalent to willful murder.
    *   Because domestic detection remained nearly impossible without vital statistics, the post-1857 colonial state enacted the comprehensive **Female Infanticide Prevention Act (Act VIII of 1870)**. Applied initially to the **North-Western Provinces**, **Punjab**, and **Oudh**, the **1870 Act** mandated compulsory police registration of all births, marriages, and deaths in proclaimed clans/villages, empowered district magistrates to conduct special censuses of juvenile sex ratios, and legally capped extravagant marriage and dowry expenditures.

#### Reconfiguring Conjugality and Property: Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act (1856) and Native Marriage Act (1872)
While over 80% of the Indian population belonging to *Shudra*, peasant, artisanal, and *Adivasi* communities had historically practiced customary widow remarriage (*Pat*, *Karewa*, *Natra*, or *Sagagi*), high-caste Hindu society strictly prohibited widow remarriage, even for child-brides widowed before the consummation of marriage.

*   **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar and the Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act (Act XV of July 26, 1856)**: Spearheaded by **Pandit Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar** (1820–1891)—Principal of the **Sanskrit College, Calcutta**, who also established **35 girls' schools** across Bengal and served as Secretary of the **Bethune School (1849)**—the campaign for widow remarriage utilized classical Sanskrit philology to break orthodox resistance. Discovering a critical verse in the ***Parashara Samhita*** (*"Gate mrte pravrajite klive cha patite patau / Panchasvapatsu narinam patiranyo vidhiyate"*—permitting a woman to take a second husband in five calamities, including the death of her spouse, and explicitly declared by Parashara to be the law for the *Kali Yuga*), Vidyasagar published his two-part treatise ***Marriage of Hindu Widows*** in **1855** and submitted a petition with **987 signatures** to the Legislative Council (countered by **Radhakanta Deb’s Dharma Sabha** petition bearing **36,763 signatures**).
    *   **Statutory Enactment and the "Carroll Anomaly"**: Drafted under **Lord Dalhousie** and signed into law by **Lord Canning** on **July 26, 1856**, the **Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act (Act XV of 1856)** legalized the remarriage of Hindu widows across all British Indian courts and declared the children born of such marriages fully legitimate heirs. However, as feminist legal historians like **Lucy Carroll** have demonstrated, **Section 2 of Act XV of 1856** introduced a devastating patriarchal clawback: it stipulated that upon remarriage, a widow automatically **forfeited all rights and maintenance claims in her deceased husband’s property** as if she had died. Worse still, colonial High Courts subsequently applied **Section 2** to lower-caste (*Shudra*) communities whose customary laws had previously allowed remarrying widows to retain their first husband’s property, thereby **Sanskritizing and disinheriting lower-caste women** through colonial statutory codification. In Western India, the widow remarriage cause was championed by **Vishnu Shastri Pandit** (who founded the **Widow Remarriage Association** in **1866**), **Karsondas Mulji** (editor of ***Satya Prakash*** in Gujarati, who exposed the sexual exploitation of female devotees in the historic **1862 Maharaj Libel Case**), and **Dhondo Keshav Karve** (who married a widow in **1893**, founded the **Widow’s Home / Hingne Stree Shikshan Samstha** in Pune in **1896**, and established the **SNDT Indian Women’s University** in **1916**).
*   **The Native Marriage Act / Civil Marriage Act (Act III of 1872)**: Initiated by the **Brahmo Samaj of India** leader **Keshab Chandra Sen**—who sought a secular civil marriage statute because Brahmos had rejected Hindu idolatrous marriage rituals—the **Native Marriage Act of 1872** banned polygamy, legalized inter-caste marriages, permitted widow remarriage, and fixed the minimum age of marriage at **14 years for girls** and **18 years for boys**. However, due to fierce pressure from orthodox Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi lobbies, the colonial legislature restricted the Act’s applicability strictly to persons who signed a legal declaration stating: ***"I do not profess the Hindu, Muhammadan, Christian, Parsi, Buddhist, Sikh, or Jaina religion."*** Furthermore, in **1878**, **Keshab Chandra Sen** himself violated the spirit of the 1872 Act by marrying his 13-year-old daughter to the underage **Maharaja of Cooch Behar** according to orthodox Hindu rites, triggering the second schism of the Brahmo Samaj and the formation of the **Sadharan Brahmo Samaj** under **Ananda Mohan Bose**, **Shibnath Shastri**, and **Umesh Chandra Dutta**.

#### The Bodily Autonomy Rupture: The Rukhmabai Case, the Age of Consent Act (1891), and the Sarda Act (1929)
During the late 19th century, the domestic bedroom and the female body became the primary ideological battleground between colonial humanitarian intervention, liberal social reformers, and militant cultural nationalists who equated the autonomous domain of the Hindu home (*Ghar*) with national sovereignty.

*   **The Rukhmabai Case (*Dadaji Bhikaji vs. Rukhmabai*, 1884–1888)**: Born into the **Sutar** (carpenter) caste in Bombay, **Rukhmabai** (1864–1955) had been married at the age of eleven to an impoverished, uneducated nineteen-year-old kinsman, **Dadaji Bhikaji**, while continuing to live with her mother and her stepfather, **Dr. Sakharam Arjun** (a prominent surgeon and botanist who educated her at home). Eleven years later, in **1884**, Dadaji—eyeing the substantial property Rukhmabai had inherited from her biological father—filed a suit in the **Bombay High Court** for **"Restitution of Conjugal Rights,"** an medieval English ecclesiastical legal remedy that the British had imported into Anglo-Indian civil procedure.
    *   Refusing to submit to a non-consensual child marriage contracted without her knowledge, **Rukhmabai** wrote scathing letters to the ***Times of India*** under the pseudonym ***"A Hindu Lady"*** (**1885**). Although **Justice Robert Hill Pinhey** initially ruled in Rukhmabai’s favor in **September 1885** (holding that restitution of conjugal rights could not be used to force an adult woman to consummate an unconsummated infant marriage), an appellate bench headed by **Chief Justice Charles Sargent** overturned Pinhey’s verdict in **1886**, ordering Rukhmabai to move into Dadaji’s house within one month or face **six months' rigorous imprisonment**.
    *   Backed by the **Rukhmabai Defence Committee** (led by **Behramji Malabari** and **M.G. Ranade**), Rukhmabai publicly declared she would gladly go to prison rather than submit her body to unwanted conjugality, while **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** attacked her in ***Kesari*** and ***Mahratta***, claiming her English education had threatened the very foundations of Hinduism. An out-of-court settlement in **1888** (where Rukhmabai paid Dadaji **Rs. 2,000** to renounce his marital claim) allowed her to travel to the **London School of Medicine for Women**, returning in **1894** to serve for 35 years as one of India’s first practicing female physicians in Surat and Rajkot.
*   **The Age of Consent Act (Act X of March 19, 1891)**: Catalyzed by the **Rukhmabai Case**, the Parsi reformer and editor of the ***Indian Spectator***, **Behramji Merwanji Malabari** (1853–1912, who later co-founded the **Seva Sadan** in **1908** with **Dayaram Gidumal** to provide shelter and vocational training for destitute women across castes), circulated his famous ***Notes on Infant Marriage and Enforced Widowhood*** (**1884**). The decisive shock occurred in **1889** with the **Phulmoni Dasi Case** in Calcutta, where a ten-year-old girl (**Phulmoni**) bled to death from catastrophic internal vaginal injuries after being forcibly raped on her wedding night by her thirty-five-year-old husband, **Hari Maiti**—who could not be prosecuted for rape under the **Indian Penal Code (1860)** because the statutory age of marital consent stood at just **10 years**.
    *   Sponsored by Law Member **Sir Andrew Scoble**, the **Age of Consent Act of 1891** amended Section 375 of the IPC, raising the minimum age of consent for sexual intercourse for both married and unmarried girls from **10 to 12 years**, classifying intercourse below 12 years as statutory rape.
    *   Supported by **M.G. Ranade**, **R.G. Bhandarkar**, **K.T. Telang**, and **Pandita Ramabai**, the Bill was fiercely opposed by **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** in Maharashtra and **Bipin Chandra Pal** and the newspaper ***Bangabasi*** in Bengal—not because Tilak personally favored infant marriage, but on the nationalist ground that an alien colonial legislature had no moral or legal right to interfere in the sacred domestic customs of Hindus (*Shastras*).
*   **The Child Marriage Restraint Act / Sarda Act (Act XIX of September 28, 1929)**: Following the horrifying findings of the **Joshi Age of Consent Committee (1928–1929, chaired by Sir Moropant Vishwanath Joshi)**—which documented that millions of girls were still being married below age 14, leading to extreme maternal and infant mortality—**Rai Sahib Harbilas Sarda** (an Arya Samajist judge and legislator from Ajmer-Merwara), heavily backed by the newly formed women’s organizations (**WIA**, **NCWI**, and **AIWC**), introduced the **Child Marriage Restraint Bill** in the Central Legislative Assembly in **1927**. Enacted on **September 28, 1929** (and coming into force on **April 1, 1930**), the **Sarda Act** applied to all communities across British India (Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Parsis), fixing the minimum age of marriage at **14 years for girls** and **18 years for boys** (subsequently raised to 15 in 1949 and 18/21 in 1978), and making the solemnization of child marriages a punishable offense with fines and up to three months' imprisonment.

### 8. Autonomous Feminist Epistemologies and All-India Institutional Mobilization

#### Subaltern and High-Caste Feminist Critiques: Tarabai Shinde (Stri Purush Tulana, 1882), Pandita Ramabai, and Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain
While 19th-century male social reformers approached the "Woman Question" primarily to create educated, companionate wives for English-educated Indian civil servants without disturbing patrilineal authority, a remarkable cohort of Indian women intellectuals articulated a direct, systemic critique of **patriarchy, caste endogamy, and religious orthodoxy** from within their own lived experience.

*   **Tarabai Shinde (1850–1910) and *Stri Purush Tulana* (1882)**: Born into a Maratha family in **Buldhana (Berar)** and closely associated with **Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule’s Satyashodhak Samaj**, **Tarabai Shinde** authored ***Stri Purush Tulana*** (*A Comparison Between Women and Men*, **1882**), widely recognized as the **first full-fledged feminist theoretical treatise in modern India**.
    *   **The Vijayalakshmi Case Catalyst**: Shinde wrote the fiery 52-page Marathi tract in response to the **1881 Surat Court Case** of **Vijayalakshmi**, a young Brahmin widow who had been sentenced to death by hanging (later commuted to transportation for life) for committing infanticide after becoming pregnant out of wedlock, and whose moral character had been viciously slandered by the conservative newspaper ***Poona Vaibhav***.
    *   **Deconstructing Male Double Standards and Puranic Ideology**: With razor-sharp satire and structural clarity, Shinde exposed how patriarchal society permitted men to marry second or third wives within days of their first wife’s death and visit courtesans with impunity, while condemning widows to lifelong celibacy and then executing them when male predators impregnated them. She demolished the ideological archetype of *Pativrata* (husband-worship) found in Puranic myths (such as *Savitri* and *Sita*) as male-authored propaganda designed to enslave women, asked why a God who supposedly created both sexes would bestow all virtue on men and all sin (*Strisvabhav*) on women, and explicitly defended the colonial British Raj strictly on the grounds that it had broken the monopoly of Peshwa-era patriarchal terror and allowed women access to the printing press and schools.
*   **Pandita Ramabai Dongre Medhavi (1858–1922)**: An extraordinary Chitpavan Brahmin prodigy whose reformist father **Anant Shastri Dongre** faced ostracism for teaching his wife and daughter Sanskrit in the forests of the Western Ghats, **Ramabai** mastered over 18,000 Sanskrit verses, earning the titles **Pandita** and **Saraswati** from the Sanskrit scholars of **Calcutta University** in **1878**. After marrying a Bengali *Shudra* lawyer (**Bipin Behari Medhavi**) in an inter-caste civil marriage in **1880** and being widowed at age twenty-three with an infant daughter (**Manorama**), **Pandita Ramabai** moved to **Pune** in **1882**:
    *   **The Arya Mahila Samaj (May 1882) and Hunter Commission Testimony**: In **May 1882**, Ramabai founded the **Arya Mahila Samaj** in Pune (with branches across the Bombay Presidency) to abolish child marriage and educate women. Appearing before the **Hunter Education Commission in September 1882** in Pune, she delivered a landmark testimony in Marathi (translated into English by Hunter himself) demanding that the colonial government train female teachers and **female medical doctors** because Indian women died in thousands rather than be examined by male physicians—a testimony that directly inspired **Queen Victoria** and **Lady Dufferin** to establish the **Countess of Dufferin Fund (1885)** for women’s medical education in India.
    *   ***The High-Caste Hindu Woman* (1887) and the Sharada Sadan (1889)**: Traveling to England (**1883**, where she converted to Christianity while fiercely resisting the racial paternalism of Anglican nuns) and the **United States (1886–88, attending the medical graduation of her cousin Dr. Anandibai Joshi in Philadelphia)**, Ramabai published her English bestseller ***The High-Caste Hindu Woman*** (**1887**). Using the proceeds and support from the **Ramabai Association of Boston**, she returned to India and opened the **Sharada Sadan** (*Home of Learning*) in **Bombay on March 11, 1889** (relocated to **Pune in 1890**), the first residential secular school and shelter for high-caste child widows in Maharashtra. When Tilak and conservative Advisory Board members boycotted the Sharada Sadan over reports that some widows had voluntarily attended Christian prayers, Ramabai relocated the mission to **Kedgaon** near Pune, establishing the sprawling **Mukti Mission (1898)**—where she personally rescued, housed, educated, and trained over **2,000 destitute lower-caste women and famine orphans** in printing, weaving, dairy farming, and nursing during the **1896–1900 famines**.
*   **Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932)**: Born into an orthodox aristocratic *Zamindari* family in **Pairaband (Rangpur, Bengal)** where tight *Purdah* forbade girls from learning Bengali or English, **Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain** was secretly taught to read by her elder brother by candlelight and encouraged by her progressive husband, **Syed Sakhawat Hossain**.
    *   ***Sultana’s Dream* (1905) and *Abarodhbasini* (1929)**: In **1905**, Rokeya published ***Sultana’s Dream*** in the *Madras Christian College Magazine*—one of the earliest feminist utopian science-fiction novellas in world literature. In her satirical utopia **Ladyland**, traditional gender roles are completely inverted: aggressive, war-mongering men are confined indoors in the **Mardana** (male *Purdah*), while women rule the public sphere through scientific universities, harnessing **solar power**, controlling rain-clouds for agriculture, and navigating via flying hydrogen air-cars without armies or police because *"men’s brains are somewhat bigger, like an elephant’s, but women’s brains are quicker and more rational."* In her Bengali sociological critique ***Abarodhbasini*** (*The Secluded Ones*, **1929**) and essays in ***Motichur*** (**1904**), she documented 47 real-life horror accounts of how extreme *Purdah* killed or maimed Muslim and Hindu women (such as women burning to death in house fires or being crushed by trains rather than being seen by strange men).
    *   **Institutional Activism**: Following her husband’s death in **1909**, she founded the **Sakhawat Memorial Girls’ High School** in Bhagalpur (moved to **Calcutta in 1911**), going door-to-door in a covered horse-carriage to persuade orthodox Muslim families to send their daughters to school, and founded the **Anjuman-e-Khawateen-e-Islam** (*Muslim Women’s Association*) in **1916** to provide literacy and vocational training to impoverished slum women in Calcutta.

#### Transition to Women-Led National Organizations: Bharat Stree Mahamandal (1910), WIA (1917), NCWI (1925), and AIWC (1927)
Prior to **1910**, organizations addressing women’s issues (such as the **Bharat Mahila Parishad**, established in **1904** under **Ramabai Ranade** as the Ladies' Social Conference wing of the male-led Indian National Social Conference) were largely auxiliary bodies directed or patronized by male reformers. Between **1910 and 1927**, Indian women broke decisively with male trusteeship, establishing four autonomous, pan-Indian women’s organizations led exclusively by women to fight for education, statutory marriage reform, and **universal female suffrage**.

*   **Sarala Devi Chaudhurani and the Bharat Stree Mahamandal (1910)**: The niece of **Rabindranath Tagore**, editor of the literary journal ***Bharati***, and a militant **Swadeshi** activist who organized physical culture clubs (*Akharas*), *Lathi-khela* tournaments, and the *Pratapaditya* festival in Bengal before moving to Punjab after marrying the Arya Samajist nationalist **Rambhaj Dutt Chaudhuri**, **Sarala Devi Chaudhurani** (1872–1945) grew frustrated with the paternalistic condescension of male reformers in the National Social Conference. In **November 1910**, at **Allahabad**, she founded the **Bharat Stree Mahamandal** (*The Great Circle of Indian Women*)—the **first permanent all-India women’s organization created by a woman specifically for women, shutting out male control**. Setting up branches in **Lahore, Allahabad, Delhi, Karachi, Amritsar, Hyderabad, Kanpur, Bankura, Hazaribagh, Midnapore, and Calcutta**, the Mahamandal recognized that millions of adult women trapped in strict *Purdah* (*Zenana*) could not attend public schools, and therefore organized a network of **home-visiting female teachers** who entered secluded households across caste and religious lines to teach reading, writing, sewing, embroidery, and music.
*   **The Women’s Indian Association (WIA, May 8, 1917)**: Founded at **Adyar, Madras**, on **May 8, 1917**, the **Women’s Indian Association (WIA)** emerged from the organizational synergy of the **Theosophical Society** and the **Home Rule Movement**. Its co-founders were the Irish suffragettes and Theosophists **Margaret Cousins** (who had served prison terms in Britain and Ireland with the militant Pankhurst suffragettes) and **Dorothy Jinarajadasa**, alongside **Annie Besant** (who became WIA’s first President) and pioneering Indian leaders **Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy**, **Malati Patwardhan**, **Ammu Swaminathan**, and **Herabai Tata**.
    *   **Print Organ and Multi-Religious Structure**: Publishing the influential monthly journal ***Stri Dharma*** (edited by **Margaret Cousins** and **Muthulakshmi Reddy** in English, Tamil, and Hindi), the WIA established over **80 branches** across India and consciously defined itself as a cross-caste, multi-religious political and social platform.
    *   **The Historic December 1917 Montagu Suffrage Deputation**: In **December 1917**, **Margaret Cousins** and the **WIA** organized a historic **14-member all-India women’s delegation** led by **Sarojini Naidu** (and including **Annie Besant**, **Ramabai Ranade**, **Herabai Tata**, and **Begum Hasrat Mohani**) to meet Secretary of State **Edwin Montagu** and Viceroy **Lord Chelmsford** in Madras, submitting the **first official Indian demand for Women’s Franchise** on the exact same terms as men rather than treating women as mere dependents. Although the British Parliament’s **Southborough Committee (1919)** refused to grant female suffrage directly in the **Government of India Act 1919**, it empowered Indian Provincial Legislatures to remove the sex disqualification by resolution—a clause the **WIA** immediately leveraged to secure women’s voting rights in **Travancore-Cochin (1920)**, **Madras (April 1921)**, **Bombay (July 1921)**, **United Provinces (1923)**, **Bengal and Punjab (1926)**, and **Bihar and Central Provinces (1929–30)**.
*   **The National Council of Women in India (NCWI, 1925)**: Founded in **1925** in **Bombay** as the national branch of the **International Council of Women (ICW)** through the initiatives of **Lady Aberdeen** and elite Indian philanthropic women led by **Lady Mehribai Tata** (Chair of the Executive Committee), **Maharani Chimnabai Gaekwad of Baroda**, **Maharani Sucharu Devi of Mayurbhanj**, **Cornelia Sorabji** (India’s first female barrister, trained at Oxford in 1892), and **Tarabai Premchand**, the **NCWI** brought together provincial councils from Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Delhi, and Bihar. While its leadership remained heavily drawn from royal, Parsi industrial, and Anglicized upper-class circles—Prompting nationalist women to critique its close alignment with British officials—the **NCWI’s Standing Sectional Committees** conducted rigorous empirical surveys on female factory labor conditions in Bombay and Bengal jute mills, maternity welfare, juvenile delinquency, prostitution law reform, and lobbied alongside WIA and AIWC for the **1929 Sarda Act**.
*   **The All India Women’s Conference (AIWC, January 1927)**: Initiated in **1926** by **Margaret Cousins**—who issued an appeal calling upon women in every province to hold constituent conferences to design an authentic Indian curriculum for female education—the **First Session of the All India Women’s Conference (AIWC)** convened at **Fergusson College Hall, Pune, on January 5–8, 1927**, attended by over **2,000 observers and 87 delegates** inherited from 22 regional constituent conferences, with **Maharani Chimnabai Gaekwad of Baroda** as first President, **Rani Lalitakumari of Mandi**, **Sarojini Naidu** (President in **1930**), **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** (First Organizing Secretary, who transformed the AIWC into a mass activist body and pioneered socialist feminism, prison satyagraha during the **1930 Dandi Salt raids**, and the revival of indigenous handicrafts), **Rajkumari Amrit Kaur** (President in **1931–33** and later independent India’s first Health Minister), **Begum Shareefah Hamid Ali**, **Rameshwari Nehru**, **Hansa Mehta**, and **Muthulakshmi Reddy**.
    *   **Expansion from Education to Comprehensive Legal and Labor Reform**: Although initially founded as the *All India Women’s Conference on Educational Reform* (establishing the **Lady Irwin College for Home Science** in New Delhi in **1932**), the **AIWC** quickly realized at its **1928 Delhi (presided over by the Begum of Bhopal)** and **1929 Patna Sessions** that female education was impossible without dismantling child marriage, *Purdah*, and property disinheritance. Amending its constitution to address all social, legal, and political spheres, the AIWC spearheaded the nationwide agitation for the **Sarda Act (1929)**, demanded maternity benefits and crèches for women working in coal mines and plantations (testifying before the **1929 Whitley Royal Commission on Labour**), and launched a ten-year campaign in the **1930s** for a comprehensive secular overhaul of Hindu personal law regarding marriage, divorce, and equal inheritance—laying the direct institutional foundation for the **Rau Hindu Law Committee (1941)** and the post-independence **Hindu Code Bills**.
    *   **The Franchise Question and Rejection of Communal Electorates (1931–1935)**: When the British government convened the **Round Table Conferences** and the **Lothian Franchise Committee (1932)**, the three major women’s organizations (**AIWC**, **WIA**, and **NCWI**) met jointly in Bombay in **1931** under **Sarojini Naidu** to draft a historic **Joint Memorandum on the Status of Indian Women**. Hosted before the Parliamentary Joint Select Committee in London in **1933** by **Rajkumari Amrit Kaur**, **Muthulakshmi Reddy**, and **Begum Hamid Ali**, the united women’s movement explicitly rejected **Separate Communal Electorates** or special property-based reserved seats for women, demanding instead **Universal Adult Suffrage without sex, property, or communal division** (*"no privilege and no favour"*), so that the unified women’s movement would not be fractured along Hindu-Muslim communal fault lines. Overriding their opposition, the **Government of India Act 1935** introduced a 1:5 female-to-male enfranchisement ratio based on "wifehood" and literacy qualifications and reserved **41 seats for women along communal lines**—yet in the subsequent **1937 Provincial Elections**, **56 women** entered the provincial legislatures (including **Vijayalakshmi Pandit**, who became India’s first female Cabinet Minister in the United Provinces), cementing women’s permanent political agency within the modern Indian state.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge: From Social Emancipation to Elite and Mass Anti-Colonial Nationalism
The profound subaltern, non-Brahmin, Dalit, and feminist mobilizations between **1829 and 1947** exposed the fundamental contradiction at the heart of modern Indian history: the struggle against external British imperial extraction could not be separated from the internal struggle against **Brahmanical caste hegemony, agrarian feudalism, and patrilineal subordination**. Every time upper-caste nationalist elites attempted to forge a unified anti-colonial front while deferring the abolition of untouchability, caste monopoly in the bureaucracy, or gender inequality to an indefinite post-independence future, autonomous movements—from **Jyotirao Phule’s Satyashodhak Samaj**, **Sri Narayana Guru’s SNDP Yogam**, **Ayyankali’s SJPS**, and **Periyar’s Self-Respect Movement** to **B.R. Ambedkar’s Scheduled Castes Federation** and the **All India Women’s Conference**—intervened to force democratization from below. 

Having examined how these subaltern and gender assertions contested both colonial law and indigenous social hierarchies, we now turn in **Part 9** to the parallel institutional trajectory of pan-Indian anti-colonial politics (**1870–1917**): tracing how regional landlord and middle-class associations coalesced into the **Indian National Congress (1885)**, how the **Moderate** economic critique of empire collided with **Curzon’s 1905 Partition of Bengal** to ignite the **Swadeshi and Boycott Rupture**, how the **1907 Surat Split**, **1909 Morley-Minto Communal Electorates**, and **1916 Home Rule Leagues** reshaped constitutional politics, and how the nationalist imagination forged a counter-colonial visual aesthetic in the **Bengal School of Art** and **Santiniketan** directly against the monumental imperial geometry of **Lutyens and Baker’s New Delhi**.
