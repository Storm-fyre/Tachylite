# Part 14: Imperial Constitutional Counter-Strategies, Wartime Famine, Quit India, the INA, and the Transfer of Power (1935–1947)

## I. The Imperial Constitutional Containment Grid, Congress Ministerial Governance, and Internal Ideological Ruptures (1935–1939)

### 1. The Government of India Act 1935: Federal Containment, Central Dyarchy, and Provincial Autonomy

The **Government of India Act 1935** (enacted in **August 1935** comprising 321 Sections and 10 Schedules following the White Paper of 1933 and the Joint Select Committee Report of Lord Linlithgow) represented the culminating statutory instrument of late-colonial constitutional engineering. Faced with the mass agrarian and urban mobilization of the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934) and the structural exhaustion of the 1919 Montagu-Chelmsford dyarchical arrangement, Whitehall designed a bifurcated constitutional architecture. Its strategic objective was to deflect the Indian National Congress away from pan-Indian anti-imperialist confrontation by channeling nationalist energies into provincial electoral competition, while insulating the imperial executive at the Centre—specifically military expenditure, debt servicing (Home Charges), currency manipulation, and foreign relations—behind a conservative bulwark of nominated Princely States and communally fragmented electorates.

#### Structural Anatomy of the 1935 Imperial Containment Grid
*   **The Proposed All-India Federation and Princely Veto**: Part II of the **Government of India Act 1935** envisaged an **All-India Federation** uniting British Indian Provinces (whose accession was compulsory) with the Princely States (whose accession was voluntary via individual Instruments of Accession). The federation could only be proclaimed once rulers representing at least half the aggregate population of the Princely States and entitled to at least half the seats allotted to states in the Upper House (Council of State) acceded. By granting the autocratic Princes 33% of seats in the Federal Assembly (125 out of 375) and 40% in the Council of State (104 out of 260) via nomination rather than election, British planners sought to construct a permanent conservative veto bloc against nationalist legislation. Because the Princes feared federal fiscal encroachments and Congress-backed *Praja Mandal* democratization, they refused to sign the Instruments of Accession; consequently, the **All-India Federation** never came into existence, and the central government continued to operate under the transitional provisions of the 1919 Act until 1947.
*   **Central Dyarchy and Fiscal Insulation**: At the Federal Centre, the Act replicated the discredited 1919 provincial mechanism by introducing **Central Dyarchy**. Executive authority was split between **Reserved Subjects**—Defense, External Affairs, Ecclesiastical Affairs, and Tribal Areas—administered exclusively by the Governor-General through Executive Councillors unaccountable to the legislature, and **Transferred Subjects**, administered by ministers subject to sweeping "safeguards" and "special responsibilities." Over 80% of the federal budget (comprising military allocations, civil service salaries, pensions, and sovereign debt servicing) was classified as non-votable, rendering the bicameral federal legislature fiscally toothless.
*   **Three Legislative Lists and Viceregal Residuary Powers**: To codify federal-provincial jurisdiction, the Seventh Schedule established three legislative lists: the **Federal List** (59 items, including currency, defense, external affairs, railways, and customs), the **Provincial List** (54 items, including public order, police, land revenue, agriculture, public health, and local government), and the **Concurrent List** (36 items, including criminal law, civil procedure, marriage, divorce, labor welfare, and trade unions). In a decisive departure from both the Canadian model (residuary powers at the Centre) and the American/Australian models (residuary powers with the States), Section 104 vested **residuary powers** exclusively in the discretionary authority of the **Viceroy**, enabling him to empower either the federal or provincial legislature to enact laws on unenumerated subjects.
*   **Provincial Autonomy (April 1937) and Coercive Safeguards**: Part III of the Act, inaugurated in **April 1937**, abolished provincial dyarchy across 11 Governor’s Provinces (including newly separated Burma, Aden, and the newly created provinces of Sindh and Orissa) and introduced **Provincial Autonomy**. Provinces acquired a distinct legal personality and direct borrowing powers, while the distinction between Reserved and Transferred provincial subjects was eliminated in favor of a unified Council of Ministers responsible to an elected legislature (bicameral in Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam, and the United Provinces; unicameral in Punjab, Central Provinces, NWFP, Orissa, and Sindh). However, this autonomy was circumscribed by the Governor’s discretionary powers, "special responsibilities" (protection of minorities, civil servants' rights, British commercial interests against discriminatory tariffs, and Princely States), ordinance-making powers, and **Section 93**, which empowered the Governor to suspend the constitution and assume total dictatorial administration in the event of a breakdown of constitutional machinery.
*   **The Federal Court and Institutional Decoupling**: The Act abolished the 1858 Council of India in London, replacing it with a small body of advisors to the Secretary of State, and established a **Federal Court** at Delhi (inaugurated on **October 1, 1937**, under Chief Justice Sir Maurice Gwyer) possessing original jurisdiction in disputes between the federation and provinces, alongside appellate and advisory functions, though final appellate jurisdiction remained vested in the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London. Simultaneously, the creation of the **Reserve Bank of India (1935)** insulated currency, exchange-rate management, and sterling remittances from Indian legislative oversight, while the extension of separate communal electorates to Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans, Women, Labor, Landlords, and Commerce (alongside Poona Pact reserved seats for the Depressed Classes) entrenched electoral fragmentation among an electorate expanded to roughly 30 million (~14% of the adult population).

### 2. The 1937 Provincial Elections, the 28-Month Congress Ministries, and the Pirpur Counter-Mobilization

Although the Indian National Congress at its Lucknow and Faizpur sessions (1936) categorically rejected the **Government of India Act 1935**—which Jawaharlal Nehru memorably characterized as a *"charter of slavery"* and a *"machine with strong brakes and no engine"*—the party resolved to contest the **1937 provincial elections** to demonstrate its mass hegemony and wreck the constitution from within. The elections, conducted in **January–February 1937**, altered the constitutional calculus of the Raj: the Congress swept the general constituencies, whereas regional landlord parties and the All-India Muslim League suffered severe electoral setbacks in Muslim-majority provinces. After extracting a tactical assurance from Viceroy Lord Linlithgow in **June 1937** that Governors would not routinely invoke their special override powers in day-to-day administration, the Congress formed ministries in **July 1937**, initiating the **28-month Congress ministries (1937–1939)** experiment in semi-sovereign governance.

#### Mechanics of Electoral Hegemony, Ministerial Governance, and Communal Reaction
*   **The 1937 Provincial Elections**: Out of 1,585 seats across the 11 provincial legislative assemblies, the Congress contested 1,161 seats and won 716, securing absolute majorities in five provinces (**Madras**, **United Provinces**, **Central Provinces**, **Bihar**, and **Orissa**) and emerging as the single largest party in four others (**Bombay**, **Assam**, **NWFP**, and **Bengal**). Initially forming ministries in six provinces in **July 1937** (Madras under C. Rajagopalachari, Bombay under B.G. Kher, Central Provinces under N.B. Khare and later Ravishankar Shukla, UP under Govind Ballabh Pant, Bihar under Sri Krishna Sinha, and Orissa under Biswanath Das), the Congress subsequently formed ministries in the **NWFP** (under Dr. Khan Sahib) and **Assam** (under Gopinath Bordoloi), governing 8 out of 11 provinces. Conversely, the All-India Muslim League captured only 109 of the 482 reserved Muslim seats (~4.8% of the total Muslim vote), failing to win a single seat in the NWFP and securing only 2 of 84 Muslim seats in Punjab (dominated by Sikandar Hayat Khan’s cross-communal **Unionist Party**) and 3 of 33 in Sindh, while Bengal was governed by A.K. Fazlul Huq’s **Krishak Praja Party** in coalition with the League.
*   **Agrarian, Labor, and Civil Liberties Record of the 28-Month Congress Ministries (1937–1939)**: Supervised centrally by the Congress Parliamentary Sub-Committee (**Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Rajendra Prasad**, and **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**), the ministries immediately dismantled the coercive apparatus of the Civil Disobedience era: emergency public safety ordinances were repealed, bans on radical organizations (such as the Hindustan Seva Dal and Youth Leagues) and proscribed books were lifted, press securities were refunded, confiscated lands in Bardoli were restored, and thousands of political prisoners (including Kakori, Deoli, and Andaman cellular jail revolutionaries) were released following a brief constitutional crisis in UP and Bihar in **February 1938**. In the agrarian sphere—constrained by landlord-dominated upper houses (Legislative Councils), lack of financial resources due to central revenue retention, and the right-wing Congress leadership’s reluctance to ignite class war—the ministries could not abolish Zamindari, but enacted significant tenancy and debt-relief legislation: the **United Provinces Tenancy Act (1939)** (granting hereditary occupancy rights to statutory tenants and abolishing arbitrary *nazarana* and *begar*), the **Bihar Tenancy Amendments (1937–38)** (reducing rents by ~25% and abolishing *abwabs* and the *danabandi* produce-rent appraisal system), the **Bombay Small Holders Relief Act**, and **Agriculturists' Debtors Relief Acts** across Madras, Bombay, Bihar, and CP that scaled down usurious interest rates. In industrial relations, while the **Bombay Industrial Disputes Act (1938)** introduced compulsory arbitration and a four-month cooling-off notice period for strikes (provoking a massive one-day protest strike engineered by communists and B.R. Ambedkar), the ministries also appointed the **Kanpur Labour Inquiry Committee** (under Rajendra Prasad) and the **Bombay Textile Inquiry Committee**, securing wage increases for millhands, while shifting toward coercive invocations of Section 144 and Section 124A against radical *Kisan Sabha* (*Bakasht*) and trade union agitations by late 1938.
*   **Coalition Collapse and the Pirpur Committee Report (1938)**: In the United Provinces, pre-election informal electoral coordination between the Congress and the Muslim League collapsed in **July 1937** when the Congress, having won a clear majority on its own (134 of 228 seats, though none of the Muslim seats initially), demanded that the Muslim League legislative party dissolve its independent group, accept Congress disciplinary whips, and cease functioning as a separate parliamentary board in exchange for two cabinet posts (targeted at Chaudhry Khaliquzzaman and Nawab Ismail Khan). Faced with political marginalization and alarmed by the Congress’s **Muslim Mass Contact Programme** (launched by Jawaharlal Nehru and coordinated by Dr. K.M. Ashraf to appeal directly to Muslim peasants over the heads of feudal elites), Muhammad Ali Jinnah pivoted the Muslim League from elite constitutionalism to mass communal mobilization. In **March 1938**, the Muslim League Council appointed an eight-member inquiry committee chaired by **Raja Syed Mohammad Mehdi of Pirpur**. Submitting its findings in **November 1938**, the **Pirpur Committee Report (1938)** (subsequently reinforced by the **Shareef Report** on Bihar in **March 1939** and A.K. Fazlul Huq’s pamphlet *Muslim Sufferings Under Congress Rule* in **December 1939**) systematically indicted the Congress ministries for establishing a "Hindu Raj." The report alleged cultural and institutional suppression through the official singing of *Vande Mataram*, the hoisting of the Congress tricolour on public buildings, the promotion of Sanskritized Hindi over Urdu, the linguistic bias of the **Wardha Scheme of Basic Education** (*Nai Talim*) and the *Vidya Mandir* scheme in the Central Provinces, economic boycotts of Muslim traders, and discriminatory policing during communal riots—charges that the Congress rejected by offering an independent investigation by the Chief Justice of the Federal Court (Sir Maurice Gwyer), which the League refused.

### 3. Industrial Planning vs. Anti-Imperial Militancy: The 1938 Haripura Session and the 1939 Tripuri Crisis

As the **28-month Congress ministries (1937–1939)** exposed the structural ceilings of working within the 1935 statutory framework, an intense ideological polarization erupted within the Indian National Congress between the Gandhian Right Wing—which favored institutional consolidation, social harmony, and tactical restraint—and the burgeoning Left Wing (comprising the Congress Socialist Party, Communists, radical Kisan Sabhas, and left-nationalists led by **Subhas Chandra Bose** and **Jawaharlal Nehru**). This internal dialectic crystallized across two consecutive annual sessions—the **1938 Haripura Session** and the **1939 Tripuri Crisis**—combining the institutional birth of techno-industrial developmental planning with a decisive organizational showdown over the timing of a mass ultimatum to the British Empire on the eve of the Second World War.

#### Techno-Industrial Planning and the Haripura-Tripuri Organizational Rupture
*   **The 1938 Haripura Session and the National Planning Committee**: Presiding over the 51st session of the Congress at **Haripura** (Surat district, Gujarat) in **February 1938**, **Subhas Chandra Bose** articulated a militant socio-economic vision that broke sharply with Gandhian rural-artisanal orthodoxy. The Haripura session modified Congress policy toward the Princely States by extending active moral support to *Praja Mandal* struggles and permitting individual Congressmen to lead agitations inside the states. More fundamentally, asserting that political freedom was inseparable from scientific industrial reconstruction and the eradication of poverty, Bose convened a conference of provincial Industry Ministers in Delhi in **October 1938** and established the **National Planning Committee (NPC)** with **Jawaharlal Nehru** as Chairman and economist **K.T. Shah** as General Secretary. Operating through 29 specialized sub-committees bridging industrial capitalists (such as Purshotamdas Thakurdas, A.D. Shroff, Walchand Hirachand, and Ambalal Sarabhai), economists, and technocrats (such as M. Visvesvaraya and Meghnad Saha), the **National Planning Committee** formulated a ten-year blueprint for state-directed heavy industrialization, public ownership of defense and key capital-goods industries, river-valley multipurpose power generation, and agrarian institutional reform, laying the direct conceptual and empirical foundation for post-independence Five-Year Planning.
*   **The 1939 Presidential Election: Subhas Chandra Bose vs. Pattabhi Sitaramayya**: In **January 1939**, defying the established convention of unanimous presidential selection by the Congress High Command, **Subhas Chandra Bose** announced his candidacy for re-election. Bose contended that the Gandhian Right Wing (led by Sardar Patel, Rajendra Prasad, and Maulana Azad) was secretly preparing to compromise with Whitehall over the Federal part of the 1935 Act, whereas the imminent outbreak of war in Europe following the Munich Agreement demanded an uncompromising, left-wing president. After Maulana Azad and Jawaharlal Nehru declined to stand, Mahatma Gandhi backed Dr. **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** of Andhra. In the election held on **January 29, 1939**, supported by Socialists, Communists, Royists, and radical nationalists, **Subhas Chandra Bose** defeated **Pattabhi Sitaramayya** by **1,580 votes to 1,377**. Gandhi immediately issued a historic statement declaring that *"Pattabhi’s defeat is more mine than his,"* transforming a policy disagreement into a plebiscite on Gandhian moral authority; twelve out of fifteen members of the Congress Working Committee (CWC)—along with Jawaharlal Nehru, who issued a separate statement—resigned, leaving Bose with only Sarat Chandra Bose remaining on the executive.
*   **The 1939 Tripuri Crisis and the Pant Resolution**: Convening in **March 1939** at **Tripuri** (near Jabalpur in the Central Provinces), a gravely ill **Subhas Chandra Bose** proposed in his presidential address that the Congress issue a six-month ultimatum to the British Government demanding complete national independence, failing which a nationwide mass Civil Disobedience Movement should be launched to capitalize on Britain’s European war vulnerability. Gandhi and the Right Wing rejected the ultimatum as premature and adventurist, arguing that organizational indiscipline, communal tension, and agrarian violence precluded an immediate non-violent mass struggle, and that no mass movement could succeed without Gandhi’s leadership. To re-establish the authority of the Old Guard, **Govind Ballabh Pant** moved the historic **Pant Resolution** before the Subjects Committee and the open session. The **Pant Resolution** expressed unconditional confidence in the past policies of the Working Committee, declared that it was imperative for Mahatma Gandhi alone to lead the Congress and the country in the coming crisis, and explicitly directed President Bose to nominate the new Working Committee *"in accordance with the wishes of Gandhiji."* The Congress Socialist Party (led by Jayaprakash Narayan and Acharya Narendra Deva) and the Communists, unwilling to split the national movement or forfeit Gandhi's mass indispensability, abstained or voted for the resolution after minor amendments, ensuring its passage.
*   **Resignation and Formation of the Forward Bloc**: A constitutional impasse ensued after Tripuri: Gandhi declined to dictate the names of the Working Committee to Bose, insisting that Bose either form his own homogeneous left-wing cabinet or step down, while Bose could not constitutionally appoint a CWC without Gandhi's explicit endorsement under the **Pant Resolution**. At the Calcutta AICC session in **April 1939**, following failed mediation by Nehru, **Subhas Chandra Bose** resigned the presidency and was replaced by **Rajendra Prasad**. Immediately thereafter, on **May 3, 1939** at Makur (Unnao, UP), Bose announced the formation of the **Forward Bloc** as a radical left-wing vanguard party functioning within the Congress fold to rally all anti-imperialist, socialist, and peasant-worker forces against constitutional compromise. When the AICC in **June 1939** passed two centralizing resolutions—prohibiting Congressmen from launching Satyagraha without the prior sanction of Provincial Congress Committees and subordinating PCCs to the ministries—Bose organized an all-India protest day on **July 9, 1939**. For this breach of organizational discipline, the CWC in **August 1939** removed Bose from the presidency of the Bengal Provincial Congress Committee and debarred him from holding any elective Congress office for three years.

## II. The Second World War Fiscal-Constitutional Crisis, the Quit India Insurrection, and Wartime Famine Mechanics (1939–1944)

### 4. Imperial War Mobilization, the Lahore Resolution, and Constitutional Deadlocks (1939–1942)

The **World War II outbreak** on **September 1, 1939**, immediately exposed the juridical hollowness of Provincial Autonomy. On **September 3, 1939**, without consulting the Central Legislative Assembly, provincial legislatures, or Indian political leaders, Viceroy Lord Linlithgow unilaterally proclaimed India a belligerent state alongside Great Britain against Nazi Germany, simultaneously pushing through the **Government of India (Amendment) Act 1939** in Whitehall and promulgating the **Defence of India Ordinance (1939)** to strip provincial ministries of their emergency administrative powers. This unilateral imperial conscription triggered a three-year constitutional stalemate (1939–1942), during which Whitehall systematically deployed Muslim League separatism and Princely treaties as counterweights to reject the Congress demand for an immediate National Government, culminating in the territorial separatism of the **Lahore Resolution (March 1940)** and the secessionist "local option" blueprint of the **Cripps Mission (March 1942)**.

#### Trajectory of the Wartime Constitutional Impasse (1939–1942)
*   **World War II Outbreak and the October 1939 Resignation of Congress Ministries**: Meeting at Wardha on **September 10–14, 1939**, the Congress Working Committee—guided by Jawaharlal Nehru’s internationalist hostility to Fascism and Nazism—adopted a resolution condemning Nazi aggression against Poland, but declared that a subject India could not fight a war for democratic freedom while that very freedom was denied to her. The Congress demanded that Britain clarify its imperial war aims regarding Democracy and Imperialism, recognize India’s right to frame her own constitution through a Constituent Assembly after the war, and immediately establish a genuinely responsible National Government at the Centre. On **October 17, 1939**, Viceroy Linlithgow rejected these demands, offering only a consultative group of Indian politicians and princes while invoking minority disagreements to block constitutional transfer. Refusing to become complicit in an imperialist war machine or to enforce the repressive Defence of India Rules against their own people, the CWC ordered the **October 1939 resignation of Congress ministries** across all eight Congress-ruled provinces during **October–November 1939** (leading to the imposition of Governors' rule under Section 93 in seven provinces, while Syed Muhammad Saadulla formed a coalition ministry in Assam). Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the Muslim League celebrated the exit of the Congress governments by observing **December 22, 1939**, as a **"Day of Deliverance"** (supported by B.R. Ambedkar and E.V. Ramasamy 'Periyar').
*   **The Muslim League’s Lahore Resolution (March 1940)**: Building upon ideological precursors—Muhammad Iqbal’s 1930 Allahabad presidential address, Choudhry Rahmat Ali’s 1933 Cambridge pamphlet *Now or Never* (coining the acronym **PAKSTAN**), and Sikandar Hayat Khan’s loose federal zonal scheme—the All-India Muslim League convened its 27th annual session at Minto Park in Lahore on **March 22–24, 1940**. In his presidential address, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** repudiated the status of Muslims as a mere numerical minority requiring constitutional safeguards, propounding instead the **Two-Nation Theory**: that Hindus and Muslims belonged to two distinct religious philosophies, social customs, and literary traditions constituting two separate nations incapable of evolving a common nationality under a unitary parliamentary democracy. Moved by Bengal Premier **A.K. Fazlul Huq** on **March 23, 1940**, and adopted on **March 24**, the **Lahore Resolution** (which did not explicitly contain the word "Pakistan") resolved that no constitutional plan would be workable or acceptable to Muslims unless geographically contiguous units were demarcated into regions—with necessary territorial readjustments—such that the areas in which Muslims were numerically in a majority, as in the **North-Western and Eastern zones of India**, were grouped to constitute **"Independent States"** in which the constituent units should be **"autonomous and sovereign."**
*   **Linlithgow’s August Offer (1940)**: Following the collapse of France, the Battle of Britain, and Winston Churchill’s assumption of the British premiership in May 1940, the Congress at its Ramgarh Session (**March 1940**, presided over by Maulana Azad) and the Poona CWC meeting (**July 1940**, where the CWC temporarily broke with Gandhi on absolute non-violence in external defense to offer conditional war cooperation if a provisional National Government was formed) sought a breakthrough. On **August 8, 1940**, from Simla, Viceroy Linlithgow issued the **August Offer (1940)**, which stipulated:
    1.  The explicit recognition of **Dominion Status** as the objective for India after the conclusion of the war;
    2.  The immediate expansion of the **Viceroy’s Executive Council** to include a majority of nominated Indian members drawn from major political parties, alongside the creation of an advisory **War Advisory Council**;
    3.  The post-war establishment of a representative constitution-making body (**Constituent Assembly**) in which the framing of the new constitution would be *"primarily the responsibility of Indians themselves"* (contrasted with the 1919/1935 British parliamentary exclusivity), subject to the fulfillment of British obligations regarding defense, minority rights, Princely States' treaties, and All-India Services; and
    4.  A sweeping **Minority Veto Clause**, guaranteeing that British power would not be transferred to any system of government whose authority was directly denied by *"large and powerful elements in India’s national life."*
    The Congress rejected the **August Offer** outright—Jawaharlal Nehru declaring that the Dominion Status concept was *"dead as a door-nail"* and Gandhi noting that it widened the gulf between nationalist India and British rulers—while the Muslim League welcomed the minority veto but rejected the offer because it failed to concede explicit partition and parity on the Executive Council.
*   **Individual Satyagraha (1940–41: Vinoba Bhave, Nehru, Brahma Dutt)**: To protest the arbitrary dragging of India into the war and the stifling of civil liberties under the Defence of India Rules, while deliberately avoiding a disruptive mass upheaval that might embarrass Britain during the Nazi blitz, Mahatma Gandhi launched the limited **Individual Satyagraha (1940–41)** in **October 1940**. The campaign centered on the fundamental democratic right of free speech: selected Satyagrahis publicly delivered an anti-war declaration (*"It is wrong to help the British war effort with men or money. The only worthy effort is to resist all war with non-violent resistance"*) after giving prior notice to the District Magistrate. If the police did not arrest the speaker, the Satyagrahi marched toward Delhi to repeat the anti-war pledge across villages, earning the movement the popular designation of the ***Delhi Chalo* Movement**. On **October 17, 1940**, at Paunar Ashram (near Wardha), **Acharya Vinoba Bhave** inaugurated the campaign as the **First Satyagrahi**; **Jawaharlal Nehru** was nominated as the **Second Satyagrahi** (arrested on October 31 at Cheoki before speaking), and **Brahma Dutt** (an inmate of Gandhi's Sevagram Ashram) served as the **Third Satyagrahi**. Launched in two distinct phases (October–December 1940 for top leadership/legislators, and January 1941 onward for rank-and-file local Congress committee members), over 25,000 Satyagrahis were imprisoned before the British released the leaders in **December 1941** in the wake of Japan's entry into the war.
*   **The Cripps Mission (March 1942: "Local Option" Provincial Secession Clause)**: The geopolitical crisis of December 1941—marked by the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, the rapid Japanese conquest of Malaya, Singapore (**February 15, 1942**), and Rangoon (**March 8, 1942**), bringing Axis forces to the borders of Assam and the Bay of Bengal—combined with intense diplomatic pressure from US President Franklin D. Roosevelt and Chinese Generalissimo Chiang Kai-shek (who visited India in February 1942), compelled Churchill’s War Cabinet to dispatch **Sir Stafford Cripps** (Lord Privy Seal, Leader of the House of Commons, and a left-wing Labour MP sympathetic to Nehru) to India on **March 22, 1942**. The **Draft Declaration** of the **Cripps Mission (March 1942)** proposed:
    1.  The post-war creation of an **Indian Union** enjoying full **Dominion Status**, carrying the explicit statutory right to secede from the British Commonwealth and to enter into international treaties;
    2.  The immediate post-war convening of a **Constituent Assembly**, whose members would be partly elected by the lower houses of the provincial legislatures through proportional representation and partly nominated by the rulers of the Princely States;
    3.  **The "Local Option" Provincial Secession Clause**: Any British Indian province that was not prepared to accept the new constitution would have the right to retain its existing constitutional position, with the British Government prepared to recognize a separate new constitution and accord equal Dominion Status to such non-acceding provinces; Similarly, non-acceding Princely States could renegotiate separate treaties with the Crown; and
    4.  During the war, the British Government would retain absolute control and direction of the **Defense of India**, with the Commander-in-Chief’s powers and the Viceroy's veto remaining totally unaltered.
    Negotiations collapsed in **April 1942** when Viceroy Linlithgow and Commander-in-Chief General Archibald Wavell—backed by Churchill—torpedoed Cripps’s initial informal assurances to Maulana Azad and Nehru that the expanded Executive Council would function by convention like a cabinet with an Indian Defense Member holding substantive authority. The Congress rejected the **Cripps Mission** because it refused immediate responsible government at the Centre, retained autocratic princely nomination rather than democratic representation for the states' 90 million people, and institutionalized the balkanization of India through the **"local option" provincial secession clause**—prompting Gandhi to characterize the offer as a **"post-dated cheque"** (to which a journalist added *"on a crashing bank"*). The Muslim League simultaneously rejected the plan because it prescribed a single Constituent Assembly with decision-making by simple majority rather than an unambiguous, immediate concession of a separate Pakistan.

### 5. The August 1942 Quit India Insurrection: Underground Networks and Parallel Subaltern Sovereignties

The failure of the Cripps Mission in April 1942 coincided with acute socio-economic and military trauma across British India. The disorderly British military retreat from Burma and Malaya exposed racial discrimination on the refugee trails (where "White Roads" with motorized transport and food rations were reserved for Europeans while hundreds of thousands of Indian evacuees trek-walked along "Black Roads" through malarial jungles), shattering the myth of British martial invincibility. Simultaneously, rampant wartime inflation, chronic shortages of rice, salt, and cloth, and scorched-earth confiscations of boats and bicycles in eastern India convinced Mahatma Gandhi that a demoralized British administration would collapse before a Japanese invasion just as it had in Malaya and Burma, turning India into a passive battlefield between two imperialisms. Formulating his demand at Wardha on **July 14, 1942**, Gandhi asserted that only an immediate British withdrawal could awaken the moral energy required for free Indians to resist Japanese fascism.

#### The Gowalia Tank Resolution, Underground Resistance, and Parallel Governments
*   **The Gowalia Tank Resolution ("Do or Die") and Operation Zero Hour**: On **August 8, 1942**, the All-India Congress Committee convened at **Gowalia Tank Maidan** (now August Kranti Maidan) in Bombay and ratified the **Quit India Resolution** (moved by Jawaharlal Nehru and seconded by Sardar Patel), demanding the immediate termination of British rule in India as an urgent necessity both for India and for the success of the United Nations against Fascism. In a fiery 70-minute speech, Mahatma Gandhi delivered his immortal exhortation: ***"Here is a mantra, a short one, that I give you. You may imprint it on your hearts and let every breath of yours give expression to it. The mantra is: 'Do or Die' (Karo ya Maro). We shall either free India or die in the attempt; we shall not live to see the perpetuation of our slavery."*** Anticipating immediate arrest before the movement could be formally launched, Gandhi issued detailed twelve-point instructions to specific social strata:
    *   **Government Servants**: Do not resign immediately, but openly declare your allegiance to the Congress and refuse to obey orders against the national movement.
    *   **Soldiers**: Do not leave the army, but strictly refuse to fire upon fellow Indians.
    *   **Students**: Leave schools and colleges only if confident of remaining firm until independence is achieved.
    *   **Peasants**: If the Zamindar is anti-government, pay mutually agreed rent; if the Zamindar is pro-government, refuse to pay any rent or land revenue altogether (*"the land belongs to those who work on it"*).
    *   **Princely State Subjects**: Support the ruler only if he is anti-British, and declare yourselves an integral part of the Indian nation.
    In the pre-dawn hours of **August 9, 1942** (**Operation Zero Hour**), Viceroy Linlithgow struck with pre-planned preemptive coercion: Gandhi, Kasturba, Mahadev Desai, and Sarojini Naidu were interned at the Aga Khan Palace in Poona, while Nehru, Patel, Azad, and the entire Working Committee were imprisoned in Ahmednagar Fort, and the AICC and all PCCs were declared unlawful associations under the Criminal Law Amendment Act.
*   **Decapitated Mass Insurrection and Sabotage**: Deprived of central Gandhian restraint, the movement erupted into the most violent anti-colonial uprising since 1857 (prompting Linlithgow to cable Churchill on August 31, 1942, that he was confronting *"by far the most serious rebellion since that of 1857, the gravity and extent of which we have so far concealed from the world for reasons of military security"*). Beginning with urban strikes in Bombay, Delhi, Kanpur, Patna (where seven students were shot dead hoisting the flag on the Secretariat on August 11), Jamshedpur (where Tata Steel workers struck for 13 days), and Ahmedabad (where millworkers sustained a three-and-a-half-month strike backed by nationalist millowners), the rebellion shifted by mid-August to the countryside of Eastern UP, Bihar, Midnapore (Bengal), Maharashtra, Odisha, and Karnataka. Crowds systematically targeted physical symbols of colonial state sovereignty: over 250 railway stations were attacked, 550 post offices were demolished or looted, 200 police stations were burned, and thousands of miles of telegraph and telephone lines were severed, temporarily cutting off British military communications between Calcutta and the Eastern Front. The Raj retaliated with 57 army battalions, machine-gunning crowds from Royal Air Force aircraft at Patna, Bhagalpur, Nadia, Talcher, and Tamluk, imposing collective punitive fines exceeding Rs 90 lakhs on recalcitrant villages, burning homesteads, and arresting over 91,000 people by the end of 1943.
*   **Clandestine Underground Networks**: With the senior leadership incarcerated, a younger generation of Congress Socialists, Gandhian конструкtivists, and Forward Bloc activists went underground to provide strategic coordination, finance, explosives, and counter-propaganda through a clandestine Central Directorate:
    *   **Aruna Asaf Ali** (who famously hoisted the national tricolour at Gowalia Tank Maidan on August 9 amidst police tear-gas shelling), **Sucheta Kripalani**, **Ram Manohar Lohia**, **Achyut Patwardhan**, **Sadiq Ali**, **Chhotubhai Puranik**, and **Biju Patnaik** (an ace pilot who ferried underground leaders and smuggled subversive literature across the country) maintained an all-India communication link with progressive industrialists (such as Sumati Morarjee) and sympathetic officials.
    *   **Usha Mehta’s Congress Radio**: Beginning on **August 14, 1942**, a 22-year-old Gandhian student, **Usha Mehta**, along with **Babubhai Khakar**, **Vithalbhai Jhaveri**, and radio technician **Nariman Printer**, operated the clandestine **Congress Radio** broadcasting on the **42.34-meter** wavelength from constantly shifting locations across Bombay. Before its interception by police triangulation on **November 12, 1942**, **Congress Radio** broadcast daily news bulletins uncensored by the Defence of India Rules, speeches by **Ram Manohar Lohia**, and eyewitness accounts of British atrocities in Bihar, Ballia, and Midnapore to the entire nation.
    *   **Jayaprakash Narayan’s Azad Dasta**: On the Diwali night of **November 9, 1942**, Congress Socialist leader **Jayaprakash Narayan** (along with Ramanand Mishra, Suraj Narayan Singh, Yogendra Shukla, and Shaligram Singh) scaled the walls of **Hazaribagh Central Jail** and escaped into the Nepal Terai jungles (Rajvilas forest). There, aided by Nityanand Singh and Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia (who ran the Radio and Publicity department), Jayaprakash Narayan organized the **Azad Dasta**, an all-India guerrilla militia that trained youth cohorts in sabotage, demolition of communications, and armed self-defense to paralyze the colonial administration across Bihar and Eastern UP.
*   **Parallel Governments (*Prati Sarkars*)**: In several districts where colonial police and revenue administration were physically swept away, insurgent populations established autonomous **parallel governments** that exercised sovereign judicial, fiscal, and welfare authority:

The **Muslim League** condemned the movement as a Congress attempt to coerce the British into handing over power to a Hindu majority without first settling Pakistan; the **Hindu Mahasabha** (under V.D. Savarkar and Shyama Prasad Mookerjee, who remained a minister in Bengal's Fazlul Huq cabinet during the brutal suppression of Midnapore) boycotted the struggle; the **Princely Rulers** and large landlords remained loyal; and the **Communist Party of India (CPI)**—which was legalized in **July 1942** after adopting the **"People’s War"** line following Nazi Germany’s invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941—opposed the August uprising as disruptive to the global anti-fascist war effort, although many rank-and-file communists participated locally. When Viceroy Linlithgow attempted to blame Gandhi and the Congress for the violent sabotage of 1942, Gandhi undertook a **21-day fast at the Aga Khan Palace (February 10 – March 3, 1943)** to protest the leonine violence of the colonial state. Although Churchill and Linlithgow coldly prepared for Gandhi's death (even drafting his obituary), Gandhi survived the ordeal, turning the fast into a massive moral rallying point that prompted three Indian members of the Viceroy’s Executive Council—**M.S. Aney**, **N.R. Sarkar**, and **H.P. Mody**—to resign in protest against the government's refusal to release him unconditionally (which occurred only on **May 6, 1944**, on medical grounds under Viceroy Wavell).

### 6. Political Economy of the 1943 Great Bengal Famine: Sterling Balances, Denial Policies, and Entitlement Collapse

While the underground networks of the Quit India Movement battled the colonial police apparatus, the eastern province of Bengal became the theater of the **1943 Great Bengal Famine**, a catastrophic man-made subsistence crisis that claimed between **3 million and 3.5 million lives** between 1943 and 1944 through starvation and subsequent post-famine epidemics of malaria, cholera, and smallpox. Far from being a Malthusian crisis of absolute ecological food scarcity (**Food Availability Decline / FAD**), the **1943 Great Bengal Famine** was a textbook **Exchange Entitlement Collapse** (as subsequently theorized by economist Amartya Sen) engineered by the monetary, fiscal, and military-logistical imperatives of British imperial war financing.

#### Structural Mechanics of Wartime Inflation, Denial Policies, and the Woodhead Commission
*   **Wartime Sterling Balance Inflation**: To finance the sprawling Allied war effort across North Africa, West Asia, and the South-East Asian theater without raising domestic Indian taxes to politically explosive levels or floating rupee loans that a hostile nationalist public would boycott, Whitehall exploited a colonial monetary loophole in the **Reserve Bank of India Act (1934)**. Under the **1940 Financial Settlement** between the British Government and the Government of India, Britain paid for Indian goods, textiles, munitions, railway usage, and overseas military operations by crediting **Sterling securities (Treasury Bills)** into a frozen account held by the Reserve Bank of India at the Bank of England in London. Against these blocked **Sterling Balances** (which ballooned to over **£1,300 million / Rs 1,733 crore** by 1945 and could not be spent to import consumer goods or capital machinery during the war), the Reserve Bank of India was statutorily authorized to print an equivalent volume of paper rupees inside India. Consequently, total currency in circulation exploded more than six-fold—from **Rs 179 crore in August 1939 to over Rs 1,100 crore by 1944–45**. This unbacked monetary expansion acted as a regressive, unlegislated war tax imposed directly on the Indian poor through hyperinflation, transferring real resources from rural subsistence consumers to the British military-industrial complex.
*   **The Boat and Rice Denial Policies (1942)**: In **March–April 1942**, following the fall of Rangoon—which abruptly severed annual Burmese rice imports (~1.5 to 2 million tons to India) and triggered an influx of half a million refugees into eastern India—the British War Cabinet and Bengal Governor **Sir John Herbert** implemented two devastating scorched-earth measures across coastal Bengal (specifically **Midnapore**, **Bakarganj/Barisal**, **Khulna**, and **24-Parganas**) to deny food and transport to a feared Japanese amphibious invasion:
    1.  **The Rice Denial Policy**: State agents and favored commercial monopolies (such as M.A. Ispahani & Co. appointed by the provincial government) were dispatched with unlimited paper currency to compulsorily buy up and remove all stocks of rice and paddy estimated to be in excess of local requirements (over 40,000 tons officially removed), triggering immediate psychological panic and speculative hoarding across the province.
    2.  **The Boat Denial Policy**: Effective **May 1942**, military authorities ordered the confiscation, sinking, or removal of every country boat capable of carrying more than ten passengers across the vast riverine delta south of a line running from Chandpur to Khulna and Midnapore. Out of 66,563 registered boats in the denial zone, over **46,000 large boats** were seized, destroyed, or left to rot at military reception stations. Because riverine boats formed the sole artery of rural commerce in deltaic Bengal, the **Boat Denial Policy** simultaneously destroyed the livelihood of hundreds of thousands of fishermen, paralyzed the movement of potters and artisans, prevented peasants from cultivating deep-water island (*char*) paddy lands, and severed the internal transport network required to move surplus grain into deficit districts.
*   **Provincial Barriers, Priority Classes, and Churchillian Callousness**: Compounding the **October 16, 1942 Midnapore Cyclone** (which killed 14,500 people, wiped out the winter *Aman* rice crop across coastal Bengal, and spread *Helminthosporium oryzae* brown-spot fungal disease), the Government of India imposed inter-provincial trade barriers in 1942, preventing surplus wheat and rice from Punjab, Orissa, and Bihar from flowing into Bengal. Simultaneously, to prevent unrest in the vital war-production hub of Calcutta, the British administration and the Bengal Chamber of Commerce instituted the **"Priority Classes" rationing scheme**, providing heavily subsidized foodgrains extracted from the countryside to over one million munitions workers, railwaymen, civil servants, and military personnel in Greater Calcutta. Deprived of both market purchasing power and state protection, rural landless agricultural laborers, *Bargadars* (sharecroppers), fishermen, Paddy-husking women, and rural weavers saw the price of rice skyrocket from **Rs 4–5 per maund in 1941 to Rs 35–50 per maund by mid-1943**, annihilating their exchange entitlements. Millions of starving rural families trekked into Calcutta begging merely for *phyan* (the starchy water drained from boiled rice) and died on the pavements outside well-stocked European restaurants and military depots. Throughout 1943, the British authorities refused to formally declare a state of famine under the **Bengal Famine Code** (as doing so would have created a statutory obligation to provide universal relief rations), while Winston Churchill’s War Cabinet repeatedly rejected urgent appeals from Secretary of State Leo Amery and Viceroy Wavell (who took office in October 1943 and deployed the Indian Army to distribute rations) to divert Allied shipping to carry Australian and Canadian wheat to India, with Churchill infamously blaming Indians for *"breeding like rabbits."*
*   **The Woodhead Commission (1944–1945) and Cultural Counter-Memory**: In **1944**, under intense public outcry, the Government of India appointed the **Famine Inquiry Commission** chaired by **Sir John Woodhead** (former acting Governor of Bengal, alongside S.V. Ramamurty, Manilal B. Nanavati, M. Afzal Husain, and nutritionist W.R. Aykroyd). Submitting its report in **1945**, the **Woodhead Commission** officially estimated the death toll at **1.5 million** (subsequently proven by anthropologist Tarakchandra Das's 1949 Calcutta University survey and modern demographic reconstructions to be at least **3 million**). While avoiding a direct indictment of Whitehall’s Sterling Balance war financing, the **Woodhead Commission** exposed the catastrophic administrative failure of the Central and Bengal governments, citing the delay in introducing statutory rationing, unchecked profiteering and hoarding in early 1943, the failure to control prices, and the breakdown of grain procurement, declaring that *"the enunciation of the principle that nobody should be allowed to die of starvation was delayed too long."* As analyzed in Part 13, this structural atrocity catalyzed a historic rupture in Indian visual and performing arts, generating the visceral socialist-realist famine sketches and linocuts of **Chittaprosad** (*Hungry Bengal*, 1943, confiscated and burned by the British), **Zainul Abedin**, and **Somnath Hore**, alongside **Bijon Bhattacharya’s** landmark IPTA play ***Nabanna*** (1944).

## III. Transnational Armed Counter-Sovereignty and the Post-War Collapse of the Colonial Coercive Apparatus (1941–1946)

### 7. Subhas Chandra Bose, the Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj), and the Imphal-Kohima Campaign (1941–1945)

While the domestic nationalist leadership remained incarcerated following the August 1942 crackdown, **Subhas Chandra Bose** opened an unprecedented transnational military front aimed at shattering the single most vital pillar of British rule in India: the loyalty of the British Indian Army. #### Genesis, Institutional Sovereignty, and Military Operations of the INA
*   **The Great Escape and the Berlin Indian Legion (1941–1943)**: Released from Presidency Jail in December 1940 following a hunger strike, **Subhas Chandra Bose** staged a legendary escape from massive police surveillance at his Elgin Road home in Calcutta on the night of **January 16–17, 1941**. Driven by his nephew Sisir Kumar Bose to Gomoh station, Bose traveled disguised as a deaf-and-dumb Pathan insurance agent named **Mohammad Ziauddin** to Peshawar, crossed the tribal frontier into Kabul with the assistance of Abad Khan and Bhagat Ram Talwar (*Rahmat Khan*), and—after Soviet officials remained non-committal—secured an Italian passport under the alias **Orlando Mazzotta** to travel via Moscow to Berlin in **April 1941**. In Germany, Bose established the **Free India Centre**, inaugurated **Azad Hind Radio** (broadcasting in English, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Gujarati, Pashto, and Persian), instituted **"Jai Hind"** as the national greeting and Rabindranath Tagore’s *Jana Gana Mana* as the national anthem, and recruited roughly 3,000 Indian POWs captured by Erwin Rommel’s Afrika Korps at Tobruk into the **Indian Legion** (*Indische Legion*), whose soldiers swore allegiance exclusively to Bose for the liberation of India rather than to German theaters. Recognizing following the Nazi invasion of the USSR that the decisive theater for Indian liberation lay on India's eastern border, Bose embarked on **February 8, 1943**, from Kiel aboard the German submarine *U-180*, transferring across treacherous waters off Madagascar to the Japanese submarine *I-29*—the only civilian exchange between two hostile navies' submarines in World War II—and arrived in Tokyo in **May 1943** to secure unequivocal backing from Japanese Prime Minister Hideki Tojo.
*   **Mohan Singh, Rash Behari Bose, and the First INA (1941–1942)**: The original seed of the **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)** was planted in the Malayan jungles in **December 1941**, when **Captain Mohan Singh** of the 1/14th Punjab Regiment, cut off near Jitra during the British retreat, entered into negotiations with **Major Iwaichi Fujiwara** (head of the Japanese intelligence agency *F-Kikan*) and Giani Pritam Singh of the Bangkok Indian Independence League. Following the British surrender at **Singapore on February 15, 1942**, Lt. Col. Hunt formally handed over **45,000 Indian POWs** to the Japanese at **Farrer Park** on February 17; Fujiwara immediately transferred them to the command of **Mohan Singh**, who appealed to them to sever their mercenary allegiance to the British Crown and form an **Indian National Army**. Simultaneously, the veteran Ghadar and Delhi Conspiracy revolutionary **Rash Behari Bose** (living in exile in Japan since 1915) convened conferences of Southeast Asian Indian associations at **Tokyo (March 1942)** and **Bangkok (June 1942)**, consolidating the **Indian Independence League (IIL)** as the overarching political authority, with a Council of Action chaired by Rash Behari Bose and Mohan Singh as Commander-in-Chief of the military wing (formally inaugurated on **September 1, 1942**, with 16,300 armed soldiers out of 40,000 volunteers). However, profound friction erupted by **December 1942** when **Mohan Singh**—suspecting that the Japanese military command (replacing Fujiwara with the rigid Colonel Hideo Iwakuro) viewed the INA merely as a fifth-column propaganda and labor adjunct rather than an equal allied army, and objecting to Japanese interference with Indian POWs—refused to deploy troops to Burma without explicit Japanese diplomatic recognition of Indian sovereignty. On **December 29, 1942**, Mohan Singh was arrested by the Japanese military police (*Kempeitai*) after issuing secret orders dissolving the first INA, leaving **Rash Behari Bose** to preserve the skeleton organization until the arrival of Subhas Chandra Bose.
*   **The Provisional Government of Free India at Singapore (1943), the Andaman Handover, and the Rani of Jhansi Regiment**: Arriving in Singapore on **July 2, 1943**, **Subhas Chandra Bose** formally assumed the presidency of the Indian Independence League and supreme command of the reorganized **Azad Hind Fauj** from Rash Behari Bose at the Cathay Building on **July 4, 1943**. Mobilizing both military POWs and civilian plantation laborers (predominantly Tamil rubber tappers in Malaya and Burmese traders) through electrifying rallies (*"Chalo Delhi!"* and ***"Give me blood, and I shall give you freedom!"***), Bose expanded the INA to nearly **43,000–50,000 combat and auxiliary personnel** financed entirely by voluntary taxes and gold donations levied on the Indian diaspora through the **Azad Hind Bank** (established in Rangoon in 1944).
    *   **Provisional Government of Free India (October 21, 1943)**: To ensure that the INA entered Indian territory not as a mercenary proxy of Imperial Japan but as the sovereign armed force of an independent state under international law, Bose proclaimed the **Provisional Government of Free India** (*Arzi Hukumat-e-Azad Hind*) at Singapore on **October 21, 1943**. Serving as Head of State, Prime Minister, and Minister for War and Foreign Affairs, Bose’s cabinet won immediate diplomatic recognition from nine Axis and Axis-aligned powers (Japan, Germany, Italy, Croatia, Burma, Thailand, Nationalist China under Wang Jingwei, the Philippines, and Manchukuo) and formally declared war on Great Britain and the United States.
    *   **Andaman and Nicobar Handover (November 1943)**: Attending the Greater East Asia Conference in Tokyo in **November 1943** as an observer head of state, Bose secured Prime Minister Tojo’s agreement on **November 6, 1943**, to transfer the Japanese-occupied **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**—the historic site of the British Cellular Jail (*Kala Pani*)—to the jurisdiction of the Provisional Government. Bose visited Port Blair on **December 29–31, 1943**, hoisted the national tricolour, renamed the islands **Shaheed** (Martyr) and **Swaraj** (Self-Rule) respectively, and appointed **Major General A.D. Loganathan** as Chief Commissioner of the Civil Administration, although actual operational and naval security control remained tightly held by the Japanese Admiralty.
    *   **Secular Integration and the Rani of Jhansi Regiment**: Inside the INA, Bose systematically dismantled the British Indian Army’s divisive "Martial Races" caste and communal regimental structure: Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, and Christians ate from common kitchens, lived in integrated barracks, and spoke Hindustani in the Roman script. He organized the army into three guerrilla brigades named after Congress leaders—the **Gandhi Brigade** (under Col. Inayat Kiani), the **Azad Brigade** (under Col. Gulzara Singh), and the **Nehru Brigade** (under Col. G.S. Dhillon)—alongside the elite **Subhas Brigade** (1st Guerrilla Regiment under **Col. Shah Nawaz Khan**) and the *Bahadur Group* for front-line intelligence and subversion. In a revolutionary strike against patriarchal military conventions, Bose established the **Rani of Jhansi Regiment** in **July–October 1943**, an all-women combat infantry and nursing regiment comprising over 1,000 young diaspora women (largely Tamil and Malayali teenagers who had never seen India) commanded by a Madras medical doctor, **Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan** (later Lakshmi Sahgal), who underwent full rifle, bayonet, and jungle-warfare training in Singapore and Burma.
*   **The 1944 Imphal-Kohima Campaign and Strategic Collapse**: Moving the Provisional Government’s headquarters to **Rangoon** in **January 1944** (from where Bose broadcast via Azad Hind Radio on **July 6, 1944**, addressing Mahatma Gandhi for the first time as the ***"Father of our Nation"*** and seeking his blessings for India's "last war of independence"), Bose committed the INA to **Operation U-Go**—the joint Indo-Japanese offensive launched in **March 1944** under Japanese Lt. Gen. Renya Mutaguchi to capture the strategic British logistics bases of **Imphal** (Manipur) and **Kohima** (Nagaland) and break out into the Assam plains. While elements of the Subhas Brigade fought in the Arakan sector and captured **Mowdok** inside Chittagong, the main INA force (~8,000–10,000 troops of the 1st Division under Maj. Gen. M.Z. Kiani, joined by the **Gandhi**, **Azad**, and **Subhas Brigades**) crossed the Chindwin River alongside the Japanese 15th Army. On **April 14, 1944**, **Colonel Shaukat Ali Malik** of the *Bahadur Group* captured **Moirang** (45 km south of Imphal in Manipur), hoisting the tricolour on the Indian mainland and establishing an INA operational administration for three months with local Manipuris (*Nikhil Manipuri Mahasabha*) supplying rations. However, the **1944 Imphal-Kohima campaign** ended in military disaster: General William Slim’s British 14th Army held the Imphal-Kohima perimeter via massive Anglo-American aerial resupply (*The Hump*), while the Japanese denied the INA heavy artillery, tanks, transport, and air cover, and the early arrival of torrential May monsoons washed away jungle supply tracks. Ravaged by starvation (surviving on boiled jungle grass), malaria, and dysentery, the Indo-Japanese forces were ordered to retreat in **July 1944**; over 4,000 INA soldiers perished during the grueling retreat across the Chindwin and Irrawaddy rivers. Following the British reconquest of Rangoon in **May 1945** (during which Bose personally walked alongside the retreating **Rani of Jhansi Regiment** women across bombed rail lines to Bangkok) and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki leading to Japan's surrender on **August 15, 1945**, **Subhas Chandra Bose** died on **August 18, 1945**, from severe third-degree burns sustained when an overloaded Japanese Mitsubishi Ki-21 bomber crashed at **Taihoku (Taipei) Airport** in Formosa (Taiwan) en route to Manchuria/Soviet Union to continue the struggle.

### 8. Constitutional Reconciliation Attempts: Rajagopalachari Formula, Desai-Liaquat Pact, and the 1945 Simla Conference

Between 1944 and mid-1945, as the Allied victory in Europe and Asia became assured and Mahatma Gandhi was released from the Aga Khan Palace on **May 6, 1944**, a series of three high-level constitutional negotiations—the **C. Rajagopalachari Formula (1944)**, the **Desai-Liaquat Pact (1945)**, and the **Wavell Plan and Simla Conference (June 1945)**—attempted to break the Congress-League deadlock and construct an interim coalition government at the Centre. Rather than resolving the impasse, these negotiations progressively elevated **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** to the status of sole constitutional arbiter of Muslim India, shifting the political baseline from minority safeguards to executive parity and territorial partition.

#### Structural Consequences of the Simla Breakdown
The collapse of the **Simla Conference (June 1945)** represented a watershed in the structural empowerment of the Muslim League. By refusing to constitute the Executive Council without the League—even though the Congress, the Punjab Unionists, and the minor parties were ready to proceed—Viceroy **Lord Wavell** handed **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** an explicit institutional veto over all future constitutional progress. Overnight, non-League Muslim formations—most notably **Khizr Hayat Khan Tiwana’s** cross-communal **Unionist Party** in Punjab, which had sustained the British war effort throughout 1939–45—were fatally undermined in the eyes of the Muslim electorate and bureaucracy, who concluded that the British Raj itself recognized Jinnah as the sole legitimate representative of Indian Muslims, setting the stage for the Muslim League’s sweeping victory in the winter elections of 1945–46.

### 9. Disintegration of the Imperial Sword Arm: The INA Red Fort Trials and the February 1946 Royal Indian Navy Mutiny

When Clement Attlee’s Labour Party swept to power in Great Britain in **July 1945** (replacing Churchill with Attlee and appointing Lord Pethick-Lawrence as Secretary of State for India), and Japan surrendered in August 1945, the colonial administration in New Delhi assumed it could resume gradualist constitutional bargaining from a position of military strength. Instead, between **November 1945 and February 1946**, the British Raj triggered its own terminal crisis by deciding to stage public courts-martial of captured **Indian National Army** officers inside the historic Red Fort in Delhi. Rather than intimidating the Indian populace or reinforcing military discipline, the **INA Red Fort Trials (Nov 1945)** ignited a volcanic, cross-communal anti-imperialist upsurge that penetrated the barracks of the Royal Indian Air Force, the British Indian Army, and the **Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (Feb 1946)**, shattering the coercive foundation upon which a tiny European cadre had ruled 400 million Indians for nearly two centuries.

#### The INA Red Fort Trials and the Three Urban Upsurges
*   **The INA Red Fort Trials (Nov 1945: Sahgal, Dhillon, Shah Nawaz Khan)**: Having categorized the ~23,000 captured INA personnel into **"Whites"** (those claimed to have joined only to escape back to British lines), **"Greys"** (those allegedly misled by propaganda), and **"Blacks"** (~7,000 hardened ideological nationalists slated for severe punishment), the British military authorities made the catastrophic blunder of placing the first trio of officers on public court-martial at the **Red Fort** in Delhi on **November 5, 1945**—the very seat from which the last Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar had been tried in 1858—and choosing one Hindu (**Col. Prem Kumar Sahgal**), one Sikh (**Col. Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon**), and one Muslim (**Maj. Gen. Shah Nawaz Khan**) for the dock. Charged under Section 121 of the Indian Penal Code with *"waging war against the King-Emperor"* alongside charges of murder and abetment of murder (for the execution of five deserters under the INA Act), the three officers instantly became living embodiments of non-sectarian Indian nationalism. At its Bombay AICC session in **September 1945**, the Congress adopted a sweeping commitment to defend the INA prisoners, establishing the **INA Relief and Enquiry Committee** and the **INA Defence Committee** featuring legal luminaries **Bhulabhai Desai**, **Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Kailash Nath Katju**, **Asaf Ali**, **Raghunandan Saran**, and **Jawaharlal Nehru** (who donned his barrister’s robes for the first time in 25 years). In a brilliant ten-hour summation before the court-martial, **Bhulabhai Desai** argued from international public law that the **Provisional Government of Free India** established at Singapore in October 1943 possessed diplomatic recognition, territory (Andaman and Nicobar), fiscal revenue, and an organized army; consequently, its soldiers were legitimate combatants of a sovereign belligerent power fighting a war of national liberation, in which instance allegiance to one's motherland superseded the coerced statutory allegiance to an alien King.
*   **Popular Upsurges and Auchinleck's Capitulation**: As the lift of wartime censorship flooded newspapers with accounts of the INA’s sacrifices and the Red Fort proceedings, an unprecedented pan-Indian agitation—observed via **INA Day (November 12, 1945)** and **INA Week (November 5–11)**—united students, workers, peasants, shopkeepers, film stars, and startlingly, Indian personnel in the British civil service and armed forces (who attended INA meetings in uniform and contributed to the defence fund). Two massive urban insurrections erupted in Calcutta: on **November 21–23, 1945**, when Forward Bloc, Communist, Congress, and Muslim League students marched together on Dalhousie Square carrying knotted Congress, League, and Red Flags, resulting in police firings that killed 33 and injured hundreds as tramworkers and taxi drivers paralyzed the city; and on **February 11–13, 1946**, protesting the seven-year rigorous imprisonment sentence imposed on INA **Captain Abdul Rashid** (defended by the Muslim League), when students and industrial workers battled British troops and Gurkha police across Calcutta, leaving 84 dead and over 300 wounded. Although the Red Fort court-martial convicted **Sahgal, Dhillon, and Shah Nawaz Khan** on **December 31, 1945**, sentencing them to **transportation for life**, dismissal, and forfeiture of arrears of pay, Commander-in-Chief **General Sir Claude Auchinleck**—warned by intelligence and provincial governors (such as Punjab Governor Bertrand Glancy and Army commanders) that executing or imprisoning the trio would trigger an immediate mutiny across the Indian Army—used his prerogative on **January 3, 1946**, to remit the sentence of transportation for life and release all three officers to tumultuous national celebration.

#### The Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (February 1946: HMIS Talwar Strike)
*   **Outbreak on *HMIS Talwar* and the Naval Central Strike Committee**: On **February 18, 1946**, the third and most menacing anti-imperialist upsurge struck directly at the armed forces when 1,100 Indian naval ratings (*sailors*) of the Royal Indian Navy’s signal training establishment, ***HMIS Talwar***, in Bombay went on a hunger strike and mutinied. The immediate triggers were chronic racial discrimination, abysmal food infested with stones and weevils, abusive racial slurs hurled by the British commanding officer (**Commander F.W. King**, who called the ratings *"sons of coolies and bitches"*), acute disparities between British and Indian pay and demobilization benefits, and the arrest of rating **B.C. Dutt** (a clandestine wireless operator who had painted **"Quit India"** and **"Jai Hind"** on the walls and dais of *HMIS Talwar* on Navy Day). By **February 19**, the ratings elected a **Naval Central Strike Committee (NCSC)** headed by a Muslim signalman, **M.S. Khan**, as President and a Sikh petty officer, **Madan Singh**, as Vice-President.
*   **Transformation into an Anti-Imperialist Insurrection**: Within 48 hours, the strike escalated into a full-scale armed naval rebellion spanning **78 ships**, **20 shore establishments (castles/barracks)**, and over **20,000 ratings** from **Karachi** (where ratings aboard ***HMIS Hindustan*** exchanged heavy artillery fire with British paratroopers and Baluch troops, leaving 8 ratings dead) to **Calcutta**, **Madras**, **Cochin**, **Visakhapatnam**, **Jamnagar**, **Andamans**, and even **Aden** and **Bahrain**. The mutinous ratings tore down the Union Jack, hoisted the flags of the **Congress, the Muslim League, and the Communist Party** tied together onto the masts of the warships, renamed the service the **"Indian National Navy,"** commandeered naval trucks to drive through Bombay chanting *"Jai Hind"* and *"Inquilab Zindabad,"* and formulated eight political and service demands:
    1.  Immediate and unconditional release of all **INA prisoners** and political detainees (including **B.C. Dutt**);
    2.  Immediate withdrawal of **Indian troops from Indonesia** and Egypt (where British commanders were using Indian soldiers to crush anti-colonial nationalist movements against Dutch and British rule);
    3.  Action against Commander King for racial insults;
    4.  Equal pay, allowances, and demobilization conditions for Indian and British naval personnel; and
    5.  Best-class Indian food.
*   **Working-Class Solidarity, Elite Containment, and Strategic Impact**: When British Admiral **John Henry Godfrey** (Flag Officer Commanding the RIN) went on the radio on **February 21** threatening to *"destroy the Navy"* with overwhelming land and air forces if the ratings did not surrender unconditionally, and British troops opened fire on ratings at **Castle Barracks** in Bombay, the NCSC trained the big naval guns of the docked ships in Bombay harbor onto the Taj Mahal Hotel and the Yacht Club and called for a civilian general strike. Defying the opposition of both Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and the local Muslim League leadership, the **Communist Party of India (CPI)** and the Congress Socialist leader **Aruna Asaf Ali** backed the ratings’ call: on **February 22, 1946**, over **300,000 Bombay textile millworkers, railwaymen, and students** downed tools, erected street barricades across Parel, Lalbaug, and Delisle Road, and fought pitched battles for 48 hours against British infantry and armored cars (commanded by Gen. Rob Lockhart), resulting in **228 to 250 civilian deaths** and over 1,000 injuries—the bloodiest urban confrontation of the 1945–46 post-war wave. Sympathetic strikes simultaneously spread to Royal Indian Air Force (RIAF) camps in Bombay, Poona, Calcutta, Ambala, and Karachi, and the Jabalpur Signal Corps. However, the senior leadership of both the Congress and the Muslim League viewed an armed military mutiny outside political control as a dangerous threat to future state discipline: Mahatma Gandhi criticized the ratings for mutinying without the guidance of a political leader and condemned the combination of Hindus and Muslims for violence as an *"unholy combination,"* while **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** (working with Purshotamdas Thakurdas and S.K. Patil) and **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** urgently advised the NCSC to lay down arms, promising that there would be no victimization. On the morning of **February 23, 1946**, the NCSC ordered the ships to lower the black surrender flag, issuing a final historic manifesto: ***"Our strike has been a historic event in the life of our nation. For the first time the blood of men in the Services and the people flowed together in a common cause... We are surrendering to India and not to Britain."*** On **February 19, 1946**—the very second day of the **RIN Mutiny**—Prime Minister Clement Attlee announced in the House of Commons the dispatch of the **Cabinet Mission** to negotiate the transfer of power, recognizing that the British Indian Armed Forces could no longer be relied upon to hold India by the sword.

## IV. The Endgame of Empire: Cabinet Mission Grouping, Communal Civil War, and the Statutory Transfer of Power (1946–1947)

### 10. The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946), Direct Action Day, and the Paralysis of the Interim Government

Prior to the arrival of the Cabinet Mission, the **Winter Elections of 1945–1946** to the Central and Provincial Legislatures (held between November 1945 and March 1946) demonstrated that Indian politics had polarized into two irreconcilable electoral monoliths. Campaigning on the heroism of the 1942 Quit India martyrs and the defence of the INA prisoners, the **Indian National Congress** swept the general (non-Muslim) constituencies: it captured **91.3% of the non-Muslim vote** and 57 out of 102 seats in the Central Assembly, while securing absolute majorities in eight provinces (**Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, Orissa, Central Provinces, Assam, and the Muslim-majority NWFP**) and forming a coalition with the Unionists and Akalis in Punjab. Conversely, campaigning almost exclusively on a single binary plebiscite—*Islam in danger* and the demand for **Pakistan**—and backed by Pir networks (*Sajjada Nashins*), defecting landlords in Punjab, and Islamic seminary students, the **All-India Muslim League** completely reversed its dismal 1937 performance: it won **all 30 reserved Muslim seats in the Central Assembly (86.6% of the central Muslim vote)** and **425 out of 492 Muslim seats (~87%)** across the provincial assemblies, forming ministries in **Bengal** (under **Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy**) and **Sindh**, and emerging as the single largest party in **Punjab** (73 of 86 Muslim seats, though kept out of office by Khizr Hayat Khan’s Congress-Unionist-Akali coalition).

#### The Cabinet Mission Plan, Direct Action Day, and the Interim Government
*   **The Cabinet Mission Plan (May 16, 1946: Three-Tier Federation and Section A/B/C Grouping)**: Arriving in New Delhi on **March 24, 1946**, the three-member **Cabinet Mission**—comprising **Lord Pethick-Lawrence** (Secretary of State for India), **Sir Stafford Cripps** (President of the Board of Trade), and **A.V. Alexander** (First Lord of the Admiralty)—attempted to broker an agreement at the Second Simla Conference (May 5–12, 1946). When Congress and the League failed to agree, the Mission issued its unilateral statement on **May 16, 1946**. In its preamble, the **Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)** systematically rejected the Muslim League's demand for a fully sovereign Pakistan, demonstrating that a large six-province Pakistan would leave vast non-Muslim minorities (37.93% in the North-West and 48.31% in the North-East, including over 20 million non-Muslims in Punjab and Bengal) trapped inside an Islamic state, whereas a smaller truncated Pakistan (partitioning Punjab and Bengal) would be unacceptable to the League and economically/militarily non-viable, while separating the two wings by 700 miles of Indian territory would paralyze defense and communications. Instead, the Plan proposed an ingenious **three-tier federation** coupled with **Section A/B/C grouping**:
    1.  **Union of India (Tier 1)**: A weak central Union embracing both British India and the Princely States, dealing exclusively with **Foreign Affairs, Defense, and Communications**, with all **residuary powers** vested in the Provinces;
    2.  **Section A/B/C Grouping (Tier 2 & Tier 3)**: For electing the **389-member Constituent Assembly** (292 from Governor's Provinces, 4 from Chief Commissioners' Provinces, and 93 from Princely States, elected by provincial legislative assemblies roughly in the ratio of 1 member per million population divided among General, Muslim, and Sikh communities) and drafting constitutions, the provinces were divided into three Sections:
        *   **Section A** (Hindu-majority): Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, Central Provinces, and Orissa;
        *   **Section B** (North-Western Muslim-majority): Punjab, North-West Frontier Province, and Sindh;
        *   **Section C** (Eastern Muslim-majority): Bengal and Assam.
        After a preliminary meeting of the full Constituent Assembly, provincial representatives would divide into **Sections A, B, and C** to draft the provincial constitutions for each province inside their section and to decide whether any Group constitution should be set up for the provinces in that Section. Only *after* the first general elections under the new constitution had been held could a province opt out of its Group by a vote of its newly elected legislature; furthermore, any province could call for a reconsideration of the terms of the Union or Group constitution after an initial period of 10 years, and at 10-yearly intervals thereafter.
    3.  **Lapse of Paramountcy and Interim Government**: Paramountcy over the Princely States would neither be retained by the British Crown nor transferred to the new Indian government, leaving states free to enter into a federal relationship or particular political arrangements with the successor government(s), while an **Interim Government** having the support of major political parties would be established immediately.
*   **The Grouping Contradiction and Collapse of Consensus**: The **Cabinet Mission Plan** contained a fatal deliberate ambiguity designed to appease both parties simultaneously: **Paragraph 15(5)** stated that *"Provinces should be free to form groups with executives and legislatures,"* whereas **Paragraph 19(v)** mandated that representatives *"shall divide up into the three Sections"* to settle provincial and group constitutions by simple majority. The **Muslim League** accepted the Plan on **June 6, 1946**, specifically because it interpreted **Section B and C grouping** as compulsory, viewing the two autonomous Muslim-majority super-groups—equipped with the 10-year constitutional reconsideration clause—as the embryonic foundation of a future sovereign Pakistan. Conversely, the **Congress** accepted the Plan on **June 24, 1946** (while declining to join the short-term Interim Government on June 25 due to disputes over parity and nationalist Muslim nomination), based on the legal interpretation that under Paragraph 15 provinces had the sovereign autonomy to refuse to join a Section from the very outset—since Congress-ruled **NWFP** (under Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan and Dr. Khan Sahib) vehemently refused to be coerced into Muslim League-dominated **Section B**, and Hindu-majority Congress-ruled **Assam** (under Gopinath Bordoloi) refused to be swallowed by Muslim-majority Bengal in **Section C** (where Bengal held 33 Muslim and 27 General seats against Assam's 7 General and 3 Muslim seats), while the Sikhs in Punjab rejected Section B domination. The fragile accord shattered on **July 10, 1946**, when newly elected Congress President **Jawaharlal Nehru** declared at a press conference in Bombay that the Congress had agreed only to enter the Constituent Assembly and considered itself *"completely unfettered by agreements and free to meet all situations as they arise,"* predicting that in all probability there would be *"no grouping at all"* as NWFP and Assam would decide against it.
*   **Direct Action Day (August 16, 1946) and Communal Civil War**: Seizing upon Nehru’s July 10 statement as proof that a Hindu-majority Congress would use a sovereign Constituent Assembly to scrap grouping and crush Muslim autonomy, **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** convened the Muslim League Council in Bombay on **July 27–29, 1946**. The League formally withdrew its acceptance of the Cabinet Mission Plan, bade farewell to constitutional methods, and called upon the Muslim nation to observe **August 16, 1946**, as **Direct Action Day** to achieve a sovereign Pakistan (*"We shall either have a divided India or a destroyed India"* and *"Today we have forged a pistol and are in a position to use it"*). On **August 16, 1946**, in Calcutta—where Muslim League Premier **Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy** declared a public holiday and neutralized police deployment after addressing a mass rally at the Ochterlony Monument—unprecedented communal carnage erupted. Known as the **Great Calcutta Killings** (August 16–19, 1946), four days of mutual slaughter between Muslim League squads and Hindu retaliation groups (organized by Gopal Patha and Marwari/Sikh elements) left **4,000 to 5,000 dead**, over 10,000 injured, and 100,000 homeless before the army restored order. The Calcutta inferno ignited a chain reaction of horrific communal massacres across northern and eastern India: in **Noakhali and Tippera** in East Bengal (**October 1946**, where Hindu minorities faced murder, forced conversions, abduction of women, and arson, prompting Mahatma Gandhi to walk barefoot from village to village for four months on a solitary peace pilgrimage), followed by massive retaliatory massacres of Muslims in **Bihar** (**October–November 1946**, where over 5,000–7,000 Muslims perished before Nehru threatened aerial bombing of rioters and Gandhi threatened a fast unto death) and **Garhmukteshwar** in UP (**November 1946**), and culminating in the League's civil disobedience campaign that toppled Khizr Hayat Khan's coalition ministry in **Punjab** in **March 1947** amidst the **Rawalpindi and Multan massacres** of Sikhs and Hindus.
*   **The Interim Government (September 1946) and Institutional Paralysis**: Alarmed that leaving the Muslim League in sole possession of the field would hand over central executive power, the Congress reversed its earlier refusal and accepted Viceroy Wavell’s invitation to form the **Interim Government**, which was sworn in on **September 2, 1946**, with **Jawaharlal Nehru** as Vice-President of the Executive Council (effectively Prime Minister, alongside Sardar Patel holding Home and Information & Broadcasting, Rajendra Prasad holding Food & Agriculture, C. Rajagopalachari holding Education, Jagjivan Ram holding Labour, Asaf Ali holding Railways, and Baldev Singh holding Defense). Realizing that excluding the League from central state patronage and policing power was strategically disastrous, Jinnah maneuvered five Muslim League nominees—led by **Liaquat Ali Khan**, alongside I.I. Chundrigar, Abdur Rab Nishtar, Ghazanfar Ali Khan, and **Jogendra Nath Mandal** (a Scheduled Caste leader from Bengal nominated by the League to retaliate against the Congress nomination of nationalist Muslims)—into the **Interim Government** on **October 26, 1946**, forcing three Congress ministers (Sarat Chandra Bose, Sir Shafaat Ahmad Khan, and Syed Ali Zaheer) to step down. However, the **Interim Government** immediately descended into deliberate institutional paralysis: the League joined not to cooperate under cabinet collective responsibility (which it openly repudiated) or to enter the **Constituent Assembly** (which convened on **December 9, 1946**, under temporary Chairman Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha and permanent President **Dr. Rajendra Prasad**, completely boycotted by the Muslim League even after the British December 6 Statement upheld the League’s compulsory grouping interpretation), but to wage the battle for Pakistan from inside the central ministries. Holding the pivotal **Finance Portfolio** (which Sardar Patel had unwisely offered to the League assuming they could not manage its technicalities), **Liaquat Ali Khan** scrutinized and vetoed every proposal from Congress-held ministries—down to the appointment of a single peon in Patel’s Home Ministry—and in **February–March 1947** presented the famous **"Poor Man’s Budget"**, imposing a punitive 25% business profits tax on profits exceeding Rs 1 lakh and establishing a commission on tax evasion aimed directly at the Hindu/Parsi big industrialists (Birla, Tata, Dalmia) who financed the Indian National Congress, convincing Patel and Nehru that a shared central government with the Muslim League was administratively impossible.

### 11. From Attlee’s Statement and Plan Balkan to the June 3 Mountbatten Plan and the Indian Independence Act (1947)

By early 1947, the British imperial apparatus in India faced irreversible structural collapse. The post-war British economy was bankrupt and dependent on US loans; demobilization pressures crippled British troop strength in India; the **Indian Civil Service (ICS)** and **Indian Police (IP)**—where European recruitment had been frozen since 1939—were depleted, demoralized, and polarizing along communal lines; and a raging communal civil war in Bengal, Bihar, and Punjab threatened to consume the Indian Army itself. After rejecting Viceroy Lord Wavell’s secret **"Breakdown Plan"** (which proposed a phased military evacuation of the British Raj province-by-province from the peaceful South to the North), Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** intervened decisively to set a fixed terminal deadline for British withdrawal and replaced Wavell with **Lord Louis Mountbatten** as the last Viceroy of India.

#### The Statutory Mechanics of Partition and Transfer of Power
*   **Attlee’s February 20, 1947 Statement**: On **February 20, 1947**, British Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** made a historic declaration in the House of Commons designed to shock Indian political parties out of their deadlock by fixing an irrevocable date for British withdrawal:
    1.  His Majesty’s Government announced its definite intention to take the necessary steps to effect the transference of power into responsible Indian hands by a date **not later than June 30, 1948**;
    2.  If a fully representative Constituent Assembly (incorporating the Muslim League) failed to work out a constitution in accordance with the Cabinet Mission Plan by that date, His Majesty's Government would have to consider to whom the powers of the Central Government in British India should be handed over on the due date: *"whether as a whole to some form of central Government for British India, or in some areas to the existing Provincial Governments, or in such other way as may seem most reasonable and in the best interests of the Indian people"*;
    3.  Regarding the **Princely States**, British paramountcy and treaty obligations would lapse with the final transfer of power and would not be transferred to any successor government in British India; and
    4.  **Lord Wavell** was recalled and replaced as Viceroy by Rear-Admiral Viscount **Louis Mountbatten** (former Supreme Allied Commander, South East Asia Command), who assumed office on **March 24, 1947**, armed with unprecedented plenipotentiary autonomy from Whitehall.
    Far from inducing compromise, **Attlee’s Feb 20, 1947 Statement**—specifically its clause contemplating transfer of power to *"existing Provincial Governments"*—triggered an immediate Muslim League offensive to capture the non-League Muslim-majority provinces of **Punjab**, **Assam**, and the **NWFP** before the deadline, precipitating the collapse of Khizr Hayat Khan’s ministry in Punjab on **March 2, 1947**, and unleashing the horrific **March 1947 Punjab massacres**. Confronted with the total breakdown of the Interim Government and the raging inferno in Punjab, the Congress Working Committee on **March 8, 1947**, passed a historic resolution demanding the **partition of Punjab** into predominantly Muslim and predominantly non-Muslim halves (which Nehru and Patel simultaneously extended to the partition of **Bengal**), signaling that the Congress was now prepared to accept the amputation of the Muslim-majority extremities in order to secure a strong, unitary, and sovereign Central Government for the rest of India rather than succumb to a paralyzed, balkanized confederation.
*   **Plan Balkan / Dickie Bird Plan (May 1947)**: After a month of intensive interviews with Gandhi (whose desperate **April 1, 1947 proposal** to invite Jinnah to form an all-Muslim League central cabinet supported by the Congress Assembly majority if Jinnah abandoned Pakistan was rejected as utopian by both Patel/Nehru and Mountbatten), Nehru, Patel, and Jinnah, Mountbatten’s staff—led by **Lord Ismay** (Chief of Staff) and **Sir George Abell** (Private Secretary)—drafted an initial devolution blueprint known as **Plan Balkan** (or the **"Dickie Bird Plan"** after Mountbatten’s nickname). Dispatched to London by Ismay on **May 2, 1947**, **Plan Balkan** proposed transferring power not to one or two central unions, but directly to **individual provinces** as separate successor authorities: the assemblies of Punjab and Bengal would first vote on whether to partition their provinces, after which *every* individual province (or partitioned half-province)—alongside the 560+ Princely States upon the lapse of paramountcy—would exercise the unrestricted right to join the existing Constituent Assembly (India), group into a new Constituent Assembly (Pakistan), or **stand out independently as separate sovereign entities** (simultaneously Encouraging H.S. Suhrawardy, Sarat Chandra Bose, and Abul Hashim’s proposal for an independent, sovereign **United Bengal**, and Travancore/Hyderabad declarations of independence). On the night of **May 10, 1947**, during a retreat at **Mashobra / Simla**, Mountbatten followed a "hunch" and privately showed the London-revised draft of **Plan Balkan** to his house guest, **Jawaharlal Nehru**. Nehru reacted with volcanic outrage, submitting a scorching written note on **May 11** warning that **Plan Balkan** was a deliberate imperial conspiracy to provoke the complete fragmentation, *"Balkanization,"* and civil disintegration of India by inviting every province and princely state to break away before any central authority existed.
*   **V.P. Menon’s Dominion Status Formula and the Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947)**: Faced with Nehru’s categorical rejection of Plan Balkan, Mountbatten turned immediately to his brilliant Indian Reforms Commissioner, **V.P. Menon**. Months earlier (in December 1946–January 1947), **V.P. Menon** had privately convinced **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**—and now persuaded **Jawaharlal Nehru** at Simla—that the only way to achieve an immediate, orderly transfer of power with a strong unitary Centre while blocking provincial fragmentation was for the Congress to drop its temporary insistence on immediate republican status and accept an interim transfer of power to **two Central Dominions (India and Pakistan)** on the basis of **Dominion Status**. For Britain, Dominion Status preserved Commonwealth prestige and strategic links; for Patel and Nehru, it ensured that power was transferred exclusively to two sovereign Central Governments under the adapted **Government of India Act 1935** (strangling independent provincial secession at birth), maintained administrative and military command continuity during the partition crisis, and accelerated the British exit by ten months. Drafted by **V.P. Menon** in four hours at Simla, approved by Whitehall during Mountbatten's flying visit to London in late May, and accepted by Nehru, Patel, J.B. Kripalani, Baldev Singh, and (by silent nod) Jinnah on **June 2**, the **Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947)** (formally the *3rd June Plan*, ratified by the AICC on **June 14–15, 1947**, where Govind Ballabh Pant moved the resolution and Gandhi reluctantly asked the AICC to honor the Working Committee’s pledge despite his personal anguish) established the definitive procedural blueprint for Partition:
    1.  **Bifurcation of the Punjab and Bengal Legislative Assemblies**: The Provincial Legislative Assemblies of **Punjab** and **Bengal** (excluding European members) would each meet in two separate parts—one representing the **Muslim-majority districts** (according to the 1941 Census) and the other representing the **rest of the province**. The members of the two parts would sit separately and vote by simple majority on whether the province should be partitioned. If **either part decided in favor of partition**, the province would be partitioned, and each part would independently decide whether its constitution would be framed by the existing Constituent Assembly (India) or a new and separate Constituent Assembly (Pakistan);
    2.  **Sindh and Baluchistan**: The Legislative Assembly of **Sindh** (excluding Europeans) would take its own decision at a special meeting, while **British Baluchistan** would decide via a joint meeting of the **Shahi Jirga** and the non-official members of the Quetta Municipality;
    3.  **Referendums in the NWFP and Sylhet**: In the **North-West Frontier Province** (which had a Congress ministry under Dr. Khan Sahib, though geographically surrounded by Muslim-majority areas if Punjab opted for Pakistan), a **referendum** of the existing Legislative Assembly electorate would be conducted under the Governor-General's aegis to choose between the two Constituent Assemblies. Similarly, if Bengal decided in favor of partition, a **referendum** would be held in the Muslim-majority **Sylhet district of Assam** to decide whether it should amalgamate with the Muslim-majority part of Eastern Bengal;
    4.  **Boundary Commissions**: As soon as a decision involving partition was taken for Punjab or Bengal, a **Boundary Commission** would be set up by the Governor-General to demarcate the exact boundaries of the two parts of the province on the basis of *"ascertaining the contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims,"* while also taking into account *"other factors"*; and
    5.  **Immediate Transfer on Dominion Status**: Legislation would be introduced in the current session of the British Parliament for the immediate transfer of power in **1947** on a **Dominion Status** basis to one or two successor authorities, without prejudice to the right of the Constituent Assemblies to decide in due course whether or not to remain within the British Commonwealth. At a press conference on **June 4, 1947**, Mountbatten stunned the world by announcing that the actual date for the transfer of power would be **August 15, 1947**—compressing the complex partition of a subcontinent into a mere **72 days**.

#### The Indian Independence Act (1947)
Introduced in the House of Commons by Prime Minister Clement Attlee on **July 4, 1947**, passed without a single amendment within a fortnight, and receiving Royal Assent on **July 18, 1947**, the **Indian Independence Act (1947)** (*10 & 11 Geo. 6 c. 30*) translated the **Mountbatten Plan** into binding statutory law:
*   **Creation of Two Independent Dominions**: Section 1 stipulated that as from the **"appointed day" (August 15, 1947)**, two independent Dominions should be set up in India, to be known respectively as **India** and **Pakistan**. Section 2 defined the territories of the two Dominions (assigning East Bengal, West Punjab, Sindh, British Baluchistan, and the NWFP/Sylhet subject to referendum results to Pakistan, and all remaining territories of British India to India), subject to the final awards of the **Boundary Commissions** appointed by the Governor-General.
*   **Governor-General as Constitutional Head**: Section 5 provided that for each of the new Dominions, there should be a **Governor-General** appointed by the King on the advice of the respective Dominion Cabinet, while empowering the same person to serve as Governor-General of both Dominions if both agreed (in practice, Congress nominated **Lord Mountbatten** as the first Governor-General of independent India, whereas **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** insisted on becoming the first Governor-General of Pakistan himself, disappointing Mountbatten's ambition to hold the joint post). Under Section 8(2)(c), the Governor-General and Provincial Governors were stripped of all personal discretionary powers, individual judgment faculties, and legislative reservation/veto powers, becoming strictly constitutional, nominal heads bound by ministerial advice.
*   **Unlimited Legislative Sovereignty of the Constituent Assemblies**: Sections 6 and 8 conferred full, unrestricted legislative sovereignty upon the **Constituent Assembly** of each Dominion. Each Constituent Assembly was empowered to function in a dual capacity—both as the constitution-making body and as the Central Dominion Legislature—possessing the plenary authority to repeal or amend any Act of the British Parliament extending to the Dominion (including the **Indian Independence Act 1947** itself), while no Act of the British Parliament passed on or after August 15, 1947, would extend to either Dominion. Pending the framing of new constitutions, each Dominion and its provinces were to be governed in accordance with the **Government of India Act 1935**, as adapted and modified by Provisional Constitution Orders issued by the Governor-General (which abolished **Section 93** Governor's rule and central dyarchy).
*   **Abolition of Whitehall Control and Lapse of Paramountcy**: The Act abolished the office of the **Secretary of State for India** and transferred Commonwealth communications to the Secretary of State for Commonwealth Relations; deleted the imperial title ***"Indiae Imperator" (Emperor of India)*** from the royal style and titles of the British Monarch; and under **Section 7(1)(b)**, enacted the **Lapse of Paramountcy**, declaring that the suzerainty of His Majesty over the Indian Princely States, together with all treaties, engagements, and sanads in force on August 15, 1947, lapsed completely—though a crucial proviso allowed existing agreements regarding **customs, transit, communications, posts, and telegraphs** to continue in force until denounced by either party or superseded by fresh agreements.

#### Outgoing Structural Bridge into Part 15
While the **Indian Independence Act (1947)** consummated the juridical transfer of sovereignty at the stroke of midnight on **August 14–15, 1947**, the compression of imperial withdrawal from June 1948 to August 15, 1947, transformed a statutory decolonization into an unprecedented cartographic, demographic, and fiscal vivisection. The procedural mechanisms embedded within the **Mountbatten Plan** and the **Indian Independence Act**—the June 1947 partitioned assembly votes in Punjab and Bengal, the contested referendums in the NWFP and Sylhet, the impossible five-week deadline handed to Sir Cyril Radcliffe to slice through integrated canal colonies and jute-textile circuits, the delayed publication of the Radcliffe Awards until August 17, 1947, the forced migration of 14 to 18 million refugees across bleeding frontiers, and the bitter division of sovereign debt, railway rolling stock, Rs 400 crore of cash balances, and military regiments under the Partition Council—Directly generated the post-partition state-building emergencies examined in **Part 15: The Partition: Boundary Commissions, Demographic Rupture, Refugee Rehabilitation, and Sovereign Asset Bifurcation (1946–1950)**.
