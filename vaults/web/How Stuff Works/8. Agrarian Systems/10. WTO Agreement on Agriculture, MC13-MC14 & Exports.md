# Part 10: Multilateral Trade in Agriculture, the WTO Agreement on Agriculture, MC13/MC14 Deadlocks, and Agrarian Export Policy

## I. The International Rules-Based Trading Regime: The WTO Agreement on Agriculture (AoA, 1995) and Its Three Structural Pillars

### 1. The Political Economy of the Uruguay Round and the Three-Pillar Architecture of AoA (1995)

For nearly five decades following the inception of the **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT, 1947)**, international agricultural trade operated in a legal vacuum outside multilateral industrial trade disciplines. Advanced industrial economies—most notably the **United States** (which secured a broad GATT waiver in 1955) and the **European Economic Community (EEC)** through its **Common Agricultural Policy (CAP, 1962)**—insulated their capital-intensive farm sectors behind import quotas, variable import levies, high domestic price guarantees, and aggressive export restitutions that dumped structural food surpluses onto global markets. Only with the conclusion of the **Uruguay Round (1986–1994)** and the signing of the **Marrakesh Agreement (1994)** did the **World Trade Organization (WTO) Agreement on Agriculture (AoA)** enter into force on **January 1, 1995**, legally binding signatory nations across **Three Structural Pillars**: **Market Access**, **Export Subsidies (Export Competition)**, and **Domestic Support**.

Beneath its uniform legal text, the **AoA (1995)** codified a profound structural asymmetry between two fundamentally incompatible agrarian models: the **land-abundant, capital-intensive commercial agro-exporters** (led by the **United States** and the **19-member Cairns Group**, including Australia, Brazil, Canada, Argentina, and Thailand), where average farm sizes span hundreds to thousands of hectares and agriculture accounts for less than 2% of the workforce; and the **labour-intensive, land-scarce smallholder agrarian economies** of the Global South (represented by the **G-33 coalition** of 47 developing nations, including **India** and Indonesia), where **Small and Marginal Farmers (>86% of holdings in India, averaging <1.08 hectares)** depend on state-backed price stabilization and input subsidies for basic livelihood survival and national food sovereignty.

#### Pillar I: Market Access — Tariffication, Bound vs. Applied Tariffs, and the Special Safeguard Mechanism (SSM)

Under **Article 4 of the AoA**, all member states were legally required to eliminate quantitative import restrictions, variable import levies, minimum import prices, and discretionary licensing regimes, converting these opaque non-tariff measures into transparent, quantifiable customs duties through a process termed **Tariffication**:

* **Tariff Reduction Commitments and the Bound-Applied Tariff Wedge:** Following tariffication, developed countries committed to cutting average agricultural tariffs by **36% over 6 years** (with a minimum 15% cut per tariff line), while developing countries committed to reducing tariffs by **24% over 10 years** (with a minimum 10% cut per line), and Least Developed Countries (LDCs) were exempted from reduction commitments. Crucially, because India had maintained quantitative restrictions under GATT **Article XVIII:B (Balance-of-Payments exception)** until the late 1990s, India strategically bound its agricultural peak tariffs at high ceiling levels (**Bound Tariffs** averaging **~113.1%**, including **100% on raw commodities**, **150% on processed agro-products**, and up to **300% on edible oils**), while maintaining significantly lower **Applied Tariffs** (averaging **~35%–39%**). This deliberate **Bound-to-Applied Tariff Wedge** grants the Indian state sovereign fiscal space to raise applied import duties unilaterally during global commodity gluts (such as palm and soybean oil price crashes) without violating WTO legal ceilings, and to slash applied duties to zero during domestic production shortfalls.
* **The Special Agricultural Safeguard (SSG) vs. the G-33 Special Safeguard Mechanism (SSM):** A major structural inequity within Pillar I lies in **Article 5 of the AoA**, which established the **Special Agricultural Safeguard (SSG)**. The SSG permits signatory states to impose automatic additional countervailing duties whenever agricultural imports exceed a specified volume trigger or fall below a 1986–88 reference price trigger—without having to prove "serious injury" to domestic producers as required under the general GATT Safeguards Agreement. However, eligibility to invoke the **SSG** was restricted strictly to the **38 mostly developed countries** that completed formal tariffication during the Uruguay Round. Because developing nations like India dismantled quantitative restrictions later via Balance-of-Payments negotiations rather than Uruguay Round tariffication, they are legally barred from using the SSG.
* **The G-33 Demand for an Operational SSM:** To rectify this protectionist asymmetry, the **G-33 coalition** (spearheaded by India and Indonesia) has consistently demanded a dedicated **Special Safeguard Mechanism (SSM)** tailored for developing countries. An operational **SSM** would empower developing nations to impose temporary safeguard tariffs above their bound rates whenever a sudden **import volume surge** (e.g., imports exceeding 110% of the preceding three-year moving average) or an international **price depression** (e.g., import c.i.f. price falling below 85% of the three-year average monthly price) threatens the livelihoods of resource-poor smallholders. At the **2008 Geneva Mini-Ministerial**, negotiations collapsed precisely over the US demand for an excessively high volume trigger (**140% import surge**) paired with restrictive remedies that would have rendered the SSM operationally toothless before domestic farm-gate prices collapsed.

#### Pillar II: Export Subsidies — The 2015 Nairobi Abolition and the WTO Sugar Panel Ruling (DS579/580/581)

Historically, **Pillar II (Export Competition)** under **Articles 8, 9, and 10 of the AoA** disciplined direct export subsidies, subsidized export credit guarantees, and international food-aid dumping. Under the original Uruguay Round schedules, only 25 WTO members with historical base-period export subsidies were permitted to retain them (subject to a 36% budgetary reduction and 21% volume reduction for developed nations), whereas developing countries without base-period export subsidies—such as India—were prohibited from introducing direct export subsidies, save for a narrow **Article 9.4 Special and Differential Treatment (S&DT)** exemption allowing developing nations to subsidize the **internal transport, freight, and international marketing costs** of agricultural exports:

* **The 2015 Nairobi Ministerial (MC10) Abolition Mandate:** At the **10th WTO Ministerial Conference (MC10) in Nairobi (December 2015)**, WTO members adopted a landmark Ministerial Decision formally **abolishing agricultural export subsidies**. Developed countries were required to eliminate their remaining scheduled export subsidy entitlements immediately (by the end of 2015), while developing countries were mandated to eliminate them by the end of 2018. Crucially, the Nairobi Decision also imposed a terminal sunset clause on the **Article 9.4 transport and marketing exemption** for developing countries, which formally **expired on December 31, 2023** (remaining valid until 2030 only for LDCs and Net Food-Importing Developing Countries).
* **The 2021 WTO Dispute Settlement Panel Ruling on Indian Sugar (DS579, DS580, DS581):** The expiration of Article 9.4 directly collided with India's domestic sugarcane pricing and surplus disposal regime. In 2019, the world's three largest commercial sugar exporters—**Brazil (DS579)**, **Australia (DS580)**, and **Guatemala (DS581)**—initiated a consolidated WTO dispute challenging both India's domestic sugarcane price regime and its export assistance schemes. In **December 2021**, the **WTO Dispute Settlement Panel** ruled comprehensively against India across two distinct legal axes:
  1. **Breach of Domestic Support Limits (Articles 6 & 7.2(b)):** Rejecting India's argument that **Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP)** and **State Advised Prices (SAP)** are paid exclusively by private sugar mills without state budgetary expenditure, the Panel ruled that statutorily mandated minimum floor prices constitute "Market Price Support" under **Annex 3 of the AoA**. Calculated against the archaic 1986–88 reference price, India's product-specific AMS for sugarcane exceeded **90% to 99% of the total value of cane production** across marketing years 2014–15 to 2018–19—vastly breaching the **10% *De Minimis* ceiling**.
  2. **Prohibited Export Subsidies (Articles 3.3, 8, and 9.1):** The Panel held that India's **Production Assistance**, **Buffer Stock**, and **Marketing and Transportation (MAEQ — Maximum Admissible Export Quantity)** schemes constituted prohibited export-contingent subsidies, and ruled that after the Nairobi Decision, India could no longer shelter these payouts under Article 9.4 after the end of 2023.
* **India's Dual Institutional Response (Appellate Vacuum + Domestic Ethanol Pivot):** In **January 2022**, India appealed the Panel's report to the **WTO Appellate Body**. Because the Appellate Body has remained non-functional since December 2019 due to the US blockade of judicial appointments, India's appeal effectively placed the ruling "into the void," preventing the complainants from legally authorizing retaliatory tariffs. Simultaneously, rather than slashing the **FRP** paid to 50 million sugarcane farmers, India executed a structural domestic demand-side pivot via the **Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) Programme**, diverting **4.5 to 5.5 million tonnes of surplus sugar annually** (via B-heavy molasses and direct cane juice/syrup) into fuel ethanol and achieving the **20% blending (E20) target in 2025–26** (analyzed in Part 5)—thereby eliminating the structural compulsion to subsidize raw sugar exports onto the global market.

### 2. Pillar III: Domestic Support and the Traffic-Light Box Framework

To discipline domestic farm subsidies without paralyzing legitimate public goods investments, **Pillar III of the AoA (Articles 6, 7, and Annex 2)** classifies domestic agricultural support into a **Traffic-Light Box Framework** governed by a single microeconomic first principle: **the degree to which a fiscal transfer alters marginal production decisions, farm-gate prices, and international trade volumes**.

#### Green Box (Annex 2), Blue Box (Article 6.5), Development Box (Article 6.2), and Amber Box (AMS & De Minimis)

* **The Green Box (Annex 2 — Non-Trade-Distorting Support):** Subsidies placed inside **Annex 2 (Green Box)** face **zero financial ceilings or reduction commitments** provided they fulfill two foundational legal criteria under Paragraph 1 of Annex 2: (1) they must be funded through **publicly funded government programmes** (including forgone revenue) rather than transfers from consumers; and (2) they must **not have the effect of providing price support to producers**. Green Box measures encompass two distinct sub-categories:
  1. **General Services (Annex 2, Para 2):** Public expenditures on agricultural research and development (**ICAR** and State Agricultural Universities), pest and disease control (**NPSS**), agricultural extension and advisory services (**ATMA**, **KVKs**, **Bharat-VISTAAR**), inspection and phytosanitary services, rural infrastructural works (feeder roads, market yards, dam construction, drainage systems—excluding subsidized on-farm operational inputs), and environmental conservation programmes (**PKVY**, **NMNF**, **NICRA**).
  2. **Decoupled Direct Payments to Producers (Annex 2, Paras 5–13):** Direct income transfers to farmers that are strictly **decoupled from current production volumes, current domestic/international prices, and current factors of production** after a fixed historical base period. India's flagship **PM-KISAN** scheme (providing ₹6,000 per year in three installments to landholding farmer families), alongside state-level direct transfers like **Rythu Bharosa** (Andhra Pradesh/Telangana) and **KALIA** (Odisha), falls squarely within **Annex 2, Paragraph 6 (Decoupled Income Support)**. Similarly, government financial participation in crop insurance (**PMFBY**) and disaster relief falls under Paragraphs 7 and 8 of Annex 2. However, the Green Box exemplifies the deep structural bias of the AoA: following the 1995 Uruguay Round, the **United States** and the **European Union** simply engaged in **"Box-Shifting"**—repackaging their massive trade-distorting price supports into decoupled direct income payments (under the **US Farm Bills** and the **EU CAP Single Farm Payment / Basic Payment Scheme**), allowing them to expand total domestic agricultural subsidies from ~$60 billion in 1995 to over **$100–$120 billion annually** while remaining technically compliant with WTO rules. Because decoupled income transfers provide vast liquidity, lower risk aversion, and cross-subsidize fixed capital costs on mega-farms, they retain substantial indirect production-enhancing effects in global markets.
* **The Blue Box (Article 6.5 — Production-Limiting Direct Payments):** Operating as an "Amber Box with conditions designed to reduce distortion," **Article 6.5** exempts direct payments made under **production-limiting programmes** from spending caps, provided such payments are based on **fixed area and yields**, or are made on **85% or less of the base level of production**, or (for livestock payments) are made on a **fixed number of head**. Crafted during the **1992 Blair House Accord** between the US and the EEC to shelter European compensatory set-aside payments and US deficiency payments, the **Blue Box** currently incurs no WTO spending cap and is utilized primarily by the **European Union, Norway, Japan, and Iceland**. For populous, food-deficit, or smallholder-dominated developing economies like India, paying farmers to leave arable land fallow or restrict output is socio-economically and ecologically absurd, rendering the Blue Box structurally unusable for the Global South.
* **The Development Box (Article 6.2 — Special and Differential Treatment for Developing Countries):** Recognizing the capital and input deficits of developing-country agriculture, **Article 6.2 of the AoA** provides a vital legal shield for developing nations (excluding LDCs from reduction commitments altogether under Article 15.2). Article 6.2 unconditionally exempts three categories of domestic support from **Amber Box (AMS)** calculation: (1) **investment subsidies which are generally available to agriculture** in developing country members; (2) **agricultural input subsidies generally available to low-income or resource-poor producers** in developing country members; and (3) domestic support to encourage diversification from growing **illicit narcotic crops**.
  * *India's Strategic Reliance on Article 6.2:* Under India's official domestic support notifications to the WTO Committee on Agriculture, India classifies **all farmers operating holdings up to 10 hectares** (which covers **99.43% of all operational holdings** per the Agriculture Census, given that Small and Marginal Farmers with <2 hectares alone constitute 86.2%) as **"low-income or resource-poor producers."** Consequently, India legally shields virtually its entire **$30 to $35 billion annual Non-Product-Specific input subsidy architecture**—comprising **Urea price controls and Nutrient Based Subsidies (NBS)** for P&K fertilizers, **free or subsidized state electricity** for agricultural pump-sets, **canal water and PMKSY micro-irrigation subsidies**, **Kisan Credit Card (KCC) Modified Interest Subvention Scheme (MISS)** credit subsidies, and seed subsidies—under **Article 6.2**. In recent WTO review cycles, the **United States, Canada, and Australia** have filed aggressive counter-notifications challenging India's 10-hectare threshold and demanding that Article 6.2 exemptions be dismantled or capped for major emerging economies.
* **The Amber Box (Article 6 — Trade-Distorting Support Subject to AMS and *De Minimis* Caps):** All domestic support measures that do not qualify for the Green Box, Blue Box, or Article 6.2 Development Box fall directly into the **Amber Box**. These include **Market Price Support (MPS)**—such as India's **Minimum Support Price (MSP)** procurement operations and **Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP)** for sugarcane—as well as non-exempt input subsidies directly linked to production quantities.
  * *Aggregate Measurement of Support (AMS) vs. De Minimis Asymmetry:* Under Article 6, Amber Box support is quantified via the **Aggregate Measurement of Support (AMS)**, disaggregated into **Product-Specific AMS** (calculated separately for each individual crop like rice, wheat, cotton, or pulses) and **Non-Product-Specific AMS** (general input subsidies not covered by Article 6.2). During the Uruguay Round base period (**1986–1988**), advanced economies like the **US, EU, and Japan** had massive existing price-support subsidies; they were permitted to lock in those massive historical levels as their **Bound Total AMS**, subject only to a modest **20% reduction over 6 years** (giving the US a permanent Bound Total AMS ceiling of **$19.1 billion** and the EU **€72.2 billion** over and above their *de minimis* allowances). Conversely, because **61 developing countries—including India—had negative or negligible AMS during 1986–1988** (as domestic procurement prices were then below high global reference prices), their **Bound Total AMS was fixed at ZERO**. Consequently, India has zero historical Bound AMS entitlement and is legally restricted strictly to the ***De Minimis* threshold** under **Article 6.4(b)**: for developing countries, Product-Specific AMS cannot exceed **10% of the total value of production of that specific basic agricultural product** during the relevant year (and Non-Product-Specific AMS cannot exceed **10% of the value of total agricultural production**), whereas developed countries have a **5% *De Minimis* limit** alongside their vast Bound Total AMS allowances (and China accepted an intermediate **8.5% *De Minimis* limit** upon its 2001 WTO accession).

## II. The Public Stockholding (PSH) Clash: The 1986–88 ERP Flaw, the Bali Peace Clause, and the MC13/MC14 Deadlocks

### 3. The Arithmetic Pathology of Market Price Support: The 1986–88 External Reference Price (ERP) Trap

The single most explosive conflict in contemporary multilateral trade negotiations revolves around the interface between **Annex 2, Paragraph 3 (Green Box Public Stockholding for Food Security Purposes)** and **Annex 3, Paragraph 8 (Amber Box Market Price Support Calculation)** of the AoA. While **Annex 2, Paragraph 3** classifies government expenditure on the accumulation and holding of public foodgrain buffer stocks (**FCI buffer and operational stocks**) as an unlimited **Green Box** measure, **Footnote 5 to Paragraph 3 of Annex 2** injects a fatal conditionality: if a developing country acquires foodgrain stocks from farmers at **"administered prices"** (such as India's **Minimum Support Price — MSP**), the difference between the acquisition price and the **External Reference Price (ERP)** *must* be accounted for in the **Amber Box AMS**.

Under **Annex 3, Paragraph 8 of the AoA**, the Product-Specific Market Price Support ($\text{MPS}_i$) for any crop $i$ in current marketing year $t$ is computed using the rigid statutory formula:

$$\text{MPS}_{i,t} = \left( P_{\text{Admin}, i, t} - P_{\text{ERP}, i, 1986\text{–}1988} \right) \times Q_{\text{Eligible}, i, t}$$

Where:
* $P_{\text{Admin}, i, t}$ = The **Applied Administered Price** (**MSP**) announced by the government in current year $t$.
* $P_{\text{ERP}, i, 1986\text{–}1988}$ = The fixed **External Reference Price (ERP)**, defined strictly as the average c.i.f. (for net importers) or f.o.b. (for net exporters) international border price of that commodity during the frozen historical base period **1986 to 1988**.
* $Q_{\text{Eligible}, i, t}$ = The volume of **Eligible Production** in current year $t$.

#### Three Compounding Structural Distortions in AoA Annex 3

This mathematical equation subjects developing countries to three compounding structural distortions that transform domestic anti-poverty procurement into an illegal trade violation:

1. **The Frozen 1986–88 Base-Year Inflation Distortion:** Comparing a contemporary **MSP** in **2025–26** (which reflects nearly four decades of cumulative inflation in diesel, labor wages, fertilizers, and seed costs) against an **External Reference Price frozen in 1986–1988** is an elementary violation of economic indexation. For instance, in India's Uruguay Round base schedule, the **1986–88 ERP for Rice** was fixed at **₹3,520 per metric tonne** (equivalent to approximately **$263/tonne** in USD terms). By 2025–26, even when India's paddy MSP (translated into milled rice equivalent at ~$390–$415/tonne) remains **lower than prevailing international f.o.b. white rice spot prices** ($500–$600/tonne during 2023–2025), subtracting the frozen 1986–88 ERP of $263/tonne generates a huge artificial "price support gap" of $130–$150/tonne. Although **Article 18.4 of the AoA** directs the Committee on Agriculture to give *"due consideration to the influence of excessive rates of inflation on the ability of any Member to abide by its domestic support commitments,"* developed agro-exporters have blocked every attempt to index the ERP for inflation.
2. **The Currency Valuation and Reporting Asymmetry:** India calculated its 1986–88 base schedules in nominal Indian Rupees when the exchange rate averaged ~₹13.4 per USD, subsequently notifying its recent domestic support tables in US Dollars to partially mitigate rupee depreciation. Even in USD terms, thirty-eight years of global dollar inflation erode the real value of the 1986–88 ERP, relentlessly pushing developing nations toward their **10% *De Minimis* ceiling**.
3. **The "Eligible Production" Multiplier Pathology:** When the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)** and state agencies procure rice or wheat at MSP to distribute to **81.35 crore beneficiaries** under the **National Food Security Act (NFSA, 2013) / Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY)**, they physically procure only **35% to 45% of total national production** (~50–55 million tonnes out of ~135–138 million tonnes of rice). However, under established WTO Appellate Body jurisprudence (**Korea – Various Measures on Beef, 2000** and **China – Domestic Support for Agricultural Producers, 2019**), if a government announces an official MSP without an explicit statutory production quota capping the volume eligible to be sold, **"Eligible Production" ($Q_{\text{Eligible}}$) is legally deemed to be 100% of total national production** (or total marketable surplus), multiplying the notional subsidy by **2.5 to 3 times** the actual fiscal expenditure incurred by FCI.

### 4. The 2013 Bali / 2014 Perpetual Peace Clause and India’s Invocation for Rice

As the enactment of the **National Food Security Act (NFSA, 2013)** expanded India's statutory grain procurement obligations precisely as nominal MSPs crossed the 1986–88 ERP threshold, India spearheaded a **G-33 coalition blockade** at the **9th WTO Ministerial Conference (MC9) in Bali (December 2013)**. Refusing to ratify the developed world's prized **Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA)** unless developing countries' food security stockholding programmes were protected, India forced the adoption of the **Bali Ministerial Decision on Public Stockholding for Food Security Purposes (WT/MIN(13)/38)**—commonly termed the **Bali Peace Clause**.

Originally drafted in Bali to expire at the 11th Ministerial Conference in 2017, India subsequently withheld formal ratification of the TFA in mid-2014 until the **WTO General Council adopted a binding decision on November 27, 2014 (WT/L/939)**. This 2014 General Council Decision upgraded the interim protection into a **Perpetual Peace Clause**, legally guaranteeing that WTO members shall refrain from challenging a developing country's compliance with its Amber Box AMS obligations through the **Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU)** *until a permanent solution on Public Stockholding is agreed and adopted by all WTO Members*.

#### Onerous Conditionalities and the 2020–2026 Rice Breach Notifications

In **March 2020**, India became the **first WTO member in history to formally invoke the Bali Peace Clause**, notifying the WTO that for **Marketing Year (MY) 2018–19**, India's total value of rice production stood at **$43.67 billion**, while product-specific Market Price Support for rice reached **$5.00 billion (11.46% of production value)**—formally breaching the **10% *De Minimis* ceiling ($4.367 billion)**. India subsequently invoked the Peace Clause continuously across notifications for **MY 2019–20 (13.18% of production value: $6.31 billion support against $47.87 billion production)**, **MY 2020–21 (13.38%)**, **MY 2021–22**, **MY 2022–23**, and **MY 2023–24 / 2024–25**. However, this legal shield is crippled by three onerous conditionalities built into the 2013 Bali text:

* **The December 7, 2013 Cut-Off Freeze:** Paragraph 2 of the Bali Decision restricts Peace Clause protection strictly to **traditional staple food crops** procured under public stockholding programmes **already existing as of the date of the Bali Decision (December 7, 2013)**. Consequently, if India expands assured MSP procurement to newly launched crop diversification initiatives—such as the **2025–2031 Mission for Aatmanirbharta in Pulses (Dalhan Aatmanirbharta Mission)** guaranteeing 100% procurement of **Tur, Urad, and Masoor** via **NAFED/NCCF**, or oilseeds under **PM-AASHA** and **NMEO-Oilseeds**—and breaches the 10% *de minimis* cap on pulses or oilseeds (which have much smaller denominators of total production value than rice), the **Cairns Group** contends that new post-2013 schemes or non-staple crops are legally ineligible for Peace Clause immunity.
* **Intrusive Statistical Notification Burdens:** Paragraph 3 forces the invoking member to submit up-to-date domestic support notifications for the entire agricultural sector and provide exhaustive crop-level procurement, inventory, release, and pricing templates according to a rigid statistical annex, allowing exporting nations to dissect every administrative vulnerability in the invoking nation's food supply chain.
* **The Anti-Circumvention and Trade-Distortion Trap (Paragraphs 4 & 5):** Most dangerously, Paragraph 4 stipulates that any developing member invoking the Peace Clause must ensure that stocks procured under such programmes **"do not distort trade or adversely affect the food security of other Members."** Because India simultaneously operates the world's largest public food-security procurement system (**FCI/NFSA**) and serves as the **world's largest exporter of rice** (accounting for **~40% of global rice trade**, or 17 to 22 million tonnes annually), the **United States, Australia, Thailand, Paraguay, and Uruguay** repeatedly accuse India at WTO Committee on Agriculture sessions of leaking subsidized FCI stocks into global export channels or using high MSPs to cross-subsidize cheap commercial exports, threatening to challenge the validity of India's Peace Clause invocation on the grounds that Paragraph 4 has been breached.

#### The PSH & SSM Stalemate: G-33 and African Group vs. the US and Cairns Group

At both **MC13 (Abu Dhabi)** and **MC14 (Yaoundé, March 2026)**, agricultural negotiations deadlocked over the sequencing and scope of reform:

* **The G-33, African Group, and ACP Coalition Proposal (Over 80 WTO Members):** Led by India and Indonesia, this coalition table-locked a joint ministerial text demanding that the **Permanent Solution on Public Stockholding (PSH)**—an explicit, legally mandated remnant of the **2013 Bali and 2015 Nairobi Ministerial Decisions**—be resolved **first and standalone**, without being held hostage to new market-access concessions. Their technical blueprint proposes four alternative or combined legal fixes:
  1. Amending **Annex 2, Paragraph 3 (Footnote 5)** so that public foodgrain procurement from low-income or resource-poor farmers at administered prices is unconditionally deemed **Green Box**, without transferring the price gap to the Amber Box.
  2. Replacing the archaic **1986–88 ERP** with a **dynamic three-year or five-year rolling average international reference price** based on the preceding years, or mandating automatic statutory adjustments for **excessive domestic inflation**.
  3. Restricting the calculation of **"Eligible Production"** strictly to the **actual physical quantity procured** by public agencies rather than total national output.
  4. Expanding coverage beyond pre-2013 programmes so that newly inducted pulses, oilseeds, and nutri-cereals (**Shree Anna / Millets**) procured for nutritional security enjoy permanent legal immunity alongside an operational **SSM**.
* **The US and Cairns Group Counter-Blockade:** The **United States** and the **19-member Cairns Group** (spearheaded by **Brazil, Australia, and Thailand**) blocked the G-33 text at both Abu Dhabi and Yaoundé. They insisted on a **"holistic package"** linking any concession on PSH to three toxic counter-demands: (a) a stringent **anti-export prohibition** barring any nation utilizing the PSH permanent solution from exporting the procured commodity (which would force India either to abandon its 40% share of global rice trade or dismantle FCI procurement); (b) capping and phasing out **Article 6.2 Development Box exemptions** for large developing countries based on their share of global agricultural production; and (c) mandatory reductions in India's high **Bound Tariffs**. Rejecting this attempt to extract fresh "payment" for a 2013 Bali mandate that developing countries had already paid for by ratifying the Trade Facilitation Agreement, India stood firm, resulting in the agriculture dossier being deferred back to Geneva at the conclusion of MC14 on March 30, 2026.

#### The Fisheries Subsidies Clash: Phase-I Entry into Force (Sept 2025) vs. MC14 Phase-II Collapse over OCOF

Anchored to **UN Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Target 14.6**, the WTO negotiations on fisheries subsidies aim to discipline the estimated **$35 billion in annual global government subsidies** ($22 billion of which are classified as capacity-enhancing) that accelerate the biological collapse of marine fish stocks. This negotiation bifurcated into two distinct institutional tracks:

* **Entry into Force of the Phase-I WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies ("Fish 1" — September 15, 2025):** Adopted at the **12th Ministerial Conference (MC12, Geneva, June 17, 2022)**, the Phase-I Agreement is the WTO's first multilateral treaty placing environmental sustainability at its core. Following ratification by more than two-thirds of WTO members, the **Phase-I Agreement formally entered into force on September 15, 2025**. It establishes binding prohibitions across three narrow pillars:
  1. **Article 3 — Subsidies Contributing to Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated (IUU) Fishing:** Prohibits any member from granting or maintaining subsidies to vessels or operators engaged in IUU fishing as determined by a coastal state, flag state, or relevant Regional Fishery Management Organization (**RFMO**).
  2. **Article 4 — Subsidies Regarding Overfished Stocks:** Prohibits subsidies for fishing or fishing-related activities regarding an **overfished stock**, *unless* the subsidies herself or other fishery management measures are implemented to **rebuild the stock to a biologically sustainable level**.
  3. **Article 5 — Subsidies for Unregulated High Seas Fishing:** Prohibits all subsidies for fishing on the high seas outside the competence of a coastal state and outside the jurisdiction of a competent **RFMO**.
  * *Exemptions and Peace Clause in Phase-I:* Crucially, **inland fisheries and aquaculture are wholly exempt** from the Agreement, and developing countries benefit from a **two-year "Peace Clause" (ramp-up period)** from the date of entry into force during which subsidies granted within their Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs) under Articles 3 and 4 cannot be challenged via WTO dispute settlement.
* **The MC13 & MC14 Collapse of Phase-II Negotiations on Overcapacity and Overfishing (OCOF — "Fish 2"):** Because the 2022 MC12 compromise deferred disciplines on general fuel, boat-building, gear-modernization, and price-support subsidies that contribute to **Overcapacity and Overfishing (OCOF)**, Phase-II negotiations dominated both **MC13 (2024)** and **MC14 (March 2026)**. Under the built-in **Article 12 Sunset Clause** of the 2022 Agreement, comprehensive OCOF disciplines must be adopted within four years of the Phase-I Agreement's entry into force (i.e., by **September 2029**), failing which the entire Agreement risks termination unless the General Council decides otherwise.
* **India's Principled Defense of 9 Million Artisanal Fishers:** At both Abu Dhabi and Yaoundé, India refused to endorse the unbalanced draft **Chair's Text (WT/MIN(24)/W/10 and its 2026 revisions)** on OCOF, exposing a glaring ecological hypocrisy:
  1. **Per-Capita vs. Aggregate Subsidy Fallacy & Distant-Water Fishing Nations (DWFNs):** Major industrial **Distant-Water Fishing Nations (DWFNs)**—including **China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and the United States**—deploy massive factory-trawler fleets across global oceans, providing **$10,000 to $75,000 in annual subsidies per fisher**. By contrast, India has virtually zero distant-water industrial fleet and provides less than **$15 to $35 per fisher per year** in basic livelihood support (kerosene/diesel rebates, ice boxes, safety transponders, and **PM Matsya Sampada Yojana / FIDF** shore infrastructure) spread across **9 million traditional, small-scale, and artisanal fishers**. Yet the draft OCOF text sought to divide developing nations into Two Tiers: exempting only developing countries Whose share of global marine capture volume is **below 0.8%** while subjecting developing nations above 0.8% (such as **India**, whose vast coastline and 4+ million tonnes of marine catch place it around ~4–5% of global marine catch due to sheer demographic scale) to rigid subsidy prohibitions after a mere 7-to-10-year transition.
  2. **India's Three Non-Negotiable OCOF Pillars:** India demanded that any Phase-II OCOF treaty must: (a) enforce the **Polluter-Pays Principle / Common But Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)** by imposing permanent bans on **Distant-Water Fishing subsidies** beyond national jurisdictions and removing historical green-box-style carve-outs for industrial nations boasting advanced stock-monitoring infrastructure; (b) grant developing countries not engaged in distant-water fishing a **25-year transition period** to build deep-sea harvesting capacity; and (c) guarantee an unconditional **sovereign exemption for fishing within a developing nation's 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)** without onerous vessel-level notification requirements that artisanal fishers cannot fulfill.

#### The March 2026 MC14 Lapse of the 1998 E-Commerce Customs Duty Moratorium

While agriculture and fisheries deadlocked, the most seismic institutional rupture at **MC14 in Yaoundé (26–30 March 2026)** occurred in the digital arena: on **March 30, 2026**, the **1998 WTO Moratorium on Customs Duties on Electronic Transmissions formally expired for the first time in 28 years** after Trade Ministers failed to reach consensus on its renewal.

* **The Fiscal and Industrial Economics of the 1998 Moratorium:** Adopted as a temporary political declaration at the **2nd WTO Ministerial Conference in Geneva (May 1998)** when the global internet economy was in its infancy, and routinely extended every two years thereafter, the Moratorium barred member states from levying customs tariffs on cross-border "electronic transmissions" (encompassing software, cloud computing services, audiovisual media, e-books, 3D-printing CAD files, and proprietary AI/data feeds). As physical tradable goods (books, films, machinery blueprints, agricultural diagnostic tools) digitized into intangible bitstreams, studies by **UNCTAD** and the **South Centre** demonstrated that developing countries were losing **$10 billion to $15+ billion annually in forgone tariff revenues**, while surrendering their infant-industry policy space to Silicon Valley and East Asian digital monopolies.
* **The Breakdown at Yaoundé (March 2026) and the Plurilateral Split:** At MC14, the **United States** initially demanded a permanent ban and subsequently proposed a five-year extension, whereas **Brazil** insisted on the strict two-year renewal norm and a coalition of developing nations (**South Africa, Indonesia, and India**) refused to perpetuate an unconditional tariff freeze that hollowed out domestic digital industrialization. Consequently, the multilateral moratorium lapsed on **March 30, 2026** (alongside the expiration of the moratorium on **TRIPS non-violation and situation complaints**). In response, **66 WTO members** (representing ~70% of global trade) announced an interim **Plurilateral E-Commerce Agreement (ECA)** binding participating signatories not to impose customs duties on electronic transmissions among themselves once 45 members ratify it, while non-signatory developing nations including **India** preserved complete sovereign regulatory and fiscal autonomy.
* **Strategic Implications for Indian Digital Agriculture:** The expiration of the 1998 Moratorium directly safeguards India's burgeoning **Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) in Agriculture** (analyzed in Part 3). As multinational agribusiness conglomerates increasingly monetize agriculture not just through physical seeds and chemicals, but through proprietary **cross-border digital agronomy subscriptions, prescription-planting algorithms, satellite hyper-spectral yield analytics, and automated IoT farm-machinery software**, the lapse of the moratorium empowers the Indian state to: (1) impose calibrated customs duties on foreign proprietary agri-data and SaaS transmissions if needed to prevent digital rent extraction; (2) protect sovereign open-source public stacks—**AgriStack (Farmer Registry, Geo-Referenced Village Maps, Digital Crop Survey)**, **Krishi-DSS**, **ONDC (Open Network for Digital Commerce)** for FPOs, and the **Union Budget 2026–27 Bharat-VISTAAR** multilingual AI advisory platform—from unfair tax-free foreign platform dumping; and (3) enforce sovereign data localization for Indian soil, aquifer, and genomic datasets. Furthermore, MC14 did successfully adopt two consensus ministerial decisions: one on the integration of small economies into the multilateral trading system, and a crucial decision **strengthening Special and Differential Treatment (S&DT) in Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) measures and Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT)**.

## III. India’s Agriculture Export Policy, Institutional Architecture (APEDA/MPEDA), and Non-Tariff Barrier (SPS/TBT) Regimes

### 6. Macroeconomics of Indian Agricultural Trade and the Agriculture Export Policy (AEP, 2018)

Despite operating on 2.4% of global land area and 4% of renewable freshwater resources while feeding 17.7% of the world's population, India has consolidated its position among the **top ten global agricultural exporters**, maintaining an annual agricultural export corridor between **$48.8 billion and $53.1 billion across FY 2022–23 to FY 2025–26** and consistently generating a net agricultural trade surplus of **$15 billion to $20 billion** (even after accounting for **$20–$25 billion in annual edible oil and pulse imports**). India's agricultural export basket is anchored by seven dominant commodity verticals: **Marine Products** ($7.3–$8.0 billion, led by frozen *Litopenaeus vannamei* shrimp), **Basmati Rice** ($5.4–$5.9 billion), **Non-Basmati Rice** ($4.5–$6.5 billion), **Spices** ($4.2–$4.5 billion), **Buffalo Meat (Carabeef)** ($3.5–$3.8 billion), **Sugar** (fluctuating between $2.0 and $5.5 billion based on export quotas), and **Fresh and Processed Fruits, Vegetables, Coffee, and Oilmeals**.

To transition Indian agrarian trade from residual surplus disposal to value-added global supply-chain integration, the Union Cabinet enacted the comprehensive **Agriculture Export Policy (AEP, 2018)**, anchored to the twin objectives of diversifying the export basket/destinations and boosting high-value, GI-tagged, and organic agricultural exports.

#### Structural Pillars of the Agriculture Export Policy (AEP, 2018) and Export Clusters

The **Agriculture Export Policy (AEP, 2018)** disaggregates state intervention into two interlocking operational pillars:

* **Pillar 1 — Strategic Policy Recommendations:**
  1. **Stable Trade Policy Regime:** Categorizing agricultural commodities into three structural buckets— essential food security commodities, organic/ethnic/GI products, and high-value processed goods—and advocating against arbitrary, knee-jerk export bans or Minimum Export Prices (MEPs), ensuring complete exemption of **organic and processed agricultural products** from export restrictions.
  2. **Reforming Domestic APMC Marketing Bottlenecks:** Encouraging states to exempt export-oriented procurement by FPOs and processors from cascading **APMC mandi fees, market cesses, and commission agent (*Arhtiya*) charges**, alongside legalizing contract farming and land leasing (via the **2016 Model Land Leasing Act**) to enable contiguous export-grade cultivation estates.
* **Pillar 2 — Operational Recommendations:**
  1. **Cluster-Based Export Production (50+ Product-District Clusters):** Recognizing that fragmented smallholders cannot individually meet global phytosanitary standards, the AEP established **over 50 specialized Export Clusters** across India (subsequently integrated with the **One District One Product — ODOP** and **Districts as Export Hubs** frameworks)—such as **Nashik and Sangli** for table grapes, **Solapur** for pomegranates, **Chittoor and Ratnagiri** for mangoes, **Anantapur and Jalgaon** for bananas, **Guntur** for chillies, **Erode** for turmeric, and **Indore/Mehsana** for processable potatoes.
  2. **Sea Protocol and Cold-Chain Logistics:** Developing specialized **Sea Protocols** (controlled-atmosphere reefer containers with ethylene scrubbers and precise humidity control) to shift perishable horticulture exports (mangoes, bananas, pomegranates) from high-cost air freight to economical maritime shipping, slashing freight costs by **60% to 70%** and unlocking European and Middle Eastern supermarket chains.

#### Statutory Export Promotion Bodies: APEDA (1985), MPEDA (1972), and Commodity Boards

India's institutional apparatus for enforcing international food-safety compliance and promoting agrarian exports under the **Ministry of Commerce and Industry** comprises two apex statutory authorities and five specialized statutory Commodity Boards (**Coffee Board, Tea Board, Rubber Board, Spices Board, and Tobacco Board**):

* **Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority (APEDA, 1985):** Established under the **APEDA Act, 1985**, APEDA holds statutory jurisdiction over the export promotion, infrastructural financing, and quality certification of **Scheduled Products** (fruits, vegetables, meat and poultry products, dairy products, confectionery, honey, jaggery, cocoa, cereal products including Basmati and non-basmati rice, groundnuts, guar gum, herbal/medicinal plants, and alcoholic/non-alcoholic beverages). APEDA also acts as the **Secretariat for the National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP)** (accrediting third-party organic certification bodies for export) and administers statutory **Geographical Indication (GI)** protection and levy funds for **Basmati Rice** via the **Basmati Export Development Foundation (BEDF)**.
  * *APEDA's Digital Traceability Stacks:* To prevent bulk border rejections by European and American food safety regulators, APEDA engineered end-to-end internet- and blockchain-enabled traceability platforms (**Hortinet** integrating **Grapenet, Mangonet, Anarnet, and Vegnet**; **Peanut.net** for groundnuts; **Meat.net**; and **TraceNet** for organic products). Every consignment of table grapes exported to the EU, for instance, carries a unique **Grapenet** digital passport linking the retail carton in Rotterdam back to the exact vineyard plot in Nashik, the state horticulture officer's field inspection report, the accredited pack-house, and the **NABL-accredited laboratory's** gas-chromatography pesticide residue report.
* **Marine Products Export Development Authority (MPEDA, 1972):** Established under the **MPEDA Act, 1972** (headquartered in Kochi), MPEDA regulates and promotes India's **$7.5+ billion marine export sector**. Because over 70% of India's seafood export value derives from cultured **Pacific White Shrimp (*Litopenaeus vannamei*)** and **Black Tiger Shrimp (*Penaeus monodon*)** destined for the United States, the European Union, China, and Japan, MPEDA operates a multi-tiered biosecurity architecture:
  1. **National Residue Control Plan (NRCP) & ELISA Screening Labs:** Operating a network of high-sensitivity **ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay)** and **LC-MS/MS** laboratories across coastal aquaculture hubs in Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu to screen shrimp broodstock, hatcheries, and harvest ponds for banned antibiotics (**Nitrofurans, Chloramphenicol, Oxytetracycline**), preventing costly **US FDA Import Alerts** and **EU Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF)** border detentions.
  2. **NETFISH and NaCSA (National Centre for Sustainable Aquaculture):** Organizing marginal coastal aqua-farmers into cluster societies to eliminate antibiotic usage through biosecure Zero-Water-Exchange probiotics and certifying sustainable marine catch under the **Shaphari** certification scheme.

### 7. The Non-Tariff Barrier (NTB) Gauntlet: WTO SPS/TBT Agreements, EU MRLs, and the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)

As the Uruguay Round bound customs tariffs and abolished quantitative import quotas, advanced economies increasingly shifted the locus of agricultural protectionism toward **Non-Tariff Barriers (NTBs)** governed by two companion WTO treaties: the **Agreement on the Application of Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures (SPS Agreement)** and the **Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT Agreement)**.

While the **SPS Agreement** governs measures necessary to protect human, animal, or plant life or health from biological and chemical risks (foodborne additives, contaminants, pesticide residues, mycotoxins, and invasive pests), **Article 2.2 and Article 5 of the SPS Agreement** strictly mandate that SPS measures must be based on **sufficient scientific evidence** and an **objective toxicological Risk Assessment**, and **Article 3** encourages harmonization around international scientific standards set by the **"Three Sisters"**—most notably the **FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius Commission (Codex)** for food safety, the **World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH)** for animal health, and the **International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC)** for plant pests. Conversely, the **TBT Agreement** governs technical regulations, voluntary standards, packaging, eco-labelling, nutritional front-of-pack markings, and production process standards, requiring under **Article 2.2** that technical standards not be more trade-restrictive than necessary to fulfill a legitimate policy objective.

#### Pesticide Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs), Mycotoxins, and Microbiological Rejections

In practice, advanced importing blocs—above all the **European Union**—routinely bypass **Codex Alimentarius** scientific benchmarks by invoking the **"Precautionary Principle"** (citing **SPS Article 5.7**) to impose unilateral **Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs)** set at the **Default Limit of Determination (LOD) of 0.01 mg/kg (10 parts per billion)**. Crucially, **0.01 mg/kg** is not derived from toxicological evidence of human health harm; it is simply the lowest threshold detectable by modern mass-spectrometry instruments—effectively operating as a zero-tolerance ban against tropical agro-chemicals that European farmers do not need in temperate climates:

* **The Basmati Rice Tricyclazole Crisis and ICAR’s Molecular Breeding Response:** In **2017–2018**, the European Union abruptly slashed the MRL for **Tricyclazole**—a systemic fungicide essential for controlling **Neck Blast (*Magnaporthe oryzae*)** in humid monsoon paddy fields—from **1.0 mg/kg down to the default 0.01 mg/kg** (followed by similar cuts for **Buprofezin, Carbendazim, and Propiconazole**), even though the US and Japan maintained Tricyclazole MRLs at 3.0 mg/kg and 8.0 mg/kg respectively. Because Tricyclazole binds tightly to the bran layer, India's exports of **husks/brown Basmati rice** to the EU collapsed from ~3.6 lakh tonnes to under 1 lakh tonnes within 24 months. To break this chemical NTB structurally, **ICAR–Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI)** deployed **Marker-Assisted Backcross Breeding (MABB)** to introgress natural resistance genes against both **Bacterial Leaf Blight (*Xa21, xa13*)** and **Rice Blast (*Pi54,Pi2*)** into India's premier export varieties (Pusa Basmati 1121, 1509, and 1401), releasing **Pusa Basmati 1847, Pusa Basmati 1885, and Pusa Basmati 1886**. Cultivated across Punjab, Haryana, and Western UP during 2023–2026, these bio-resistant varieties eliminate the agronomic need to spray Tricyclazole or Streptocycline altogether, restoring India's European Basmati market share.
* **Ethylene Oxide (EtO) and Aflatoxin Rejections in Spices and Oilseeds:** Between 2020 and 2025, Indian shipments of **Sesame Seeds** to the EU and blended spice shipments (**MDH and Everest** curry powders in Singapore, Hong Kong, the EU, and the US in 2024) faced severe border recalls over traces of **Ethylene Oxide (EtO)**—a gaseous sterilizing agent used post-harvest to control *Salmonella* and *E. coli* microbial contamination in humid tropical spices, which the EU classifies as a genotoxic carcinogen with a 0.02–0.1 mg/kg MRL. Simultaneously, Indian **Groundnuts, nutmeg, and red chillies** face recurrent EU RASFF rejections over **Aflatoxin B1/B2/G1/G2** (carcinogenic mycotoxins produced by *Aspergillus flavus* fungi during damp post-harvest storage). In response, the **Spices Board of India** mandated compulsory pre-shipment **EtO and Aflatoxin testing**, banned EtO sterilization in export processing lines, and accelerated the rollout of **steam-sterilization and gamma-irradiation** facilities under **PM Kisan SAMPADA Yojana**.

#### Green Protectionism: The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) and Carbon Border Dynamics

Beyond classical SPS food-safety filters, Indian agrarian exports now confront a new generation of **unilateral extraterritorial environmental Non-Tariff Barriers** spearheaded by the **European Green Deal**:

* **Anatomy of the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR — Regulation (EU) 2023/1115):** Enacted in June 2023 (with operational enforcement phased in across 2025–2026 following a 12-month implementation extension), the **EUDR** prohibits placing seven core agricultural and forestry commodities—**Coffee, Cocoa, Natural Rubber, Oil Palm, Soy, Cattle (including Beef and Leather), and Wood/Paper/Furniture**—and their derived products onto the European Union market (or exporting them from the EU) unless the operator submits a legally binding **Due Diligence Statement** proving three cumulative conditions:
  1. **Deforestation-Free Post-2020 Cut-Off:** The commodity was not produced on land that underwent deforestation or forest degradation after **December 31, 2020** (incorporating the FAO definition of forest: land spanning >0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 meters and a canopy cover of >10%).
  2. **Plot-Level Polygon Geolocation Coordinates:** The operator must collect and transmit to EU customs authorities the exact **GPS latitude and longitude coordinates (to at least six decimal places)** of every single agricultural plot where the raw commodity was harvested—requiring a single GPS point for plots **under 4 hectares** and full **polygon perimeter mapping** for plots **above 4 hectares**, with zero mixing of compliant and unknown-origin beans or latex throughout the milling and shipping pipeline.
  3. **Compliance with Domestic Land-Use and Labour Legislation:** Documented proof that the harvest complied with all domestic land-tenure, environmental protection, forest-rights (**FRA 2006**), and labour statutes of the country of production.
* **Asymmetric Burden on Indian Coffee, Rubber, and Leather Smallholders (~$1.3 Billion Exposure):** While India experiences net afforestation rather than commodity-driven deforestation, the **EUDR** directly hits roughly **$1.3 billion of annual Indian exports to the EU**—most critically **Indian Coffee** (where ~70% of India's ~$1.28 billion coffee exports go to Europe, primarily Italy, Germany, and Belgium, grown by **3.66 lakh smallholders in Kodagu, Chikkamagaluru, Hassan, and Wayanad** using shade-grown agro-forestry under native forest canopies) and **Natural Rubber/Tyres** grown by marginal farmers in **Kerala and Tripura**. Paradoxically, because Indian coffee is **shade-grown beneath dense native tree canopies (>10% canopy cover)**, satellite optical sensors used by European auditors frequently misclassify sustainable Indian shade-coffee plantations as "forest degradation" whenever shade trees are pruned! Furthermore, because **Presumptive Land Titling** and informal oral tenancies persist in hilly plantation belts (analyzed in Part 1), proving formal statutory land-title legality for every 1-hectare grower imposes prohibitive compliance costs on smallholders.
* **India's Dual Institutional Response (Geolocation DPI + WTO Challenge):** To prevent the exclusion of Indian coffee and rubber smallholders from European supply chains, the **Coffee Board of India** and the **Rubber Board** rapidly deployed the **India-EUDR Geolocation & Traceability Platform** in 2024–2025, geo-tagging over 4 lakh coffee and rubber farm plots free of cost for smallholders and issuing blockchain-verified deforestation-free certificates. Simultaneously, at the **WTO Committee on Trade and Environment** and during **MC13/MC14** ( culminating in the **March 2026 Yaoundé Ministerial Decision on strengthening S&DT in SPS and TBT measures**), India and a coalition of developing agro-exporters (Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Colombia) formally challenged the EUDR and the EU's long-term trajectory toward extending its **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)** to fertilizers and agro-products as discriminatory, WTO-incompatible unilateral green protectionism that violates the **Rio Principle of Common But Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)**.

## IV. Domestic Price-Control Export Bans, Minimum Export Prices (MEPs), and Strategic Trade Calibration

### 8. The Domestic Consumer vs. Producer Surplus Dilemma: Export Bans, MEPs, and Export Duties

While external Non-Tariff Barriers constrain market access abroad, the most severe self-inflicted volatility in India's agricultural trade stems from its domestic **"Stop-Go Consumer-Bias Trade Policy."** Operating under Section 3 and Section 5 of the **Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992**, the **Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT)** and the **Department of Revenue (Ministry of Finance)** frequently deploy sudden border export restrictions whenever erratic monsoons, heatwaves, or international geopolitical shocks threaten to elevate domestic food prices.

The political-economy driver of this interventionism lies in the macroeconomic structure of Indian inflation: **Food and Beverages carry an overwhelming 45.86% weight in India's Consumer Price Index (CPI-Combined)**—far higher than in advanced economies (13–15%) or peer emerging markets (20–25%). Because the **Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** is statutorily mandated to keep headline CPI inflation within the **4% ± 2% target band**, and because over 80 crore citizens are net food buyers whose real wages are rapidly eroded by cereal, pulse, and vegetable price spikes, the state systematically prioritizes **short-term domestic consumer surplus and electoral price stability** over **long-term producer export realization**.

#### Anatomy of the 2022–2025 Export Restrictions and Their 2024–2026 Calibrated Unwinding

Following the February 2022 Russia–Ukraine conflict, the March 2022 terminal heatwave across the Indo-Gangetic Plains, and the 2023–24 El Niño monsoon deficit, the Indian state deployed an unprecedented matrix of **four border restriction instruments** across essential crops, before executing a calibrated unwinding across late 2024 to 2026 once **FCI buffer stocks** and Kharif harvests recovered:

1. **Outright Quantitative Export Bans (Prohibited Category):**
   * **Wheat (May 2022 – Present):** After declaring in April 2022 that India would "feed the world," a sudden March heatwave shaved domestic wheat yields and caused private traders to outbid FCI at mandis, dropping FCI wheat procurement from 43.3 million tonnes (2021–22) to 18.8 million tonnes (2022–23). On **May 13, 2022**, DGFT abruptly banned commercial wheat exports (subsequently extending restrictions to wheat flour/atta, maida, and semolina).
   * **Broken Rice (September 2022) & Non-Basmati White Rice (July 2023 – September 2024):** In **July 2023**, India banned all commercial exports of **Non-Basmati White Rice**—instantly withdrawing ~10 million tonnes of supply from the global market and triggering a **15-year high in the FAO All Rice Price Index** across import-dependent West African and Asian nations.
   * **Onions (December 2023 – May 2024):** Following erratic Rabi storage and delayed Kharif arrivals in Maharashtra, the government banned onion exports outright during the peak winter harvest window, crashing farm-gate prices in **Lasalgaon and Pimpalgaon mandis** from ₹4,000/quintal to ₹1,200–₹1,500/quintal.
2. **Minimum Export Prices (MEPs — Price Floor Barriers):**
   * An **MEP** prohibits customs clearance of any export contract priced below a state-mandated dollar floor per metric tonne. In **August 2023**, fearing that traders would misclassify restricted non-basmati white rice as aromatic Basmati to evade the July ban, the government imposed an exorbitant **$1,200/tonne MEP on Basmati Rice**—even though over 60% of India's traditional Basmati export varieties (such as Pusa 1509 and 1121 parboiled/steam) traded globally between **$850 and $1,050/tonne**. Only after intense protests from exporters and farmers in Punjab and Haryana was the Basmati MEP lowered to **$950/tonne in October 2023**, and finally **abolished entirely in September 2024**. Similarly, an **$800/tonne MEP** (later **$550/tonne**) was imposed on **Onions** before being scrapped in **September 2024**.
3. **Ad Valorem Export Duties (Fiscal Border Taxes):**
   * Simultaneously with the white rice ban, the Ministry of Finance imposed a **20% export duty on Parboiled Rice and Husked (Brown) Rice** and a **40% export duty on Onions** (later reduced to 20% in September 2024 and eliminated in early 2025), using fiscal taxation to capture windfall global price spreads and discourage outbound shipments.
4. **Quantitative Export Quotas & The 2024–2026 Calibrated Unwinding:**
   * In the **Sugar sector**, having exported a record **11.1 million tonnes in Sugar Season (SS) 2021–22**, the government capped exports at **6.1 million tonnes in SS 2022–23**, imposed a total freeze in **SS 2023–24**, and subsequently permitted a calibrated **1 million tonne export quota in early 2025** after domestic inventories and ethanol diversion stabilized.
   * Similarly, by **September–October 2024** (and continuing through **2025–2026**), as FCI rice inventories surged past **50 million tonnes** (more than 3.5 times the buffer norm), India systematically dismantled its rice and onion choke-points: **removing the Basmati MEP completely**, **lifting the ban on Non-Basmati White Rice** (initially with a $490/tonne MEP in Sept 2024, which was itself scrapped in Oct 2024 alongside the zeroing out of export duties on parboiled and brown rice).

### 9. Microeconomic and Geopolitical Pathologies of Stop-Go Export Controls

When evaluated through the lens of agrarian political economy and multilateral trade theory, arbitrary stop-go export bans and MEPs inflict three severe structural pathologies on Indian agriculture:

* **Implicit Taxation of Farmers and Negative Producer Support Estimates (PSE):** Whenever domestic harvests are abundant, Indian farmers face depressed farm-gate prices (often 15% to 25% below MSP for unprocured crops); yet the very moment a global supply shock or El Niño event elevates international commodity prices—creating a rare opportunity for Indian cultivators to capture high market-driven export realizations—the state closes the border via export bans or 40% export duties. This asymmetric intervention creates a structural **"Heads the Consumer Wins, Tails the Farmer Loses"** regime. Consequently, joint studies by **ICRIER and the OECD** demonstrated that between 2000 and 2023, despite receiving massive fertilizer, electricity, and irrigation input subsidies, Indian farmers suffered a deeply **negative Producer Support Estimate (PSE) averaging -14% to -18% of gross farm receipts**, because domestic price-depressing export restrictions extracted far more income from farmers than input subsidies returned to them.
* **Permanent Loss of Global Market Share ("The Unreliable Supplier Discount"):** Global retail chains, food processors, and sovereign grain importers in the Middle East, Europe, East Africa, and Southeast Asia require predictable, year-round supply-chain contracts. When India imposed the **$1,200/tonne MEP on Basmati Rice** in 2023, **Pakistan** (whose Basmati is grown in contiguous agro-climatic zones across the border without an MEP floor or at a lower $1,050/tonne floor) increased its Basmati exports by **over 40% to nearly 1 million tonnes**, capturing prime supermarket shelf space across the UK, EU, and Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC). Similarly, India's 2023–24 onion export ban allowed **Egypt, Turkey, China, and Pakistan** to displace Indian red onions in Bangladesh, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, and the UAE.
* **Hawala Rent-Seeking, Misclassification, and WTO Article 12 Contestation:** Artificially high MEPs induce pervasive commercial corruption: when global market prices for Basmati stood at $900/tonne while India's statutory MEP was fixed at $1,200/tonne, exporters were forced to invoice shipments on paper at $1,200/tonne and remit the $300/tonne difference back to foreign buyers via illegal **hawala channels** ("under-the-table discounts"), criminalizing legitimate trade. At the multilateral level, while **Article XI:2(a) of GATT 1994** permits temporary export prohibitions to prevent or relieve *"critical shortages of foodstuffs,"* **Article 12.1 of the WTO AoA** mandates that any member instituting a new export restriction must give due consideration to the food security of importing members, provide advance written notice to the **Committee on Agriculture**, and consult with affected importers (though **Article 12.2** exempts developing country members unless they are "net-food exporters of the specific foodstuff concerned"—a threshold India clearly crosses in **Rice**, subjecting India's 2022–2024 rice bans to intense legal scrutiny by Japan, the US, the EU, and West African nations at the WTO).

#### Institutionalizing Predictable Trade Calibration: Rules-Based Tariff-Rate Corridors and NCEL

To reconcile domestic food-inflation stability with export credibility and farmer income realization, India's trade architecture requires replacing discretionary administrative bans with two institutionalized mechanisms:

1. **Formulaic, Rules-Based Variable Export Tariffs vs. Blunt Quantitative Bans:** As recommended by the **Ashok Dalwai Committee on Doubling Farmers' Income** and the **Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP)**, outright export bans and static MEPs should be replaced by a **transparent, rules-based sliding-scale export tariff corridor** linked automatically to objective empirical triggers (such as FCI buffer stock-to-norm ratios and 90-day moving averages of wholesale/retail prices). A sliding tariff allows high-value branded and long-term contractual exports to flow continuously while generating fiscal revenue that can be ring-fenced into the **Price Stabilization Fund (PSF)** to subsidize targeted urban consumer retail intervention (e.g., *Bharat Atta, Bharat Rice, Bharat Dal*) without crashing rural mandi prices.
2. **Sovereign G2G Food Diplomacy via National Cooperative Exports Limited (NCEL):** Established in **January 2023** under the **Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act, 2002** (jointly promoted by **GCMMF/AMUL, IFFCO, KRIBHCO, NAFED, and NCDC**), **National Cooperative Exports Limited (NCEL)** acts as an umbrella cooperative export house aggregating produce directly from **63,000+ computerized PACS** and **10,000+ FPOs**. Even during periods when commercial private exports were restricted (2023–2024), the Indian government utilized **NCEL** to execute **Government-to-Government (G2G) food security exports** of over **1.6 to 2.5 million tonnes of non-basmati rice, onions, and sugar** to vulnerable partner nations (**UAE, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Mauritius, Singapore, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, and Mali**)—returning **at least 50% of net export profit margins directly into the bank accounts of supplying PACS/FPO member farmers** while preserving India's geopolitical standing as the Global South's food-security anchor.
