# MASTER COMPENDIUM OF HEURISTICS & RAZORS

*A unified cheat sheet of mental models spanning epistemology, scientific inquiry, ethical reasoning, and systems productivity.*

## 1. Epistemological Heuristics (Truth & Logic)

### Occam's Razor
*"Entities should not be multiplied without necessity."*

Or as fancy folks say, "The simplest explanation is often the best one."

When you have two competing theories that make the exact same predictions, the simpler one is usually the correct one. You should shave off unnecessary assumptions.

> **Eg:** If your lamp won't turn on, assume the bulb is burnt out (simple), not that the power company specifically targeted your house (complex).

### Sagan's Standard
*"Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence."*

The weight of the evidence must match the weight of the claim.

> **Eg:** If you claim to ride a horse, I believe you. If you claim to ride a dragon, I need more than your word; I need irrefutable physical proof to justify that knowledge.

### Zebra Principle
*"When you hear hoofbeats, think of horses, not zebras."*

A probabilistic razor used often in diagnostics. Common explanations are statistically more likely to be true than rare, exotic ones.

> **Eg:** If you have a headache, assume you are dehydrated, not that you have a rare tropical brain disease.

### The Duck Test
*"If it looks like a duck, swims like a duck, and quacks like a duck, then it probably is a duck."*

A form of abductive reasoning. It implies that a person can identify an unknown subject by observing its habitual characteristics.

### Survivorship Bias (Wald's Principle)
*"Examine the silent evidence that failed to survive the selection filter, not just the visible winners."*

Formulated from mathematician Abraham Wald's WWII analysis of returning bombers. Focusing exclusively on the data points that "survived" a process leads to inverted, dangerously flawed conclusions.

> **Eg:** WWII commanders wanted to add armor to the wingtips where returning bombers had the most bullet holes. Wald pointed out they should armor the engines—where returning planes had zero bullet holes—because planes hit in the engine never made it back to be counted.

## 2. Philosophy of Science Heuristics

### Popper's Razor (Falsifiability Principle)
*"For a theory to be considered scientific, it must be testable and refutable."*

If a theory cannot be proven wrong by any conceivable test, it is not science—it is pseudoscience or religion. Science requires risk; a theory must be capable of failing a test to be valid.

The statement "Invisible ghosts exist" is not science because there is no test you could ever run to prove it wrong.

### Alder's Razor (Newton's Flaming Laser Sword)
*"What cannot be settled by experiment is not worth debating."*

This is a pragmatic razor. It suggests that if a philosophical argument has no physical consequences and cannot be tested, debating it is a waste of time.

> **Eg:** Debating whether we live in a Matrix simulation is a waste of time. If the simulation is perfect, it is indistinguishable from reality. Since you can never test it, stop arguing about it and live your life.

### Einstein's Razor
*"Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler."*

This acts as a check against Occam's Razor. It warns that if you strip away too much complexity, you lose the nuance required to describe reality accurately. Models should be streamlined, but not to the point of error.

> **Eg:** Teaching that "electrons orbit the nucleus like planets do" is simple and helpful. But for a chemist, it is too simple because it fails to explain how atoms actually bond. You need the complex quantum model for that.

### Feynman's Razor
*"If you cannot explain a concept in simple terms without jargon, you do not truly understand it."*

Acts as a safeguard against the illusion of explanatory depth. Hiding behind technical vocabulary and buzzwords often masks a lack of fundamental comprehension of the underlying mechanism.

> **Eg:** Memorizing the term "sonoluminescence" does not mean you understand the physics; explaining how a collapsing sound-wave bubble in water compresses gas until it flashes light proves genuine understanding.

## 3. Rhetorical & Meta-Ethical Heuristics

### Hitchens's Razor
*"What can be asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence."*

This defines the Burden of Proof.

> **Eg:** If someone says Hitler lives on the far side of the moon without providing the evidence, I don't need to send rovers to verify if the statement is true; I'll just refute the premise.

### Hanlon's Razor
*"Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity."*

Negative outcomes are usually the result of neglect, ignorance, or incompetence rather than an active, evil intent to cause harm.

> **Eg:** The auto driver who cut you off probably just didn't see you in their blind spot; he isn't actively trying to ruin your day.

### Grice's Razor & The Principle of Charity
*"Address what the speaker actually meant, rather than the literal interpretation of their words."*

Productive conversation relies on interpreting people's conversational intent rather than multiplying literal word definitions (**Grice's Razor**), and not nitpicking their grammar or phrasing. Attack their best, most rational argument, not their clumsy word choice (**The Principle of Charity**).

> **Eg:** If someone says "I'm literally dying of hunger," cook them Maggi; don't lecture them on the definition of the word "literally."

### Hume's Guillotine (The Is-Ought Problem)
*"You cannot derive an 'ought' from an 'is'."*

Facts about the world (how things are) do not automatically create moral rules (how things should be). You cannot use "nature" or "tradition" as the sole justification for what is "good."

> **Eg:** Just because animals kill each other in nature (it is natural), doesn't mean humans ought to kill each other.

## 4. Systems & Productivity Mental Models

### Pareto Principle (The 80/20 Rule)
*"For many outcomes, roughly 80% of consequences come from 20% of the causes."*

A law of unequal distribution often used in business and economics. Focus on the vital few rather than the trivial many.

### Parkinson's Law
*"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."*

If you give yourself a week to complete a two-hour task, the task will artificially increase in complexity to take the entire week.

### Goodhart's Law
*"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."*

Once an organization ties rewards or penalties strictly to a single metric, people optimize and game the metric itself—often destroying the actual underlying goal the metric was meant to track.

### Brooks' Law
*"Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later."*

On complex projects, the time spent bringing new people up to speed (and the overhead of increased communication) cancels out their added productivity, ultimately delaying the project further.

### Law of Least Effort (Zipf's Principle of Least Effort)
*"People will naturally gravitate towards the path that requires the least effort in cognitive and physical tasks."*

Formulated by linguist George Kingsley Zipf (who also formulated *Zipf's Law* for mathematical word-frequency distributions). Useful for system design: if you want people to adopt a new behavior or use a new tool, it must be the path of absolute least resistance.

### Murphy's Law
*"Anything that can go wrong, will go wrong."*

Often used in defensive engineering and risk management to stress-test designs and ensure backup systems are in place before catastrophic failure occurs.

### Chesterton's Fence
*"Do not remove a fence until you know why it was put up in the first place."*

A core rule of second-order systems thinking: before deleting legacy code, abolishing an old rule, or redesigning an institution that seems pointless, first discover what original failure mode it was built to prevent.
