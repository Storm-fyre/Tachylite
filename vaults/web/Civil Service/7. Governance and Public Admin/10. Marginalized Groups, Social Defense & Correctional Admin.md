# Marginalized Populations, Social Defense & Correctional Administration

Protective legislation in India rarely fails for want of statutory text; it fails in the friction zone between constitutional promise and street-level administrative culture. Across women’s safety, child protection, tribal forest governance, disability access, transgender personhood, and prison custody, India has constructed one of the developing world’s densest rights-based legal grids—yet conviction deficits, non-constituted local committees, bureaucratic turf resistance, and custodial overcrowding routinely convert substantive rights into paper entitlements.

Between 2023 and November 2026, this domain experienced both major judicial expansions of dignity—Supreme Court verdicts abolishing caste segregation in prisons (*Sukanya Shantha*, 2024), declaring disability accessibility a fundamental right (*Rajive Raturi*, 2024), tripling sewer-death compensation (*Dr. Balram Singh*, 2023), and enforcing transgender workplace equality (*Jane Kaushik*, 2025)—and sharp legislative realignments, notably **PM-JANMAN**, **Dharti Aaba JGUA**, and the contested **Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026**.

## Gender Justice & Child Protection Architecture

### Workplace Safety & Domestic Protection Statutes
- **POSH Act, 2013 (*Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act*):** Codifying the Supreme Court’s *Vishaka* (1997) guidelines, POSH covers both formal and informal workplaces—explicitly including **domestic workers**. Every employer with **10 or more employees** must constitute an **Internal Committee (IC)** headed by a senior woman employee, with at least two employee members, one external NGO/legal member, and **at least 50% women**; districts must maintain a **Local Committee (LC)** for workplaces under 10 employees, domestic workers, or complaints against the employer directly, with inquiries mandated within **90 days**.
- ***Aureliano Fernandes v. State of Goa* (May 2023) & Revamped SHe-Box:** Exposing a "sorry state of affairs" where 16 of 30 national sports federations lacked functional ICs, the Supreme Court issued binding nationwide directives for institutional compliance audits, officer training, and strict natural justice (*audi alteram partem*). To operationalize visibility, MWCD launched the **revamped SHe-Box Portal (August 2024)**—a centralized national repository tracking public and private ICs nationwide and enabling confidential complaint routing directly to designated Nodal Officers.
- **PWDVA 2005, Dowry Prohibition & PCPNDT Acts:** The **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (PWDVA), 2005** provides rapid **civil remedies** (Protection, Residence, Monetary, and Custody Orders) covering physical, sexual, verbal, emotional, and economic abuse across married and **live-in relationships**, enforced via Protection Officers and Magistrates (criminal liability attaches only upon breaching a Protection Order). Complementing it, the **Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961** (paired with cruelty/dowry-death offences under BNS Sections 80 and 85) suffers from low conviction rates and protracted trial delays, while the **PCPNDT Act, 1994** banning pre-natal sex determination struggles against clandestine portable ultrasound clinics, leaving India’s Sex Ratio at Birth (SRB) slow to normalize (NFHS-5: 929; SRS 2021–23: ~917).

### Mission Shakti & Gender Budgeting
Operationalized by the **Ministry of Women & Child Development (MWCD)** across the 15th Finance Commission cycle (2021–26), **Mission Shakti** consolidates India’s women-centric safety and empowerment schemes into two sharply differentiated sub-schemes:
- ***Sambal* (Safety & Security — 100% Central Funding via Central Sector / Nirbhaya Fund):** Integrates **One Stop Centres (Sakhi)** providing integrated police/legal/medical/shelter relief, **181 Women Helpline**, **Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (BBBP)**, and the newly piloted ***Nari Adalat*** (community-level women’s alternative dispute resolution collectives).
- ***Samarthya* (Empowerment — 60:40 CSS for general states; 90:10 NE/Hill):** Houses **Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY)** (maternity DBT of **₹5,000 for the first child** plus an expanded **₹6,000 for a second child provided it is a girl**), **Shakti Sadan** (merging Swadhar Greh and Ujjawala anti-trafficking shelters), **Sakhi Niwas** (Working Women Hostels), ***Palna*** (revamped National Crèche Scheme expanding Anganwadi-cum-crèches to address women’s unpaid care barrier), and the National Hub for Empowerment of Women.

**Gender Budgeting (Statement 13):** Initiated in FY 2005–06 to track pro-women fiscal allocations across **Part A** (100% women-specific schemes), **Part B** (30%–99% allocation), and—introduced in FY 2024–25—**Part C** (<30% allocation), India’s Gender Budget crossed a historic **₹5.01 lakh crore (9.37% of total Union Budget)** in **FY 2026–27 BE** (up from 8.86% in FY 2025–26). Analytical scrutiny nonetheless flags that bulk allocations sit inside gender-neutral household infrastructure (PMAY, JJM, PMGKAY) rather than dedicated childcare (*Palna*), workplace mobility, or urban safety needed to lift urban female labour-force participation.

### Child Protection — POCSO 2012, JJ Act 2015/2021 & Mission Vatsalya
- **POCSO Act, 2012 & the Adolescent Consent Dilemma:** The gender-neutral **Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012** protects all minors **below 18 years** through Special Courts, in-camera trials, mandatory reporting (Sec 19), and—post its 2019 amendment—the death penalty for aggravated penetrative assault. Raising the statutory age of consent from 16 to **18** produced an unintended criminalization of consensual adolescent romance (ages 16–18): empirical High Court audits reveal roughly a quarter of POCSO trials stem from parents filing retaliatory charges in inter-caste or inter-faith elopements, incarcerating teenage boys. In its **283rd Report (September 2023)**, the Law Commission opposed lowering the general consent age to 16 (citing grooming and trafficking risks) but recommended **guided judicial sentencing discretion** for close-in-age consensual cases (16–18). By **January 2026 (*State of UP v. Anurudh*)**, the Supreme Court urged Parliament to enact a calibrated **"Romeo–Juliet" clause** shielding genuine adolescent autonomy without weakening deterrence against predators.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 & 2021 Amendment:** Separates **Child in Need of Care and Protection (CNCP)** (overseen by **Child Welfare Committees [CWCs]**) from **Child in Conflict with Law (CCL)** (adjudicated by **Juvenile Justice Boards [JJBs]**). Following public outrage post-2012, the 2015 Act permitted juveniles aged **16–18** accused of **heinous offences** (carrying a statutory *minimum* punishment of ≥7 years) to be tried as adults following a JJB preliminary psychological assessment (Sec 15). In ***Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi* (2020)**, the Supreme Court closed a critical loophole, ruling that offences carrying a maximum >7 years but **no prescribed minimum** (or <7 years minimum) fall into **"serious offences," NOT heinous**, thereby barring adult trial.
- **JJ (Amendment) Act, 2021 (Effective Sept 2022) & Mission Vatsalya:** The 2021 amendment transferred **adoption order jurisdiction from Civil Courts to District Magistrates (DMs) and ADMs** to clear chronic judicial backlogs (with appeals to Divisional Commissioners), strengthened DM oversight of Child Care Institutions (CCIs), and—controversially—reclassified serious offences against children (3–7 years jail, such as illegal CCI operation or child sale/exploitation under Sec 75–81) as **non-cognizable**, requiring magistrate approval before police can register an FIR or arrest. Child-rights networks warn this weakens emergency police rescue while overloading DMs lacking specialized child-psychology capacity. **Mission Vatsalya** (MWCD CSS) funds the delivery backbone—integrating Child Protection Services, CARA adoptions, ₹4,000/month non-institutional sponsorship/foster care, and merging the **1098 Childline** helpline into MHA’s **112 ERSS** (raising concern over loss of child-friendly civil-society responsiveness).

## SCs, Atrocity Deterrence & Sanitation Dignity

### SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 — The Conviction Deficit
Enacted under Articles 17 and 15 to combat structural caste violence, the **SC/ST (PoA) Act, 1989** (strengthened via **2015** and **2018 Amendments**) defines specific caste atrocities (social boycott, land dispossession, public humiliation), mandates **Exclusive Special Courts**, fixes statutory investigation by an officer **not below Deputy Superintendent of Police (DSP)**, provides graded victim relief, and bars anticipatory bail under **Section 18** (restored by the 2018 Amendment and upheld in ***Prathvi Raj Chauhan v. Union of India*, 2020** after *Subhash Kashinath Mahajan* triggered nationwide protests).

Ground-level deterrence nonetheless remains severely blunted. Per **NCRB’s *Crime in India 2023*** (released 2025), India registered **57,789 crimes against SCs** and **12,960 crimes against STs**, heavily concentrated in Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh. Systemic bottlenecks cripple justice delivery: **conviction rates hover around ~30%–32% for SCs and ~22%–28% for STs** (far below general cognizable averages), **pendency exceeds 85%–90%** across trial courts, most states still lack notified Exclusive Special Courts, DSP-level investigations face chronic delays, and economic dependence of Dalit witnesses on dominant-caste landlords drives rampant witness hostility.

### Manual Scavenging Act 2013, NAMASTE & *Dr. Balram Singh* (2023)
Despite the **Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013** criminalizing dry latrines, manual excreta handling, and hazardous manual entry into sewers/septic tanks without mechanized gear, toxic gas asphyxiation inside urban drains continues to kill sanitation workers—predominantly Dalits (377 officially recorded sewer deaths between 2019–2023 per MoSJE, and 400+ through 2024).

- **NAMASTE (*National Action for Mechanised Sanitation Ecosystem*):** Replacing the legacy SRMS scheme (FY 2023–24 to FY 2025–26, **₹349.73 crore**, **100% Central Sector Scheme** jointly implemented by **MoSJE and MoHUA** through **NSKFDC**), NAMASTE targets **zero sanitation fatalities** and **100% mechanized desludging**. Its workflow validates **Sewer and Septic Tank Workers (SSWs)** (88,000+ profiled across 4,800+ ULBs by 2026), enrols families under Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY, supplies PPE kits, grants **capital subsidies up to ₹5 lakh** for sanitation vehicles/desludging machines, institutionalizes **Emergency Response Sanitation Units (ERSUs)** in ULBs, and—following its 2024–25 expansion—now profiles **informal waste pickers** as well.
- ***Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India* (October 20, 2023):** Delivering a decisive constitutional mandate under Articles 17 and 21, the Supreme Court ordered both Union and States to achieve **100% eradication of manual scavenging**, tripled **statutory compensation for sewer deaths from ₹10 lakh to ₹30 lakh**, fixed **₹20 lakh minimum for permanent disability** (₹10 lakh for other disabilities), directed fresh scientific surveys of manual scavengers, and mandated scholarships and livelihood rehabilitation for victims’ kin. Reinforcing accountability, the Court in **January 2025 banned manual sewer cleaning across six major metros** (Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, and Hyderabad). Yet municipal subcontracting chains routinely obscure principal-employer liability when contractual workers die inside private apartment septic tanks.

## Tribal Welfare FRA 2006 and PM-JANMAN

### Statutory Architecture & Implementation Friction of FRA, 2006
The **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006** (**FRA 2006**) explicitly acknowledges in its Preamble the **"historical injustice"** inflicted on forest-dwelling communities by colonial forestry statutes (Indian Forest Act, 1927) that declared ancestral homelands state property and treated inhabitants as encroachers. Crucially—a high-frequency UPSC Prelims anchor—the **nodal agency at the national level is the Ministry of Tribal Affairs (MoTA)**, *not* the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC).

- **Eligible Claimants & Cut-Off Norms:**
  1. **Forest Dwelling Scheduled Tribes (FDSTs):** Primarily residing in and depending on forests for bona fide livelihood needs prior to **December 13, 2005**.
  2. **Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (OTFDs):** Primarily residing in and depending on forest land for at least **three generations (75 years)** prior to **December 13, 2005**. Clarifying statutory boundaries on **November 6, 2026**, the Supreme Court ruled that seasonal silk-cotton cultivators in Tamil Nadu’s Agasthyamalai biosphere who reside *temporarily* or *outside* the forest do not qualify as forest dwellers under FRA, while directing Tamil Nadu to constitute remaining Gram Sabhas within 30 days.
- **Four Categories of Statutory Rights:** **(i) Individual Forest Rights (IFR)** over self-cultivated land, capped at **4 hectares** (heritable, non-transferable, jointly titled in both spouses’ names); **(ii) Community Forest Rights (CFR)** over grazing, water bodies, and **ownership/access/disposal of Minor Forest Produce (MFP)**—explicitly classifying **bamboo and tendu as MFP**; **(iii) Community Forest Resource Rights (CFRR — Section 3(1)(i))**, granting Gram Sabhas statutory authority to *protect, regenerate, conserve, and manage* traditional community forests; and **(iv) Habitat Rights for PVTGs (Section 3(1)(e))** recognizing customary territorial, cultural, and ecological landscapes.
- **Three-Tier Verification & the Gram Sabha Primacy:** Under Section 6, the **Gram Sabha** (assisted by its Forest Rights Committee) is the **sole statutory authority to initiate** the process of determining rights. Resolutions pass upward for verification to the **Sub-Divisional Level Committee (SDLC)** and final approval by the **District Level Committee (DLC)**.
- **Ground Reality — High Rejection & CFR Stagnation:** Official MoTA tracking through 2025–2026 records over **51.2 lakh claims filed**, yielding **~25.11 lakh titles distributed** (**~23.89 lakh IFR** and only **~1.22 lakh CFR**—an overall approval rate of ~49%) alongside **~18.6 lakh claims rejected (~36%)**. Rejection is overwhelmingly driven by institutional resistance from state Forest Departments unwilling to cede territorial control: insisting illegally on revenue/satellite records over oral elder testimonies permitted under FRA Rules, issuing unreasoned rejections without informing claimants of their 60-day appeal right, and neglecting OTFDs entirely. Furthermore, CFRR titles cover only a tiny fraction (~10–15%) of India’s estimated minimum CFR potential (40+ million hectares), while the Supreme Court’s stayed **February 2019 *Wildlife First* order** (which had threatened mass eviction of over 10 lakh rejected claimants) remains an unresolved legal overhang.

### 360-Degree Multi-Disciplinary Tension Around Tribal Forest Governance
- **Constitutional & Environmental Jurisprudence Lens:** Anchored in **Article 244 (Fifth and Sixth Schedules)** and **PESA 1996**, FRA operationalizes democratic decentralization over natural resources. In the landmark ***Orissa Mining Corporation v. MoEF* (*Niyamgiri*, 2013)** verdict, the Supreme Court held that Gram Sabhas possess binding statutory authority to decide whether bauxite mining infringes religious and cultural rights under FRA—establishing India’s closest equivalent to **Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC)**. That safeguard collided sharply with the **Forest (Conservation) Rules, 2022** and the **Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023 (*Van Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan Adhiniyam*)**, which removed mandatory *prior* Gram Sabha consent before the Centre grants Stage-I "in-principle" forest diversion clearance—pushing FRA settlement to states *after* central approval and creating *fait accompli* pressure against village assemblies. Simultaneously, declaring **Critical Wildlife Habitats (CWH)** under FRA (requiring community consent and scientifically proven impossibility of coexistence) frequently clashes with coercive core-area relocations under the **Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972**.
- **Economic & Ecological Stewardship Lens:** Granting secure CFRR and MFP ownership transforms forest communities from precarious wage gatherers into ecological stewards. Empirical evidence from Maharashtra (Mendha Lekha, Gadchiroli) and Odisha demonstrates that Gram Sabha-managed bamboo and tendu harvesting generates crores in village revenues while reducing forest fires and illegal logging far more effectively than top-down policing.
- **Internal Security & Ethical Lens:** Across Central India’s mineral-rich Fifth Schedule belt, arbitrary FRA rejections, uncompensated mining displacement, and non-framing of state PESA rules historically alienated Adivasi youth and sustained **Left-Wing Extremism (LWE)** recruitment. Ethically, tribal governance confronts a tension between **Nehru’s *Panchsheel* (1958)** / **Virginius Xaxa Committee (2014)** principles—self-determined development respecting tribal agency—and top-down extractive "fortress conservation" or welfare paternalism.

### PM-JANMAN, Dharti Aaba JGUA & Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS)
To bridge acute infrastructural deficits that rights statutes alone cannot build, the Union rolled out India’s largest targeted tribal saturation stack across 2023–2026:

- **PM-JANMAN (*PM Janjati Adivasi Nyaya Maha Abhiyan*):** Launched on **November 15, 2023** (*Janjatiya Gaurav Diwas*, Khunti, Jharkhand), PM-JANMAN specifically targets India’s **75 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs)**—originally identified as Primitive Tribal Groups by the **Dhebar Commission (1973)** (renamed PVTGs in 2006) across **18 States and 1 UT (Andaman & Nicobar Islands)** based on four official criteria: **pre-agricultural technology, stagnant/declining population, extremely low literacy, and subsistence economy** (Odisha hosts the largest number with 13 PVTGs). With a total outlay of **₹24,104 crore** (Central share ₹15,336 crore; State share ₹8,768 crore), it converges **11 critical interventions across 9 Union Ministries** to saturate ~4.90 lakh pucca PMAY-G homes, 8,000 km PMGSY roads, Har Ghar Jal taps, Mobile Medical Units, 500 hostels, 2,500 Anganwadis, 1,000 Multipurpose Centres, 500 **Van Dhan Vikas Kendras**, off-grid solar power, and BharatNet telecom towers in remote PVTG habitations.
- **Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (DA-JGUA):** Expanding PM-JANMAN’s saturation model to the wider Scheduled Tribe population, the Prime Minister launched **DA-JGUA** on **October 2, 2024** (Hazaribagh, Jharkhand) for five years (2024–29) with a massive outlay of **₹79,156 crore** (Central ₹56,333 crore; State ₹22,823 crore). Coordinating **25 interventions across 17 Ministries** (using existing **Development Action Plan for Scheduled Tribes [DAPST]** allocations), DA-JGUA targets **~63,843 tribal-majority villages** (≥500 population with ≥50% ST share, or Aspirational Blocks with ≥50 STs) across **549 districts in 30 States/UTs**, benefitting **5+ crore tribal citizens**—notably funding dedicated **FRA Cells** to resolve pending IFR/CFR claims and integrating homestays/tribal tourism.
- **Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS):** Managed autonomously by the **National Education Society for Tribal Students (NESTS)** under MoTA, EMRS delivers quality CBSE-aligned residential schooling (Classes VI–XII) targeting **728 schools**—one in every block with **>50% ST population and at least 20,000 tribal persons**—for ~3.5 lakh ST learners. By mid-2026, **~500–510 schools** were operational (~1.42–1.55 lakh students enrolled). Implementation bottlenecks include forest-land clearance delays for school campuses, chronic teaching-staff vacancies in remote LWE blocks, and linguistic disconnect where English/Hindi CBSE curricula marginalize tribal mother tongues.

## Social Defense — PwDs, Transgender Persons, Senior Citizens, Unorganized Workers & Substance Abuse

### Disability Rights — RPwD Act 2016, *Sugamya Bharat* & UDID
Aligned with the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD)**, the **Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016** shifted Indian disability law from medical charity to a rights-based social model:
- **Expanded Statutory Entitlements:** Increased recognized disabilities from **7 to 21 conditions** (newly including acid-attack survivors, Sickle Cell Disease, Thalassemia, Hemophilia, Autism Spectrum Disorder, Parkinson’s Disease, and Specific Learning Disabilities); defined **Persons with Benchmark Disability (≥40%)**; enhanced quotas from 3% to **4% in government employment** and **5% in government/aided higher education**; guaranteed free schooling up to **age 18** (vs. 14 under RTE); and established Chief/State Commissioners for PwDs (CCPD/SCPDs).
- **Accessibility as a Fundamental Right (*Rajive Raturi*, Nov 2024):** Under **Accessible India Campaign (*Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan*)**, targets spanned built public environments, public transport, and ICT websites—yet progress remained patchy and largely cosmetic. In ***Rajive Raturi v. Union of India* (November 8, 2024)**, the Supreme Court held that **accessibility is a fundamental right** under Articles 14 and 21, invalidated the non-binding, suggestive phrasing of **Rule 15 of the RPwD Rules, 2017**, and directed the Centre to frame **mandatory, enforceable accessibility standards** with strict compliance audits.
- **UDID (*Unique Disability ID*):** Designed for pan-India benefit portability (~1.16–1.3+ crore cards issued by 2026), UDID nonetheless suffers from district medical-board certification backlogs, poor private-sector hiring (<1% representation), and severe attitudinal insensitivity across public offices.

### Transgender Rights — 2019 Act, *Jane Kaushik* (2025) & the Contested 2026 Amendment
Transgender jurisprudence in India now sits at a dramatic constitutional crossroads between expansive judicial autonomy and restrictive statutory gatekeeping:

- **From *NALSA* (2014) to the 2019 Act & SMILE:** In ***NALSA v. Union of India* (2014)**, the Supreme Court recognized the "third gender" and affirmed every person’s right to **self-identified gender without compulsory medical or surgical intervention** under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(a), and 21. The **Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019** prohibited discrimination and set up a two-step District Magistrate certification (transgender ID on self-declaration; revised male/female certificate only post-surgery), while fixing lighter penalties (6 months–2 years) for abuse than general criminal law. To support livelihoods, **MoSJE** launched the Central Sector **SMILE** (*Support for Marginalized Individuals for Livelihood and Enterprise*) umbrella—covering **both transgender persons and persons engaged in begging**—funding ***Garima Greh*** shelter homes, **Ayushman Bharat TG Plus** (₹5 lakh cover including gender-affirming surgery), scholarships, and skill training.
- ***Jane Kaushik v. Union of India* (October 17, 2025):** Finding that two private schools terminated a transgender teacher due to discriminatory prejudice—and holding the Union and States accountable for administrative omission in failing to establish **Transgender Welfare Boards** and workplace Complaint Officers under the 2019 Act—the Supreme Court awarded monetary compensation and constituted a high-level **Equal Opportunity Policy Committee chaired by Justice Asha Menon (Retd.)** to frame binding anti-discrimination rules across public and private workplaces.
- **Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026 (Act No. 3 of 2026):** Enacted by Parliament in March 2026 and accorded Presidential assent on **March 30, 2026** (triggering **UPSC Mains 2026 GS-II Q1**), the Amendment fundamentally alters legal gender recognition:
  1. **Narrowed Statutory Definition (Sec 2(k)):** Repeals the 2019 self-perceived gender definition and **deletes "trans-man," "trans-woman," and "genderqueer"**, explicitly excluding persons with different sexual orientations or self-perceived identities. Coverage is restricted strictly to enumerated groups: traditional socio-cultural identities (*kinner, hijra, aravani, jogta*), intersex variations at birth, *eunuchs*, and persons coerced into transgender identity via mutilation, castration, or hormonal/chemical procedures.
  2. **Mandatory Medical Board Screening (Sec 6):** Replaces self-declaration with a mandatory recommendation from a **designated Medical Board headed by a Chief Medical Officer (CMO) or Deputy CMO** before the District Magistrate can issue a transgender identity certificate.
  3. **Hospital Reporting & Coercion Offences:** Introduces hospital reporting obligations (Sec 7(1A)) and harsher criminal penalties against forced gender conversion, abduction, and mutilation.
  4. **Constitutional & Governance Tension:** While the government justifies verification to prevent welfare-benefit misuse and curb coerced castration rackets, legal scholars, the **NHRC Advisory 2.0**, and pending Supreme Court/High Court writ petitions argue the Act directly collides with ***NALSA* (2014)** and ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)** proportionality tests—re-medicalizing gender identity, risking exclusion of trans men/women from documents and healthcare, and creating intrusive state gatekeeping over Article 21 privacy.

### Senior Citizens, Unorganized Workers (e-Shram) & Substance Abuse
- **Senior Citizens — MWPSC Act 2007 & AVYAY:** With India’s 60+ share (~10.5%) projected to double past **20% by 2050**, the **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007** creates fast-track Maintenance Tribunals (capped at ₹10,000/month — the 2019 Amendment Bill proposing removal of this cap lapsed with the 17th Lok Sabha) and empowers tribunals under **Section 23** to declare gift/property transfers void if transferees abandon elder care (generously construed in favour of elders by the Supreme Court in ***Urmila Dixit*, Jan 2025**). **MoSJE’s** Central Sector **Atal Vayo Abhyuday Yojana (AVYAY)** integrates **IPSrC** old-age homes, **Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana (RVY)** (free hearing aids/wheelchairs/spectacles for BPL/≤₹15k income seniors), **Elderline (14567)** national helpline, **SACRED** re-employment portal, and **SAGE** (equity funding up to ₹1 crore for silver-economy startups).
- **Unorganized & Gig Workers — e-Shram Portal (MoLE):** Launched in August 2021 to build India’s first **National Database of Unorganized Workers (NDUW)**, **e-Shram** issues a 12-digit **Universal Account Number (UAN)** across 400+ occupations (migrants, agricultural, construction, domestic, and gig/platform workers), crossing **31+ crore registrations by 2026** (>53% women). Upgraded into **e-Shram One-Stop-Solution (October 2024)**, it integrates **14+ Central welfare schemes** (ONORC, PM-JAY, PM-SYM pension, PMJJBY, PMSBY, PM-SVANidhi, VB–G RAM G, PM-KISAN) with Bhashini multilingual access and platform-aggregator onboarding. Yet registration remains a *directory*, not an automatic entitlement: without operationalizing the **Social Security Code, 2020** gig-aggregator cess and state co-contribution, informal workers remain exposed to livelihood shocks.
- **Substance Abuse — Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (NMBA):** Led by **MoSJE** (under NAPDDR) from August 15, 2020 and scaled from 272 vulnerable districts to **all districts nationwide**, NMBA deploys a tri-agency model combining **MoSJE** (demand reduction, Integrated Rehabilitation Centres for Addicts [IRCAs], Outreach & Drop-In Centres, toll-free helpline **14446**), **Narcotics Control Bureau / MHA** (supply interdiction), and **MoHFW** (Addiction Treatment Facilities). Having reached 15+ crore citizens through youth and community mobilization, it still faces acute shortages of qualified de-addiction clinicians and stigma-free inpatient beds.

## Correctional & Prison Administration Reforms

### The Undertrial Overcrowding Crisis
Prisons fall squarely under the **State List (Entry 4)**, producing stark inter-state disparities in infrastructure and custodial rights. According to **NCRB *Prison Statistics India* (PSI 2022 & PSI 2023 released late 2025)**, India’s 1,330+ prisons house **~5.3 to 5.7 lakh inmates** at a national occupancy rate of **~121%–131%**—spiking above 170%–200% in Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Delhi, and Bihar. Most damningly, **~73.5% to 75.8% of all prisoners are undertrials** awaiting completion of investigation or trial—among the highest shares globally. Disproportionately drawn from SCs, STs, Muslims, and unorganized labourers who cannot furnish cash sureties or hire private counsel, this crisis reverses the constitutional axiom *"bail is the rule, jail is the exception"* (extensively documented by the **Justice Amitava Roy Committee on Prison Reforms, 2018–2023**).

### BNSS Section 479 Bail Mandate & Policing Interface
To decompress undertrial crowding, **Section 479 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** (effective July 1, 2024, replacing CrPC Section 436A) introduced two targeted correctional reliefs: **first-time offenders** (excluding offences punishable with death or **life imprisonment**) must be released on bond after undergoing detention for **one-third of the maximum prescribed imprisonment** (while retaining the legacy **one-half** threshold for other undertrials), and **Prison Superintendents** are placed under an affirmative statutory duty to apply to courts on behalf of eligible inmates. In **August 2024 (*In Re: Inhuman Conditions in 1382 Prisons*)**, the Supreme Court ruled Section 479 applies **retrospectively** to all pending undertrials nationwide. Yet **Section 479(2)** inserts a formidable statutory bottleneck: any undertrial facing **more than one pending offence or multiple cases** is categorically disqualified from statutory bail—allowing overlapping FIRs to nullify de-congestion gains.

From a governance standpoint, prison de-crowding cannot succeed purely inside jail gates without reforming arrest discretion, remand scrutiny, forensic timelines, and police modernization upstream—for the complete statutory architecture of policing reforms and the BNS–BNSS–BSA criminal-law overhaul, see [[11. Intelligence Grid, CAPFs, Police Reforms & BNS-BNSS Overhaul#SMART Policing and BNS-BNSS-BSA Security Overhaul|BNS-BNSS Policing & Security Clauses]].

### Open Prisons, *Sukanya Shantha* (2024) & Model Prisons Act 2023
- **Open Prisons (Correctional De-Carceration):** Trust-based, minimum-security open correctional settlements allow screened long-term convicts to reside with their families and earn market livelihoods by day while returning to roll-call by dusk—slashing per-inmate custodial costs to roughly one-tenth of walled prisons while minimizing recidivism (exemplified by Rajasthan’s **Sanganer Open Air Camp** and Maharashtra’s open-colony model). Despite Supreme Court nudges (*Suhas Chakma* / *Inhuman Conditions*, 2024–25) urging nationwide expansion, India operates only ~91 open jails—heavily skewed toward Rajasthan and Maharashtra, underutilized, and routinely excluding women convicts.
- ***Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India* (October 3, 2024):** In a watershed verdict by a three-judge bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud, the Supreme Court **struck down colonial-era state prison manual rules across 11+ States** (including UP, WB, MP, AP, Odisha, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Rajasthan, and Bihar) that assigned sweeping and latrine cleaning to marginalized castes, reserved kitchen/cooking duties for dominant castes, segregated barracks along caste lines, and branded **De-notified Tribes (DNTs)** as "habitual offenders." Holding such manuals violative of **Articles 14, 15, 17 (Abolition of Untouchability), 21, and 23 (Prohibition of Forced Labour)**, the Court ordered the immediate deletion of "caste" columns from undertrial and convict registers, directed States and MHA to amend State Manuals and the Model Prison Manual 2016 within three months, and opened a continuing *suo motu* compliance docket (*In Re: Discrimination Inside Prisons in India*).
- **Model Prisons and Correctional Services Act, 2023:** Circulated by **MHA in May 2023** to replace three archaic colonial laws—the **Prisons Act, 1894**, **Prisoners Act, 1900**, and **Transfer of Prisoners Act, 1950**—the Model Act pivots penology from **retributive incarceration to reformation, vocational rehabilitation, and social reintegration**. It codifies **Open and Semi-Open Prisons**, transparent **Parole, Furlough, and Remission** norms for good conduct, electronic **video-conferencing courts**, **high-security enclosures** separating hardened offenders from first-time youth, **dedicated wards for women and transgender inmates**, legal-aid clinics, prison development boards, and structured post-release after-care. Because prisons are a **State List** subject, the Model Act is advisory; sluggish state adoption through 2025–2026 underscores the need to tie MHA’s Modernisation of Prisons financial grants directly to legislative enactment and *Sukanya Shantha* compliance.

- **Forest & Tribal Agency Inversions:** Attributing **FRA 2006** to MoEFCC instead of **MoTA**, attributing claim initiation to the District Level Committee or Forest Officer instead of the **Gram Sabha**, or inflating the count of notified **PVTGs** beyond **75** (spread across 18 States & 1 UT).
