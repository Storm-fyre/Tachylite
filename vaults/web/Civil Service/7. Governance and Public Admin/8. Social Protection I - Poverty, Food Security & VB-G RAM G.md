# Social Protection I — Poverty Metrics, Food Security & Rural–Urban Livelihoods (VB–G RAM G Transition)

India’s social protection architecture sits at a structural inflection point in 2026. For two decades after 2005, welfare governance rested on rights-based, open-ended statutory entitlements—exemplified by MGNREGA (2005) and the National Food Security Act (2013)—running parallel to targeted physical asset schemes. Between 2024 and July 2026, that equilibrium shifted sharply toward **convergence-driven, fiscally capped, digitally authenticated saturation**, culminating in the repeal of MGNREGA and nationwide enforcement of the **VB–G RAM G Act, 2025** on July 1, 2026.

Understanding this domain at an administrative level requires treating poverty measurement, food security, public works, and informal livelihood missions as one coupled fiscal-political system. Errors in how the state *counts* deprivation directly distort who receives ration cards, how federal funds are rationed across states, and whether welfare builds durable human capability or merely cushions subsistence.

## Poverty Measurement, Consumption Trends & Welfare Design

### Evolution of India’s Official Poverty Lines
Measuring monetary poverty in India has always relied on **household consumption expenditure** (captured by the NSSO/MoSPI) rather than income, because informal and agrarian earnings fluctuate violently across seasons and are routinely under-reported. Every methodological committee from 1979 to 2014 wrestled with three technical variables: the **minimum normative basket** (calories vs. non-food essentials), the **recall reference period**, and the **price deflator** used to update lines across states and years.

- **Y.K. Alagh Task Force (1979):** Anchored India’s first official poverty line strictly to nutritional adequacy—**2,400 kcal/person/day in rural areas** and **2,100 kcal/person/day in urban areas**—based on physical exertion differentials. Using 1973–74 NSSO consumption data under a Uniform Reference Period (URP — 30-day recall for all items), it fixed monthly per capita expenditure (MPCE) lines at ₹49.09 (rural) and ₹56.64 (urban). Its core flaw was treating food calories as a static proxy for total welfare while ignoring state-level price heterogeneity.
- **D.T. Lakdawala Expert Group (1993):** Retained the Alagh calorie norms and national consumption basket but ended the uniform all-India price deflator. It disaggregated the poverty line into **state-specific lines** updated annually via **CPI–Agricultural Labourers (CPI-AL)** for rural areas and **CPI–Industrial Workers (CPI-IW)** for urban areas. Over time, decoupling from changing consumer preferences caused the Lakdawala basket to understate non-food costs (health, education, transport) as public provisioning shrank.
- **Suresh Tendulkar Committee (2009):** Executed three decisive breaks. First, it **abandoned explicit calorie anchoring**, noting weak empirical correlation between calorie intake and nutritional outcomes. Second, it adopted the urban poverty line basket (PLB) as a uniform reference across rural and urban India, explicitly factoring in private expenditure on **health and education**. Third, it shifted from URP to the **Mixed Reference Period (MRP)** (365-day recall for five infrequent non-food items—clothing, footwear, durables, education, institutional health—and 30-day recall for all else) and used implicit Fisher price indices. For **2011–12**, Tendulkar set the line at **₹816/month (rural)** and **₹1,000/month (urban)**, estimating **21.9% poverty** (25.7% rural; 13.7% urban).
- **C. Rangarajan Expert Group (2014):** Appointed after public backlash that Tendulkar’s line (~₹27/day rural, ₹33/day urban) represented a "starvation line," Rangarajan restored separate rural and urban baskets anchored to ICMR 2010 nutritional norms (**2,155 kcal rural / 2,090 kcal urban**, plus explicit **protein and fat floors**). It added four normative non-food essentials (clothing, rent, conveyance, education) and shifted to the **Modified Mixed Reference Period (MMRP)**—7-day recall for perishable foods, 30-day for cereals/fuel/regular services, and 365-day for infrequent non-food items. For **2011–12**, Rangarajan fixed the line at **₹972/month (rural)** and **₹1,407/month (urban)**, yielding **29.5% poverty** (30.9% rural; 26.4% urban). Crucially, **the Union Government never officially adopted the Rangarajan line**; India has not notified a fresh official monetary poverty line since Tendulkar (2011–12).

### HCES 2022–23 & 2023–24 — Consumption Shifts and Statistical Caveats
After withholding the 2017–18 consumption survey over "data quality" concerns, MoSPI/NSSO conducted two consecutive nationwide **Household Consumption Expenditure Surveys (HCES)** in **2022–23** and **2023–24** (fieldwork Aug 2023–July 2024 across 2,61,953 households). Both rounds redesigned data capture: item coverage expanded from **347 to 405 items**, Pen-and-Paper interviews shifted to **CAPI**, and a single omnibus visit was replaced by **three separate monthly visits** canvassing distinct questionnaires—**FDQ** (Food), **CSQ** (Consumables & Services), and **DGQ** (Durables)—under MMRP.

The two consecutive rounds map a structural transformation in Indian household budgets:

- **Rising MPCE & Welfare Imputation:** Average nominal MPCE climbed from **₹3,773 (rural) / ₹6,459 (urban)** in 2022–23 to **₹4,122 (rural) / ₹6,996 (urban)** in 2023–24 (a ~9% rural and ~8% urban nominal rise; ~3.5% real growth). When imputing the market value of free goods received via welfare schemes (PMGKAY grains, school uniforms, laptops, bicycles—*excluding* free public health/education services like PM-JAY), 2023–24 MPCE rises to **₹4,247 (rural)** and **₹7,078 (urban)**.
- **Engel’s Law & the Sub-50% Rural Food Threshold:** For the first time in post-Independence survey history, food fell below half of rural household spending—dropping from **52.9% (2011–12)** to **46.38% (2022–23)** and **47.04% (2023–24)**. Urban food share fell from **42.6% (2011–12)** to **39.17% (2022–23)** and **39.68% (2023–24)**. Within food, cereal budget shares more than halved in rural areas (10.69% in 2011–12 to ~4.9%), while **beverages, refreshments, and processed foods** emerged as the single largest food sub-category (~11.09% urban; ~9.6–10% rural), followed by milk and vegetables. In non-food spending (53% rural; 60% urban), **conveyance** (8.46% urban) leads.
- **Narrowing Spatial & Consumption Disparities:** The urban–rural MPCE gap contracted steadily from **84% (2011–12)** to **71% (2022–23)** and **70% (2023–24)**. Consumption inequality measured by the **Gini coefficient** declined in both sectors between 2022–23 and 2023–24: **0.266 → 0.237 (rural)** and **0.314 → 0.284 (urban)**, with the bottom 5–10% fractiles recording the sharpest percentage MPCE growth. Inter-state dispersion remains stark: **Sikkim** leads (rural ₹9,377; urban ₹13,927) while **Chhattisgarh** sits at the floor (rural ₹2,739; urban ₹4,927).

**Epistemic Caution for Mains Analysis:** Treating HCES 2022–24 as seamless proof of collapsing inequality invites two statistical traps. First, switching from single-visit URP/MRP to multi-visit MMRP with 405 items mechanically captures higher consumption (reducing recall bias), which means direct comparability against 2011–12 carries an upward methodological level-shift. Second, **consumption Gini systematically understates income and wealth inequality** because top-decile households save and invest a massive fraction of earnings while under-reporting luxury consumption in NSSO surveys—partly explaining the persistent widening gap between NSSO aggregate consumption and National Accounts Statistics (PFCE).

### Dual-Standard Global Poverty Benchmarks (World Bank 2025–2026 Revision)
Exam preparation must maintain strict **dual-standard parity** after the World Bank’s **June 2025** adoption of **2021 Purchasing Power Parities (PPPs)**, which superseded the 2017 PPP baselines across all three international lines:

- **Extreme Poverty Line:** Revised upward from **$2.15/day (2017 PPP)** to **$3.00/day (2021 PPP)**. Using HCES 2022–23 (MMRP), India’s extreme poverty fell to **2.35%** under the legacy $2.15 line and **5.25% (5.3%)** under the new $3.00 threshold. Incorporating HCES 2023–24, the **World Bank’s October 2026 Poverty & Equity Brief** places India’s extreme poverty ($3.00/day) at just **2.6% in 2023–24**—down from **27.1% in 2011–12**.
- **Lower-Middle-Income Country (LMIC) Line:** Revised from **$3.65/day (2017 PPP)** to **$4.20/day (2021 PPP)**. India’s headcount dropped from **57.7% (2011–12)** to **16.1% (2023–24)**—with rural poverty (19.3%) still more than double urban poverty (8.6%).
- **Upper-Middle-Income Country (UMIC) Line:** Revised from **$6.85/day (2017 PPP)** to **$8.30/day (2021 PPP)**. Roughly **80% of Indians** remained below $8.30/day in 2023–24.

The policy implication is unambiguous: **acute destitution has been nearly eradicated, yet mass economic vulnerability persists**. A single uninsurable catastrophic health shock or monsoon failure can push tens of millions clustered between $3.00 and $4.20 back across the threshold.

### NITI Aayog National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)
Because monetary consumption cannot capture non-market public goods (clean water, sanitation, maternal care), NITI Aayog—in technical partnership with **OPHI (Oxford)** and **UNDP**—instituted India’s **National MPI** using the **Alkire–Foster (AF) dual-cutoff counting methodology**. A household is identified as multidimensionally poor if its weighted deprivation score equals or exceeds **33.33% (one-third)** across three equally weighted dimensions (**Health 1/3, Education 1/3, Standard of Living 1/3**). The composite index multiplies two metrics: $MPI = H \times A$, where **$H$** is the Headcount Ratio (incidence) and **$A$** is the average deprivation share among the poor (intensity).

A high-yield Prelims distinction—directly tested by UPSC in **Prelims 2026**—separates India’s National MPI from the UNDP/OPHI Global MPI:

- **Global MPI (10 Indicators):** Health (*Nutrition 1/6, Child Mortality 1/6*); Education (*Years of Schooling 1/6, School Attendance 1/6*); Standard of Living (*Cooking Fuel, Sanitation, Drinking Water, Electricity, Housing, Assets — 1/18 each*).
- **NITI Aayog National MPI (12 Indicators):** Retains all 10 Global indicators and adds **two India-specific indicators**—**Maternal Health** (under Health, adjusting each Health indicator weight to **1/9**, except Nutrition which NITI retains at **1/6** while Child/Adolescent Mortality and Maternal Health take **1/12** each) and **Bank Account** (under Standard of Living, adjusting each of the 7 living-standard indicators to **1/21**).

Based on **NFHS-4 (2015–16)** and **NFHS-5 (2019–21)**, NITI Aayog’s *Progress Review (2023)* recorded a decline in multidimensional poverty from **24.85% to 14.96%** (~13.5 crore escaping poverty). NITI Aayog’s **January 2024 Discussion Paper** (*Multidimensional Poverty in India since 2005–06*) interpolated the trajectory over nine years, estimating a decline from **29.17% in 2013–14 to 11.28% in 2022–23**, lifting **24.82 crore people** out of multidimensional poverty—led in absolute numbers by **Uttar Pradesh (5.94 crore)**, **Bihar (3.77 crore)**, and **Madhya Pradesh (2.30 crore)**.

**Analytical Caveat:** Critics note two vulnerabilities in NITI’s 2024 projection. First, the 2013–14 and 2022–23 headcounts are not directly observed survey points; they are **compound-growth-rate extrapolations** derived from NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 (2019–21), meaning the post-COVID shock and post-2021 trajectory await direct verification from NFHS-6. Second, MPI indicators capture *access* (school attendance, having a bank account, electricity connection) rather than *quality* (foundational learning poverty, zero-balance dormant accounts, or 24×7 power/water reliability).

### Cash Transfers (DBT / UBI) vs. In-Kind & Capability-Building Welfare
Indian social policy faces an enduring design dilemma: should the state transfer liquid cash directly to citizens (via DBT or Universal Basic Income) or deliver subsidized commodities, wage work, and public infrastructure?

- **The Case for Cash & Quasi-UBI:** Advocated in the *Economic Survey 2016–17* (which proposed a 75% Quasi-UBI anchored to JAM), cash maximizes beneficiary agency, slashes physical procurement/storage overhead (such as FCI’s mounting carrying costs), eliminates diversion in supply chains, and reduces administrative discretion. State-level unconditional women’s cash transfers (e.g., *Ladli Behna*, *Kalaignar Magalir Urimai*, *Gruha Lakshmi*) have proliferated rapidly across states between 2023 and 2026, demonstrating high political velocity and immediate consumption smoothing.
- **Structural Limits of Pure Cash:** Cash transfers fail where **markets are thin or inflationary** (remote Fifth Schedule blocks where grain shops are scarce), suffer **real value erosion** when transfers are not CPI-indexed, risk **intra-household diversion** toward demerit goods when not credited exclusively to women, and cannot substitute for **non-excludable public goods** (primary health centres, public schooling, drainage).
- **The "New Welfarism" vs. Capability Critique:** Following **Amartya Sen’s Capability Approach**, income is merely a means; true poverty reduction expands substantive freedoms (health, literacy, agency). Economists Arvind Subramanian and Josh Felman observe that post-2014 Indian welfare prioritized **"New Welfarism"**—highly visible, digitally trackable, privately consumable goods (bank accounts, LPG cylinders, toilets, pucca houses, tap connections, free grain)—over harder-to-deliver public goods (learning outcomes and primary clinical care). While New Welfarism accelerated reductions in MPI living-standard indicators, unconstrained state-level cash transfers in 2024–26 risk **crowding out capital expenditure and health/education budgets**, trading long-term human capital for short-term fiscal populism.

## Food Security Architecture - NFSA 2013, PMGKAY & PDS Modernization

### Statutory Architecture of the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013
Rooted in **Article 47** (Directive Principle to raise nutrition levels) and judicialized under **Article 21** via the landmark ***PUCL v. Union of India* (2001)** Right to Food PIL, the **NFSA, 2013** converted food distribution from executive charity into a justiciable statutory entitlement. It covers up to **75% of the rural population and 50% of the urban population** (~67% overall), yielding a statutory ceiling of **81.35 crore beneficiaries** anchored to the 2011 Census (with ~79.4–80.6 crore actively identified).

Key statutory provisions frequently targeted in UPSC Prelims include:
- **Two-Tier Identification (Not BPL-Only):** NFSA abolished the flawed BPL/APL classification for central grain entitlements, replacing it with state-identified **Priority Households (PHH)** entitled to **5 kg of foodgrains per person per month**, and **Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY)** poorest-of-the-poor households entitled to **35 kg per family per month**. State governments determine inclusion/exclusion criteria within state-wise ceilings fixed by the Centre.
- **Life-Cycle & Gendered Entitlements:** Under **Section 13**, the **eldest woman aged 18 years or above** in every eligible household is designated the head of the household for issuing ration cards. Under **Sections 4–6**, pregnant women and lactating mothers receive free nutritious meals via Anganwadis plus a statutory **maternity benefit of not less than ₹6,000** (operationalized via PMMVY, though narrowed in practice to first/second births); children aged 6 months to 6 years receive nutrition via ICDS, and children aged 6–14 receive hot cooked meals under **PM-POSHAN**.
- **Accountability & Food Security Allowance:** If states fail to supply entitled grains or meals, beneficiaries have a legal right to a cash **Food Security Allowance** (Sec 8). The Act mandates District Grievance Redressal Officers (DGROs), independent **State Food Commissions** (Sec 16), vigilance committees at every tier, and periodic **social audits** by local authorities (Sec 28).

### PMGKAY Merger & the Frozen 2011 Census Crisis
During the COVID-19 shock (April 2020–December 2022), the Centre ran **Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY)** Phases I–VII as an *additional* 5 kg free grain allocation atop NFSA. From **January 1, 2023**, the Union merged PMGKAY into NFSA—abolishing the legacy NFSA Central Issue Prices (₹3/kg rice, ₹2/kg wheat, ₹1/kg coarse grains) so that the entire statutory entitlement (5 kg PHH / 35 kg AAY) became **100% free of cost**. In November 2023, the Union Cabinet extended free foodgrains under PMGKAY for five years—from **January 1, 2024 to December 2028**—with a 100% centrally borne outlay of **₹11.80 lakh crore**.

Yet this massive fiscal shield (~₹2 lakh crore annual food subsidy) conceals an acute **demographic exclusion error**. Because **Section 9 of NFSA** locks population coverage strictly to the **last published Census (2011: 121 crore)**, India’s delayed decennial Census has frozen the NFSA cap at 81.35 crore even as population crossed ~140+ crore. Economists Jean Drèze and Reetika Khera estimate **10 to 14 crore otherwise eligible citizens**—disproportionately newly formed households, young families, and circular migrants in high-fertility states like Uttar Pradesh and Bihar—are locked out of statutory ration cards.

In ***In Re: Problems and Miseries of Migrant Labourers*** (Suo Motu W.P. (C) No. 6 of 2020), the Supreme Court repeatedly intervened—passing stern directives in April 2023, March 2024, and **October 4, 2024**—ordering States and UTs to issue ration cards to roughly **8 crore migrant and unorganized workers** registered on the **e-Shram portal** who lacked NFSA coverage despite state quota ceilings. State governments routinely cite exhausted central NFSA quotas, highlighting the constitutional friction between a frozen statutory cap and live demographic need.

### TPDS Modernization, e-PoS & ONORC Portability
Historically, the Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS) bled heavily through ghost cards, transit pilferage, and Fair Price Shop (FPS) open-market diversion—peaking at **~54% leakage in 2004–05** and **~42–46% in 2011–12** (NSSO/Planning Commission/Shanta Kumar estimates). End-to-end computerization over the past decade rewired this physical network:

- **Biometric Plumbing:** Over **99.7% of NFSA ration cards** are Aadhaar-seeded, **87%+ beneficiaries** had completed e-KYC by early 2026, and virtually all **~5.33–5.4 lakh Fair Price Shops** operate biometric **electronic Point of Sale (e-PoS)** devices (~95–99% of grain distributed via authenticated transactions).
- **One Nation, One Ration Card (ONORC):** Operationalized across **all 36 States and UTs** via the **IM-PDS** central repository and **Mera Ration** app, ONORC decouples ration access from a beneficiary’s home FPS. Crucially, it supports **split-family partial lifting**: a migrant construction worker in Surat or Mumbai can draw their individual 5 kg share via Aadhaar biometrics while family members simultaneously draw the balance back in Bihar or Odisha.
- **Street-Level Failure Modes:** While digitization sharply curtailed ghost cards (pushing estimated leakages down toward the sub-20% range per recent studies, though disputed across states), it did not eliminate **quantity fraud ("cut-back")**. Underpaid FPS dealers facing thin commission margins routinely authenticate a beneficiary’s thumbprint for 25 kg on the e-PoS machine while physically handing over 22 kg. Moreover, biometric failures among elderly manual labourers with worn fingerprints and poor rural telecom connectivity cause localized denials unless strict manual override protocols are enforced. Inter-state ONORC transactions also remain a small fraction of total portability (which is overwhelmingly intra-state), constrained by destination-state stock mismatches, unfamiliar grain preferences (wheat vs. rice), and unawareness among seasonal brick-kiln and construction migrants.

### Universal Rice Fortification (2024–2028) & the Nutritional Debate
Calorie sufficiency has not cured **"hidden hunger"**—NFHS-5 documented **67.1% anemia among children (6–59 months)** and **57% among women of reproductive age (15–49)**. Because rice is a dietary staple for 65% of Indians, the Centre scaled up universal blending of Custom Milled Rice with extruded **Fortified Rice Kernels (FRK)** enriched with **Iron, Folic Acid, and Vitamin B12** per FSSAI standards. Following universal phase-in by March 2024, the Union Cabinet (**October 9, 2024**) extended universal free fortified rice across PMGKAY, ICDS, and PM-POSHAN through **December 2028** as a **100% Central Sector initiative (₹17,082 crore outlay)**.

Public health experts and nutritionists nonetheless raise two sharp cautions. First, **biomedical safety in tribal hemoglobinopathy belts**: excess dietary iron poses oxidative toxicity risks for individuals with **thalassemia and sickle-cell anemia**—prevalent across tribal Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, and Madhya Pradesh—prompting FSSAI to mandate caution labels on fortified rice sacks while critics urge screening before universal blanket dosing. Second, **dietary diversity**: micronutrient-sprayed polished rice cannot replace protein, fats, pulses, eggs, and **millets (*Shree Anna*)**; without diversifying the PDS basket beyond rice and wheat, food security remains structurally decoupled from nutritional security.

### Shanta Kumar High-Level Committee (2015) Critique of FCI & Procurement
The Shanta Kumar Committee exposed the structural contradictions linking MSP procurement to PDS distribution:
- **Extreme Procurement Concentration:** Only **~6% of Indian farming households** directly sold rice or wheat at MSP, heavily concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh, inducing ecological ruin (groundwater depletion and stubble burning) while leaving eastern smallholders at distress market prices.
- **FCI Fiscal & Storage Drag:** Open-ended procurement inflated buffer stocks far beyond operational norms, escalating carrying costs and post-harvest storage losses.
- **Core Reform Blueprint:** The committee recommended **(i)** rationalizing NFSA coverage from **67% down to 40%** and pegging issue prices for non-Antyodaya beneficiaries to 50% of MSP (a recommendation politically rejected by the PMGKAY free-grain expansion); **(ii)** handing procurement in surplus states (Punjab, Haryana, MP, Chhattisgarh) to state agencies via **Decentralized Procurement (DCP)** so FCI concentrates on deficit eastern states (Bihar, Eastern UP, WB, Assam); **(iii)** piloting **cash DBT in lieu of physical grain** in cities above 1 million population (presently live only in Chandigarh, Puducherry, and parts of Dadra & Nagar Haveli); and **(iv)** modernizing FCI logistics through PPP steel silos, negotiable warehouse receipts, and end-to-end GPS/IoT supply-chain tracking.

## VB-G RAM G Act 2025 and Rural Livelihoods

### The Watershed Statutory Reset — Repealing MGNREGA (2005)
The **Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025** (**VB–G RAM G Act, 2025**)—administered by the **Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD)**—represents the most consequential structural transformation in India’s social protection statute book since 2005. Introduced in the Lok Sabha on **December 16, 2025**, passed by Parliament on **December 18–19, 2025**, and accorded Presidential assent on **December 21, 2025**, the Act came into nationwide force on **July 1, 2026**, formally **repealing the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (MGNREGA)** (Section 37).

Rather than treating rural public works purely as an open-ended distress dole, the statute re-anchors rural wage employment to **Viksit Bharat @2047** through four operational pillars: **enhanced household income floor (125 days)**, **convergence-driven infrastructure saturation**, **normative federal cost-sharing**, and **full-stack digital governance**. For FY 2026–27, the Union budgeted a record **₹95,692.31 crore** central share at the Budget Estimate stage—the highest initial rural employment outlay in India’s history—paired with a **June 30, 2026 wage notification** instituting a **₹300/day national wage floor** (with 15–25% hikes in lagging eastern/northern states and rates reaching ₹360–₹409/day in Kerala, Haryana, Punjab, and Karnataka).

### Statutory & Operational Architecture of VB–G RAM G Act, 2025
- **Expanded 125-Day Statutory Guarantee (Section 5):** Every rural household whose adult members volunteer for unskilled manual work is statutorily guaranteed **not less than 125 days** of wage employment per financial year (up from 100 days under MGNREGA). Existing e-KYC verified MGNREGA job cards remain valid during transition toward unified **Gramin Rozgar Guarantee Cards**. As of November 2026, MoRD’s dashboard records 15.19 crore cards issued (7.89 crore active), 11 crore active workers, and ~48.55 crore person-days generated since commencement.
- **60-Day Peak Agricultural Season Pause:** Addressing two decades of farmer complaints that public works starved agriculture of labour and spiked sowing/harvest wages, the Act mandates that **State Governments notify in advance an aggregated pause period of up to 60 days per financial year** (customizable by agro-climatic zone, district, or block) during peak sowing and harvesting windows, during which public works remain suspended. Importantly, the 125-day entitlement remains intact outside this window.
- **Viksit Gram Panchayat Plans (VGPPs) & National Rural Infrastructure Stack:** All works must originate bottom-up in participatory **Viksit Gram Panchayat Plans (VGPPs)** prepared and approved by the **Gram Sabha and Gram Panchayat**. Every VGPP is spatially mapped and digitally aggregated into the **Viksit Bharat National Rural Infrastructure Stack** and integrated with **PM Gati Shakti** to eliminate fragmented, low-durability earthwork and synchronize multi-departmental saturation.
- **Four Core Rural Infrastructure Themes:** Shifting away from MGNREGA’s sprawling list of 266 loosely grouped permissible activities, VB–G RAM G channels works strictly into four high-multiplier verticals:
  1. **Water Security** (check dams, watershed recharge, village tanks, micro-irrigation feeders);
  2. **Core Rural Infrastructure** (all-weather internal connectivity, drainage, community facilities);
  3. **Livelihood-Related Infrastructure** (storage godowns, rural haats, farm-gate assets, SHG workspaces);
  4. **Extreme Weather & Climate Resilience** (flood embankments, heat/drought mitigation, disaster preparedness works).
- **Normative CSS Funding Pattern (Section 22):** This is the statute’s sharpest structural departure. VB–G RAM G converts rural employment into a **Centrally Sponsored Scheme (CSS)** with a unified cost-sharing ratio of **60:40 (Centre:State)** for general states and UTs with legislatures, **90:10** for **North-Eastern States, Himalayan States (Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand), and the UT of Jammu & Kashmir**, and **100% Central funding** for UTs without legislatures. Under **Section 22(4)–(5)**, the Centre determines annual **State-wise normative allocations** based on prescribed objective parameters; **any expenditure incurred in excess of a state’s normative allocation must be borne entirely by the State Government**.
- **Timely Wages, Administrative Capacity & Digital Accountability:** Wages must be disbursed on a **weekly basis**, and in no case later than a fortnight after work completion. The statutory **unemployment allowance** (payable by the State if work is not allotted within 15 days) continues. Crucially, the **administrative expenditure ceiling is raised from 6% to 9%** to finance dedicated technical staff, engineers, and training at block/panchayat tiers. Monitoring embeds **biometric authentication, mobile attendance, geospatial/GIS planning, AI-enabled fraud analytics, weekly public disclosure systems, and mandatory 6-monthly social audits** (operationalized via the *Draft Audit of Scheme Rules, 2026*), overseen nationally by a **National Level Steering Committee** (Section 14).

### MGNREGA (2005) vs. VB–G RAM G Act (2025) Structural Shift Table

| Structural Parameter | MGNREGA, 2005 *(Repealed July 1, 2026)* | VB–G RAM G Act, 2025 *(Enforced July 1, 2026)* |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Policy Paradigm** | Rights-based, demand-driven social safety net ("work as a distress fallback") | Convergence-driven livelihood + durable rural asset creation aligned with *Viksit Bharat @2047* |
| **Statutory Work Guarantee** | Not less than **100 days**/rural household/year (150 days only in notified droughts/tribal forest rights holders) | Not less than **125 days**/rural household/year uniformly across notified rural areas |
| **Entitlement Identity Document** | MGNREGA Job Card | **Gramin Rozgar Guarantee Card** (existing e-KYC job cards valid in transition) |
| **Budgeting Mechanism** | Open-ended, demand-driven Labour Budget (Centre legally obliged to reimburse verified demand) | **Budget-capped Normative Allocation** fixed annually by Centre via objective parameters (Sec 22(4)) |
| **Centre–State Cost Sharing** | Centre paid **100% unskilled wages + 75% material** (effectively ~90:10 overall); States paid 25% material + unemployment allowance | **Unified CSS Ratio:** **60:40** (general states & UTs with legislature); **90:10** (NE, Himalayan States & J&K); **100%** Centre (UTs without legislature) |
| **Excess Demand Liability** | Supplementary central releases upon verified state demand | **100% borne by the State Government** beyond central normative allocation (Sec 22(5)) |
| **Agricultural Peak Season Rule** | No statutory suspension; operated year-round | Mandatory **State-notified pause of up to 60 aggregated days/year** during peak sowing/harvesting |
| **Wage Payment Cycle & Floor** | Fortnightly (within 15 days); state-wise CPI-AL notified wages with no national floor | **Weekly** disbursement (outer limit fortnightly); **₹300/day national wage floor** notified June 30, 2026 |
| **Planning Architecture** | Standalone Gram Panchayat shelf of projects (266 loosely grouped permissible works) | **Viksit Gram Panchayat Plans (VGPPs)** approved by Gram Sabha, digitally integrated into **PM Gati Shakti** & **National Rural Infrastructure Stack** |
| **Thematic Asset Focus** | Predominantly NRM, agriculture-allied earthworks, rural connectivity | Strictly **4 priority verticals**: Water Security, Core Rural Infra, Livelihood Infra, Extreme Weather/Climate Resilience |
| **Administrative Expenditure Cap** | **6%** of total programme expenditure | Raised to **9%** to strengthen technical staffing, engineering supervision & capacity building |
| **Transparency & Monitoring Tech** | NREGASoft MIS, geo-tagging (GeoMGNREGA), NMMS app, ABPS, Social Audit (Sec 17) | Biometric auth, mobile dashboards, geospatial planning, **AI analytics**, **weekly public disclosure**, mandatory **6-monthly social audits** |
| **Apex Institutional Oversight** | Central Employment Guarantee Council (CEGC) | **National Level Steering Committee** (Sec 14) with stronger central investigation & fund-suspension powers |

### Legacy MGNREGA Lessons Carried into the VB–G RAM G Era
Two decades of MGNREGA operation yielded three hard empirical lessons that remain decisive tests for VB–G RAM G’s ground execution:

1. **The Wage Indexation Distortion:** Under MGNREGA, Centre-notified wages were indexed to the outdated **CPI–Agricultural Labourers (CPI-AL, base 1986–87)** rather than state statutory minimum wages under the Minimum Wages Act. Because CPI-AL underweighted modern rural consumption, MGNREGA wages lagged statutory farm minimum wages in over 20 states (an anomaly flagged by both the **Mahendra Dev Committee 2014** and **Anoop Satpathy Committee 2019**, which recommended shifting to **CPI–Rural**). While the June 30, 2026 VB–G RAM G notification established an encouraging ₹300/day floor, unless the underlying indexation formula migrates permanently from CPI-AL to CPI-Rural (anchored to HCES 2023–24 weights), real wage erosion will recur within three years.
2. **Digital Friction at the Last Mile (NMMS & ABPS):** MGNREGA’s mandatory rollout of the **National Mobile Monitoring System (NMMS)**—requiring two time-stamped, geo-tagged worksite photographs daily—and the **Aadhaar-Based Payment System (ABPS)** curtailed ghost muster rolls. Yet field audits (including LibTech India and parliamentary standing committee notes) documented massive **exclusion errors**: network blackouts in Fifth Schedule blocks prevented photo uploads, forfeiting an entire day’s earned wage, while minor Aadhaar–bank–job-card spelling mismatches triggered mass deletion of genuine job cards or misdirected payments to dormant accounts. Embedding mandatory biometric auth and AI monitoring in VB–G RAM G without **offline-first capture and statutory liability for technical failures** risks replicating "welfare disentitlement by design."
3. **The Feminization Paradox (>58% Female Share):** Though MGNREGA mandated a 33% floor for women, female participation rose steadily from **48% (FY 2013–14)** to **58.15% (FY 2024–25)** (peaking at 58.89–59.25% in FY 2023–24) and surged further to **~61.5%** in the opening months of VB–G RAM G (FY 2026–27). This reflects genuine empowerment—equal statutory pay, local worksites compatible with unpaid care duties, and direct bank credits boosting intra-household bargaining power. Simultaneously, it exposes a structural labour-market deficit: rural men increasingly migrate to higher-wage urban construction, leaving women trapped in lower-wage manual public works due to scarce rural non-farm enterprise opportunities. Ensuring real worksite crèches, female mates from SHGs, and livelihood-asset creation for women’s collectives remains indispensable.

### 360-Degree Multi-Disciplinary Interrogation of VB–G RAM G (Primary Anchor Lens)

- **Constitutional & Cooperative Federalism Lens:** MGNREGA operationalized **Article 41** (DPSP: Right to Work) as a legally enforceable, demand-driven right. Critics and constitutional scholars argue that **Section 22’s normative allocation cap** combined with **Section 5’s central notification of covered rural areas** effectively converts an open-ended statutory right into a **centrally rationed, budget-capped scheme**. Moving from an effective ~90:10 central share to a **60:40 CSS split** imposes an estimated **five-to-six-fold surge in states’ fiscal liability** (roughly ₹50,000+ crore annually across major states, with fiscally stressed states like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, and Rajasthan facing steep matching burdens). The Centre counters that shared skin-in-the-game curtails moral hazard, ghost billing, and politicized muster-roll inflation while improving fiscal predictability. Yet if financially strained states informally suppress work demand to stay within normative caps—so they avoid paying 100% of excess expenditure—the **125-day statutory guarantee risks shrinking on paper**, as civil society groups flagged in June 2026 interim-allocation analyses showing budgeted averages of only 14–42 person-days per active card in major states. Similarly, aligning village VGPPs to central PM Gati Shakti templates tests the substantive autonomy of Gram Sabhas under the **73rd Constitutional Amendment**.
- **Macroeconomic & Agrarian Labour-Market Lens:** Raising the entitlement to 125 days and lifting the wage floor to ₹300/day strengthens the **rural reservation wage**, stimulates rural consumption multipliers, and cushions rural demand during monsoon shocks. Simultaneously, the **60-day peak agricultural pause** seeks a pragmatic Pareto balance—preventing public works from driving up input costs for small and marginal farmers during Kharif/Rabi sowing and harvesting. However, labour economists caution that suspending the fallback option precisely when private agrarian demand peaks strips landless Dalit and Adivasi workers of their sharpest seasonal bargaining leverage against local landlords, potentially depressing private agricultural wages.
- **Internal Security, Climate & Migration Lens:** Rural public employment historically operated as India’s premier **counter-cyclical stabilizer against distress circular migration**—most visibly during the 2020 pandemic reverse-migration wave—and weakened insurgent recruitment in Left-Wing Extremism (LWE) hinterlands by inserting formal state wages into tribal economies. VB–G RAM G’s explicit fourth vertical—**extreme weather mitigation and climate resilience**—repurposes rural labour into a front-line climate adaptation corps (building flood bunds, recharge shafts, and heat-resilient watershed assets). Conversely, if normative state caps choke work availability during localized droughts or unseasonal crop failures, distress migration to informal urban slums will rebound sharply.
- **Administrative & Ethical Lens:** At its core, the MGNREGA → VB–G RAM G transition pits **technocratic efficiency and asset productivity** against **rights-based dignity (*haq*)**. Raising the administrative cap to 9% and introducing weekly wages directly redress two ethical failures of MGNREGA (chronically delayed wages forcing workers into predatory moneylender debt, and starved field-engineering supervision producing washed-away mud roads). Yet accountability remains structurally asymmetrical: states bear legal liability for unemployment allowances and excess demand, while the Union controls normative ceilings, biometric authentication rails, and fund-suspension levers. Ethical governance demands enforceable safeguards so algorithmic flags never punish honest workers for institutional delays.

## Asset-Building & Informal Livelihood Missions

Wage floors and food rations prevent destitution, but permanent poverty exits require **physical household assets, spatial market connectivity, potable water, and formal micro-enterprise credit**. Five flagship missions—all updated between 2024 and 2026—anchor this second leg of India’s social protection grid.

### Pradhan Mantri Awaas Yojana – Gramin (PMAY-G, Phase 2024–29)
Reconstituted from Indira Awaas Yojana in 2016 to deliver "Housing for All" with basic amenities (minimum 25 sq m pucca house including a hygienic cooking space), **PMAY-G** received Union Cabinet approval in **August 2024** for a five-year expansion (**FY 2024–25 to FY 2028–29**) targeting **2 crore additional pucca houses** with a total outlay of **₹3,06,137 crore** (Central share ₹2,05,856 crore; State matching share ₹1,00,281 crore). By August 2026, against a cumulative allocated target of 4.18 crore houses, **3.91 crore had been sanctioned and 3.12 crore completed**.

- **Financing & Convergence Bundle:** Unit assistance stands at **₹1.20 lakh in plain areas** (60:40 Centre–State) and **₹1.30 lakh in NE, Himalayan States, J&K/Ladakh, and IAP districts** (90:10; 100% in UTs without legislature). Every PMAY-G home is structurally bundled via convergence with **₹12,000 for a toilet** (SBM-G), **90/95 person-days of unskilled wage labour** (transitioning from MGNREGA to VB–G RAM G), free **Ujjwala LPG**, **JJM tap water**, electricity, and an optional **₹70,000 institutional loan at 3% interest subvention**. Stage-wise DBT disbursements require geo-tagged photographic verification via **AwaasSoft/AwaasApp**.
- **2024 Revised Exclusion Criteria (Major Prelims Trigger):** Updating beneficiary identification beyond the ageing SECC-2011 Permanent Wait List via the fresh **Awaas+ (2024–25) mobile survey** (covering 5.05+ crore households by Jan 2026), the Centre pruned the legacy **13-point automatic exclusion list down to 10 points**. Specifically, owning a **motorized two-wheeler** or a **fishing boat** no longer disqualifies a poor household, and the monthly income exclusion cutoff was raised from **₹10,000 to ₹15,000**.
- **Street-Level Bottleneck:** Roughly **34.28 lakh surveyed households (~7%)** in Awaas+ are **landless**. Without proactive state provisioning of homestead land (*pattas*), the most deprived Dalit and tribal families remain structurally barred from drawing PMAY-G grants; furthermore, inflation in cement and steel has widened the gap between the static ₹1.20 lakh unit grant (unchanged since 2016) and actual construction costs, forcing beneficiary borrowing.

### Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana – Phase IV (PMGSY-IV, 2024–29)
Celebrating 25 years in December 2025 with **7.87+ lakh km** of rural all-weather roads constructed since 2000, PMGSY has empirically proved to be India’s highest-return rural anti-poverty infrastructure investment—lowering transport costs, raising farm-gate realization, expanding rural non-farm employment, and reducing maternal mortality via emergency hospital access.

- **PMGSY-IV Mandate:** Approved by the Union Cabinet on **September 11, 2024** for **FY 2024–25 to FY 2028–29** with an outlay of **₹70,125 crore** (Central share ₹49,087.50 crore; State share ₹21,037.50 crore), Phase IV targets **62,500 km** of new all-weather roads and bridges linking **25,000 unconnected habitations** that crossed population thresholds under **Census 2011**.
- **Graded Population Eligibility Norms (Census 2011):** **500+** in plain areas; **250+** in Special Category Areas (North-Eastern & Hill States/UTs, Desert Areas, **Schedule V Tribal Areas**, and **Aspirational Districts/Blocks**); and **100+** in **MHA-notified Left-Wing Extremism (LWE) districts**.
- **Governance & Maintenance Spine:** Phase IV converges directly with **Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (DA-JGUA)** and **PM-AJAY** for SC/ST habitations. Quality control relies on a 3-tier audit mechanism, GPS/OMMAS tracking, mandatory green technologies (waste plastic, cold-mix, fly ash), and a **5-year post-construction maintenance contract** tracked on **e-MARG**—though state-level post-5-year maintenance funding remains a chronic vulnerability.

### Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM — *Har Ghar Jal*, Extended to 2028)
Launched on **August 15, 2019** under the **Department of Drinking Water and Sanitation (Ministry of Jal Shakti)**, JJM marked a structural break from legacy habitation-level handpump schemes (NRDWP) by guaranteeing individual **Functional Household Tap Connections (FHTCs)** supplying at least **55 litres per capita per day (LPCD)** of potable water meeting **BIS:10500** quality norms. Rural household tap coverage vaulted from **3.23 crore (17%) in August 2019** to **~15.82–15.86 crore (~81.5–82%) by mid-2026**.

- **Restructuring & Extension to December 2028 (JJM 2.0):** Recognizing that the remaining ~3.5 crore unconnected households sit in arid, hilly, and quality-affected tails—and that completed pipes risk turning dry without institutional upkeep—the Union extended and restructured JJM through **December 2028**, lifting total mission outlay to **₹8.69 lakh crore** (central share raised to ₹3.59 lakh crore; FY 2026–27 BE **₹67,670 crore**). Funding follows a **50:50 ratio for general states** (a critical Prelims distinction from 60:40 CSS norms), **90:10** for NE/Himalayan states, and **100%** for UTs.
- **Community & Digital Governance:** Grassroots operation and maintenance rests with **Village Water and Sanitation Committees (*Pani Samitis*)** requiring **at least 50% women members**, alongside five trained women per village conducting water-quality checks using **Field Test Kits (FTKs)** (~24.8 lakh women trained). JJM 2.0 introduces **Sujalam Bharat** (end-to-end digital source-to-tap mapping with a unique *Sujal Gaon* Service Area ID) and **Jal Arpan** (formal handover commissioning to Gram Panchayats only after verified O&M readiness).
- **Implementation Failure Modes:** Field audits and vigilance investigations exposed **"paper taps"** (non-functional standposts reported as active FHTCs, prominently flagged in Manipur), contractor-engineer procurement scams (e.g., Rajasthan PHED probes), **source depletion** where multi-village piped schemes tap over-exploited aquifers without recharge convergence, and severe **greywater stagnation** turning village lanes into disease vectors when 55 LPCD arrives without parallel drainage.

### PM–SVANidhi (Restructured August 2025 – March 2030)
Urban informal livelihoods—specifically street vendors who anchor low-cost urban supply chains—lacked collateral, formal identity, and social protection until **PM Street Vendor’s AtmaNirbhar Nidhi (PM-SVANidhi)** launched in June 2020 as a **100% Central Sector Scheme**. Having disbursed over **1.21 crore loans worth ₹20,000+ crore to ~81 lakh vendors** (95% first-time formal credit borrowers), the Union Cabinet (**August 27, 2025**) approved a major **restructuring and extension through March 31, 2030** with an outlay of **₹7,332 crore**, targeting **1.15 crore beneficiaries** (including 50 lakh new vendors).

- **Joint Governance & Spatial Expansion:** Implemented jointly by the **Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA)** and the **Department of Financial Services (DFS, Ministry of Finance)**, the restructured scheme expands coverage beyond statutory towns in a graded manner to **Census Towns and peri-urban areas**.
- **Upgraded Credit Ladder & Digital Incentives:** Collateral-free working capital tranches were raised from ₹10k/₹20k/₹50k to **₹15,000 (1st tranche, 12 months)**, **₹25,000 (2nd tranche, 18 months)**, and **₹50,000 (3rd tranche, 36 months)**, backed by a **7% per annum interest subsidy**. Vendors repaying the second tranche qualify for a **UPI-linked RuPay Credit Card with up to ₹30,000 credit limit** for emergent liquidity, plus **digital cashback incentives up to ₹1,600** (₹1,200 retail + ₹400 wholesale).
- **Welfare Convergence & Urban Friction:** The **SVANidhi se Samriddhi (SSS)** arm profiles vendor families to plug them into **8 Central social security schemes** (PM-JAY, PMJJBY, PMSBY, ONORC, Janani Suraksha, etc.). Yet credit alone cannot shield livelihoods where municipal enforcement undermines the **Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014**: dormant Town Vending Committees, undemarcated vending zones, and arbitrary anti-encroachment eviction drives persist across Indian cities.

### PM Vishwakarma Scheme (FY 2023–24 to FY 2027–28)
Launched on **September 17, 2023**, **PM Vishwakarma** is a **100% Central Sector Scheme** with a five-year outlay of **₹13,000 crore** jointly implemented by the **Ministry of MSME (nodal)**, **Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship (MSDE)**, and **Department of Financial Services (MoF)**. It targets traditional artisans and craftspeople in **both rural and urban areas** working with hands and tools across **18 notified trades** (*Carpenter, Boat Maker, Armourer, Blacksmith/Metal Artisan, Hammer & Tool Kit Maker, Locksmith, Goldsmith, Potter, Sculptor/Stone Breaker, Cobbler, Mason, Basket/Mat/Broom/Coir Weaver, Traditional Toy Maker, Barber, Garland Maker, Washerman, Tailor, and Fishing Net Maker*).

- **End-to-End "Samman, Samarthya, Samriddhi" Package:** Following three-tier verification (Gram Panchayat/ULB → District Implementation Committee → State Screening), registered artisans receive: **(i) PM Vishwakarma Certificate & ID Card** (onboarded to Udyam Assist Platform); **(ii) Basic Training (5–7 days)** and **Advanced Training (15+ days)** with a **₹500/day stipend**; **(iii) ₹15,000 modern toolkit e-voucher**; **(iv) Collateral-free Enterprise Development Loans** up to **₹3 lakh** in two tranches—**₹1 lakh (18 months)** and **₹2 lakh (30 months)**—at a fixed **5% concessional interest rate** (with **8% GoI interest subvention**); **(v) ₹1/digital transaction incentive** (up to 100 transactions/month); and **(vi) marketing/branding linkages** via GeM, e-commerce platforms, and trade fairs. By September 2026, registrations achieved the **30 lakh beneficiary milestone**, with over 24.29 lakh completing basic skill training.
- **Sociological & Financial Critique:** Two design tensions merit Mains scrutiny. First, bank loan sanctions and disbursements trail portal registrations significantly due to conservative branch-level risk aversion toward collateral-free micro-enterprises. Second, sociologists warn that subsidizing hereditary *Guru-Shishya* manual occupations risks **reinforcing caste-segmented occupational immobility** unless beneficiaries are actively bridged into modern, scalable MSME clusters and high-margin design/export value chains rather than subsistence self-employment.
