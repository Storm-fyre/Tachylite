# Chapter 4 - The Regulatory State & Independent Regulatory Agencies (IRAs)

## The Rise of the Regulatory State - Theoretical & Institutional Foundations

Before 1991, the Indian state operated as an **interventionist "positive state"**—a direct producer of steel, telecom, electricity, and credit, rationing market entry through the License-Permit-Quota Raj. Post-1991 liberalization did not shrink the state; it mutated its governing technology from **ownership and direct provision** to **rule-making, market-making, and arm's-length supervision**. Political scientist Giandomenico Majone termed this institutional transformation the **Regulatory State**, where authority migrates away from line ministries toward specialized, non-majoritarian **Independent Regulatory Agencies (IRAs)**.

### Why Markets Require Non-Majoritarian Umpires

Four structural problems explain why a liberalizing democracy cannot simply hand market oversight back to traditional line ministries:

1. **The Credible Commitment Problem:** Infrastructure sectors—power grids, telecom spectrum, gas pipelines, ports—require massive sunk capital with 15-to-30-year payback horizons. An elected government operating on a five-year electoral cycle faces overwhelming political temptation to freeze tariffs or rewrite concession terms once private capital is sunk (*time-inconsistency problem*). Delegating tariff-setting and licensing to an insulated IRA signals **credible commitment** across electoral cycles, lowering the risk premium demanded by private investors.
2. **State-as-Player Conflict of Interest:** In India, liberalization rarely meant full privatization. Public Sector Undertakings—BSNL, NTPC, state DISCOMs, LIC, SBI, NPCIL—continued competing directly against private entrants. When the line ministry acts as both majority shareholder of the incumbent PSU and umpire of the sector, private rivals face systemic bias; an arm's-length regulator separates **sovereign ownership** from **market refereeing**.
3. **Correcting Natural Monopolies & Information Asymmetries:** Transmission wires, city gas pipelines, and telecom towers exhibit declining average costs over the entire range of market demand, making parallel infrastructure duplication economically wasteful. Unregulated natural monopolies extract monopoly rents; IRAs simulate competitive outcomes through **price-cap regulation**, mandatory **non-discriminatory open access**, and **interconnection mandates**. Similarly, in financial markets, healthcare, and food safety, severe **information asymmetry** prevents consumers from verifying solvency or toxicity, necessitating statutory disclosure and scientific standard-setting.
4. **Technical Complexity & Dynamic Rule-Making:** Parliament lacks both the legislative bandwidth and domain specialization to update algorithmic trading circuit-breakers, spectrum sharing protocols, or pesticide residue limits in real time. Statutory delegation allows IRAs to issue flexible subordinate legislation (regulations) responsive to rapid technological shifts.

### Principal–Agent Architecture & George Stigler's Theory of Regulatory Capture

In public choice and institutional economics, delegating authority to an IRA creates a classic **Principal–Agent problem** with multiple, often competing principals. The Legislature (and ultimately the electorate) delegates technical authority to the Regulator (Agent) to discipline regulated firms; yet the Agent possesses superior domain information (*information asymmetry*), creating scope for **agency drift**—where the regulator diverges from statutory public interest toward either bureaucratic self-preservation or subservience to the executive.

Nobel laureate **George Stigler (1971)** inverted the naive "public interest theory of regulation" with his **Economic Theory of Regulation (Regulatory Capture)**. Stigler argued that regulation is not merely imposed upon industry to protect consumers; rather, **as a rule, regulation is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit**. Concentrated producer groups face high per-capita stakes and low coordination costs (Mancur Olson's logic of collective action), allowing them to lobby, litigate, fund technical studies, and shape regulatory rules far more effectively than millions of dispersed consumers facing low individual stakes.

Capture operates through two distinct mechanisms in Indian administration:
- **Material Capture:** Driven by the **"revolving door"** (regulators migrating to corporate boards or industry consultancies post-retirement), lobbying, and opaque informal consultations that erect licensing entry barriers protecting incumbents from disruptive startups.
- **Cognitive (Cultural) Capture:** Often subtler and deadlier than bribery. Regulators rely heavily on regulated firms for real-time market data, technical modelling, and industry benchmarks; over time, regulators internalize the worldview, risk metrics, and profitability anxieties of the industry they police, treating sectoral disruption as systemic instability.

In India, regulatory failure displays a **dual-capture pathology**: alongside Stiglerian **industry capture** (visible in legacy Medical Council corruption or sluggish telecom quality-of-service enforcement), Indian IRAs suffer acute **political-executive capture**. Ministries strip regulators of financial autonomy, stuff boards with retired civil servants loyal to the administrative hierarchy, and issue sweeping "policy directives" that nullify technical independence.

## Sectoral IRA Architecture - Statutes, Mandates & 2024–2026 Reforms

India never adopted a uniform overarching statute governing regulators—unlike the 2013 draft **Regulatory Reform Bill**, which lapsed without enactment. Instead, IRAs evolved through ad hoc sectoral legislation, creating wildly uneven statutory powers, appointment structures, and appellate routes.

### Telecom & Broadcasting - TRAI and TDSAT

Established under the **Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) Act, 1997**—making it India's first sector-specific infrastructure IRA—TRAI initially combined recommendatory, tariff-setting, and dispute-adjudication powers. Constant friction with the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) and court challenges prompted the **TRAI (Amendment) Act, 2000**, which split adjudicatory functions into an independent **Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT)** while leaving TRAI with tariff-fixing, Quality of Service (QoS) norms, interconnection regulations, and **non-binding** recommendatory authority over licensing and spectrum. Broadcasting and cable services were brought under TRAI's ambit via a 2004 Central Government notification under Section 2(1)(k) of the TRAI Act.

The **Telecommunications Act, 2023** (notified in phases from June 2024 through June 2026, replacing legacy licensing with Central Government "authorisations" while formal repeal of the 1885 Telegraph Act under Section 60 awaits full operationalization) significantly altered telecom governance:
- **Board Qualification Overhaul (effective July 5, 2024):** Amended the TRAI Act to open the Chairperson post to private-sector executives with **30 years** of professional experience (having served as Director or CEO) and Member posts to those with **25 years** of experience, while removing the requirement that civil-servant nominees hold Secretary/Additional Secretary rank for at least three years. Supporters view this as breaking bureaucratic monopoly; critics warn of heightened Stiglerian capture if former telecom CXOs assume the chair without strict cooling-off rules.
- **Executive Adjudication Layer:** For contraventions under Chapter VIII (such as authorisation breaches), the 2023 Act inserts two executive-appointed tiers—an **Adjudicating Officer** (Joint Secretary rank or above) and a **Designated Appeals Committee** (senior bureaucrats)—before aggrieved parties can appeal to **TDSAT** (or Civil Courts for Third Schedule offences). Routing disputes through executive officers first marks a structural retreat from the 2000 TRAI–TDSAT separation model.

### Antitrust & Market Contestability - Competition Commission of India (CCI)

Constituted under the **Competition Act, 2002** (enacted following the Raghavan Committee Report to replace the command-era MRTP Act, 1969), the **Competition Commission of India (CCI)** functions as India's economy-wide market watchdog under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA). Unlike sectoral regulators that prescribe ex-ante prices and technical norms, the CCI traditionally operates **ex-post** on anti-competitive agreements (Section 3: cartels, bid-rigging, vertical restraints) and abuse of dominant position (Section 4), while exercising **ex-ante merger control** over "combinations" (Sections 5–6) that risk causing an **Appreciable Adverse Effect on Competition (AAEC)**. Appeals lie before the **National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)** (which absorbed the erstwhile COMPAT in 2017) and thereafter to the Supreme Court.

The **Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023** (assented April 2023; operationalized across three waves in May 2023, February–March 2024, and September 2024) re-engineered India's antitrust toolkit for digital and globalized markets:
- **Deal Value Threshold (DVT — effective September 10, 2024):** Plugs the **"killer acquisition"** loophole where Big Tech incumbents acquire asset-light, pre-revenue startups (such as legacy deals like WhatsApp or Myntra) that escaped asset-and-turnover merger thresholds. Under Section 5(d) and the 2024 Combination Regulations, any M&A transaction exceeding **₹2,000 crore** requires mandatory CCI approval if the target possesses **Substantial Business Operations (SBO) in India**, overriding the *de minimis* target exemption. Simultaneously, the overall merger review clock shrank from **210 days to 150 days**, and the **Green Channel** (deemed same-day approval for zero-overlap combinations) received statutory backing.
- **Global Turnover Penalties (effective March 6, 2024):** Amended Section 27 to compute maximum penalties (up to 10%) on an enterprise's **global turnover derived from all products and services**—statutorily overriding the Supreme Court's narrow "relevant turnover" ceiling in *Excel Corp Care v. CCI* (2017). To temper disproportionality fears, the **CCI (Determination of Monetary Penalty) Guidelines, 2024** anchor base calculations in relevant turnover while reserving global turnover adjustments for deterrent calibration against multi-product tech conglomerates.
- **Settlements, Commitments & Leniency Plus (effective Feb–March 2024):** Under Sections 48A and 48B, firms facing vertical-restraint or abuse-of-dominance inquiries (not cartels) may offer **Commitments** before the Director General's (DG) investigation report or **Settlements** after the DG report but before the final order; crucially, **no appeal lies against settlement or commitment orders**, accelerating market correction. **Leniency Plus** incentivizes a cartel whistleblower under investigation in one market to expose a second, undisclosed cartel in exchange for penalty reductions in both. Hub-and-spoke cartels and a 3-year limitation period for filing informations were also codified.

**The Digital Competition Bill (DCB) Debate (2024–2026):** Following the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance's 53rd Report (Dec 2022), MCA's Committee on Digital Competition Law (CDCL) published a **Draft Digital Competition Bill, 2024** (March 2024) modelled loosely on the EU's Digital Markets Act. It proposed **ex-ante prohibitions** (banning self-preferencing, data cross-usage, anti-steering, and tying) on designated **Systemically Significant Digital Enterprises (SSDEs)** across nine Core Digital Services. However, startups and economists warned that rigid user thresholds risked ensnaring fast-growing Indian platforms before empirical harm was established. Consequently, in **August 2025**, the Union Government **paused/withdrew the 2024 draft** and floated an RFP (November 2025) for an empirical market study on thresholds and digital market structure. In its **37th Action Taken Report (August 10, 2026)**, the Standing Committee on Finance accepted this evidence-first sequencing while spotlighting a crippling enforcement deficit: of **₹20,350.46 crore** in CCI penalties imposed up to April 2025, **₹18,512.28 crore (~91%)** stood stayed or dismissed in appellate courts, underscoring how weak judicial defense blunts antitrust deterrence.

### Financial Sector Regulators & Macro-Prudential Coordination

India's financial architecture remains largely **entity-based and fragmented** across six statutory regulators, coordinated by one non-statutory apex council:

- **Reserve Bank of India (RBI):** Established under the **RBI Act, 1934** (nationalized 1949) and empowered by the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949**, **FEMA 1999**, and **Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007**. Combines central banking (monetary policy via the Monetary Policy Committee) with banking/NBFC supervision and payment-system regulation. Notably, RBI lacks a dedicated independent appellate tribunal like SAT; statutory appeals against specific licensing/directive actions generally lie to the **Central Government**, with constitutional writ review before High Courts.
- **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI):** Created by executive resolution in 1988 and granted statutory status via the **SEBI Act, 1992** (after the Harshad Mehta scam); absorbed the Forward Markets Commission (commodities) in 2015. Regulates capital markets, intermediaries, mutual funds, and Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs), with appeals going to the **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)**. In **December 2025**, the government introduced the **Securities Markets Code, 2025 (Bill No. 200 of 2025)**—under examination by the Standing Committee on Finance through 2026—to consolidate the SEBI Act 1992, SCRA 1956, and Depositories Act 1996 into a single rulebook. While modernizing decriminalized civil penalties and setting an eight-year investigation limit, legislative analysts (PRS India) flag serious independence regressions: executive discretion to terminate members without independent inquiry, narrowing of orders appealable to SAT, and keeping the SEBI Ombudsperson staffed by SEBI's own officers.
- **Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI):** Statutory body under the **IRDA Act, 1999** (Malhotra Committee recommendation) policing insurers, intermediaries, and policyholder solvency under the Insurance Act, 1938; appeals lie before **SAT**.
- **Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA):** Initially executive (2003), became statutory under the **PFRDA Act, 2013** to regulate the National Pension System (NPS), Atal Pension Yojana, and pension funds; appeals also lie before **SAT**.
- **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI):** Statutory regulator under the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016** (under MCA) overseeing Insolvency Professionals (IPs), Insolvency Professional Agencies (IPAs), Information Utilities (IUs), and registered valuers, alongside framing CIRP/liquidation regulations. A major governance gap was closed by the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026** (notified effective **May 26, 2026**), which inserted **Section 220(7)** creating a direct statutory right of appeal to **NCLAT** within 30 days against IBBI Disciplinary Committee orders—ending the anomaly where aggrieved professionals had to invoke High Court writ jurisdiction.
- **International Financial Services Centres Authority (IFSCA):** Established in **April 2020** under the **IFSCA Act, 2019** (headquartered at **GIFT City, Gandhinagar**) as India's first **unified financial regulator**. Within notified IFSCs, IFSCA exercises consolidated powers previously split across RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, and PFRDA—covering banking, capital markets, insurance, bullion, aircraft leasing, and (notified November 2026) commodity trading contracts—with appeals routed to **SAT** under relevant statutory enactments.
- **Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC):** Established in **December 2010 by an executive order** (non-statutory!) under the Ministry of Finance following the Raghuram Rajan Committee (2008) and the bitter 2010 SEBI–IRDAI turf war over ULIPs. **Chaired by the Union Finance Minister**, its members include heads of RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, IBBI, and IFSCA alongside senior Finance/MCA Secretaries and the Chief Economic Adviser. Its **FSDC Sub-Committee**, chaired by the **RBI Governor**, handles technical macro-prudential surveillance and inter-regulatory coordination without superseding any regulator's statutory autonomy.

### Energy & Infrastructure Regulators - CERC/SERCs and PNGRB

**Electricity Regulatory Commissions (CERC & SERCs):** Constituted under the **Electricity Act, 2003** (which consolidated earlier 1998 reforms and delicensed thermal generation), the **Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC)** regulates tariffs of centrally owned generators and inter-state transmission/trading, while **State Electricity Regulatory Commissions (SERCs)** determine intra-state retail tariffs, license DISCOMs, and monitor Renewable Purchase Obligations (RPOs). Both answer on appeal to the **Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL)**. By 2025–26, CERC expanded into market-making for India's energy transition via **Virtual Power Purchase Agreement (VPPA) Guidelines (2025)** and **Carbon Credit Certificate Trading Regulations (2026)**. Yet SERCs remain textbook sites of **populist political capture**: state governments delay tariff petitions, leave statutory Section 65 agricultural subsidies unpaid, and let DISCOMs accumulate massive **"regulatory assets"** (deferred cost recoveries). To break this cycle, the Ministry of Power floated the **Draft Electricity (Amendment) Bill, 2025** (October 2025, under consultation in 2026), proposing mandatory cost-reflective tariffs, **suo motu tariff determination** by commissions from April 1 if DISCOMs stall, shared distribution network access, 120-day adjudicatory deadlines, expanded APTEL bench strength, and cross-government removal references against CERC/SERC members.

**Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB):** Statutory regulator created under the **PNGRB Act, 2006** to foster competitive gas markets, authorize City Gas Distribution (CGD) networks and common-carrier pipelines, and protect consumers. Crucially for Prelims precision, PNGRB regulates **midstream and downstream** activities—refining, processing, storage, transportation, distribution, marketing, and sale—while **explicitly excluding upstream production of crude oil and natural gas** (which remains with the Directorate General of Hydrocarbons / Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas). Appeals against PNGRB orders lie before **APTEL**.

### Consumer, Real Estate, Health & Safety Regulators - RERA, FSSAI, NMC & AERB

**Real Estate Regulatory Authorities (RERAs):** Enacted under the **Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016** to cure chronic builder diversion of buyer funds, opaque area definitions, and delayed possession in an asymmetric housing market. Key statutory guardrails include:
- Mandatory prior registration of residential/commercial projects where land exceeds **500 sq metres** OR proposed units exceed **8 apartments** (inclusive of all phases).
- **70% Escrow Ring-Fencing:** Promoters must deposit **70% of buyer receivables** in a separate scheduled-bank account strictly dedicated to land and construction costs of that specific project, withdrawable only in proportion to certified completion (jointly authenticated by an engineer, architect, and chartered accountant).
- **Carpet-Area Standardization:** Eliminated misleading "super built-up area" pricing by mandating sales strictly on net usable **carpet area**, paired with a five-year structural defect liability period.
- **Adjudication & Implementation Deficit:** State RERAs handle regulatory compliance and project delays, while a dedicated **Adjudicating Officer** determines compensation, with appeals lying to the **Real Estate Appellate Tribunal (REAT)** and then the High Court. Street-level execution, however, suffers from diluted state rules (excluding ongoing projects), toothless recovery certificates ignored by District Collectors, and severe staffing deficits.

**Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI):** Created as an autonomous statutory body under the **Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006**—which repealed the colonial-era **Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954** alongside seven fragmented food orders—to transition India from punitive adulteration policing to **science-based standards** governing manufacture, storage, distribution, sale, and import. Administratively housed under the **Union Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW)**—and **not** under the Director General of Health Services (DGHS)—FSSAI operates through Scientific Panels and a Scientific Committee, with food-safety offences adjudicated by designated Adjudicating Officers and appealed to the **Food Safety Appellate Tribunal (FSAT)**. Enforcement remains constrained by fragmented state Food Safety Commissioners, chronic shortage of NABL-accredited testing laboratories, and delayed front-of-pack nutritional labelling norms.

**National Medical Commission (NMC):** Established under the **National Medical Commission Act, 2019**, repealing the **Indian Medical Council Act, 1956** to dismantle the elected **Medical Council of India (MCI)**—which Parliamentary Standing Committees and the Supreme Court (*Modern Dental College*, 2016) had indicted as an entrenched cartel plagued by inspection bribery and regulatory capture. The 33-member NMC replaces purely professional self-regulation with a predominantly nominated regulatory architecture organized into **four Autonomous Boards**: (1) Under-Graduate Medical Education Board (UGMEB), (2) Post-Graduate Medical Education Board (PGMEB), (3) Medical Assessment and Rating Board (MARB — inspecting/rating colleges), and (4) Ethics and Medical Registration Board (EMRB). A **Medical Advisory Council** provides the federal platform for State/UT views, while a common licentiate **National Exit Test (NExT)** is statutorily mandated (still awaiting operationalization as of late 2026). Structurally, however, NMC exemplifies **executive concentration**: first appeals against Autonomous Boards go to NMC itself, and second appeals (as well as appeals against NMC orders under Section 9(6)) lie directly with the **Central Government**—not an independent appellate tribunal.

**Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB) & the SHANTI Act 2025 Shift:** For over four decades after its creation in November 1983 through an executive notification (S.O. 4772 under the Atomic Energy Act, 1962), AERB stood as India's starkest textbook illustration of structural regulatory capture. As the **CAG (2012)** indicted, AERB lacked statutory autonomy, reported administratively to the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) and Department of Atomic Energy (DAE)—the very entity promoting nuclear power and operating NPCIL—and had no power to frame rules independently or impose deterrents. The enactment of the **Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Act, 2025** (assented December 21, 2025; repealing the 1962 Atomic Energy Act and 2010 CLND Act) formally elevated AERB to **statutory status** as private-sector entry opens, made it accountable to Parliament, and designated **APTEL** as the appellate tribunal for nuclear disputes.

Yet statutory elevation alone does not automatically guarantee insulation from executive capture—a tension underscored when the Supreme Court issued notices in **August 2026** scrutinizing, among other constitutional questions, whether Section 17(4) governing AERB appointments perpetuates an institutional conflict of interest. For the full statutory architecture of civil nuclear licensing, tiered liability caps, private-reactor governance, and AERB's technical safety mandate under the new Act, see [[2. Nuclear Physics, 3-Stage Cycle, PFBR, SMRs & SHANTI Act 2025#SHANTI Act 2025 and Statutory AERB|SHANTI Act 2025 & Statutory AERB]].

## Systemic Regulatory Pathologies & Reform Architecture

Creating dozens of statutory agencies did not automatically produce a high-trust regulatory state. Across sectors, Indian IRAs exhibit six recurring institutional pathologies that undermine both investor certainty and constitutional accountability.

### 1. Fusion of Legislative, Executive & Quasi-Judicial Functions (The FSLRC Critique)

Modern IRAs operate as **"miniature states"** inside their domains, violating classical separation of powers: the same board drafts subordinate regulations (**legislative**), licenses and inspects market participants (**executive**), prosecutes alleged violations, and imposes crippling financial penalties or market bans (**quasi-judicial**). This concentration strains the natural-justice principle of ***nemo judex in causa sua*** (no one should be judge in their own cause)—an investigator or whole-time member who approves a show-cause notice suffers strong confirmation bias when adjudicating guilt.

The **Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission (FSLRC, 2013)**, chaired by **Justice B.N. Srikrishna**, diagnosed this structural flaw across Indian financial regulators and proposed the draft **Indian Financial Code (IFC)** built on three institutional firewalls:
- **Internal Structural Separation:** Creating a distinct, insulated **Administrative Law Wing / Adjudicating Cadre** strictly separated from investigation and supervision staff, governed by codified due-process rules (reasoned speaking orders, mandatory disclosure of adverse material, proportionality in penalties). Notably, PRS India's 2026 review of the pending **Securities Markets Code Bill, 2025** highlights that even a decade later, the Bill settles for mere *case-by-case* officer separation rather than FSLRC's independent adjudicating cadre.
- **Consolidating Fragmented Watchdogs:** Proposing a **Unified Financial Agency (UFA)** merging SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, and FMC alongside a consolidated **Financial Sector Appellate Tribunal (FSAT)**—a blueprint partially piloted in GIFT City through IFSCA, yet resisted by turf-conscious domestic ministries.
- **Principles-Based Ownership of Failure:** Replacing ad hoc circulars with transparent, time-bound rule-making backed by formal legal review.

### 2. Retired-Bureaucrat Colonization & Appointment Capture

While statutes mandate "persons of ability, integrity, and standing" with domain expertise in law, economics, engineering, or finance, empirical audits (including 2nd ARC and CUTS International studies) reveal that senior IRA chairs and whole-time memberships have overwhelmingly functioned as **post-retirement sinecures for IAS and allied civil servants**. Selection committees are typically dominated by the Cabinet Secretary and Secretary of the parent line ministry, creating an incentive structure where serving bureaucrats defer to ministerial preferences to secure lucrative post-retirement regulatory postings.

This colonization produces two distortions: first, **domain-expertise deficit**, leaving regulators ill-equipped to audit complex algorithmic pricing, derivative leverage, or spectrum engineering; second, **re-bureaucratization**, where IRAs replicate rigid, file-pushing secretariat culture rather than agile market supervision. Conversely, opening top posts to corporate executives (as under the 2023 Telecom Act for TRAI) without statutory multi-year **cooling-off periods**, blind-trust asset disclosures, and parliamentary confirmation risks trading bureaucratic capture for Stiglerian industry capture.

### 3. Ministry Turf Wars & Fiscal Dependence

Line ministries rarely surrender discretion gracefully after Parliament creates an IRA. Executive control is routinely reasserted through three statutory levers:
- **Sweeping "Policy Directive" Clauses:** Almost every Indian regulatory statute (e.g., Section 25 of the TRAI Act, Section 16 of the SEBI Act, Section 55 of the Competition Act, Section 107 of the Electricity Act) empowers the Central Government to issue binding directions on "questions of policy," with the government's own judgment on what constitutes "policy" declared final. Ministries routinely stretch operational tariff or licensing disputes into "policy" to override technical orders.
- **Financial Strangulation:** Unlike self-funded regulators (SEBI or RBI, which retain fee/seigniorage revenue), bodies such as TRAI, CCI, PNGRB, and FSSAI depend on annual budgetary grants routed through their parent ministry's Demand for Grants—turning administrative staffing and research budgets into instruments of subtle discipline.
- **Parallel Licensing Monopoly:** Retaining licensing authority inside the ministry (DoT for telecom, MoP/state governments for power, Central Government for nuclear licenses under SHANTI Act) leaves the IRA policing rules it cannot ultimately enforce through license revocation.

### 4. Overlapping Jurisdictions & Regulatory Arbitrage

Because Indian regulators grew sector-by-sector without a statutory coordination protocol, converging technologies—FinTech, digital telecom, patented biotech, hybrid insurance-investment products—trigger costly turf wars and forum-shopping:

- **SEBI vs. IRDAI (2010 ULIP Dispute):** When Unit Linked Insurance Plans blended insurance with mutual-fund-like equity investment, SEBI banned 14 insurers from issuing ULIPs without its approval, prompting an unprecedented open clash with IRDAI. Parliament intervened via the Securities and Insurance Laws (Amendment and Validation) Act, 2010 awarding ULIPs to IRDAI, and the government hastily created **FSDC** to prevent future financial turf wars.
- **CCI vs. TRAI (*CCI v. Bharti Airtel*, 2018):** When Reliance Jio accused incumbent telecom operators (Airtel, Vodafone, Idea) of cartelizing to deny Points of Interconnection (PoI), the Supreme Court drew a **sequencing doctrine** to harmonize sectoral and antitrust jurisdiction. It held that technical telecom compliance questions (licensing obligations, PoI adequacy) must **first** be decided by the sectoral expert (**TRAI**); only after TRAI finds a statutory breach can the **CCI** exercise follow-on jurisdiction to examine anti-competitive cartelization. Critics note this sequencing risks delaying antitrust intervention until market harm becomes irreversible.
- **CCI vs. Controller of Patents (*Ericsson* & *Vifor* Disputes, 2023–2026):** In July 2023, a Delhi High Court Division Bench quashed CCI investigations into Standard Essential Patent (SEP) royalty practices by Ericsson and Monsanto, holding that the **Patents Act, 1970** (Chapter XVI governing compulsory licensing and patent abuse) is a *special law* that overrides the general Competition Act, 2002. In **September 2025**, the Supreme Court dismissed CCI's appeal in *Ericsson* because private parties had settled, expressly leaving the jurisdictional law open. Then, in **February 2026**, the Supreme Court stayed a similar NCLAT exclusion order in the *Vifor International* pharmaceutical patent case and agreed to definitively adjudicate whether patent exercise is wholly immune from antitrust scrutiny.
- **Emerging Cross-Sector Frictions:** Digital lending apps, crypto-assets, and data-driven platforms straddle RBI, SEBI, CCI, MeitY, and the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI)—highlighting the obsolescence of siloed jurisdiction when data monopolies act simultaneously as privacy infringements and competition distortions.

### 5. Democratic Deficit & Weak Parliamentary Oversight

IRAs wield coercive quasi-legislative and taxing-equivalent powers (fees, penalties, tariff hikes) without facing the ballot box. In mature regulatory democracies (US Congressional oversight hearings; UK Select Committees), insulation from the **executive** is counterbalanced by rigorous accountability to the **legislature**.

In India, this accountability loop is broken. Annual reports tabled by IRAs receive perfunctory scrutiny; Parliamentary Standing Committees lack dedicated technical secretariats to audit complex economic regulations; and the **Committee on Subordinate Legislation** reviews only a fraction of thousands of regulatory circulars issued annually. The result is an accountability paradox: regulators are simultaneously **too dependent on the political executive** and **too unaccountable to Parliament and citizens**.

### 6. Absence of Mandatory Regulatory Impact Assessments (RIA)

Sound regulatory governance rests on **Regulatory Impact Assessment (RIA)**—a systematic, ex-ante and ex-post methodology evaluating whether a proposed regulation solves a genuine market failure, estimating compliance costs vs. public benefits, testing proportionality against lighter alternatives, and conducting mandatory **public notice-and-comment consultations**.

Both the **FSLRC (2013)** and the **Damodaran Committee on Reforming the Regulatory Environment (2013)**—reiterated by the Economic Survey and NITI Aayog—recommended making pre-legislative consultation and cost-benefit RIA legally mandatory across all Indian IRAs. While **TRAI** (under statutory transparency mandate, Section 11(4)), **IBBI** (under its 2018 Mechanism for Issuing Regulations), **SEBI** (via 2025 consultation framework), and **CCI** (mandatory public consultation added by the 2023 Amendment) have progressively adopted consultation papers, **no cross-cutting Indian statute mandates binding RIA or sunset clauses**. Consequently, regulators frequently issue overnight circulars imposing steep compliance burdens on MSMEs and startups without empirical cost-benefit validation or reasoned responses to stakeholder objections.

> - **First Independent Regulator Trap:** **TRAI (1997)**—not PNGRB (2006) and not SEBI (executive 1988, statutory 1992 capital-markets watchdog)—is routinely cited by UPSC Prelims keys as India's first sector-specific infrastructure regulator; never let a statement swap PNGRB or CERC as the "first regulatory body set up by GoI".
> - **PNGRB Upstream Exclusion (Verified Prelims 2025 Trap):** PNGRB regulates **midstream and downstream** petroleum/gas activities (refining, processing, storage, pipelines, CGD, marketing/sale) and **explicitly excludes upstream production** of crude oil and natural gas (handled by DGH/MoPNG).
> - **APTEL's Triple Appellate Umbrella:** Appeals to the **Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL)** cover **three** distinct sectors—**CERC/SERCs** (Electricity Act 2003), **PNGRB** (PNGRB Act 2006), and now **AERB** (designated under the **SHANTI Act, 2025**).
> - **SAT's Quadruple Financial Umbrella:** The **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)** hears appeals not only against **SEBI**, but also **IRDAI**, **PFRDA**, and specified enforcement orders of **IFSCA**—whereas **RBI** has *no* dedicated appellate tribunal, and **IBBI** Disciplinary Committee orders now go to **NCLAT** (w.e.f. May 26, 2026 under Section 220(7) IBC).
> - **FSDC Status & Chair Distinction:** **FSDC** is a **non-statutory** executive body under the Ministry of Finance (not an organ of NITI Aayog), chaired by the **Union Finance Minister**; its **Sub-Committee** is chaired by the **RBI Governor**.
> - **FSSAI Administrative Control Trap (Prelims 2018):** FSSAI is an autonomous statutory body under **MoHFW** headed by a Secretary-rank Chairperson and CEO—it is **NOT** under the charge of the Director General of Health Services (DGHS).
> - **AERB Statutory Transition (2025–26 Shift):** Legacy notes call AERB a *non-statutory* executive body (1983 order under DAE/AEC); post-December 2025, AERB holds **statutory status under the SHANTI Act, 2025**.
> - **Situational Natural-Justice Trap (*Nemo Judex* & Proportionality):** In Prelims situational scenarios involving a regulator who both drafts a show-cause allegation and sits on the final penalty bench, or imposes a blanket multi-year ban without considering the respondent's written submissions (as seen in recent NCLAT/High Court quashing of IBBI orders), the legally sound administrative remedy requires **structural separation of investigation and adjudication**, **reasoned speaking orders**, and **proportionality**—not blanket immunity for executive discretion.

>   - ***CCI v. Bharti Airtel* (2018):** Harmonious sequencing—sectoral regulator (TRAI) adjudicates technical/licensing breach first; CCI exercises follow-on antitrust scrutiny.
>   - ***Excel Corp Care v. CCI* (2017) vs. 2023 Amendment:** Judicial proportionality ("relevant turnover") recalibrated by statutory global-turnover deterrence + 2024 Penalty Guidelines.
>   - ***Ericsson v. CCI* (Delhi HC 2023; SC Sept 2025) & *Vifor* Stay (SC Feb 2026):** Unresolved statutory boundary between Patents Act (special law) and Competition Act.
> - **Structural Way Forward ("Independent Yet Accountable" 4-Pillar Reform):**
>   1. **Enact a Cross-Sectoral Regulatory Governance Framework** mandating transparent Search-cum-Selection Committees with domain-expert quotas, fixed non-extendable tenures, and 3-year post-retirement cooling-off bars.
>   2. **Mandate Statutory RIA & Notice-and-Comment Rule-Making** with compulsory reasoned disposal of public objections and 5-year sunset reviews.
>   3. **Institutionalize Functional Separation** (FSLRC model) separating investigation from an independent adjudicating cadre, paired with tribunal capacity upgrades (avoiding executive-first bottlenecks like Telecom Act 2023's AO/DAC layers).
>   4. **Shift Accountability from Ministry to Parliament** via dedicated Parliamentary Standing Committees backed by technical research staff, independent fee-based financing, and statutory inter-regulatory coordination protocols (modeled on FSDC / mandatory MoUs).

### Prelims Trap Signature
