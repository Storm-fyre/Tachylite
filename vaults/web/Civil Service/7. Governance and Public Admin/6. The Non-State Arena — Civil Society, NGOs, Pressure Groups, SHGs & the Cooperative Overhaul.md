# Chapter 6 - The Non-State Arena — Civil Society, NGOs, Pressure Groups, SHGs & the Cooperative Overhaul

## Civil Society, NGOs & the FCRA Regulatory Architecture

Modern governance operates across a **tri-sectoral equilibrium**: the **State** (coercive authority and public goods), the **Market** (price discovery and private allocation), and **Civil Society**—the "Third Sector" of voluntary associations, charitable trusts, community federations, and advocacy movements. In a continental democracy like India, where formal bureaucracy often thins out beyond the block headquarters, civil society discharges two irreducible functions: **participatory last-mile delivery** (reaching tribal hamlets, urban slums, and disaster zones where state machinery struggles) and **diagonal accountability** (mobilizing social audits, PIL litigation, and legislative pressure that birthed rights frameworks from RTI to MGNREGA).

### Legal Registration Statutes & the NGO-DARPAN Framework

India lacks a single unified Non-Profit Statute; voluntary organisations (VOs) and Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs) incorporate under three distinct legal tracks depending on governance structure and scale:

1. **Societies Registration Act, 1860:** Requires a minimum of **seven persons** (for state-level) or members across states (for all-India) with a Memorandum of Association and Rules, registered before the State Registrar of Societies—favoured by cultural, educational, and grassroots charitable bodies.
2. **Indian Trusts Act, 1882 & State Public Trusts Acts:** While the 1882 Central Act technically governs *private* trusts, *public charitable trusts* operate under general equitable principles or specific state laws (such as the Maharashtra Public Trusts Act, 1950), managed by a Board of Trustees via an irrevocable trust deed.
3. **Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013 (formerly Section 25, 1956 Act):** Grants corporate legal personality with limited liability to non-profit ventures promoting commerce, science, education, social welfare, or environmental protection, prohibiting dividend distribution and mandating full profit reinvestment under strict Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) oversight.

To address chronic opacity across an estimated **30+ lakh registered societies/trusts** (many defunct or paper entities), **NITI Aayog** administers the **NGO-DARPAN** portal. Any VO/NGO seeking grants from Union Ministries or public agencies must register on NGO-DARPAN to obtain a mandatory **Unique ID (Darpan ID)** linked to the PAN details of its governing office-bearers and verified against Income Tax (Sections 12A/80G) databases—creating a centralized audit trail across central ministries.

### FCRA (2010 & 2020 Amendment) - Security Sovereignty vs. Grassroots Capacity

Foreign philanthropic inflows into Indian civil society are policed not by the Ministry of Finance, but by the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)** under the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act (FCRA), 2010** (which replaced the Emergency-era FCRA, 1976). Any association, person, or company accepting "foreign contribution" for a cultural, economic, educational, religious, or social programme must hold either a **5-year renewable FCRA registration** or project-specific **Prior Permission**, and is strictly prohibited from engaging in electoral politics or activities deemed "detrimental to the national interest."

Citing misuse of foreign funds for illicit religious conversions, anti-infrastructure agitation, and opaque pass-through layering (including intelligence inputs linking certain foreign-funded outfits to stalled power and mining projects), Parliament enacted the **FCRA (Amendment) Act, 2020** (effective September 29, 2020), fundamentally re-engineering four structural rules:

1. **Absolute Ban on Sub-Granting (Amended Section 7):** Previously, an FCRA-registered organization could transfer foreign grants to another FCRA-registered NGO. The 2020 Amendment imposed a **complete prohibition on transferring foreign contribution to any other person or entity**. While closing layering loopholes, this broke the classic **"hub-and-spoke" philanthropic model**, where large urban capacity-builders vetted and routed international funds to small rural, tribal, and disability grassroots groups lacking administrative bandwidth to court foreign donors directly.
2. **Administrative Expense Cap Slashed from 50% to 20% (Amended Section 8):** Capped overheads—salaries of non-field staff, rent, accounting, legal compliance, and research coordination—at **20%** of annual foreign receipts. While curbing extravagant urban headquarters, the ceiling disproportionately penalizes **policy think-tanks, legal-aid clinics, and public-health research outfits**, where human intellectual capital *is* the core developmental output rather than bricks-and-mortar procurement.
3. **Single Primary FCRA Account at SBI, New Delhi Main Branch (Amended Section 17):** Mandated that every FCRA-registered body nationwide receive all foreign inflows exclusively into a designated primary account at the **State Bank of India (SBI), New Delhi Main Branch (NDMB)**—though NGOs may open secondary "utilization accounts" in any scheduled bank thereafter—enabling real-time central surveillance via the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND).
4. **Mandatory Aadhaar & Expanded Prohibitions (Sections 3, 12A & 13):** Added **"Public Servants"** (as defined in IPC/BNS) explicitly to the list of entities barred from accepting foreign contributions; mandated **Aadhaar** identification for all office-bearers and directors (passport/OCI for foreign nationals); doubled the maximum suspension period from 180 to **360 days**; and empowered MHA to halt utilization of existing unspent funds via a **summary inquiry** on mere suspicion of violation.

In ***Noel Harper v. Union of India* (April 2022)**, a three-judge Supreme Court bench **upheld the constitutional validity of all 2020 FCRA amendments**. Doctrinally, the Court held that **no citizen or association possesses an absolute Fundamental Right under Article 19(1)(c) or 19(1)(g) to receive foreign charity**; foreign funds can subtly reshape domestic polity and sovereign policy, justifying heightened statutory scrutiny. The bench offered only a narrow procedural softening—reading down Section 12A to clarify that **Indian passports** could also be accepted alongside Aadhaar for Indian office-bearers.

**Implementation Realism (2024–2026):** Active FCRA-registered NGOs contracted from ~22,762 in late 2021 to roughly **~16,000+** by 2025–26 following strict renewal scrutiny, cancellations, and MHA clarifications (including late-2024/2025 compliance drives barring foreign funds for unauthorized political/advocacy publications). Tightening oversight further, MHA notified the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Rules, 2026** (effective **June 22, 2026**), mandating activity-specific and State/UT-specific registration mapped to a prescribed Schedule (explicitly specifying *"excluding proselytisation"* for religious activities), codifying a broad statutory definition of **"Key Functionary"** (directors, partners, trustees, HUF *Karta*, and governing members—ordinarily barring associations with non-PIO foreign nationals as key functionaries), introducing a **minimum ₹10 lakh foreign-contribution utilization threshold** for certificate renewal, and requiring **75% utilization** before receiving subsequent instalments under Prior Permission. Parallelly, the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026** (referred on **August 12, 2026** to a 31-member Joint Parliamentary Committee chaired by **Dr. Sanjay Jaiswal**; *not yet enacted*) proposes creating a statutory **Designated Authority** in which all assets created from foreign contributions vest upon cancellation, surrender, or non-renewal of an FCRA certificate—sharpening civil-society concerns over permanent asset forfeiture—while lowering maximum imprisonment for certain violations from 5 years to 1 year. Paradoxically, aggregate foreign inflows remained resilient (~₹21,000–22,000+ crore annually), indicating **consolidation rather than disappearance**: large institutional and faith-based charities absorbed the compliance overhead, while smaller secular grassroots rights, environmental, and rural-research NGOs experienced acute funding contraction—prompting urgent reliance on domestic capital.

## Domestic Philanthropy (CSR & Social Stock Exchange) and Pressure Groups

### Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR - Section 135, Companies Act 2013)

India became the first major economy to mandate corporate social spending by statute through **Section 135 and Schedule VII of the Companies Act, 2013** (effective April 2014, administered by MCA). Any company meeting **at least one** of three thresholds in the immediately preceding financial year—**Net worth ≥ ₹500 crore**, **Turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore**, or **Net profit ≥ ₹5 crore**—must constitute a Board-level CSR Committee and spend **at least 2% of its average net profits of the preceding three financial years** on specified **Schedule VII** developmental activities (eradicating hunger/poverty, healthcare, education, gender equality, environmental sustainability, rural development, slum redevelopment, disaster management, or notified national funds like PMNRF/PM CARES). Crucially, expenditures undertaken in the normal course of business or directly benefiting the company's own employees are **strictly excluded** from CSR.

Originally designed as a soft **"Comply-or-Explain"** norm, the **Companies (Amendment) Acts of 2019 and 2020** hardened CSR into a **mandatory statutory obligation**: unspent funds for non-ongoing projects must be transferred within **6 months** of fiscal year-end to a Schedule VII fund, while unspent funds for multi-year ongoing projects must be parked in a dedicated **"Unspent CSR Account"** for three years (failing which they transfer to a Schedule VII fund), enforced by decriminalized civil monetary penalties. Marking a landmark 2026 statutory bridge between corporate philanthropy and capital-market transparency, MCA notified the **Companies (Corporate Social Responsibility Policy) Amendment Rules, 2026** on **May 27, 2026** (G.S.R. 415(E) & 416(E)), inserting **item `(xiii)` into Schedule VII** and **Rule 4A** into the CSR Rules, 2014—officially permitting companies to route **up to 10% of their annual mandatory CSR expenditure** through **Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP)** instruments on the Social Stock Exchange (exempting SSE-listed NPOs from separate Form CSR-1 registration and exempting subscribing companies from commissioning separate corporate impact assessments, with NPOs required to complete projects within 3 financial years).

**Empirical Assessment & Structural Skew:** According to MCA's National CSR Portal, total domestic CSR spending crossed **₹34,908 crore in FY 2023–24** across more than 27,000 companies (a massive domestic capital pool exceeding annual FCRA inflows). Yet two structural distortions blunt its developmental equity:
- **Severe Regional Concentration:** Because Section 135(5) directs companies to *"give preference to the local area and areas around it where it operates,"* industrialized corporate hubs—**Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Delhi-NCR**—absorb nearly **half of all localized CSR expenditure**, leaving capital-starved Aspirational Districts in Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, and the North-East chronically underserved.
- **Sectoral & Governance Skew:** Over **60%** of CSR spending clusters around visible, low-risk **education and healthcare** infrastructure, starving structural deficits like malnutrition, tribal forest livelihoods, climate adaptation, and mental health—while several corporations route CSR through captive in-house foundations to manufacture brand equity rather than partnering with independent grassroots NGOs.

### SEBI Social Stock Exchange (SSE) - Unlocking Retail Philanthropy

Conceived in Union Budget 2019–20 and operationalized by **SEBI** (July 2022 ICDR/LODR amendments) as a dedicated segment within **NSE and BSE**, the **Social Stock Exchange (SSE)** bridges transparent domestic capital with verifiable social impact. Eligible entities—both **Not-for-Profit Organisations (NPOs)** and **For-Profit Social Enterprises (FPSEs)**—must establish **primacy of social intent** (at least **67%** of their 3-year average revenue, expenditure, or target beneficiary base concentrated in **16–17 notified social sectors** ranging from poverty alleviation and nutrition to climate livelihoods).

NPOs raise funds via **Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP) Instruments**—non-tradable philanthropic securities issued exclusively on SSE where investors receive **audited social return** rather than financial interest or principal repayment, verified annually by SEBI-empanelled **Social Impact Assessors (SIAs)**. Recognizing sluggish early uptake caused by high ticket sizes and rigid thresholds, **SEBI rolled out phased SSE liberalizations across December 2023, 2025, and its April 15, 2026 Circular**: retaining the **minimum ZCZP issue size at ₹50 lakh** (reduced from ₹1 crore in December 2023), slashing the **minimum application size** from ₹2 lakh → ₹10,000 → **₹1,000** to draw retail citizen donors, lowering the **minimum subscription threshold from 75% to 50%** (effectively ₹25 lakh on a ₹50 lakh issue) for modular/per-unit projects so partial funding still enables execution without full refund, and extending **NPO registration validity without fundraising from 2 years to 3 years** (subject to SSE approval for the 3rd year).

Even with ~140+ NPOs onboarded by 2026, actual funds raised via ZCZP issues remained modest (roughly ₹30–50+ crore across ~15–20 issues) prior to the **May 27, 2026 MCA CSR–SSE Rules** permitting companies to channel up to 10% of mandatory CSR into ZCZP instruments. Three structural bottlenecks still explain slow retail adoption: absence of superior Income Tax incentives beyond standard Section 80G deductions (unlike UK Social Investment Tax Relief), steep audit and SIA compliance fees for small rural NPOs, and low awareness among retail citizen investors.

### Pressure Groups - Taxonomy, Policy Facilitation & Capture Risks

Unlike political parties, **pressure groups** (interest groups) do not contest elections to capture formal state power; instead, they aggregate organized interests to influence legislation, executive rulemaking, and judicial outcomes. Following Almond and Powell's institutional taxonomy adapted to India, four distinct formations dominate public policy:

1. **Business & Industry Chambers:** Peak federations—**FICCI (1927)**, **CII (Confederation of Indian Industry)**, **ASSOCHAM**, and sectoral lobbies like **NASSCOM** and **IAMAI**—operate as institutional insiders through pre-Budget consultations, WTO/FTA negotiating briefs, sectoral regulatory committees, and economic research. While supplying vital real-time market intelligence that improves technical policymaking, the **complete absence of a statutory Lobbying Disclosure Act in India** blurs legitimate policy advocacy into opaque **crony lobbying and regulatory capture**.
2. **Agrarian & Farmers' Organisations:** Mass-based mobilizational groups—**Bharatiya Kisan Union (BKU)**, **All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS)**, **Shetkari Sanghatana**, and coalition fronts like the **Samyukt Kisan Morcha (SKM)**—wield unmatched electoral veto power through street blockades, *gheraos*, and vote-bank signalling. Their decisive campaign forced the **repeal of the three Central Farm Laws in November 2021** and continues shaping debates on statutory MSP guarantees, electricity subsidies, and trade tariffs—though critics note that affluent surplus-producing farmers in Punjab, Haryana, and Western UP often dominate agendas over marginal dryland farmers and landless tenants.
3. **Trade Unions:** Historically aligned with political parties—**BMS** (Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, RSS-affiliated), **INTUC** (Congress), **AITUC** (CPI), **CITU** (CPI-M), and **HMS**—representing largely the ~10% formal workforce via tripartite bargaining at the **Indian Labour Conference (ILC)** and nationwide strikes over privatization and the four Labour Codes. Recent governance evolution includes nascent informal/gig-worker unions (e.g., IFAT for platform delivery riders) pushing state-level gig-worker social security laws (Rajasthan, Karnataka).
4. **Civic, Rights & Ecological Lobbies:** Issue-based movements such as **MKSS** (Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan — architects of RTI and social audits), **ADR** (Association for Democratic Reforms — whose PIL led to the Supreme Court striking down the **Electoral Bonds Scheme in February 2024**), **CSE** (Centre for Science and Environment), and grassroots ecological movements (Narmada Bachao Andolan, Hasdeo Aranya forest resistance) expand constitutional rights and environmental compliance. Their democratic paradox lies in **uneven legitimacy**: when unelected urban or transnational advocacy groups stall vetted infrastructure projects through repeated litigation without bearing local livelihood costs, accountability tensions sharpen.

## Women's Self-Help Groups - From Microcredit to SHE-Marts

India's **Women's Self-Help Group (SHG)** movement represents the country's most successful indigenous institutional innovation in participatory poverty alleviation and gender agency. Organized as informal affinity collectives of **10–20 rural poor women** practicing *Panchasutra* (regular meetings, regular savings, internal lending, timely repayment, and transparent bookkeeping), SHGs transform isolated, collateral-less women into bankable, socially empowered economic actors.

### DAY-NRLM & the NABARD SHG–Bank Linkage Backbone

- **DAY-NRLM (Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana – National Rural Livelihoods Mission):** Launched in 2011 by the **Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD)** (restructuring the failure-prone SGSY), DAY-NRLM builds a **three-tier community institutional architecture**: primary **SHGs** aggregate into **Village Organisations (VOs)**, which federate upward into **Cluster Level Federations (CLFs)**—ensuring women's collectives endure long after state project staff withdraw. By **2025–26**, DAY-NRLM has mobilized over **10.05 crore rural women** across roughly **90.9 lakh SHGs**, backed by Revolving Funds, Community Investment Funds (CIF), and collateral-free bank credit up to **₹20 lakh**.
- **NABARD SHG–Bank Linkage Programme (SHG-BLP):** Pioneered by **NABARD in 1992** (and **not** initiated by SBI—a classic UPSC Prelims trap!), SHG-BLP connects informal groups directly to **Scheduled Commercial Banks, Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), and Cooperative Banks** based on **joint peer liability** rather than physical collateral. According to NABARD's *Status of Microfinance in India (2024–25)*, the programme encompasses **~144+ lakh savings-linked SHGs** nationwide, disbursing **over ₹2 lakh crore annually** in formal bank credit while maintaining extraordinary credit discipline—with gross **NPAs falling below ~2% (~1.7–2%)**, decisively outperforming formal corporate lending books.
- **Community Cadres — Bank Sakhis & BC Sakhis:** Under DAY-NRLM's **"One GP – One BC Sakhi"** mission, trained SHG women operate as village-level Banking Correspondents equipped with AePS micro-ATMs—delivering doorstep pension withdrawals, insurance enrolment, and cash deposits across unbanked Gram Panchayats, dramatically reducing travel costs and domestic patriarchy barriers for rural women (complemented by **Krishi Sakhis** for natural farming and **Pashu Sakhis** for livestock care).

### Scaling Value Chains - Lakhpati Didi, Namo Drone Didi & Budget 2026–27 SHE-Marts

Despite massive credit outreach, legacy SHG reviews flagged a **"micro-enterprise trap"**: most SHG women remained stuck in low-margin, subsistence activities (*papad*, pickles, tailoring) lacking branding, working capital, or market linkages, while male kin occasionally co-opted loans ("proxy borrowing"). Three flagship 2024–2026 interventions directly target this value-chain ceiling:

1. **Lakhpati Didi Initiative:** Aims to graduate SHG households out of subsistence micro-credit into diversified, sustainable livelihoods earning **at least ₹1 lakh per annum** (verified across four agricultural/business cycles). After the Union Government expanded the national target from 2 crore to **3 crore Lakhpati Didis**, official PIB assessments in **2026** confirmed **over 2 crore SHG women** had crossed the ₹1 lakh threshold through integrated skilling, value-addition, and multi-ministry convergence.
2. **Namo Drone Didi:** A Central Sector Scheme (approved outlay **₹1,261 crore**, 2024–25 to 2025–26) equipping **15,000 selected Women SHGs** with agricultural drones—providing **80% central financial assistance up to ₹8 lakh** per drone package plus **3% interest subvention** via the Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF) on the remaining loan, alongside DGCA-certified drone pilot training. SHGs rent precision spraying of nano-urea and liquid fertilizers to local farmers, positioning rural women at the cutting edge of precision agri-tech while generating ₹1+ lakh/year in collective fee income.
3. **SHE-Marts (Self-Help Entrepreneur Marts — Union Budget 2026–27):** Announced on **February 1, 2026**, SHE-Marts tackle the single biggest structural bottleneck facing SHGs—**permanent retail market access**. Building upon the Lakhpati Didi momentum, SHE-Marts will be established as **community-owned retail outlets embedded within existing Cluster Level Federations (CLFs)**, backed by enhanced and innovative institutional financing. Complementing physical **SARAS Ajeevika Melas** and the **e-SARAS** digital platform, SHE-Marts signal a strategic pivot from **credit-led micro-livelihoods to collective retail enterprise ownership**, giving women producers permanent shelf space, quality standardization, and direct consumer margin capture.

## The Ministry of Cooperation Overhaul (2021–2026) - *Sahakar se Samriddhi*

India possesses the world's largest cooperative network—over **8.5 lakh registered cooperative societies** spanning ~30 crore members across rural credit (PACS), dairy (Amul/NDDB), sugar, fisheries, and housing. Yet outside vibrant pockets in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Kerala, and Karnataka, Indian cooperatives languished for decades under three pathologies: **acute politicization** (delayed elections and local elite capture), **manual accounting opacity** (enabling embezzlement in Primary Agricultural Credit Societies), and **severe capital and regional disparity** (weak penetration across Eastern and North-Eastern India).

In **July 2021**, the Union Government carved out a dedicated **Ministry of Cooperation** (from the Ministry of Agriculture) under the vision ***"Sahakar se Samriddhi"*** (Prosperity through Cooperation). Crucially, this overhaul operates within a tight **constitutional federal boundary**: under the Seventh Schedule, intra-state cooperatives belong exclusively to the **State List (Entry 32)**, whereas only Multi-State Cooperatives fall under the **Union List (Entry 44)**. In ***Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (July 2021)**, the Supreme Court struck down **Part IXB** (inserted by the 97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011) insofar as it regulated *state* cooperatives for lacking Article 368(2) ratification by half the state legislatures, while **upholding Part IXB strictly for Multi-State Cooperatives**. Consequently, the Ministry's 2021–2026 strategy splits cleanly into two instruments: **cooperative federalism (voluntary Model Bye-Laws + fiscal convergence)** for state-level PACS, and **binding statutory reform** for Multi-State Cooperative Societies.

### Six Structural Pillars of the 2021–2026 Cooperative Transformation

1. **ERP Computerization of Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS):** Approved initially to computerize **63,000 functional PACS** (outlay **₹2,516 crore**, subsequently expanded to **₹2,925+ crore** onboarding **79,630+ PACS** by 2026) onto a single unified **NABARD National ERP software** platform linked directly with District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs) and State Cooperative Banks (StCBs). Real-time digital ledgers eliminate double-entry fraud, automate statutory audits, accelerate Kisan Credit Card (KCC) disbursals, and integrate seamlessly with the newly created **National Cooperative Database (NCD)** mapping all 8.5+ lakh societies nationwide.
2. **Model Bye-Laws for Multipurpose PACS (M-PACS):** Circulated voluntarily in January 2023 and adopted by **32 States/UTs**, the Model Bye-Laws liberate PACS from being narrow, loss-making seasonal crop-loan shops into financially viable **multipurpose rural service hubs** authorized to run **25+ economic activities**—including **Common Service Centres (CSCs)**, **Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samriddhi Kendras (PMKSK)** for fertilizers, **Pradhan Mantri Bharatiya Janaushadhi Kendras** (generic pharmacies), LPG/petrol retail dealerships, *Paani Samitis*, FPO aggregation, and rural procurement—paired with a national campaign to register **2 lakh new Multipurpose PACS, Dairy, and Fishery cooperatives** across uncovered Gram Panchayats.
3. **World's Largest Decentralized Grain Storage Plan in the Cooperative Sector:** Approved in May 2023 to construct **700 lakh metric tonnes (70 MMT)** of decentralized warehouse, silo, custom-hiring, and primary-processing infrastructure right at the **PACS level** over five years—financed innovatively through **convergence of eight existing Central schemes** (including Agriculture Infrastructure Fund [AIF], Agricultural Marketing Infrastructure [AMI], SMAM, and PMFME) rather than a fresh standalone subsidy silo. Storing grain locally curbs post-harvest spoilage, eliminates costly round-trip FCI transport back to rural PDS shops, and enables farmers to avoid distress harvest-time sales by securing **pledge loans** against stored produce.
4. **Three National-Level Multi-State Cooperative Societies (and Tribhuvan Sahkari University):** Registered in January 2023 under the MSCS Act, 2002 (jointly promoted by apex bodies like IFFCO, KRIBHCO, NAFED, NDDB, and NCDC) to supply global scale to small village cooperatives:
   - **Bharatiya Beej Sahakari Samiti Limited (BBSSL):** Production, testing, and supply of certified quality and traditional seeds under the **"Bharat Beej"** brand.
   - **National Cooperative Organics Limited (NCOL):** End-to-end aggregation, residue testing, organic certification, and domestic/global branding under **"Bharat Organics."**
   - **National Cooperative Exports Limited (NCEL):** Umbrella export aggregator capturing international market margins directly for member cooperatives (crossing thousands of crores in agri-commodity exports).
   - Complementing these three commercial pillars, Parliament enacted the **Tribhuvan Sahkari University Act, 2025** (transforming IRMA at Anand, Gujarat into India's first national Cooperative University) to cure the sector's chronic shortage of trained professional managers.
5. **National Cooperation Policy (NCP), 2025 (Unveiled July 24, 2025):** Replacing the dated 2002 policy and drafted by a 48-member expert committee chaired by **Suresh Prabhu**, NCP 2025 lays out a **20-year (2025–2045) strategic roadmap** organized around **six core pillars** (Strengthening Foundations, Promoting Vibrancy, Future-Ready Tech/ERP, Inclusivity for Youth/Women/Marginalized, Expansion into New Sectors like renewable energy, tourism, insurance, and taxi-platforms, and Cooperative Education). Its flagship quantifiable targets include **tripling the cooperative sector's GDP contribution by 2034**, expanding active membership to **50 crore citizens**, raising society count by 30%, establishing at least one cooperative in every uncovered village, and ensuring mandatory professional management and board diversity.
6. **Multi-State Co-operative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023 (Assented August 3, 2023):** Overhauled governance across India's ~1,500+ Multi-State Cooperatives (spanning IFFCO, multi-state urban cooperative banks, and interstate federations) to inject democratic hygiene and member accountability:
   - **Co-operative Election Authority (CEA):** Establishes an independent statutory body (Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, up to 3 Members; 3-year term appointed by Central Government) exclusively empowered to prepare electoral rolls and conduct free, fair, timely MSCS board elections—breaking decades of rigged or postponed polls.
   - **Co-operative Ombudsman:** Appointed by the Central Government to investigate member complaints (deposits, equitable distribution, governance abuses) with civil-court powers and mandatory disposal within **3 months** (appeals to the Central Registrar within 1 month).
   - **Co-operative Information Officer (CIO):** Every MSCS must designate an internal officer furnishing operational and financial information to members—bringing RTI-style internal transparency to cooperatives.
   - **Board Diversity, Anti-Nepotism & Sick-Society Revival:** Mandates reserving at least **2 seats for women** and **1 seat for SC/ST** on every MSCS Board; strictly prohibits recruiting blood relatives of board directors; mandates concurrent audits for large societies (turnover/deposits >₹500 crore); and establishes a **Co-operative Rehabilitation, Reconstruction and Development Fund** (funded by profitable MSCS contributing **1% of net profits or ₹1 crore**, whichever is less) to revive sick multi-state societies.
   - **Autonomy & Federalism Critique:** Two clauses drew sharp parliamentary dissent—first, barring any MSCS with government equity from redeeming government shares without **prior government approval** (risking permanent executive tutelage); second, allowing the Central Government to supersede boards or pool profitable societies' surplus into the Rehabilitation Fund, which critics argue taxes well-run cooperatives to bail out politically mismanaged ones.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **FCRA Ministry, *Noel Harper* (2022) & 2026 Rules/Bill Nuances:** FCRA is administered by the **Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA)**—**NOT** the Ministry of Finance or Corporate Affairs. The 2020 Amendment **completely banned** sub-granting (even to other FCRA-registered NGOs!), cut administrative caps from **50% to 20%**, and mandated primary receipts exclusively at **SBI, New Delhi Main Branch** (secondary utilization accounts in other banks remain allowed!). In ***Noel Harper* (2022)**, SC upheld all amendments (no Fundamental Right to foreign charity) while allowing **Indian passports** as an alternative to Aadhaar under Sec 12A. Under the **June 22, 2026 FCRA Amendment Rules**, renewal requires **₹10 lakh minimum foreign-contribution utilization**, registration is activity- and State/UT-specific (*excluding proselytisation*), and non-PIO foreign **Key Functionaries** are ordinarily barred; the **FCRA (Amendment) Bill, 2026** (proposing a **Designated Authority** for asset vesting) remains **pending before a JPC** (NOT yet enacted!).
> - **CSR Thresholds, Exclusions & May 2026 SSE Link (Verified Prelims 2024 Trap):** CSR under Section 135 applies if **ANY ONE** (not all three!) of the thresholds is crossed: Net worth ≥ **₹500 cr**, Turnover ≥ **₹1,000 cr**, OR Net profit ≥ **₹5 cr**. Spending is **mandatory** (minimum **2%** of 3-yr average net profit—not voluntary!), and expenditure directly benefiting the company or its employees is **excluded** from CSR. Under the **May 27, 2026 CSR Amendment Rules** (Schedule VII item xiii & Rule 4A), companies can channel **up to 10%** of annual CSR spend into **SSE ZCZP instruments** without separate CSR-1 registration for SSE-listed NPOs or separate corporate impact assessment.
> - **Social Stock Exchange (SSE) ZCZP Trap:** **Zero Coupon Zero Principal (ZCZP)** instruments on NSE/BSE SSE pay **zero interest and return zero principal** (pure philanthropic social return) and are **non-tradable**. The **minimum ZCZP issue size remains ₹50 lakh** (NOT ₹25 lakh!), while the **minimum application size is ₹1,000**. Under SEBI's **April 15, 2026 circular**, the **minimum subscription threshold** was cut from 75% to **50%** for modular projects (= ₹25 lakh minimum subscription on a ₹50 lakh issue), and NPO registration validity without fundraising was extended from **2 to 3 years**.
> - **SHG Origin & Liability Trap (Verified Prelims 2023 Trap):** The SHG–Bank Linkage Programme was launched by **NABARD (1992)** (building on early NGO pilots like MYRADA/SEWA)—**NOT** originally initiated by the State Bank of India! In an SHG, **all members collectively guarantee repayment** for individual member loans, and both **RRBs and Scheduled Commercial Banks** finance SHGs.
> - **SHE-Marts (Budget 2026–27) vs. Namo Drone Didi:** **SHE-Marts** are **community-owned retail outlets** embedded inside **Cluster Level Federations (CLFs)** (not government-run e-commerce portals!), whereas **Namo Drone Didi** provides **80% subsidy up to ₹8 lakh** to **15,000 Women SHGs** for agricultural drone rentals.
> - **97th CAA Part IXB & Federal Cooperative Split:** In ***UoI v. Rajendra N. Shah* (2021)**, the Supreme Court struck down **Part IXB** ONLY for **State Cooperatives** (Entry 32, State List—lacked half-state ratification under Art 368(2)) while **upholding Part IXB for Multi-State Cooperatives** (Entry 44, Union List). Therefore, the **Co-operative Election Authority** and **Co-operative Ombudsman** under the **MSCS (Amendment) Act, 2023** govern **ONLY Multi-State Cooperatives**—NOT state-level PACS!
> - **Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) Trap (Verified Prelims 2021 Trap):** UCBs are regulated and supervised by the **RBI** (brought under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 via a **1966 amendment**, tightened in **2020**)—**NOT** by local boards set up by State Governments—and can issue equity and preference shares with RBI approval.
> - **Three National Multi-State Coops Acronym Clarity:** **BBSSL** = Seeds (*Bharat Beej*); **NCOL** = Organics (*Bharat Organics*); **NCEL** = Exports—all registered under the **MSCS Act, 2002**, NOT created by separate Acts of Parliament! (By contrast, **Tribhuvan Sahkari University** was established by a standalone **2025 Act**.)
