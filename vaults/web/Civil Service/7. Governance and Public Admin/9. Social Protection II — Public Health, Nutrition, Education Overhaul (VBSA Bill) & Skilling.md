# Chapter 9 - Social Protection II - Public Health, Nutrition, Education Overhaul (VBSA Bill) & Skilling

If Chapter 8 examined the floor preventing destitution—calories, wage guarantees, and basic shelter—this chapter dissects India’s **human-capability escalator**: healthcare, nutrition, foundational and higher education, formal vocational skilling, and sports governance. India’s demographic window is narrowing even as its workforce remains under-skilled (<5% formally trained), its children face persistent micronutrient deficits, and its public health expenditure remains well short of its own statutory and policy benchmarks.

Across 2024–2026, state capacity in the social sector has been reshaped by four structural shifts: universalizing catastrophic health insurance for 70+ citizens, rolling back automatic school promotion to confront learning poverty, moving to dismantle legacy higher-education regulators through the **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan (VBSA) Bill, 2025**, and replacing executive circulars with binding law in sports administration. Each intervention exposes the recurring tension of Indian social governance—expanding digital scale rapidly while struggling with last-mile quality, federal friction, and regulatory capture.

## Public Health Delivery Architecture & Financing

### National Health Policy (NHP) 2017 & Dual-Standard Financing Parity
The **National Health Policy (NHP), 2017** anchored India’s roadmap toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC), explicitly targeting an increase in **Government Health Expenditure (GHE) to 2.5% of GDP by 2025**, allocating two-thirds of public funds to primary care, and curbing household medical impoverishment. Yet public financing has stubbornly missed that milestone—a shortfall formally flagged by the **175th Report of the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Health & Family Welfare (August 2026)**, which noted the 2025–26 window closed unmet and recommended an escalated long-term goal.

Because official figures shifted across post-pandemic releases, exam precision requires **dual-standard parity** across the National Health Accounts (NHA) series published by the **National Health Systems Resource Centre (NHSRC), MoHFW**:

- **NHA 2021–22 (COVID Surge Baseline):** Temporary pandemic outlays lifted GHE to **1.84% of GDP** and compressed household **Out-of-Pocket Expenditure (OOPE)** to **39.4% of Total Health Expenditure (THE)**—the figure still cited across many legacy compilations.
- **NHA 2022–23 (Released May 27, 2026):** As emergency COVID allocations tapered, GHE normalized to **₹3.85 lakh crore (1.43% of GDP**; **1.48%** under the revised 2022–23 base-year GDP series), while **OOPE rebounded to 43.4% of THE**.
- **NHA 2023–24 (Released November 15, 2026):** GHE reached **₹4.14 lakh crore (₹2,959 per capita)**—a threefold nominal rise over ₹1.30 lakh crore in 2013–14—yet remained flat at **1.43% of GDP** (43.5% of THE). **OOPE stood at 42.7% of THE**—a major structural decline of 21.5 percentage points from 64.2% in 2013–14, yet still forcing Indian households to pay over **₹4 lakh crore directly at the point of care**.

**Federal Financing & Rural Human-Resource Asymmetry:** Public health is a **State List** subject (Entry 6); accordingly, **State Governments finance ~67.9% of GHE** compared to the Union’s **~32.1%**. Chronic under-investment manifests most acutely in specialist availability: **Rural Health Statistics** document a **79.5%–80% shortfall of specialist doctors** (surgeons, obstetricians-gynaecologists, physicians, and paediatricians) at rural **Community Health Centres (CHCs)**. While ~71% of Indians reside in rural areas, only ~36% of the health workforce serves there, and nearly 80% of allopathic doctors practice in an urban-skewed private sector—even as **Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs)** now drive **~66% of India’s mortality**.

### Ayushman Bharat — Primary Care, PM-JAY 70+ Expansion & Accountability Fault Lines
Launched in 2018 to move from fragmented vertical programmes toward a continuum of care, **Ayushman Bharat** rests on four pillars—**Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (AAMs)**, **AB PM-JAY**, **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)**, and **PM-ABHIM** (infrastructure).

- **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (formerly AB-HWCs):** Upgrading existing rural and urban Sub-Health Centres (SHCs), PHCs, and AYUSH centres, **1.79 to 1.84 lakh+ AAMs** were operational by February–mid-2026. Led by mid-level Community Health Officers (CHOs), they deliver **Comprehensive Primary Health Care (CPHC)** spanning 12 expanded packages (reproductive/child health, universal NCD screening for hypertension/diabetes/oral-breast-cervical cancer, mental health, geriatric/palliative care, and free essential drugs/diagnostics). Complemented by **eSanjeevani**—India’s national telemedicine grid operating in hub-and-spoke AAM and direct OPD variants—public primary care crossed **49 crore tele-consultations** by August 2026.
- **AB PM-JAY & Universal 70+ Senior Citizen Expansion:** Administered by the **National Health Authority (NHA)** as a Centrally Sponsored Scheme (60:40 general; 90:10 NE/Himalayan; 100% UTs without legislature), PM-JAY provides **₹5 lakh per family per year** for cashless secondary and tertiary hospitalization across empanelled public and private hospitals—with **no cap on family size, age, or gender**, covering all pre-existing conditions from Day 1. On **October 29, 2024**, the Union launched the **Ayushman Vaya Vandana Card**, universalizing **₹5 lakh annual cashless cover to every Indian aged 70 years and above irrespective of socioeconomic or income status**. Seniors within existing PM-JAY families receive an **exclusive ₹5 lakh top-up** not shared with younger members; seniors outside PM-JAY receive an independent ₹5 lakh family-shared senior cover. Seniors covered under CGHS/ECHS/CAPF must choose one scheme, whereas holders of private health insurance or ESIC remain fully eligible.
- **CAG Audit Findings & Market-Failure Pathologies:** Purchasing tertiary care from a lightly regulated private hospital market introduces severe principal–agent risks. The **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) Performance Audit (Report No. 11 of 2023)** exposed glaring database and claims vulnerabilities: **~7.5 lakh beneficiaries** registered under a single dummy mobile number (`9999999999`) (plus 1.39 lakh on `8888888888` and 96,000 on `9000000000`), **₹6.97 crore disbursed for 3,446 patients already marked deceased** in the system (3,903 claims), simultaneous multi-hospital admissions of identical patients, and private facilities performing packages reserved strictly for public hospitals.
- **Structural Blind Spots:** Beyond fraud, PM-JAY faces three systemic limitations. First, **OPD consultations and outpatient medicines account for over 60% of Indian OOPE**, yet PM-JAY covers only inpatient hospitalization. Second, **marginal-costing Health Benefit Package (HBP) rates** (flagged in the 175th Standing Committee Report of Aug 2026 as up to 70% below market oncology costs) deter top specialty hospitals from empanelling and incentivize smaller private facilities toward **up-coding**, unnecessary surgeries, or illegal **"balance billing"** charged out-of-pocket to poor patients. Third, India lacks uniform private-sector pricing and quality regulation because the **Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010**—enacted under **Article 252**—has been adopted by only **12 States** (plus UTs), with weak district-level inspection capacity even in adopting states.

### Affordability, Disease Elimination, Mental Health & One Health Missions
To bridge outpatient drug costs, immunization gaps, genetic burdens, and pandemic risks, five specialized architectures operate alongside Ayushman Bharat:

- **Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana (PMBJP):** Implemented by the **Pharmaceuticals & Medical Devices Bureau of India (PMBI)** under the **Department of Pharmaceuticals (Ministry of Chemicals and Fertilizers)**—not MoHFW—PMBJP expanded to **18,646+ Jan Aushadhi Kendras** by February 2026. Offering over **2,000 quality-tested unbranded generics and 300 surgical devices** at **50%–80% discounts** below branded prices (complemented by **AMRIT Pharmacies** discounting cancer and cardiovascular implants by 50%–90%), it directly compresses outpatient medicine OOPE. Supply-chain stockouts in remote blocks and persistent private-doctor prescription of costly branded generics remain key bottlenecks.
- **U-WIN Digital Immunization Platform:** Rolled out nationwide on **October 29, 2024** after a 63-district pilot, **U-WIN** adapts Co-WIN’s open digital architecture to India’s **Universal Immunization Programme (UIP)**. It maintains real-time digital vaccination registries and QR-verifiable certificates across **11 life-saving vaccines** for pregnant women and children (birth to 16 years), allowing migrant families anywhere-anytime slot booking to close India’s **"zero-dose" children** gap.
- **TB Elimination & National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission (NSCAEM 2047):** India shoulders **~25% of the global TB burden** (WHO 2025). While India’s aspirational 2025 elimination target (five years ahead of SDG 2030) proved unmet, intensified nationwide active case-finding under **Pradhan Mantri TB Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan** (scaled across all districts from December 2024) screened **20+ crore vulnerable individuals** and notified **28.6 lakh new cases**—including **9.8 lakh asymptomatic transmitters** previously missed. Addressing TB’s bidirectional link with undernutrition, monthly DBT under **Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana** was doubled from ₹500 to **₹1,000/month** (effective Nov 2024), backed by community **Ni-kshay Mitra** nutritional adoption. Parallelly, **NSCAEM (launched 2023)** targets eliminating genetic **Sickle Cell Disease by 2047** through screening **7 crore citizens (aged 0–40)** across 17 tribal-dominated states, issuing colour-coded genetic status cards for pre-marital counselling and ensuring free **Hydroxyurea** access.
- **Tele-MANAS (Mental Health):** Facing an acute deficit of mental-health specialists (<1 psychiatrist per lakh population against the WHO norm of 3), MoHFW launched **Tele-MANAS** in October 2022 with **NIMHANS (Bengaluru)** as apex nodal centre. Operating 24×7 via toll-free **14416** (1800-891-4416) in **20 languages across all 36 States/UTs** through **53 Tele-MANAS Cells** and 23 mentoring institutes, it crossed **43.64 lakh distress calls** by August 2026, upgrading to **Tele-MANAS 2.0** (video consultations and the *Asmi* chatbot). However, Common Review Missions flag low community awareness and weak block-level clinical referral follow-up.
- **National One Health Mission (NOHM):** Recognizing that **60%–70% of emerging human infectious diseases are zoonotic** (COVID-19, Nipah, Avian Influenza, Lumpy Skin Disease), NOHM operates as a **PM-STIAC whole-of-government mission** anchored by the **Office of the Principal Scientific Adviser (PSA)**—with its Executive Steering Committee chaired by the Union Health Minister and its Scientific Steering Committee chaired by PSA Prof. Ajay K. Sood. Uniting **13+ Ministries/Departments** (expanded in August 2025 to induct MoES, ISRO, and Dept. of Fisheries alongside MoHFW/ICMR/NCDC, DAHD, MoEFCC, DBT, DST, CSIR, DRDO, AYUSH, and NSCS) around the **National Institute for One Health (NIOH), Nagpur**, NOHM integrates human, livestock, wildlife, and wastewater surveillance. Pandemic readiness is stress-tested via cross-sectoral national mock drills—***Vishanu Yudh Abhyas* (Aug 2024)** and ***Viral Sankraman Abhyas* (Nov 2025)**—with state-level operational frameworks initiated at Nagpur in March 2026.

## Nutritional Governance — Hidden Hunger, GHI Debates & Life-Cycle Missions

### NFHS-5 Indicators & the Anatomy of "Hidden Hunger"
Despite India’s transition from food-deficit nation to grain surplus—and free statutory cereals under PMGKAY—child and maternal malnutrition remains India’s deepest human-capital tax. **NFHS-5 (2019–21)** exposed an alarming divergence between caloric sufficiency and nutritional security:

- **Anthropometric Persistence (Children Under 5):** **Stunting** (low height-for-age, signaling chronic deprivation) declined sluggishly from 38.4% (NFHS-4) to **35.5%**; **Wasting** (low weight-for-height, signaling acute recent nutrition/infection shock) barely budged from 21.0% to **19.3%**; and **Underweight** stood at **32.1%** (down from 35.7%).
- **Worsening "Hidden Hunger" (Micronutrient Anemia):** Anemia worsened across every cohort between NFHS-4 and NFHS-5—surging to **67.1% among children (6–59 months)** (from 58.6%), **57.0% among women of reproductive age (15–49)** (from 53.1%), and **59.1% among adolescent girls**—concurrent with rising adult overweight/obesity in urban India (the **"triple burden of malnutrition"**).

Nutrition is not merely an agronomic variable; it is a **gender, WASH, and governance failure**. Early marriage, intra-household food discrimination (women eating last and least), open defecation/enteric infections preventing nutrient absorption, and cereal-heavy diets starved of protein and dietary fats trap families in an **intergenerational cycle** during the decisive **first 1,000 days** (conception to age two).

### Global Hunger Index (GHI) 2025–2026 & Methodological Critiques
Published annually by German NGO **Welthungerhilfe** and partners (formerly Ireland’s Concern Worldwide; the 2026 edition with IFHV and Helvetas), the **Global Hunger Index** combines four indicators on a 100-point scale: **Undernourishment** (1/3 weight), **Child Stunting** (1/6), **Child Wasting** (1/6), and **Under-5 Mortality** (1/3). In **GHI 2025**, India ranked **102nd out of 123 countries** (score **25.8** — "Serious"). In **GHI 2026 (October 2026)**, India improved marginally to **100th out of 122 countries** with a score of **24.1** (still **"Serious"**), reflecting **9.8% undernourishment**, **29.3% stunting**, **19.0% wasting**, and **2.7% under-5 mortality**.

The **Government of India (MWCD & MEA)** formally rejects GHI rankings on four substantive methodological grounds:
1. **Mislabeling Child Health as Population Hunger:** Three of four indicators (two-thirds of total weight) exclusively measure children under five—where stunting and wasting reflect sanitation, maternal stature, infections, and care practices rather than pure caloric starvation.
2. **Small-Sample Polling Bias in PoU:** Historically, FAO’s Proportion of Undernourished (PoU) incorporated Gallup World Poll’s **Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES)** based on an opinion sample of roughly **3,000 respondents** for a 1.4-billion population, failing to reflect real-time PMGKAY free foodgrains.
3. **Exclusion of Live National Data:** GHI relies on lagged NFHS-5 figures rather than India’s live **Poshan Tracker** anthropometric census covering 9+ crore children, which reports substantially lower wasting levels.
4. **Cross-Country Non-Comparability:** Combining mismatched reference years across conflict and non-conflict nations distorts ordinal ranks.

*Balanced Synthesis:* While GoI’s critique of GHI’s "hunger" label and tiny opinion-poll inputs holds analytical merit, dismissing the underlying signal would be fatal. India’s own gold-standard **NFHS-5** corroborates stubbornly high **child wasting (~19%)** and **67% child anemia**, demonstrating that calorie-centric welfare cannot alone unlock biological human potential.

### Saksham Anganwadi & Mission Poshan 2.0 vs. PM-POSHAN
India’s life-cycle nutrition architecture splits cleanly across two ministries—a frequent UPSC Prelims trap:

- **Saksham Anganwadi & Mission Poshan 2.0 (Ministry of Women & Child Development):** Announced in Budget 2021–22 (2021–26 umbrella), Poshan 2.0 integrates **Anganwadi Services (ICDS 6 core services)**, **Poshan Abhiyaan** (behavioural *Jan Andolan* convergence), and the **Scheme for Adolescent Girls** (14–18 years in Aspirational Districts and NE states). It targets upgrading **2 lakh Anganwadi Centres (AWCs)** into modern **Saksham Anganwadis** (LEDs, water filters, rainwater harvesting, smart learning aids, and **Poshan Vatikas** nutri-gardens) while strengthening ECCE via ***Poshan Bhi Padhai Bhi***. Digitally, the **Poshan Tracker** DPI monitors **14.02 lakh AWCs** and **9.14+ crore registered beneficiaries** in real time for daily attendance, growth monitoring, and SAM/MAM identification. From **August 1, 2025**, MWCD introduced **biometric/Facial Recognition System (FRS) authentication** for Take-Home Ration (THR) to curb ghost leakages—though field groups flag exclusion risks for migrant mothers facing Aadhaar/phone mismatches. Structurally, AWCs suffer from overburdened, under-compensated Anganwadi Workers (classified as "honorary volunteers"), contractor capture of sub-standard THR, and weak linkage of identified SAM children to Nutrition Rehabilitation Centres.
- **PM-POSHAN (*Pradhan Mantri Poshan Shakti Nirman* — Ministry of Education):** Rechristening the Mid-Day Meal Scheme for **2021–22 to 2025–26 (₹1,30,794.90 crore outlay)**, PM-POSHAN serves one hot cooked meal daily across **11.20 lakh Government and Government-aided schools** to **~11.80 crore children** in **Classes I–VIII**, and—aligning with NEP 2020—extended coverage to pre-primary ***Bal Vatikas***. Statutory nutritional norms mandate **450 kcal / 12g protein** (Bal Vatika & Primary) and **700 kcal / 20g protein** (Upper Primary). Effective **May 1, 2025**, MoE revised daily **material/cooking costs** upward to **₹6.78/child** (Bal Vatika & Primary) and **₹10.17/child** (Upper Primary), alongside ~26 LMT free central foodgrains. Innovations include School Nutrition Gardens, community *Tithi Bhojan*, mandatory social audits, and fortified rice/millet menus. Street-level weaknesses persist: delayed cook-cum-helper honoraria (frozen at ₹1,000/month centrally since 2009), rural caste discrimination against Dalit cooks, food-inflation compression of egg/vegetable inclusion, and non-implementation of NEP 2020’s recommendation to add a **universal school breakfast** (implemented proactively only by select states like Tamil Nadu).

## Education Architecture, NEP 2020 & the VBSA Bill 2025 Overhaul

### RTE Act, 2009 — Inclusion Mandates & Abolition of No-Detention
Operationalizing **Article 21A** (86th Constitutional Amendment, 2002), the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009** guaranteed free elementary schooling (ages 6–14, Classes 1–8). Two statutory clauses sit at the heart of Indian education governance:

- **Section 12(1)(c) — 25% EWS & Disadvantaged Quota:** Mandates private unaided non-minority schools to reserve **at least 25% of entry-level seats** for children from Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) and Disadvantaged Groups, reimbursed by the State at per-child public expenditure norms. While upheld by the Supreme Court in ***Pramati Educational & Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014)**, the verdict exempted both aided and unaided **minority institutions** from the entire RTE Act—inadvertently triggering private schools to seek minority status to bypass inclusion mandates, alongside chronic state reimbursement arrears and opaque admission portals.
- **Rollback of the No-Detention Policy (Section 16):** Originally, Section 16 barred schools from failing or expelling any child up to Class 8, pairing automatic promotion with **Continuous and Comprehensive Evaluation (CCE)** to eliminate dropout-inducing exam fear. In practice, CCE collapsed into zero assessment, allowing learning deficits to compound silently until catastrophic failure in Class 10 board exams. Parliament enacted the **RTE (Amendment) Act, 2019**, empowering appropriate governments to hold regular exams in **Classes 5 and 8**; 18 States/UTs abolished no-detention while 16 States/UTs (including Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, UP, AP) retained it. On **December 21, 2024**, the Centre notified the **RTE (Amendment) Rules, 2024**, formally **scrapping no-detention in Classes 5 and 8 across ~3,000 Central Government schools** (Kendriya Vidyalayas, Jawahar Navodaya Vidyalayas, Sainik Schools). Crucially, detention is not instantaneous: a failing student receives targeted remedial instruction and a **re-examination within two months**; detention applies only upon failing the re-test, and **no child can ever be expelled** before completing Class 8. Critics caution that unless teacher accountability and remedial pedagogy are financed, detention risks accelerating dropouts among first-generation Dalit, tribal, and girl learners.

### Learning Poverty — ASER 2024 & PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024
While India achieved near-universal elementary enrolment (>95% for ages 6–14), **learning poverty** remains the central drag on human capital. Two landmark assessments released in 2025 reveal both encouraging post-COVID foundational recovery and a stubborn middle-school cliff:

- **ASER 2024 (Pratham Foundation, released January 2025 — 6.49 lakh rural children across 605 districts):** Documented India’s sharpest learning rebound in over a decade, remarkably **led by government schools** reflecting **NIPUN Bharat** classroom focus. Among **Class 3** government-school students, basic reading (Class 2 text) jumped from 16.3% (2022) to **23.4% (2024)**—exceeding pre-COVID 2018 (20.9%)—while all-India Class 3 subtraction climbed to **33.7%** (up from 25.9% in 2022). Class 5 government-school reading recovered to **44.8%** (division at **30.7%**), and Class 8 reading reached **67.5%**. Yet the absolute deficit stays sobering: **over three-fourths (76.6%) of Class 3** and **over half of Class 5** rural students still cannot read a Class 2 story. Pre-primary enrolment climbed sharply (77.4% for 3-year-olds; 83.4% for 4-year-olds), predominantly in Anganwadis.
- **PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 (NCERT, conducted Dec 2024 / released 2025):** India’s revamped competency survey (replacing NAS) evaluated **21.15 lakh students** across **74,000+ schools and 781 districts** at NEP’s developmental checkpoints—**Grades 3, 6, and 9**. Grade 3 posted strong foundational gains (**65% proficient in Math**, up from 42% in NAS 2021; **57% in Language**, up from 39%) with girls slightly ahead in Language and State Government schools outperforming expectations. Conversely, **Grades 6 and 9 revealed sharp competency erosion** in Mathematics and Science—proving that foundational gains dissipate without secondary subject-teacher capacity and lab infrastructure. UDISE+ further highlights infrastructure drag: only **57.2% of schools had functional computers** and **53.9% had internet connectivity** (2023–24), while ASER 2024 flagged a surge in tiny government primary schools with under 60 students (52.1% of schools).

### Five+ Years of NEP 2020 — Structural Gains & Federal Friction
Crossing five years of execution in 2025–2026, the **National Education Policy (NEP), 2020** (K. Kasturirangan Committee) has rewired structural architecture across school and tertiary tiers:

- **5+3+3+4 Curricular Overhaul & Foundational Mission:** Replaced the 1986 `10+2` schema with **Foundational (5 yrs: 3 pre-primary + Grades 1–2), Preparatory (3 yrs: Grades 3–5), Middle (3 yrs: Grades 6–8), and Secondary (4 yrs: Grades 9–12)**, operationalized through **NCF–Foundational Stage (2022)** and **NCF–School Education (2023)**. **NIPUN Bharat** targets universal FLN by Grade 3 by **2026–27**, while **PARAKH** (National Assessment Centre under NCERT) standardizes school boards and **Holistic Progress Cards (HPCs)**.
- **PM-SHRI Schools & Federal Contestation:** Targeting **14,500** upgraded government exemplar schools (₹27,360 crore outlay; **13,076+ selected** across 33 States/UTs by Dec 2025) as living NEP laboratories, PM-SHRI triggered sharpest cooperative-federalism friction when non-BJP states (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal) resisted mandatory NEP MoU clauses—especially the **three-language formula**—resulting in stalled central **Samagra Shiksha** fund releases.
- **Higher Education Flexibility, Credit DPI & GER Gap:** NEP targets lifting Higher Education **Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER)** from **~28.4%** to **50% by 2035** and public education expenditure to **6% of GDP** (still unmet since the 1966 Kothari Commission, hovering around ~4–4.6%). Structural rails now operational include the **4-Year Undergraduate Programme (FYUP)** with Multiple Entry–Multiple Exit (Certificate/1 yr, Diploma/2 yrs, Degree/3 yrs, Honours-Research/4 yrs); the **National Credit Framework (NCrF)** unifying academic, vocational, and experiential credits (**1 credit = 30 notional hours**); and the **Academic Bank of Credits (ABC)** (2,469+ HEIs onboarded) linked to the **APAAR ID** (*Automated Permanent Academic Account Registry* — voluntary 12-digit "One Nation, One Student ID" on DigiLocker, reaching **26.35 crore verified IDs** by July 2026).
- **Foreign University Campuses:** Under **UGC Foreign Higher Educational Institutions (FHEI) Regulations, 2023** (and GIFT City IFSCA rules), **15–19 global universities** gained approval by 2026. Operational campuses include **Deakin University** and **University of Wollongong** (GIFT City, Gujarat) and **University of Southampton** (Gurugram, August 2025), alongside 2026–27 approvals for **Queen’s University Belfast**, **Coventry University**, **University of Liverpool (Bengaluru)**, and **Illinois Institute of Technology (Mumbai — first US campus)**. While curbing outbound student forex outflow (>1.3 million Indians abroad), Parliamentary Standing Committees warn of fee elitism, domestic faculty poaching, and the need for strict regulatory equivalence.

### The Regulatory Overhaul — *Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan (VBSA) Bill, 2025*
To end overlapping, inspection-heavy higher-education governance (what NEP 2020 termed "light but tight" regulation), Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan introduced the **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan (VBSA) Bill, 2025** (Bill No. 194 of 2025 — evolving from the earlier HECI concept) in the Lok Sabha on **December 15, 2025**. Facing opposition objections over centralization and federalism, the Bill was referred to a **31-member Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC)** (21 Lok Sabha + 10 Rajya Sabha members, chaired by **BJP MP D. Purandeswari**). *Epistemic Status (November 2026):* Following extensions past the Monsoon Session 2026 and continued stakeholder hearings through September 2026, the Bill **remains under JPC scrutiny through the Winter Session 2026 and has NOT yet been enacted into law**.

- **Single Apex Umbrella Repealing Three Legacy Acts:** Establishes a 12-member apex **Viksit Bharat Shiksha Adhishthan** (led by an **honorary Chairperson**, unlike UGC’s full-time salaried Chair) and **repeals the UGC Act (1956), AICTE Act (1987), and NCTE Act (1993)**. Separating functions previously fused inside UGC to cure regulatory conflict of interest, it creates **three independent Councils**:
  1. ***Viksit Bharat Shiksha Viniyaman Parishad* (Regulatory Council):** Single-window faceless regulator overseeing governance norms, financial probity, graded autonomy, degree-granting authorization (with prior central approval), and student grievances; empowered to impose fines up to **₹75 lakh** for repeated contraventions and a **minimum ₹2 crore penalty** for unauthorized universities.
  2. ***Viksit Bharat Shiksha Gunvatta Parishad* (Accreditation Council):** Outcome-based meta-accreditor overseeing empanelled external accrediting agencies (replacing NAAC’s operational monopoly).
  3. ***Viksit Bharat Shiksha Manak Parishad* (Standards Council):** Determines minimum academic standards, learning outcomes, and credit/curriculum frameworks.
- **Jurisdictional Scope, Exclusions & Appellate Route:** Regulates general higher education, technical education, teacher training, and **architecture education**—and notably brings **Institutes of National Importance (IITs, IIMs, NITs, IISERs)** under the Commission’s umbrella. Crucially, unlike the UGC, AICTE, and NCTE Acts, the **Architects Act, 1972 is NOT repealed**: the **Council of Architecture (CoA)** continues to regulate the professional practice of architects and functions as a **Professional Standard Setting Body (PSSB)** with representation on the VBSA Councils. It **explicitly excludes medical and legal education** (retained under NMC and Bar Council of India) and other non-notified professional councils. Under **Clause 37**, appeals against decisions of the Commission or any of the three Councils lie directly to the **Central Government** (not an independent appellate tribunal).
- **Four Critical Fault Lines Under JPC Review:**
  1. **Omission of the Fourth Vertical (Funding Power):** NEP 2020 recommended *four* independent verticals, including a **Higher Education Grants Council (HEGC)**. VBSA drops funding entirely—leaving direct grant allocation inside the **Ministry of Education**, sparking fears of executive fiscal leverage over autonomous universities.
  2. **Federal Centralization (Concurrent List Friction):** Education sits on the **Concurrent List** (Entry 25), yet the draft Bill provided state/UT nominees only a token **one-year rotating term** and **zero state representation on the Accreditation Council**—prompting the JPC’s July 2026 draft deliberations to demand at least **three state nominees per council on zonal rotation (3-year term)** and statutory consultation with **State Higher Education Councils**.
  3. **Institutional Autonomy Rollback:** PRS India notes the Bill brings statutory **INIs (IITs/IIMs)** under Commission jurisdiction—a position JPC draft notes initially declined to exempt—and curtails accredited universities’ existing autonomy to establish constituent units, while omitting faculty/teacher representation.
  4. **Clause 47 Executive Supersession:** Authorizes the Union to supersede the Commission or any Council on broad grounds ("unable to discharge functions" / "persistent default")—flagged by opposition MPs and NDA allies alike before the JPC as a threat to regulatory independence requiring strict objective guardrails.

### AI in Education & Ed-Tech / Coaching Regulation
Indian education is rapidly pivoting from pandemic-era broadcast lectures to **AI-enabled adaptive pedagogy**, backed by a **₹500 crore Centre of Excellence in AI for Education** (Budget 2025–26), the **Bharatiya Bhasha Pustak Scheme** (multilingual digital textbooks across Indian languages), **50,000 Atal Tinkering Labs**, rural secondary school BharatNet broadband, and DIKSHA’s 35-language repository. Yet ASER 2024 exposes a sobering reality check: while **89% of rural 14–16-year-olds have home smartphone access**, **76% use them for social media versus only 57% for education**, with sharp gender gaps in digital ownership and online safety literacy. Without teacher upskilling and vernacular guardrails, generative AI risks widening cognitive inequality and eroding independent analytical writing.

Concurrently, predatory Ed-Tech marketing, opaque refund denials, and shadow-coaching mental-health crises prompted decisive consumer-protection enforcement. Under the **Consumer Protection Act, 2019**, the **Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)** notified the **Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisement in Coaching Sector (November 2024)**—prohibiting false "100% selection" guarantees, undisclosed paid endorsements of top rankers, fake urgency/dark patterns, and deceptive free-trial billing, backed by steep statutory penalties enforced across 2025–2026. Complementing CCPA, MoE’s **Model Guidelines for Regulation of Coaching Centres (2024)** urged states to bar enrolment of children below **16 years**, mandate transparent pro-rata fee refunds, regulate faculty qualifications, and enforce student psychological counselling.

## Skilling Ecosystem & Sports Governance Reforms

### India’s Formal Skilling Paradox (<5% Formally Trained)
India’s demographic dividend (median age ~28) faces a stark human-capital cliff. According to **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS 2023–24 and 2025 annual rounds)**, only **~4.1% of India’s working-age population**—and **4.9%–5.0% of youth aged 15–29**—have received **formal vocational or technical training**, contrasting sharply with **60%–80%+ in Germany, Japan, and South Korea**. Over 95% of Indian workers acquire skills informally through uncertified on-the-job apprenticeships (*ustad–shagird*), locking productivity and wages into low-equilibrium informality.

India has largely solved **skilling throughput**—expanding to **14,600+ Industrial Training Institutes (ITIs)** (~3,300 public, ~11,300 private) and certifying crores under Skill India—yet failed on **labour-market conversion**. Core pathologies include supply-driven short-duration courses (150–300 hours) misaligned with district industrial clusters, obsolete ITI workshop machinery, minimal private employer participation in curriculum design, and deep socio-cultural stigma treating vocational diplomas as inferior to general degrees (PARAKH 2024 found only **47% of schools offer Grade 9+ skill courses**, with just **29% student uptake**).

### PMKVY 4.0, SIDH, PM-SETU (ITI Upgradation) & PM Internship Scheme
To pivot from supply-driven certification toward employer-anchored employability, the Union restructured four major instruments across 2024–2026:

- **Restructured Skill India Programme (SIP) & PMKVY 4.0:** On **February 7, 2025**, the Union Cabinet approved continuation and consolidation of the **Skill India Programme (2022–23 to 2025–26)** with an **₹8,800 crore outlay** under the **Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship (MSDE)**, integrating three verticals:
  1. **PMKVY 4.0:** Free Short-Term Training (STT) with mandatory On-the-Job Training (OJT) and Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL) across 600+ industry-aligned future courses (AI, drones, solar, EVs, mechatronics; ~27 lakh+ trained).
  2. **PM-NAPS (NAPS-2):** Apprenticeship promotion under the **Apprentices Act, 1961**, reimbursing **25% of apprentice stipends (up to ₹1,500/month)** directly via DBT (~9.9 lakh apprentices enrolled in FY26 across 6,100+ enterprises).
  3. **Jan Shikshan Sansthan (JSS):** Community-level non-formal vocational training targeting rural women, SCs/STs, and neo-literates.
- **Skill India Digital Hub (SIDH):** MSDE’s unified Digital Public Infrastructure for skilling, integrating courses, apprenticeships, **NCVET-regulated stackable micro-credentials** (bankable in the Academic Bank of Credits), **e-Shram**, **National Career Service (NCS)**, and DigiLocker so informal and gig workers can convert modular training into formal diplomas.
- **National Scheme for ITI Upgradation (*PM-SETU* — ₹60,000 Crore):** Approved by the Union Cabinet in **May 2025** as a five-year Centrally Sponsored Scheme under **MSDE (Directorate General of Training)**, PM-SETU tackles hardware obsolescence head-on. It upgrades **1,000 Government ITIs** through a **hub-and-spoke cluster architecture** (200 Hub ITIs linked to 800 Spoke ITIs) alongside establishing **5 National Centres of Excellence (NCoEs) for Skilling** with global industry partnerships ("Make for India, Make for the World"). Funding splits into **₹30,000 crore Centre** (**50% co-financed jointly by ADB and the World Bank**), **₹20,000 crore States**, and **₹10,000 crore Industry**. Crucially, cluster management shifts to industry-anchored Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) with a **51% Industry : 24.5% Centre : 24.5% State** equity split—moving beyond earlier government-run STRIVE efforts (with initial cluster SPVs such as Surat and Visakhapatnam advancing in October 2026).
- **Prime Minister’s Internship Scheme (PMIS):** Announced in Budget 2024–25 and administered by the **Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA)**—*not* MSDE or MoLE—PMIS targets **1 crore youth over five years** for 12-month real-world business exposure across India’s **top 500 CSR-spending companies**. Eligibility covers youth aged **21–24** (not in full-time education/employment, household income ≤₹8 lakh, excluding IIT/IIM/premier professional graduates). Interns receive a **₹5,000/month stipend** (**₹4,500 via GoI DBT + ₹500 from company CSR funds**), a **₹6,000 one-time joining grant**, and PMJJBY/PMSBY insurance.
- **Implementation Realism on PMIS (2025–2026 Pilot Audit):** RTI and parliamentary disclosures through mid-2026 exposed severe conversion friction. Across Pilot Rounds 1 and 2, companies posted nearly **3 lakh opportunities** and made **~1.73 lakh offers**, yet **only ~18,000 youth joined and ~5,200 completed** the full year, with **dropout rates exceeding 50% in Round 1 and 33% in Round 2** (over 7,600 dropouts). Four street-level design flaws explain this gap: a ₹5,000 stipend cannot finance urban rent/migration for rural youth; geographic mismatch between rural applicants and metro industrial locations; limited post-internship job conversion guarantees; and corporate hesitation to train non-graduate generalists—prompting structural recalibration in 2026.

### Sports Governance Overhaul — *Khelo Bharat Niti 2025* & *National Sports Governance Act, 2025*
For decades, Indian sports federations suffered from entrenched political patronage, lifetime presidencies, opaque selection trials, protracted civil-court injunctions, and international suspensions (e.g., IOA, AIFF, WFI), because the **National Sports Development Code, 2011** operated merely as an executive guideline lacking statutory teeth. Between July 2025 and November 2026, Parliament and the Union Cabinet executed a comprehensive legal and policy reset:

- **National Sports Policy 2025 (*Khelo Bharat Niti 2025*):** Approved by the Union Cabinet on **July 1, 2025**, superseding the 2001 policy, *Khelo Bharat Niti* rests on **five strategic pillars**: **(1)** Excellence on the global stage (anchored to India’s **2036 Olympic Games bid** and 2030 CWG roadmap); **(2)** Sports for economic development (sports manufacturing, tourism, startups); **(3)** Sports for social development and inclusion (women, tribal/rural talent, para-athletes); **(4)** Sports as a people’s movement (*Fit India*); and **(5)** Full integration with **NEP 2020 education**. Union Budget 2026–27 reinforced this with the **Khelo India Mission**, paired with revised **₹7,386.74 crore** NSF assistance norms (October 2026) funding Olympic Training Centres and international hosting.
- **National Sports Governance Act, 2025 (Act 25 of 2025):** Passed by Parliament on **August 11–12, 2025** and granted Presidential assent on **August 18, 2025** (select provisions brought into force on **January 1** and **May 12, 2026**), the Act codifies binding statutory governance over the **National Olympic Committee, National Paralympic Committee, National Sports Federations (NSFs), and Regional Sports Federations**. It establishes **three statutory institutions**:
  1. **National Sports Board (NSB):** Grants, suspends, or cancels recognition of National Sports Bodies (prerequisite for Central funding), enforces international charter compliance, and conducts inquiries into athlete welfare. Comprising a Chairperson and two Members selected by a Cabinet Secretary-led panel, the **first NSB was formally constituted on November 13, 2026** (assuming charge November 16, 2026) under **Chairperson Devesh Chaturvedi (Retd. IAS)**.
  2. **National Sports Election Panel:** A 20-member independent panel (including former Chief Election Commissioners, **notified September 10, 2026**) supervising free and fair federation and athlete-committee polls (with IOA and AIFF elections mandated by December 31, 2026).
  3. **National Sports Tribunal (NST):** Specialized fast-track forum headed by a sitting/former **Supreme Court Judge or High Court Chief Justice** (plus two eminent members) to resolve selection and governance disputes—excluding international-federation games and internal federation disputes—with **appeals lying strictly to the Supreme Court** (or **CAS Lausanne** where international charters mandate). Tribunal Rules were notified in May 2026, though formal constitution of the Tribunal remains pending as of November 2026.
- **Democratic Caps & the BCCI–RTI Compromise:** Under **Section 4(1)(b)**, the Act caps every federation’s Executive Committee at **15 members**, establishing three statutory representation floors: **(i) at least four women**, **(ii) at least two sportspersons of outstanding merit**, and **(iii) two representatives elected from the Athletes’ Committee**. Office-bearers face an age band of **25–70 years** (extendable up to **75 years** only where international federation rules permit) and a cap of **three consecutive terms** (followed by a mandatory **4-year cooling-off period** for the President, Secretary General, and Treasurer). Crucially, in a contentious legislative carve-out, recognized sports bodies are designated Public Authorities under the **RTI Act, 2005 ONLY regarding utilization of government financial grants**—effectively keeping the cash-rich, non-grant-receiving **BCCI outside RTI**. While BCCI falls within the Act’s statutory governance ambit, on **September 8, 2026**, the Supreme Court issued notice to the BCCI asking why the Act should not apply to it—a constitutional test that remains pending.
