# Chapter 8 - Corporate Governance, Stakeholder Capitalism, CSR, Greenwashing & Global Probity

The modern corporation is not a private partnership writ large; it is a state-chartered legal person granted perpetual succession and limited liability in exchange for a **social license to operate**. Because that statutory shield transfers catastrophic tail risks away from equity holders onto creditors, workers, and ecosystems, corporate power is inherently a question of political and moral philosophy, not mere commercial efficiency.

A foundational conceptual boundary separates **Corporate Management** from **Corporate Governance**. Management is operational optimization—allocating inputs, pricing products, and hitting quarterly targets—whereas governance is the constitutional architecture of rules, fiduciary controls, and institutional checks that determines *whose interests prevail when profit collides with legality or public welfare*.

In economic theory, corporate governance exists to contain two distinct agency pathologies. Western dispersed-ownership capitalism primarily battles the **Type-I Agency Problem** (Berle–Means separation of ownership and control), where salaried executives expropriate atomized shareholders through empire-building and excessive pay.

Indian capitalism, where promoter families control roughly three-fifths of the top 500 listed entities, confronts a sharper **Type-II Agency Problem**: controlling majority promoters siphoning value away from dispersed minority shareholders, lenders, employees, and the exchequer. Every institutional reform in India—from Clause 49 to Independent Directors and NFRA—is an ongoing attempt to police this asymmetric power structure.

## 1. Philosophy of Corporate Purpose - From Shareholder Primacy to Trusteeship

Debates over corporate ethics rarely turn on whether theft or fraud is wrong; they turn on a deeper teleological question: *for whose benefit does a business enterprise morally exist?* Five competing normative paradigms define the modern spectrum of corporate purpose.

### Milton Friedman’s Shareholder Primacy (1970)
In his landmark 1970 essay, Nobel laureate **Milton Friedman** articulated a deceptively rigorous fiduciary defense of shareholder primacy: *"the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits."* Far from endorsing lawless greed, Friedman built his claim on two normative premises—**agency fidelity** and **democratic legitimacy**.

First, corporate executives are hired agents of equity owners; spending shareholder funds on social causes unauthorized by owners constitutes private misappropriation. Second, deciding which social ills deserve subsidy is a political function belonging to elected legislatures via taxation, not unelected CEOs exercising *"taxation without representation."*

Yet Friedman’s own qualification exposes the model’s structural fragility: executives must maximize profit *"while conforming to the basic rules of the society, both those embodied in law and those embodied in ethical custom."* Three real-world market failures shatter that boundary condition:

- **Unpriced Externalities & Information Asymmetry:** When carbon emissions, toxic effluents, or predatory financial instruments go unpriced by slow-moving statutes, profit maximization mechanically incentivizes offloading private costs onto the public commons.
- **Regulatory Capture:** Friedman assumed firms passively accept exogenous legal rules. In reality, concentrated corporate lobbies actively fund campaigns, shape regulatory definitions, and dilute enforcement—rewriting the very rules meant to constrain them.
- **Short-Termism (Quarterly Financialization):** Tying executive stock options to short-term share price encourages share buybacks, underinvestment in R&D, safety cuts, and layoffs that inflate quarterly earnings while hollowing out productive capital.

### R. Edward Freeman’s Stakeholder Theory (1984)
Challenging shareholder monism, **R. Edward Freeman** reconceptualized the firm as a multilateral value-creation network dependent on **stakeholders**—any group that affects or is affected by organizational objectives, including employees, customers, suppliers, creditors, local communities, regulators, and the natural environment.

Stakeholder theory operates on two distinct philosophical registers. **Instrumental Stakeholder Theory** is pragmatic consequentialism: firms that honor employee dignity, supplier fairness, and community trust lower transaction costs, attract talent, and sustain superior long-term returns. **Normative Stakeholder Theory** rests on a **Kantian imperative**: workers and affected communities possess intrinsic moral dignity as ends-in-themselves and cannot be reduced to expendable instruments for equity appreciation.

Its sharpest analytical vulnerability is the **Multi-Objective Optimization Problem**, highlighted by economist Michael Jensen. A firm instructed to maximize five conflicting variables simultaneously—wages, consumer discounts, ecological preservation, tax payments, and shareholder dividends—lacks a single mathematical objective function, risking managerial unaccountability where executives justify self-serving choices by invoking whichever stakeholder suits the moment.

### John Elkington’s Triple Bottom Line (1994) and Its 2018 "Recall"
Coined by **John Elkington** in 1994, the **Triple Bottom Line (TBL)** sought to operationalize stakeholder capitalism by replacing single-metric financial accounting with a triad of performance ledgers: **Profit** (economic value), **People** (social equity and labor welfare), and **Planet** (ecological carrying capacity).

Crucially for high-scoring Mains analysis, Elkington issued an extraordinary **"Product Recall" of the Triple Bottom Line in 2018** (*Harvard Business Review*). He argued that corporate boards had domesticated a framework intended to trigger systemic economic redesign into a superficial accounting trade-off—treating minor philanthropic or recycling gains as offsets permitting continued ecological overshoot.

### Michael Porter & Mark Kramer’s Creating Shared Value (CSV, 2011)
**Michael Porter and Mark Kramer** attempted to rescue business ethics from both Friedmanite cynicism and peripheral charity through **Creating Shared Value (CSV)**. Whereas conventional CSR treats social responsibility as a *post-profit redistribution cost* tacked onto the margins of business, CSV embeds social problem-solving directly inside the **core competitive profit engine**.

CSV operates via three structural pathways: reconceiving products and markets for underserved populations (e.g., low-cost cardiac care or point-of-care diagnostics), redefining value-chain productivity (e.g., **ITC’s e-Choupal** eliminating rural procurement middlemen while lifting farmer realization, or **HUL’s Project Shakti** empowering rural women micro-entrepreneurs), and strengthening local industrial clusters.

Yet CSV carries a serious philosophical blind spot. It illuminates lucrative *"win-win"* intersections where commercial efficiency and social welfare align, but falls silent on genuine **structural trade-offs**—such as phasing out profitable fossil-fuel assets, respecting tribal veto rights over mineral-rich forests, or refusing addictive algorithmic design—where ethical restraint genuinely lowers corporate profits.

### Mahatma Gandhi’s Doctrine of Trusteeship
Rooted in the *Isha Upanishad* (*Tena tyaktena bhunjithah* — enjoy wealth through detachment), **Aparigraha** (non-possession), and **Ahimsa**, **Mahatma Gandhi’s Trusteeship** offers the deepest philosophical subversion of absolute property ownership. Industrialists and wealth-creators retain stewardship over productive capital, but only as **fiduciary trustees of society**, entitled to a modest commission commensurate with their service while dedicating surplus wealth to collective uplift (*Sarvodaya* and *Antyodaya*).

Trusteeship rejects both poles of twentieth-century economic ideology: it repudiates laissez-faire capitalism for sanctifying unlimited accumulation amidst deprivation, yet rejects Marxist state expropriation because coercive confiscation replaces private greed with violent state monopoly while destroying entrepreneurial talent.

In Indian corporate history, Trusteeship is not abstract utopia. Roughly **two-thirds of Tata Sons’ equity** is held by philanthropic **Tata Trusts**, structurally routing commercial dividends back into healthcare, research, and rural livelihoods; similar fiduciary commitments animate the **Azim Premji Foundation** and historic **Bajaj** enterprises. Statutorily, **Section 166(2) of the Companies Act, 2013** codifies an explicit stakeholder-trusteeship duty—mandating every corporate director to act in good faith not merely for shareholders, but for *"the employees, the shareholders, the community and for the protection of environment."*

### Shareholder vs. Stakeholder vs. Trusteeship Comparison Table

## 2. Anatomy of Corporate Failures - Global Benchmarks, Cronyism & Indian Startup Collapses

Corporate collapses rarely stem from isolated rogue employees; they emerge from **systemic incentive misalignment** combined with **gatekeeper capture**. When statutory auditors, independent directors, credit-rating agencies, and venture boards derive their fees or mandates from the very executives they are supposed to police, oversight degenerates into ceremonial rubber-stamping.

### Enron (2001) - Financial Engineering and Auditor Capture
**Enron’s** implosion remains the textbook Type-I governance pathology. Executives manipulated **mark-to-market accounting** to book decades of hypothetical energy-trading revenues immediately, while hiding billions in liabilities inside off-balance-sheet **Special Purpose Entities (SPEs)** run by its own CFO.

The fatal moral failure lay with its gatekeeper, **Arthur Andersen**. Earning massive non-audit consulting fees alongside statutory audit fees created an acute conflict of interest—culminating in systematic document shredding and Andersen’s collapse. The crisis forced the United States to pass the **Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX), 2002**, barring auditors from concurrent consulting, requiring personal CEO/CFO certification of financial statements, and creating an independent audit oversight regulator (**PCAOB**).

### Satyam Computer Services (2009) - India’s Governance Inflection Point
If Enron exposed executive agency failure, **Satyam** laid bare India’s promoter-driven Type-II pathology. In January 2009, founder-chairman **B. Ramalinga Raju** confessed to a multi-year accounting fabrication involving over ₹7,000 crore in fictitious cash balances and inflated revenues—likening his fraud to *"riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten."*

Crucially, the fraud unraveled only when Raju attempted a classic **related-party tunnelling** maneuver: deploying Satyam’s supposed cash reserves to acquire **Maytas Properties** and **Maytas Infra** (firms controlled by his family), which provoked an institutional investor revolt. Despite boasting a marquee board of distinguished independent directors and Big-Four auditor Price Waterhouse, neither questioned fake fixed-deposit confirmations. Satyam catalyzed India’s statutory overhaul under the **Companies Act, 2013**, introducing mandatory auditor rotation, statutory status for the **Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO)**, class-action suits (Section 245), and an independent audit watchdog (**NFRA**).

### Related-Party Siphoning and Crony Capitalism
Beyond outright balance-sheet forgery, the most pervasive ethical rot in Indian business is **related-party tunnelling**—the legalistically camouflaged transfer of listed-company assets to promoter-controlled private entities. Common vectors include inflated brand-royalty payments, non-arm’s-length procurement contracts, unsecured inter-corporate loans to promoter shells, and pledging promoter equity to fund leveraged expansions.

When private governance failures fuse with political patronage, they mutate into **crony capitalism**. India’s twin-balance-sheet crisis—epitomized by **IL&FS (2018)** (an opaque labyrinth of 300+ subsidiaries hiding defaults while enjoying AAA ratings from fee-dependent credit agencies) and **Yes Bank (2020)** (promoter-concentrated lending to stressed borrowers with concealed non-performing assets)—illustrates how lax corporate boards convert private moral hazard into systemic taxpayer bailouts. In democratic theory, the convergence of oligarchic corporate capital, opaque political financing, and captive regulatory discretion erodes both Schumpeterian competition and constitutional equality.

### Startup Governance Collapses (2022–2026) - The Unicorn Moral Hazard
India’s venture-capital boom exposed a third governance failure mode: **pre-IPO unicorn governance vacuums**. Private startups fall outside SEBI’s listed-company scrutiny, while venture investors—driven by blitzscaling and next-round valuation mark-ups—frequently tolerate weak internal controls and charismatic founder dominance until liquidity tightens.

- **Byju’s (Think & Learn Pvt. Ltd.):** Once valued at **$22 billion**, the edtech major unraveled through interlocking governance failures: aggressive debt-fuelled acquisitions, disputed revenue-recognition practices, and chronic non-submission of audited accounts. In **June 2023**, statutory auditor **Deloitte Haskins & Sells** abruptly resigned citing protracted delays in FY22 financial statements and unresolved audit modifications, coinciding with the exit of three investor board nominees (Peak XV Partners, Prosus, and Chan Zuckerberg Initiative). Subsequent litigation with US Term Loan B lenders represented by **GLAS Trust Company LLC** centered on $1.2 billion in debt and allegations of concealing **$533 million** routed through special-purpose vehicle *Byju’s Alpha*. Following NCLT’s admission of BCCI’s ₹158.9 crore insolvency plea in **July 2024**, the **Supreme Court (October 2024 and July/November 2025)** rejected private promoter–creditor settlement bids, reaffirming that once Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) begins, proceedings become collective (*in rem*) and withdrawal strictly requires **90% approval of the Committee of Creditors under Section 12A of the IBC**.
- **BharatPe (Resilient Innovations Pvt. Ltd.):** The fintech unicorn suffered an acute founder–board rupture after an independent forensic audit by **Alvarez & Marsal** flagged alleged misappropriation exceeding **₹81 crore** via non-existent vendors, dummy recruitment invoices, and undisclosed related-party networks tied to former MD **Ashneer Grover** and family associates. Following Grover’s removal in March 2022 and two-and-a-half years of acrimonious civil, NCLT, and Delhi Police Economic Offences Wing proceedings, both sides executed a **definitive settlement on September 30, 2024**, under which Grover exited the cap table entirely (transferring shares to the Resilient Growth Trust and a family trust) and both parties dropped pending legal actions.

From an ethical standpoint, startup collapses reveal a troubling asymmetry: private out-of-court settlements may restore operational peace for venture investors, yet dropping criminal and fiduciary accountability mid-stream weakens public deterrence and leaves employees, minority ESOP holders, and retail consumers bearing the collateral damage.

## 3. Evolution of Indian Corporate Governance Reforms - Committees, SEBI LODR & NFRA

India’s corporate governance architecture did not arrive overnight; it evolved through successive crisis-driven committees that progressively shifted governance from voluntary industry etiquette to enforceable statutory law.

### From Birla to Murthy - Building the Clause 49 Spine
Following the **CII Desirable Corporate Governance Code (1998)**, SEBI constituted the **Kumar Mangalam Birla Committee (May 1999)** to formalize investor protection. Its recommendations birthed **Clause 49 of the Listing Agreement**, establishing India’s foundational governance pillars: mandatory board balance (at least one-third Independent Directors under a non-executive chair, and one-half under an executive chair), independent Audit Committees, and transparent Management Discussion & Analysis disclosures.

After Enron, the **Naresh Chandra Committee (2002)** focused on auditor independence, restricting non-audit consulting and clarifying independent director liabilities. SEBI then appointed the **Narayana Murthy Committee (2003)** to close Clause 49 loopholes. Murthy strengthened Audit Committee scrutiny over **Related-Party Transactions (RPTs)** and IPO proceeds, mandated board-level risk assessment, instituted formal **CEO/CFO financial certification**, and introduced India’s **Whistleblower Policy** framework (later codified into mandatory Vigil Mechanisms under Section 177 of the Companies Act, 2013). The **J.J. Irani Committee (2005)** subsequently translated these regulatory insights into the legislative architecture of the **Companies Act, 2013**.

### The Uday Kotak Committee (2017) & SEBI (LODR) Architecture
Recognizing that formal compliance masked persistent promoter dominance, SEBI constituted the 25-member **Uday Kotak Committee in June 2017** (Report submitted **October 2017**), leading to sweeping amendments to the **SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015**. Three structural fronts define the post-Kotak regime and its live 2026 ground truth:

- **Separation of Chairperson and MD/CEO (The Voluntary Reversal):** To prevent a single individual from simultaneously executing strategy and chairing the board that supervises execution, Kotak recommended mandating top 500 listed entities to separate the Chairperson (non-executive and unrelated to the MD/CEO) from the MD/CEO. SEBI initially mandated implementation from April 1, 2020 and deferred it to April 1, 2022. However, confronting only **54% compliance** by December 2021—driven by strong resistance from family-owned conglomerates—**SEBI reverted the separation to a *voluntary* provision in February–March 2022**, a status that remains unchanged as of 2026. This retreat exposes the political-economy ceiling of governance reform in promoter-heavy markets.
- **Independent Directors (IDs) & Board Composition:** Following Kotak, SEBI mandated a **minimum of six directors** (top 2,000 listed firms), at least **one independent woman director** (top 1,000), a cap of **seven listed directorships** (maximum three ID seats for a serving Whole-Time Director), quorum requiring at least one ID, mandatory Directors & Officers (D&O) liability insurance for IDs, and Audit Committees with at least **two-thirds Independent Directors** wielding exclusive approval power over RPTs. To break promoter control over board appointments, SEBI inserted **Regulation 25(2A) (2021, refined November 2022)** requiring a **Special Resolution (75% vote)** for appointing, re-appointing, or removing an ID—paired with a minority-protective proviso allowing appointment where simple majority and a *majority of public (non-promoter) shareholders* vote in favor.
- **The Sociological Paradox of Independent Directors:** Statutory checkboxes cannot conjure moral courage. Because promoters still nominate candidates from elite social and professional networks, many Indian IDs remain *"independent in form, deferential in spirit"*—quietly resigning *"for personal reasons"* upon spotting red flags rather than triggering public whistleblower escalation.

### National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) - Ending Audit Self-Regulation
Until 2018, Indian statutory auditors were policed exclusively by their own professional guild, the **Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI)**—a structural conflict between guild solidarity and public investor protection. Constituted on **October 1, 2018** under **Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013**, the **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)** established independent statutory oversight over audits of Public Interest Entities (listed companies, banks, insurers, and large unlisted corporates).

Armed with civil-court investigative powers, NFRA can levy penalties ranging from **₹1 lakh up to five times audit fees** for individuals and **₹5 lakh up to ten times audit fees** for firms, alongside **debarment for six months to ten years**, automatically preempting parallel ICAI proceedings once NFRA assumes jurisdiction.

Between 2024 and 2026, NFRA escalated from reactive disciplinary orders into proactive systemic policing through recurring **Audit Quality Inspection Reports** (guidelines updated April 2026) targeting top CA networks. Two live institutional faultlines define NFRA’s trajectory as of late 2026:

1. **The SA 600 Group Audit Impasse:** In November 2024, NFRA recommended 40 revised auditing standards aligned with global norms, proposing a stricter **SA 600** making the principal group auditor unequivocally accountable for subsidiary audits—directly closing the loophole exploited in multi-tier collapses like IL&FS and Reliance Capital. **ICAI** pushed back with an alternative December 2025 revision preserving reliance on component auditors with added safeguards, leaving final notification pending with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs.
2. **Statutory Expansion:** The **Corporate Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026**, introduced in the Lok Sabha in March 2026 and referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee, proposes further reinforcing NFRA’s governance and enforcement architecture.

## 4. CSR Tokenism, Automation Ethics & ESG Greenwashing Integrity

Modern corporate ethics has shifted decisively from balance-sheet compliance to three contested frontiers: whether mandated philanthropy masks operational harm, how firms distribute the human shock of artificial intelligence, and whether ESG disclosures inform markets or manufacture green illusions.

### Section 135 CSR - Legal Architecture and Critique of Compliance Tokenism
India became the first major economy to codify mandatory Corporate Social Responsibility under **Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013** (effective April 2014). Every company—public or private—meeting **any one** of three thresholds in the preceding financial year (**Net Worth ≥ ₹500 crore**, OR **Turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore**, OR **Net Profit ≥ ₹5 crore**) must spend at least **2% of its average net profits** of the preceding three financial years on activities enumerated in **Schedule VII**.

Under **Rule 2(1)(d) of the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014**, expenditures undertaken in the normal course of business, activities directly benefitting the company’s own employees, political donations, brand-marketing sponsorships, and projects outside India (except national/international sports training) are **strictly disqualified** from counting as CSR. Furthermore, under Explanation 2 to **Section 37(1) of the Income Tax Act, 1961**, CSR expenditure is **not tax-deductible** as a business expense.

Amendments enacted in 2019–2020 (notified January 2021) transformed Section 135 from a soft *"comply-or-explain"* norm into a binding expenditure regime. Unspent funds must be transferred within 30 days to an **Unspent CSR Account** (for ongoing multi-year projects, spendable within three years) or within six months to designated **Schedule VII national funds**. Simultaneously, the **Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020 decriminalized** CSR defaults—replacing the earlier three-year imprisonment threat with **civil monetary penalties under Section 135(7)** (up to twice the unspent amount or ₹1 crore for the company; one-tenth or ₹2 lakh for officers in default), exempting firms with annual obligations ≤ ₹50 lakh from forming CSR Committees, and permitting excess spend set-offs. Large spenders (average obligation ≥ ₹10 crore) must commission independent third-party **Impact Assessments** under Rule 8 for completed projects of ₹1 crore or more.

Empirically, statutory CSR has mobilized massive capital. Per official Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) filings reported in February 2026, cumulative CSR spending over five years (FY 2019–20 to FY 2023–24) exceeded **₹1,44,159 crore**, with official FY 2023–24 expenditure reaching **₹34,908.75 crore**—nearly 3.5 times the FY 2014–15 baseline of ₹10,066 crore (and preliminary FY 2024–25 portal filings crossing ₹40,794 crore).

Yet behind impressive headline outlays lie **five deep ethical pathologies**:

1. **Moral Licensing and the "2% vs. 98%" Fallacy:** Statutory CSR invites **compliance tokenism**—treating ethics as a 2% philanthropic tax while keeping the 98% core business engine extractive. A conglomerate can deplete groundwater, crush collective bargaining, or sell ultra-processed foods while collecting CSR awards for building classrooms. True corporate ethics governs *how money is made*, not merely *how 2% of surplus is donated*.
2. **Headquarter Bias and Geographic Inequity:** CSR capital gravitates toward industrial hubs where corporate headquarters sit rather than where human deprivation is acute. In FY 2023–24 and FY 2024–25 MCA datasets, Maharashtra (~17–21%), Gujarat, Karnataka, Delhi, and Tamil Nadu absorbed over half of traceable CSR flows, whereas high-poverty states such as Bihar (~1% of traceable FY 2023–24 CSR) and the North-East received a sliver. Education (~35%) and healthcare (~20%) dominate, while environmental restoration and rural livelihoods remain underfunded.
3. **Erosion of Local Agency & State Abdication:** Allowing easy compliance via bulk transfers into central relief funds weakens community-embedded development, while creating a political temptation for the state to substitute voluntary corporate charity for accountable, tax-funded public welfare.
4. **Captive Trusts & Round-Tripping:** Before mandatory MCA **Form CSR-1** registration of implementing agencies, shell NGOs and promoter-run foundations frequently recycled CSR outlays into reputational vanity projects or kickback loops.
5. **Input Fixation over Capability Outcomes:** Most CSR board disclosures still audit *rupees disbursed* rather than **Amartya Sen’s capability expansion**—measuring toilets constructed rather than sustained sanitation outcomes.

### Ethics of AI-Driven Workforce Downsizing and Algorithmic Labor
The rapid enterprise deployment of generative and agentic AI across software engineering, customer operations, financial analysis, and content moderation has reopened a classic moral dilemma of industrial capitalism at unprecedented velocity. While neoclassical economics frames automation through Schumpeterian *creative destruction*, moral philosophy refuses to treat displaced human beings as frictionless adjustment costs.

- **Epistemic Appropriation & Kantian Instrumentalization:** In enterprise AI rollouts, veteran employees routinely document workflows, label edge cases, and refine prompt pipelines—effectively encoding their tacit institutional knowledge into proprietary models that subsequently eliminate their roles. Dismissing those workers abruptly while capturing 100% of productivity surpluses as executive bonuses and shareholder buybacks violates **Kant’s Humanity Formula**, reducing human cognitive labor to a disposable scaffolding.
- **Asymmetric Distribution & Rawlsian Just Transition:** AI productivity gains accrue concentrically to capital owners and frontier compute holders, whereas displacement shocks hit mid-tier white-collar workforces—a particularly acute ethical risk for India’s IT-BPM services backbone. Under **Rawls’s Difference Principle**, institutional efficiency gains are morally legitimate only if structured to protect the worst-affected through **Just Transition compacts**: advance transparency on automation roadmaps, dedicated corporate reskilling endowments, internal redeployment tracks before retrenchment, and dignified severance buffers.
- **Algorithmic Management & Denial of Natural Justice:** Across platform and gig economies, automated scheduling, dynamic pay opacity, and robotic ID-deactivation replace human supervisors with inscrutable code. Firing or penalizing workers via black-box algorithms without reasoned notice or human appeal strips labor of **procedural fairness (*audi alteram partem*)** and erodes psychological dignity.

### ESG Greenwashing Deception & India’s Anti-Greenwashing Architecture
As global institutional capital migrated toward Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) mandates, corporate marketing discovered a lucrative arbitrage: harvesting the brand premium and lower cost of capital associated with sustainability without bearing the operational expense of decarbonization.

**Greenwashing** encompasses selective disclosure (highlighting minor Scope 1 energy savings while concealing massive Scope 3 supply-chain emissions), unverified buzzwords (*"100% eco-friendly"*), misleading carbon offsets purchased from non-additional forestry credits, and cherry-picked lifecycle claims (advertising a recyclable cap on a non-recyclable bottle). Its mirror pathology is **Greenhushing**—firms quietly shelving or concealing climate commitments to evade legal scrutiny—alongside **Social-washing**, where firms trumpet diversity slogans while suppressing contract-worker wages.

Philosophically, greenwashing inflicts a **compound ethical harm**. Deontologically, it is deliberate **epistemic deception** that subverts the informed autonomy of consumers and retail investors. Consequentialistically, it distorts price signals and **misallocates finite climate capital** toward cosmetic polluters instead of genuine green innovators—accelerating planetary overshoot. Notably, **UPSC Mains 2024 GS4 (Case Study Q7)** placed this exact tension center-stage: a tech CEO confronting a 48% surge in GHG emissions driven by AI data centers, forcing a choice between narrative spin and real decarbonization.

Between 2023 and 2026, India erected a **two-pillar regulatory firewall** against greenwashing spanning capital markets and consumer protection:

- **Pillar 1 — SEBI BRSR & BRSR Core Assurance Framework:** Built on the nine principles of the **National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC)**, the full **Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR)** has been mandatory for India’s **top 1,000 listed entities** by market capitalization since FY 2022–23. To stop self-reported ESG puffery, SEBI introduced **BRSR Core (July 12, 2023)**—a verifiable subset of **9 measurable ESG attributes** spanning GHG footprint, water, energy, circularity/waste, employee wellbeing/safety, gender diversity, India-specific inclusive development (such as job creation in smaller towns and PPP-adjusted intensity ratios), fairness toward customers/suppliers, and openness of business. Independent verification followed a strict four-year glide path: **top 150 (FY 2023–24)** → **top 250 (FY 2024–25)** → **top 500 (FY 2025–26)** → **full top 1,000 from FY 2026–27**. Crucially, to safeguard integrity, SEBI prohibits any verifier (or its associates) from selling consulting or non-audit services to the company or its group entities.
- **SEBI’s March 2025 Ease-of-Doing-Business Recalibration (Critical 2025–2026 Nuance):** Via Circular dated **March 28, 2025**, SEBI softened strict *"reasonable assurance"* into **"assessment or assurance"** (permitting third-party assessment under **Industry Standards Forum [ISF]** standards alongside full assurance), added a voluntary Principle 6 leadership indicator on **Green Credits**, and downgraded **ESG Value Chain disclosures** (upstream/downstream partners each ≥ 2% of purchases/sales, up to 75% coverage) for the top 250 listed firms from "comply-or-explain" to **voluntary** from FY 2025–26 (with voluntary verification from FY 2026–27). While lowering SME compliance friction, ethicists warn that making value-chain reporting voluntary creates a **Scope 3 accountability loophole**, enabling parent corporations to outsource carbon-heavy and labor-intensive abuses to unlisted suppliers.
- **Pillar 2 — CCPA Greenwashing Guidelines (October 15, 2024):** Exercising powers under **Section 18 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019**, the **Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)** notified the *Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Greenwashing or Misleading Environmental Claims, 2024*. Applicable across manufacturers, sellers, service providers, ad agencies, and celebrity endorsers, the guidelines prohibit vague claims (*"green"*, *"clean"*, *"natural"*, *"carbon-neutral"*) unless qualified by verifiable scientific evidence or credible third-party certification accessible via **QR code or web link**. Aspirational pledges (e.g., *"Net Zero by 2040"*) are permitted only when backed by **clear, actionable implementation plans**. Violations constitute unfair trade practices carrying Section 21 penalties up to **₹10 lakh** (extending to **₹50 lakh** for repeat offenses).

## 5. Global Probity Architecture - UNCAC (2003), OECD Anti-Bribery & Extraterritorial Integrity

In a borderless economy of multinational supply chains and offshore secrecy havens, purely national anti-corruption laws hit a structural wall. Transnational bribery generates a classic **Collective Action Dilemma**: if Jurisdiction A prosecutes its corporations for bribing foreign officials while Jurisdiction B permits tax-deductible "commission payments," honest enterprises lose international contracts in the short run. Global anti-corruption conventions exist to break this race-to-the-bottom equilibrium.

### United Nations Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC, 2003)
Adopted by the UN General Assembly on **31 October 2003** and entering into force on **14 December 2005** (with **UNODC** as secretariat), **UNCAC** is the sole legally binding *universal* anti-corruption treaty, boasting **193 States Parties** as of late 2026. Structured across eight chapters and 71 articles, it rests on five substantive pillars:

1. **Preventive Measures (Chapter II):** Codes of conduct, transparent public procurement, beneficial-ownership visibility, and private-sector accounting standards.
2. **Criminalization and Law Enforcement (Chapter III):** Mandatory criminalization of bribery of national public officials, **active bribery of foreign public officials (Article 16)**, embezzlement, and money laundering, alongside optional provisions covering private-sector bribery and illicit enrichment.
3. **International Cooperation (Chapter IV):** Binding frameworks for extradition, joint investigations, and Mutual Legal Assistance (MLA).
4. **Asset Recovery (Chapter V):** Elevating repatriation of stolen public wealth parked in offshore financial centers to a *"fundamental principle"* of international law—vital for developing nations stripped by kleptocratic elites and complicit global banks.
5. **Technical Assistance & Peer Review (Chapters VI–VII):** Capacity-building and the Implementation Review Mechanism.

**India’s Compliance Record and the Article 16 Blind Spot:** India signed UNCAC in December 2005 and deposited its instrument of **ratification on May 9, 2011** (concurrently with UNTOC). To align domestic corporate liability with global norms, Parliament amended the **Prevention of Corruption Act (PCA), 1988 in 2018**, inserting **Section 9** (holding commercial organizations criminally liable if any associated person bribes an Indian public servant to secure business, subject to an **"adequate procedures"** compliance defense) and **Section 10** (imposing 3–7 years’ imprisonment on consenting or conniving directors/officers).

Yet a glaring normative and statutory gap persists: despite repeated draft bills (2011, 2015, and 2019), **India has still not enacted standalone legislation fulfilling UNCAC Article 16** to criminalize bribery of *foreign* public officials by Indian corporations operating abroad. As Indian conglomerates expand across African infrastructure, Southeast Asian energy, and global defense tenders, this omission creates an ethical asymmetry between domestic probity and outbound commercial conduct.

### OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (1997 / Effective 1999)
Whereas UNCAC spans both demand and supply sides universally, the **OECD Convention on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International Business Transactions** (signed **December 17, 1997**; effective **February 15, 1999**) is laser-focused on disrupting the **supply side** of transnational corruption—punishing exporting corporations and intermediaries that offer bribes abroad.

Its **46 Parties**—comprising all 38 OECD member nations plus eight non-OECD economies (Argentina, Brazil, Bulgaria, Croatia, Peru, Romania, South Africa, and Russia, whose participation in the Working Group on Bribery was suspended in April 2022)—collectively account for **over two-thirds of global exports and roughly 75% of outward FDI**. Enforcement is policed through stringent multi-phase peer evaluations conducted by the **OECD Working Group on Bribery (WGB)**, which explicitly prohibits tax deductibility of foreign bribes and mandates strict corporate books-and-records controls.

**India’s Non-Signatory Status:** **India is NOT a party or signatory to the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention**. India collaborates selectively through the G20 Anti-Corruption Working Group and the ADB/OECD Anti-Corruption Initiative for Asia-Pacific, and partner nations (notably the UK during India’s 2023 G20 Presidency) have repeatedly urged New Delhi both to enact UNCAC Article 16 and accede to the OECD framework.

### Extraterritorial Anti-Bribery Laws and Asymmetric Global Ethics
In practice, global corporate probity has long been anchored by aggressive extraterritorial domestic statutes—chiefly the **US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA, 1977)** and the **UK Bribery Act (2010)** (which goes beyond FCPA by criminalizing private-to-private commercial bribery, banning "facilitation payments" entirely, and establishing a strict corporate offense of *"failure to prevent bribery"*).

Two ethical tensions cloud this global architecture in 2026. First, as the OECD WGB launched stakeholder consultations in mid-2026 for **Phase 5 of its peer-review cycle**, legal monitors noted growing enforcement fragility following a sharp pullback in US foreign-bribery caseloads—exposing the risk of over-relying on a single hegemonic enforcer. Second, developing nations rightly highlight **moral double standards**: Western jurisdictions routinely fine corporations for bribing Global South officials, yet tolerate opaque corporate registries, shell trusts, and tax havens within their own overseas territories that launder the illicit proceeds—turning global probity into selective enforcement unless beneficial-ownership transparency and Chapter V asset recovery are enforced symmetrically.

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature
- **Chair–MD Separation Trap:** Statements asserting that SEBI LODR *mandates* top 500 listed companies to separate Chairperson and MD/CEO roles are **incorrect**; SEBI reverted this Uday Kotak Committee provision to a **voluntary** requirement in February–March 2022, and it remains voluntary in 2026.
- **Section 135 CSR Threshold, Tax & Criminality Traps:** Three classic inversions—(a) the three eligibility thresholds (Net Worth ≥ ₹500 Cr, Turnover ≥ ₹1,000 Cr, Net Profit ≥ ₹5 Cr) are **disjunctive (OR)**, not cumulative (AND); (b) CSR spending is **not tax-deductible** under Section 37(1) of the Income Tax Act; and (c) CSR non-compliance **no longer carries imprisonment**, having been decriminalized into civil monetary penalties under Section 135(7) by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020.
- **BRSR Core & Value-Chain Mandatory-Status Trap:** Full BRSR is mandatory for the top 1,000 listed firms (since FY 2022–23), and BRSR Core verification reached the full top 1,000 by FY 2026–27—**but** SEBI’s March 2025 circular replaced strict *"reasonable assurance"* with *"assessment or assurance"* AND shifted **value-chain ESG disclosures** for the top 250 firms from "comply-or-explain" to **voluntary**.
- **Global Anti-Bribery Convention Membership Trap:** Claiming India has ratified *both* UNCAC and the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention is **false**—India ratified **UNCAC in May 2011**, is **not a party** to the 46-nation OECD Anti-Bribery Convention, and still lacks enacted domestic legislation specifically criminalizing foreign-public-official bribery under UNCAC Article 16.
