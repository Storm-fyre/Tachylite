# Chapter 17 - Intellectual Property Jurisprudence, Patents Sec 3(d)/84, WIPO 2024 Treaty, SCOMET & Frontier Tech Ethics

Intellectual property law is the constitutional bargain between private innovation incentives and public access to knowledge: the state grants a temporary statutory monopoly in exchange for full technical disclosure into the public domain. In India, this architecture stretches simultaneously across **public-health jurisprudence** (Section 3(d) anti-evergreening and Compulsory Licensing), **community heritage and biodiversity defense** (GIs, TKDL, and the May 2024 WIPO GRATK Treaty), **national-security export controls** (DGFT's expanded 2025 SCOMET regime), and **unsettled frontier legal collisions** over AI inventorship, LLM copyright ingestion, and human neurorights.

---

## India's Eight Intellectual Property Classes and Institutional Architecture

### Institutional Spine - DPIIT, CGPDTM, and Post-IPAB Judicialization

Under the **National Intellectual Property Rights Policy, 2016** (*"Creative India; Innovative India"*), the **Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT)** (formerly DIPP, Ministry of Commerce & Industry) functions as the nodal department coordinating IPR policy while explicitly reiterating India's commitment to both the **WTO TRIPS Agreement** and the **Doha Development Agenda** (tested verbatim in **UPSC Prelims 2017**). Operational registration and examination across Patents, Designs, Trademarks, Geographical Indications, and Copyright (transferred from Education to DPIIT in 2016) reside with the **Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM)**.

Two administrative caveats trip Prelims candidates routinely. First, **two of India's eight IP regimes sit completely outside DPIIT**: **Plant Varieties** operate under the **PPV&FR Authority (Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare)**, and **Semiconductor IC Layout-Designs** operate under **MeitY**. Second, the **Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021** abolished the **Intellectual Property Appellate Board (IPAB)** (which existed when UPSC tested it in Prelims 2019); all appellate IP jurisdiction now vests directly in **High Courts**, prompting creation of specialized **Intellectual Property Divisions (IPDs)** at the Delhi, Madras, Calcutta, and Himachal Pradesh High Courts.

### The Eight IPR Classes (Statutory Precision & 2024–2026 Status)

1. **Patents (*Patents Act, 1970*, amended 1999, 2002, 2005):** Grants a territorial, **non-renewable 20-year monopoly** from filing date for novel products or processes satisfying three cumulative tests—**novelty**, **inventive step** (non-obviousness), and **industrial applicability**—in exchange for enabling public disclosure.
2. **Trademarks (*Trade Marks Act, 1999*):** Protects distinctive signs (words, logos, packaging shapes, sound marks, color combinations) distinguishing commercial origin of goods or services. Valid for **10 years and renewable indefinitely** upon payment of renewal fees; prevents consumer deception and brand dilution.
3. **Copyright (*Copyright Act, 1957*):** Protects original **expressions** (never raw ideas, facts, or procedures) across literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, cinematograph films, sound recordings, and **computer software (explicitly protected as "literary works")**. Arises **automatically upon creation** (registration is evidentiary, not mandatory). Term: **author's lifetime + 60 years** for literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works; **60 years from publication** for films, sound recordings, photographs, government works, and posthumous works.
4. **Industrial Designs (*Designs Act, 2000*):** Protects solely the **aesthetic and ornamental visual features** (shape, configuration, pattern, ornament) judged purely by the eye on an industrial article—excluding any mode of mechanical construction or pure technical function. Term: **10 years**, extendable once by **5 years** (**15 years maximum**).
5. **Geographical Indications (*GI of Goods [Registration and Protection] Act, 1999*, effective Sept 15, 2003):** Administered by the **GI Registry, Chennai**, conferring a **collective community right** (non-transferable and non-licensable to individuals outside the geographic zone) identifying agricultural, natural, or manufactured/handicraft goods whose quality or reputation is essentially attributable to a specific territory (**Darjeeling Tea** was India's first GI in 2004–05). Valid for **10 years, renewable indefinitely**. Maintaining **dual-standard numeric parity**: India crossed **600–658+ registered GIs in 2024–25** and stood at **822 registrations on the official Chennai register as of March 31, 2026** (note: this official tally includes separate logo entries and foreign GIs such as *Champagne* and *Scotch Whisky*, with **Uttar Pradesh** leading among Indian states). The **GI (Amendment) Rules, 2025** slashed GI application fees by **80%** and cut renewal fees from ₹3,000 to ₹500 to accelerate artisan onboarding.
6. **Plant Varieties & Farmers' Rights (*PPV&FR Act, 2001*):** Because India bars patenting plant varieties under Patents Act Section 3(j), the PPV&FR Act supplies an effective ***sui generis* system** complying with **TRIPS Article 27.3(b)**. Administered by the **PPV&FR Authority (MoA&FW)**, protection spans **15 years** for new/extant field crops (initial 6 + 9 yr renewal) and **18 years** for trees and vines (initial 9 + 9 yr). Uniquely in global jurisprudence, **Section 39** codifies **Farmers' Rights**—guaranteeing every Indian farmer the unrestricted right to **save, use, sow, resow, exchange, share, or sell farm-saved seed** of a protected variety, provided it is *not sold as branded seed* (the statutory shield invoked during PepsiCo's withdrawn infringement suits over its registered **FC5** potato variety).
7. **Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout-Designs (*SICLD Act, 2000*):** Administered by the **SICLD Registry under MeitY**, protecting the original three-dimensional physical topography (mask work) of transistors and interconnects on an IC chip. Valid for a **non-renewable 10-year term** from filing or first commercial exploitation anywhere in the world.
8. **Trade Secrets (Undisclosed Commercial Information — TRIPS Art. 39):** Protects secret formulas, manufacturing know-how, algorithms, and client lists deriving commercial value from secrecy (e.g., Coca-Cola's syrup formula, chip lithography recipes) for **potentially perpetual duration** so long as confidentiality holds—with **zero public disclosure** (the exact inverse of patents). Crucially, **India currently has NO codified Trade Secrets Act**: disputes rely on common-law breach of confidence, equity, Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act (which restricts post-employment non-compete clauses), the IT Act, and criminal provisions under BNS.
   - **22nd Law Commission 289th Report (*Trade Secrets and Economic Espionage*, March 2024):** Chaired by **Justice Ritu Raj Awasthi**, the Commission submitted the draft ***Protection of Trade Secrets Bill, 2024*** (13 sections, 4 chapters). Notably, the Commission **rejected treating trade secrets as "property"** (framing protection instead around unfair competition/misappropriation), proposed **civil remedies**, whistleblower and reverse-engineering exceptions, a narrow **public-interest compulsory licensing** provision, and recommended a **separate statute for state-sponsored economic espionage**. As of November 2026, Parliament has not yet enacted the Bill—even as modern trade deals like the **India–UK CETA** (effective July 2026) tighten expectations for statutory protection of undisclosed business information.

## Patents Act 1970 to 2005, TRIPS Flexibilities, Procedural Overhaul, and SEPs

### From Process Patents to Product Patents - The Doha Balancing Act

Following the **Justice N. Rajagopala Ayyangar Committee Report (1959)**, India's **Patents Act, 1970** abolished **product patents** for pharmaceuticals, foods, and agrochemicals, permitting only short-term **process patents**. By legally allowing Indian chemists to invent alternative synthesis pathways for patented Western molecules, the 1970 statute catalyzed India's domestic generic pharmaceutical industry—making India the *"Pharmacy of the World"* (supplying affordable antiretrovirals that slashed global HIV therapy from ~$10,000/year to <$100/year).

Joining the WTO in 1995 bound India to the **TRIPS Agreement**, mandating uniform **20-year product patents** across all fields of technology. Utilizing the 10-year developing-country transition window, Parliament passed the **Patents (Amendment) Act, 2005** reintroducing pharmaceutical product patents—while deliberately weaving in every public-health safeguard affirmed by the **2001 WTO Doha Declaration on the TRIPS Agreement and Public Health** (which declared that TRIPS *"does not and should not prevent Members from taking measures to protect public health"*).

### Section 3 Exclusions - Anti-Evergreening, Software, and Life Materials

**Section 3** defines non-patentable subject matter—forming the core legal shield against abusive monopolies:

- **Section 3(d) — Anti-Evergreening Firewall (*Novartis v. Union of India*, 2013):** Evergreening is the pharmaceutical strategy of obtaining fresh 20-year monopolies on expiring blockbuster drugs by patenting trivial tweaks—new salts, esters, polymorphs, isomers, particle sizes, or fixed-dose combinations. Section 3(d) explicitly bars patenting *"the mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy of that substance."* In its landmark **April 1, 2013 judgment** rejecting **Novartis AG's** patent claim on the beta-crystalline polymorph of leukemia drug **Imatinib Mesylate (*Glivec*)**, the **Supreme Court** ruled that for medicines, **"efficacy" strictly denotes clinical therapeutic efficacy**—excluding mere physico-chemical advantages (better flowability, thermodynamic stability, lower hygroscopicity, or even ~30% higher bioavailability unless empirically proven to improve therapeutic cure/treatment outcomes). Section 3(d) remains actively decisive: in 2023, the Indian Patent Office rejected Johnson & Johnson's secondary fumarate-salt evergreening application on MDR-TB cornerstone **Bedaquiline**, unlocking low-cost generics, while patient groups have invoked it against secondary filings on breakthrough HIV preventative **Lenacapavir**.
- **Section 3(k) — Software and Algorithms:** Bars patenting *"a mathematical or business method or a **computer programme per se** or algorithms."* Pure code sits under Copyright. However, judicial rulings (Delhi HC in *Ferid Allani* and *Microsoft*) and revised **Computer-Related Inventions (CRI) Guidelines** clarify that software-implemented inventions **are patentable** when integrated with hardware or demonstrating a verifiable **"technical effect / technical contribution"** (e.g., improving chip encryption speed, network bandwidth efficiency, or industrial sensor control) beyond abstract mathematical execution.
- **Section 3(j) — Plants, Animals, and Micro-Organisms (2019 Prelims Hit):** Excludes *"plants and animals in whole or any part thereof **other than micro-organisms**, but including seeds, varieties and species and essentially biological processes for production or propagation of plants and animals."* Consequently, neither **plant varieties** nor **essentially biological processes to create seeds** can be patented in India—yet **non-natural, genetically engineered or isolated micro-organisms** (and non-biological genetic-engineering methods) *remain patentable* under TRIPS Article 27.3(b).

### Compulsory Licensing and Regulatory Flexibilities

TRIPS Article 31 permits sovereign governments to override patent exclusivity via **Compulsory Licensing (CL)**—authorizing a third party to manufacture a patented product without the patentee's consent in return for reasonable royalty:

- **Section 84 (General CL — *Natco v. Bayer*, 2012):** Any interested applicant may apply **after 3 years from patent grant** if *any one* of three statutory triggers holds: (1) reasonable requirements of the public are unmet, (2) the patented invention is unavailable at a **reasonably affordable price**, or (3) it is **not worked in the territory of India**. On **March 9, 2012**, India granted its **first—and still sole—Compulsory License** to **Natco Pharma** against **Bayer Corporation's** kidney/liver cancer drug **Sorafenib Tosylate (*Nexavar*)**, slashing monthly patient cost by **~97% from ₹2.80 lakh to ₹8,880** (ordering Natco to pay Bayer a 6–7% royalty; upheld across IPAB, Bombay HC, and Supreme Court). Crucially, India exercises CL with high restraint: subsequent applications (*BDR Pharma / Dasatinib*; *Lee Pharma / Saxagliptin*) were rejected for insufficient voluntary-licensing efforts or market evidence, even as rare-disease advocates pressed for Section 84/92 intervention on Roche's SMA drug *Risdiplam* in 2024–25.
- **Section 92 (Emergency CL) & Section 92A (Export CL):** Section 92 empowers the Central Government to notify CL **at any time (waiving the 3-year wait)** during **national emergency, extreme urgency, or public non-commercial use**. Section 92A implements **TRIPS Article 31bis** (the WTO Paragraph 6 system), authorizing CL solely for **exporting patented medicines** to Least Developed Countries or nations lacking domestic pharmaceutical manufacturing capacity. Complementing these, **Section 100** empowers direct government acquisition/use for sovereign public purposes.
- **Section 107A — The "Bolar Exemption" and Parallel Imports:** Section 107A(a) allows generic firms to manufacture, use, sell, or import a patented molecule strictly for conducting clinical trials and regulatory filings **prior to patent expiry**—eliminating *de facto* patent extension and letting affordable generics enter pharmacies on Day 1 post-expiry. Section 107A(b) codifies **international exhaustion (parallel importation)**, permitting lawful import of patented goods purchased from any authorized global seller.

### Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024 (Notified March 15, 2024)

While keeping India's substantive statutory safeguards (Sec 3(d), Sec 84) completely intact, DPIIT notified the **Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024**—the most extensive procedural modernization since 2003—to compress examination backlogs as annual Indian patent grants crossed 1,00,000 in FY 2023–24:

- **Faster Examination (RFE Cut):** Slashed the deadline to file a **Request for Examination (RFE)** from **48 months to 31 months** from the earliest priority date (applicable to applications filed on/after March 15, 2024).
- **Form 27 (Commercial Working Statement) Relaxed:** Reduced mandatory filing of commercial working disclosures from **once every year to once every 3 financial years** with simplified non-revenue fields—cutting business compliance friction, though public-health scholars warn it reduces granular pricing/supply data vital for proving Section 84 CL claims.
- **Streamlined Foreign Filing Disclosures (Form 3 / Sec 8):** Replaced onerous six-monthly updates with just two submissions (within 6 months of filing and within 3 months of the First Examination Report), directing Controllers to consult public international patent databases.
- **Pre-Grant Opposition Guardrails (Sec 25(1)):** Introduced official filing fees for pre-grant oppositions and empowered the Controller to conduct an initial ***prima facie* maintainability screening** before issuing notice—deterring tactical frivolous delays while preserving civil-society anti-evergreening checks.
- **Inventor & Prosecution Incentives:** Formalized **Form 31** for claiming the **12-month grace period** following prior disclosure; allowed voluntary **divisional applications** based on provisional or complete specifications; offered a **10% fee discount** on $\ge 4$-year advance annuity payments; broadened **Rule 138** condonation/extension discretion up to 6 months across Rules deadlines; and created a formal **Certificate of Inventorship** honoring individual scientists.

### Standard Essential Patents (SEPs) and FRAND Jurisprudence

Modern telecommunications (3G/4G/5G), Wi-Fi, and video codecs depend on universal technical specifications adopted by **Standard Setting Organizations (SSOs)** such as 3GPP, ETSI, and IEEE. A patent whose claims are technically unavoidable to comply with such a standard is a **Standard Essential Patent (SEP)**. Because smartphone or IoT implementers cannot legally "design around" an SEP, SSOs require SEP owners to commit to licensing on **FRAND (Fair, Reasonable, and Non-Discriminatory)** terms.

FRAND law walks a tightrope between two market pathologies: **patent hold-up** (SEP owners threatening sales injunctions to extort supra-competitive royalties) and **patent hold-out** (implementers stalling licensing negotiations while selling millions of infringing devices). On **March 28, 2024**, the **Delhi High Court** delivered India's watershed first final post-trial telecom SEP judgment in ***Telefonaktiebolaget LM Ericsson v. Lava International Ltd.***—finding 7 of Ericsson's 8 asserted 2G/3G patents valid and essential, declaring Lava an *"unwilling licensee"* for failing to make good-faith FRAND counter-offers, and awarding **₹244 crore (~$30 million)** in damages plus 5% annual interest calculated on end-device value (comparable licensing approach), firmly anchoring India's IP courts inside global FRAND jurisprudence.

## Biopiracy Defense, the May 2024 WIPO GRATK Treaty, and Dual-Use SCOMET Controls

### Defending Bio-Sovereignty - TKDL, BD Act 2023, and the WIPO GRATK Treaty (May 2024)

Megadiverse developing countries have historically suffered **biopiracy**—foreign entities patenting biological extracts or traditional medicinal uses (notably 1990s US/EU patents on Indian turmeric wound-healing, neem fungicides, and basmati rice) without Prior Informed Consent (PIC) or Access and Benefit-Sharing (ABS) under the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and Nagoya Protocol. India's defensive architecture now combines three concentric layers:

1. **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL, est. 2001):** Jointly operated by **CSIR and the Ministry of AYUSH**, TKDL digitizes **~5.2 lakh** classical Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Yoga formulations into **five international patent-office languages** (English, French, German, Japanese, Spanish) shared via Non-Disclosure Access Agreements with **16+ global patent offices** (including IP Australia joining July 2026), blocking or revoking 265+ erroneous biopiracy claims as prior art.
2. **Patents Act Sec 10(4)(d)(ii)(D) + Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023:** The Patents Act requires every complete specification to disclose the source and geographical origin of any biological material used. **Crucial 2023–2024 Statutory Shift (Dual-Standard Parity vs. Legacy 2012 PYQ):** While the original Biological Diversity Act, 2002 required *all* applicants to obtain prior **National Biodiversity Authority (NBA)** approval for bio-linked IPR, the **Biological Diversity (Amendment) Act, 2023** (effective **April 1, 2024**) and **Biological Diversity Rules, 2024** (effective December 2024) bifurcate compliance cleanly: **foreign / Section 3(2) entities** must still secure **prior NBA approval** before patent grant (Form 7), whereas **Indian citizens/entities (Section 7)** only need to **register with the NBA before patent grant** (Form 8) and seek **prior NBA approval only at the stage of commercialization** (Form 9). Notably, the **BD Rules 2024** explicitly extend NBA compliance to **Digital Sequence Information (DSI)** utilized in obtaining IPR.
3. **WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge (GRATK Treaty — Adopted May 24, 2024):** Concluded by consensus in Geneva after over two decades of WIPO Intergovernmental Committee (IGC) negotiations, this landmark accord is the **first WIPO treaty linking patent law to biodiversity** and the first explicitly recognizing **Indigenous Peoples and local communities**.
   - **Mandatory Origin Disclosure:** Obliges Contracting Parties to require patent applicants whose claimed inventions are *"based on"* genetic resources (GR) or associated traditional knowledge (ATK) to disclose the **country of origin** (or source, if unknown) and/or the Indigenous Peoples or local community providing ATK—creating global prior-art and ABS transparency across Western patent offices.
   - **Compromise Safeguards & Entry-into-Force Status (Nov 2026):** To bridge North–South resistance, the treaty bars states from revoking or invalidating a patent *solely* for failure to disclose unless **fraudulent intent** is established—mandating an opportunity to rectify errors alongside proportionate administrative sanctions—and explicitly recognizes **Information Systems** like India's TKDL for examiner searches. The treaty enters into force three months after **15 ratifications or accessions**; as of late 2026, it has **45+ signatories and 4 ratifying parties** (Albania, Malawi, Peru, Uganda) and is **not yet in force**—while India already enforces a stricter domestic disclosure regime.

### Dual-Use Strategic Trade Controls - WMD Act 2005 (Amended 2022) and DGFT SCOMET

Because frontier technologies—centrifuges, viral toxins, sub-45nm lithography, quantum processors, encryption, and UAVs—serve both civilian industry and **Weapons of Mass Destruction (WMDs)**, India enforces a rigorous national strategic-export architecture anchored on two statutory pillars:

- **WMD Act, 2005 (Amended August 2022):** The *Weapons of Mass Destruction and their Delivery Systems (Prohibition of Unlawful Activities) Act, 2005* criminalizes unlawful manufacture, transfer, transit, transshipment, and brokering of nuclear, chemical, and biological weapons and delivery missiles (implementing UNSC Resolution 1540, CWC, and BWC). The **2022 Amendment** inserted explicit prohibitions against **financing of WMD proliferation activities**, empowering the Central Government to freeze, seize, or attach funds and financial assets of proliferators in line with **FATF** standards. Operational export enforcement integrates into **Chapter IVA of the Foreign Trade (Development & Regulation) Act, 1992** and **Chapter 10 of Foreign Trade Policy (FTP) 2023**.
- **Multilateral Regime Alignment:** India is a full member of three of the four global export-control regimes—**MTCR** (2016), **Wassenaar Arrangement** (2017), and **Australia Group** (2018)—and maintains complete national harmonization with the control lists of the **Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG)** despite pending membership.

#### The SCOMET List (Categories 0 to 8) and the Landmark September 2025 Overhaul

Notified by the **Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT)** under Appendix 3 to Schedule 2 of ITC (HS), **SCOMET (*Special Chemicals, Organisms, Materials, Equipment and Technologies*)** classifies controlled dual-use goods, software, and intangible technology transfers across **nine categories (Category 0 to Category 8)** vetted through an **Inter-Ministerial Working Group (IMWG)**—with licensing jurisdiction split across three authorities: **DAE** handles **Category 0** (nuclear materials/facilities), **Department of Defence Production (DDP, MoD)** handles **Category 6** (Munitions List, fully unified under DDP in September 2024), and **DGFT** licenses Categories 1–5, 7, and 8:

- **Category 0:** Nuclear materials, nuclear-related facilities, equipment, and technology (**DAE**).
- **Category 1:** Toxic chemical agents and CWC-linked chemical precursors.
- **Category 2:** Micro-organisms, human/animal/plant pathogens, and biological toxins (BWC / Australia Group).
- **Category 3:** Specialty materials and materials-processing equipment (expanded in September 2025 to cover automated **peptide synthesizers $\ge 1$ mmol** scale for biosecurity).
- **Category 4:** Nuclear-related dual-use equipment, assemblies, and components outside Category 0.
- **Category 5:** Aerospace systems, rockets, propulsion test equipment, and UAVs/drones (MTCR).
- **Category 6:** **Munitions List** (**DDP, MoD**; expanded in September 2025 to explicitly cover **Directed-Energy Weapons [laser, high-power RF/microwave, particle-beam]** and sub-orbital vehicles).
- **Category 7 — Activated September 23, 2025 (Effective October 23, 2025) as *"Emerging Technologies"*:** Previously left *"Reserved,"* DGFT formally populated Category 7 to control strategically sensitive frontier technologies *beyond* legacy Wassenaar lists—specifically covering **quantum computers ($\ge 34$ qubits with controlled error rates)**, **cryogenic CMOS integrated circuits**, **advanced semiconductor lithography equipment ($\le 45\text{ nm}$ resolvable feature size)**, **isotopically pure silicon/germanium and ultra-pure semiconductor fluorides/hydrides**, and **vacuum metal additive-manufacturing systems**. Crucially, controls apply strictly to items meeting notified technical thresholds (not a blanket ban on all AI or quantum R&D), aligning India to receive bilateral tech-transfer waivers from Western partners.
- **Category 8:** Wassenaar dual-use list spanning special materials, electronics, high-end computers, telecom, information security/cryptography, sensors, lasers, avionics, marine, and propulsion.

To prevent export controls from throttling legitimate Indian deep-tech and GCC exports, DGFT deploys streamlined **General Authorizations (bulk licenses)**:

- **GAEC (*General Authorisation for Export of Chemicals*):** One-time bulk authorization valid **5 years** for exporting eligible SCOMET chemicals to non-sanctioned, trusted partner countries.
- **GAICT (*General Authorisation for Intra-Company Transfers*):** Advance bulk authorization letting Indian R&D subsidiaries and Global Capability Centres (GCCs) re-export SCOMET software/technology (including chip design and encryption code) back to overseas parent/affiliate entities without per-shipment licensing delays.
- **GAED (*General Authorisation for Export of Drones*):** One-time **3-year** bulk license accelerating export of low-risk **civilian UAVs** (maximum range $\le 25\text{ km}$, payload $\le 25\text{ kg}$), complementing India's civilian drone manufacturing push.

## Frontier IP and Tech Ethics - AI Inventorship, LLM Copyright, and Neurorights

### AI Inventorship - The *DABUS* Global Consensus and India's 2026 Refusal

Can a machine be legally named as the "inventor" on a patent? Dr. Stephen Thaler stress-tested global patent statutes by filing synchronized applications worldwide listing his autonomous AI system **DABUS** (*Device for the Autonomous Bootstrapping of Unified Sentience*) as sole inventor of a fractal-surface food container and an emergency light beacon.

Every major examining patent jurisdiction converged on an **anthropocentric bright line**: **only a natural human person can be named an inventor** because inventorship entails legal capacity to hold, transfer, and assign property rights:

- **Global Rulings:** Rejected by the **European Patent Office (EPO)** (2020/2021), the **US Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit** (*Thaler v. Vidal*, 2022; US Supreme Court declined review 2023), **Australia's Full Federal Court** (2022), and unanimously by the **UK Supreme Court** in ***Thaler v. Comptroller-General of Patents*** (**December 20, 2023**). **South Africa** remains the sole outlier granting a DABUS patent in 2021 solely because its depository registry conducts *formal-only* checks without substantive legal examination.
- **India's Dual Refusal — Patent Inventorship (April 15, 2026) & Copyright Authorship (August 31, 2026):** Although **Section 2(1)(y)** of the Patents Act defines "true and first inventor" only by exclusion without explicitly saying "natural person," the **Indian Patent Office (April 15, 2026 order under Section 15)** refused Thaler's DABUS applications by reading Section 2(1)(y) together with **Section 6** to hold that statutory inventorship requires a **natural human being** (under appeal before the **Delhi High Court** as of late 2026; separately, in a Section 25(1) order, the Controller clarified that pre-grant opposition cannot be used to challenge inventorship). Extending this human-centric boundary to copyright, the **Registrar of Copyrights (Indian Copyright Office, New Delhi)** issued a parallel order on **August 31, 2026** rejecting **DABUS as an author under Section 2(d)(vi) of the Copyright Act, 1957** (for the AI-generated artwork *"A Recent Entrance to Paradise"*)—holding that authorship of a computer-generated work vests strictly in *"the person who causes the work to be created"*, which must be a natural or juristic person recognized in law. Thus, both Indian patent inventorship and copyright authorship formally exclude autonomous AI systems—while **AI-*assisted* inventions remain fully patentable** globally (reaffirmed by USPTO's revised November 2025 guidance) whenever a human researcher contributes significantly to conceiving the claimed invention.

### Generative AI Training Data versus Copyright - Fair Dealing and DPIIT's 2025 Proposal

Training frontier Large Language Models (LLMs) requires ingesting billions of copyrighted books, news reports, academic papers, images, and code—splitting global jurisdictions into three rival paradigms:

- **United States:** Litigates AI training case-by-case under flexible four-factor **Fair Use** (2025–26 federal rulings like *Bartz v. Anthropic* and *Kadrey v. Meta* probing transformative non-expressive statistical learning while penalizing pirated shadow libraries and direct market substitution).
- **European Union & Japan:** The EU DSM Directive provides a **Text and Data Mining (TDM)** exception subject to explicit machine-readable **rightsholder opt-outs**, reinforced by the EU AI Act's mandatory training-data summary disclosures; Japan allows broad TDM ingestion unless it unreasonably prejudices copyright holders.
- **India's Collision — Delhi HC *ANI v. OpenAI* (July 2026) vs. DPIIT's *One Nation One License One Payment* (Dec 2025):** India's **Copyright Act, 1957** contains **no express TDM or AI-training provision** and relies on a narrow, closed statutory list of **"Fair Dealing"** exceptions under **Section 52(1)(a)** (private/personal use including research, criticism, review, news reporting). Two landmark Indian developments crystallized a sharp policy divergence:
  1. **DPIIT Working Paper (*Generative AI and Copyright — Part 1*, December 8, 2025):** A government-constituted DPIIT committee concluded that unauthorized ingestion of copyrighted content for commercial AI training infringes copyright and proposed a **hybrid mandatory blanket statutory license**—titled ***"One Nation One License One Payment"***—under which AI developers receive lawful access to all lawfully accessible Indian works in exchange for centrally collected, revenue-linked, tariff-determined royalties administered by a proposed Central Government–designated non-profit umbrella body, the **CRCAT (*Copyright Royalties Collective for AI Training*)**, for distribution to rightsholders without permitting paralyzing opt-outs.
  2. **Delhi High Court Interim Judgment (*ANI Media Pvt. Ltd. v. OpenAI OpCo LLC*, July 24, 2026):** Dismissing news agency ANI's interim injunction plea (while the full suit proceeds to trial), the Delhi High Court provisionally brought statistical AI model training within **Section 52(1)(a) fair dealing** ("research" / non-expressive pattern extraction serving public interest)—creating a live tension between judicial fair-dealing pragmatism and DPIIT's proposed statutory royalty architecture awaiting legislative resolution.

### Brain–Computer Interfaces (BCIs) and Neurorights

Invasive cortical implants (e.g., Neuralink, Synchron) and non-invasive consumer EEG headbands/earbuds now decode raw electrical brain activity using AI into synthesized speech, robotic prosthesis control, focus metrics, and latent emotional states. Because the human brain is the final physical sanctuary of unexpressed thought, neurotechnology introduces risks fundamentally distinct from conventional data privacy—including **neuro-surveillance, covert emotional/neuromarketing manipulation, workplace cognitive monitoring, and neural hacking**.

To safeguard mental sovereignty, neuroethicists **Marcelo Ienca and Roberto Andorno (2017)** articulated **four foundational Neurorights**:

1. **Cognitive Liberty:** Mental self-determination—the freedom to alter one's own mental states via neurotech or strictly refuse unsolicited cognitive intervention.
2. **Mental Privacy:** Absolute protection against unconsented reading, extraction, decoding, or commercialization of neural signals (**neurodata**).
3. **Mental Integrity:** Protection of brain circuitry against unauthorized malicious interference, adversarial BCI hacking, or coercive neuro-modulation.
4. **Psychological Continuity:** Preserving personal identity, agency, and autobiographical coherence against unintended behavioral or personality drift induced by closed-loop neuro-implants.

**Global Milestones and India's Statutory Gap (2021–2026):**

- **Chile's Constitutional Precedent (2021 & 2023):** **Chile** became the **first country in the world** to amend its Constitution (2021) explicitly protecting brain activity and neural data, judicially enforced in a historic **2023 Chilean Supreme Court ruling** ordering consumer-EEG company *Emotiv* to erase a user's stored brainwaves.
- **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology (Adopted November 11, 2025):** Building on the OECD's 2019 principles, **UNESCO's 43rd General Conference (194 Member States)** adopted by acclamation the world's **first global normative standard for neurotechnology**. Though non-binding, it formally distinguishes raw *"neurodata"* from cognitive-inference data, enshrines **mental privacy and cognitive liberty**, restricts non-therapeutic commercial/workplace neuro-monitoring, and calls for strict safeguards protecting children's developing brains.
- **India's Regulatory Vacuum:** India's **Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023** consciously eliminated the concept of *"Sensitive Personal Data"* and contains **no distinct classification or heightened protection for neural data**—exposing consumer neuro-wearables to standard broad consent and research/state exemptions. Closing this gap requires anchoring neurorights within **Article 21** (*Justice K.S. Puttaswamy* informational privacy and mental autonomy), establishing CDSCO/ICMR safety standards for non-clinical consumer BCIs, and enacting dedicated neurodata guardrails.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **IPAB No Longer Exists (2019 PYQ Trap Updated):** While UPSC Prelims 2019 treated *"no IPAB"* as false, the **Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 abolished the IPAB**—appeals now go directly to **High Courts** (IP Divisions).
> - **Not All 8 IPRs Sit Under DPIIT:** Patents, Trademarks, Designs, GIs, and Copyright sit under **DPIIT (Commerce)**, *but* **PPV&FR** sits under **Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare** and **SICLD (Chip Layouts)** sits under **MeitY**.
> - **Section 3(j) Micro-Organism Carve-Out (2019 Prelims PYQ):** Plants, animals, plant varieties, seeds, and essentially biological processes are **not patentable** in India—*however*, **non-natural / genetically engineered micro-organisms ARE patentable** under Section 3(j).
> - **Trade Secrets Still Lack a Codified Statute:** India does **not** yet have a enacted Trade Secrets Act; the **22nd Law Commission's 289th Report (March 2024)** only proposed a *Draft Protection of Trade Secrets Bill, 2024*.
> - **Biological Diversity Act 2023 Split (Updating 2012 Prelims PYQ):** Under the amended **Section 6 (effective April 2024)**, **foreign / Sec 3(2) entities** still need **prior NBA approval** before patent grant, whereas **Indian entities (Sec 7)** only **register** with the NBA before patent grant and seek prior approval at **commercialization**; BD Rules 2024 also bring **Digital Sequence Information (DSI)** under NBA ambit.
> - **WIPO GRATK Treaty (May 2024) Status & Sanction Limit:** Adopted **May 24, 2024**, requiring **15 ratifications** to enter into force (**not yet in force** as of Nov 2026); critically, it **forbids revoking a patent solely for non-disclosure** unless *fraudulent intent* is proven.
> - **Section 84 vs. Section 92 Waiting Period:** General CL (**Section 84**) requires a **3-year wait** after patent grant; national emergency / public non-commercial CL (**Section 92**) can be invoked **at any time** with zero wait.
> - **SCOMET Licensing Split & Category 7 (Sept 2025):** Not all SCOMET categories are licensed by DGFT—**Category 0** (nuclear) is licensed by **DAE** and **Category 6** (munitions, including DEWs) by **DDP (MoD)**. In **September 2025**, DGFT populated previously "Reserved" **Category 7** for **Emerging Technologies** ($\ge 34$-qubit quantum computers, $\le 45\text{ nm}$ lithography, cryogenic CMOS, vacuum metal 3D printers).
> - **DABUS Dual Indian Refusal (Patent & Copyright) & CRCAT Acronym:** Major examining offices (US, UK SC 2023, EPO, Australia) require a human inventor; in India, both **Patent inventorship** (IPO order under Sec 15, **April 15, 2026**) and **Copyright authorship** (Registrar of Copyrights order under Sec 2(d)(vi), **August 31, 2026**) have formally rejected **DABUS**, while **South Africa** alone granted a DABUS patent due to its non-examining depository system. Under DPIIT's December 8, 2025 *"One Nation One License One Payment"* Working Paper, the proposed central non-profit umbrella body collecting and distributing AI-training royalties is named **CRCAT (*Copyright Royalties Collective for AI Training*)**.
> - **Neurorights & DPDP Act 2023 Trap:** **Chile** (2021) was the first country to constitutionalize neurorights and **UNESCO** adopted its non-binding **Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology in November 2025**; India's **DPDP Act, 2023 does NOT classify neural data as a special sensitive category**.

> [!info] Mains Arsenal
> - **The Patent Filing vs. Commercialization Paradox (Direct 2024 GS3 Hit):** India crossed **1,00,000+ annual patent grants (FY 2023–24)** and surged to **~38th–39th in WIPO's Global Innovation Index**, yet commercial utilization remains low (<10–15% academic patents) due to weak university–industry Technology Transfer Offices (TTOs), academic "publish-or-patent" metric gaming, capital shortages across the TRL 4–7 "valley of death," and fragmented IP valuation—directly addressed by **ANRF**, the **₹1 lakh crore RDI scheme**, and **Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024**.
> - **Public Health Sovereignty vs. Innovation Trade-Off (GS3 / GS2):** Defend India's balanced TRIPS architecture: **Section 3(d)** (*Novartis Glivec* 2013; *Bedaquiline* 2023) targets *incremental evergreening*, not genuine breakthrough molecules; **Section 84** (*Natco–Bayer* 2012, India's sole CL) shows disciplined restraint rather than arbitrary expropriation; **Section 107A Bolar** ensures Day-1 generic entry—making India indispensable to Global South health equity while still climbing into original biologics under **Biopharma SHAKTI**.
> - **SEP / FRAND & Strategic Tech Credibility:** Cite **Delhi HC's *Ericsson v. Lava* (March 2024, ₹244 crore)** alongside **DGFT's September 2025 SCOMET Category 7** (quantum/sub-45nm chips) and **WMD Amendment Act 2022** (proliferation financing) to show India maturing from an IP "rule-taker" into a trusted, rule-enforcing deep-tech partner for **iCET / TRUST** semiconductor, quantum, and defense co-development.
> - **Generative AI Copyright Governance — Charting India's Third Way:** Contrast **US Fair Use litigation**, **EU opt-out TDM**, **Delhi HC's *ANI v. OpenAI* (July 2026)** interim fair-dealing ruling, and **DPIIT's Dec 2025 *"One Nation One License One Payment"*** statutory royalty proposal administered via **CRCAT (*Copyright Royalties Collective for AI Training*)**—arguing that a transparent, tiered licensing framework must simultaneously prevent foreign Big Tech free-riding on Indian journalism/vernacular corpora *and* avoid pricing out domestic sovereign startups (*BharatGen, Sarvam*).
> - **Anticipatory Neuro-Governance (GS4 / GS3):** Pair **UNESCO's Nov 2025 Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology** and **Chile's 2021/2023 neurorights precedent** with **Article 21 (*Puttaswamy*)** to argue for amending India's **DPDP Rules** to carve out high-tier protections for **neurodata**, preventing cognitive surveillance before consumer BCIs scale.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Statutory Time-Warp Traps:** UPSC tests live post-amendment law against outdated coaching notes—watch for statements assuming **IPAB still exists** (abolished 2021), **Form 27 is still filed annually** (now once in 3 years under 2024 Rules), **RFE is still 48 months** (cut to 31 months), or **Indian entities still need prior NBA approval before filing a patent** (2023 BD Act shifted Indian entities to registration before grant + prior approval at commercialization).
- **Absolute Exclusion Swaps under Section 3:** Turning *"micro-organisms are patentable"* into *"no living organism can be patented"*, or claiming *"software can never be patented in India"* (ignoring the technical-effect exception to *"computer programme per se"*).
- **International Treaty Over-Claims:** Asserting that the **May 2024 WIPO GRATK Treaty** is already in force or mandates automatic patent revocation for non-disclosure, or claiming **UNESCO's Nov 2025 Neurotechnology Recommendation** is a legally binding treaty.
- **SCOMET & IPR Nodal Ministry Inversions:** Misattributing **PPV&FR** (MoA&FW) or **SICLD** (MeitY) to DPIIT, or attributing **SCOMET Category 0** (DAE) and **Category 6** (DDP, MoD) licensing to DGFT.
