# Chapter 2 - Company Statehood — Diwani Extraction, Statutory Charter Acts, Conquests & Early Colonial Aesthetics (1765–1849)

Between 1765 and 1849, the English East India Company mutated from a coastal joint-stock enterprise into a continental bureaucratic autocracy stretching from the Khyber approaches to the Irrawaddy delta. This transformation ran along four converging axes: ruthless fiscal extraction in Bengal, Westminster’s progressive statutory capture of Company governance through the Charter Acts, the systematic military-diplomatic dismantling of every surviving regional rival, and the projection of imperial legitimacy through Palladian urbanism and documentary art.

---

## Dual Government, the 1770 Famine and Early Revenue Experimentation (1765–1772)

### Clive’s Dual Government and "Plassey Plunder"

Following the Treaty of Allahabad (1765), **Robert Clive** deliberately avoided direct British administration in Bengal. Lacking trained European personnel and fearing both indigenous revolt and British parliamentary scrutiny, he constructed the **Dual Government (1765–1772)**—a structural separation of fiscal authority from civic responsibility.

Under this arrangement, the Company held the ***Diwani*** (land-revenue collection and civil justice), while the puppet Nawab (**Najm-ud-daulah**) nominally retained the ***Nizamat*** (defense, police, and criminal justice). Yet because the Company controlled the army and forced the Nawab to delegate *Nizamat* execution to two Company-selected Indian deputies—**Muhammad Reza Khan** (Bengal) and **Raja Shitab Rai** (Bihar), who simultaneously served as the Company’s *Naib Diwans*—the EIC exercised total **power without responsibility**, leaving the Nawab with **responsibility without power**.

**The "Plassey Plunder" and the *Nabob* Paradox:** Between 1757 and 1772, Bengal endured unrestrained mercantile-fiscal pillage. Company servants coerced artisans through native agents (***Gomasthas***) enforcing punitive advance contracts (***Dadan***), monopolized internal staples (salt, betel nut, tobacco) via Clive’s **Society of Trade (1765)**, and remitted millions of pounds sterling back to England—earning opulent returning officials the scornful label **"Nabobs"** as they purchased rotten-borough seats in Westminster. Paradoxically, while individual servants amassed colossal private fortunes, rampant corruption and war costs pushed the **corporate EIC treasury to near-bankruptcy by 1772**, compelling the Directors to beg the British Parliament for a £1 million emergency bailout loan and handing Westminster its long-awaited pretext for statutory intervention.

### The Great Bengal Famine of 1770 (*Chhiyattorer Monnontor*)

The institutional rot of Dual Government culminated in the catastrophic **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**, which killed an estimated **10 million people**—roughly one-third of Bengal’s population—and depopulated entire northern districts across Bengal and Bihar. Though precipitated by the 1769 monsoon failure, its lethal scale was overwhelmingly man-made:

- **Predatory Tax Inflexibility:** Rather than suspending land revenue during crop failure, the Company **increased** actual land-revenue collections from ₹1.52 crore in 1768–69 to ₹1.57 crore in 1771, levying an extraordinary 10% surcharge (***Najai***) on surviving peasants to recover the unpaid arrears of dead neighbors.
- **Hoarding and Cash-Crop Distortion:** Company servants and their *banias* speculated aggressively in scarce rice stocks while forcing cultivators to divert food-grain acreage toward commercial opium and indigo—inaugurating the colonial pattern of **entitlement-failure famines** that recurred across the 18th and 19th centuries.
- **Long-Term Ecological Scar:** Massive peasant mortality turned nearly one-third of Bengal’s arable acreage into jungle, triggering the **Sanyasi–Fakir uprisings** (Ch 4) and exposing Dual Government as financially untenable even for London’s shareholders.

### Warren Hastings’ Administrative Overhaul and *Izaredari* Farming

Appointed Governor of Bengal in **1772**, **Warren Hastings** liquidated Dual Government on direct orders from London to "stand forth as *Diwan*." He abolished the posts of *Naib Diwans* (prosecuting Reza Khan and Shitab Rai, though both were later acquitted), transferred the imperial treasury (*Khalsa*) from Murshidabad to **Calcutta**, slashed the Nawab’s pension by half to ₹16 lakh, and established a **Board of Revenue** alongside European **District Collectors**.

Simultaneously, Hastings constructed Bengal’s foundational colonial court architecture (1772): each district received a ***Mofussil Diwani Adalat*** (civil court presided over by the European Collector applying Hindu/Muslim personal laws assisted by *pandits* and *maulvis*) and a ***Mofussil Faujdari Adalat*** (criminal court run by Indian *Qazis* and *Muftis* under European oversight), with apex appellate courts—***Sadar Diwani Adalat*** and ***Sadar Nizamat Adalat***—located in Calcutta.

**Failure of the *Izaredari* Revenue Farming System (1772–1777):** To maximize cash predictability after the 1770 famine, Hastings launched the **Quinquennial (5-Year) *Izaredari* Settlement (1772)**—auctioning land-revenue collection rights to the highest bidders (***Ijaradars*** / revenue farmers). Displacing hereditary *zamindars* with speculative urban contractors backfired catastrophically: overbidders mercilessly rack-rented cultivators to recoup inflated bids, abandoned estates when arrears mounted, and precipitated widespread agrarian flight. Forced to concede failure, Hastings replaced the five-year lease with **Annual Settlements (1777)** favoring hereditary *zamindars*—setting the stage for Cornwallis’s Permanent Settlement (Ch 3).

## Parliamentary Oversight and the Statutory Charter Acts (1773–1853)

Over eight decades, British Parliament executed a methodical, step-by-step constitutional takeover of the East India Company. Two metropolitan currents propelled these statutes: initial political anxiety over corrupt *Nabobs* and corporate insolvency (1773–1786), followed by **British Industrial Revolution** manufacturers demanding free trade and **Benthamite-Utilitarian** reformers pushing administrative centralization (1813–1853).

### Regulating Act of 1773 and the Amending Fixes (1781, 1786)

- **Regulating Act of 1773:** The first direct parliamentary intervention regulating the Company’s political and administrative structure in India. It elevated the Governor of Bengal (**Warren Hastings**) to **Governor-General of Bengal** assisted by a **four-member Executive Council** operating by majority vote—giving Hastings only a tie-breaking casting vote, which enabled hostile councillors (**Philip Francis**, Clavering, and Monson) to paralyze his administration for years. It partially subordinated Bombay and Madras Presidencies to Bengal in war and peace (except during "urgent emergencies"), barred Company servants from private trade or accepting bribes, and mandated the Court of Directors to submit revenue, civil, and military correspondence to the British government.
- **Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774) and Jurisdictional Chaos:** The 1773 Act established a Crown **Supreme Court at Fort William (1774)** with Chief Justice **Sir Elijah Impey** and three puisne judges (Chambers, Le Maistre, Hyde). Because the statute left unclear whether the Court held jurisdiction over Indian *zamindars*, Company revenue collectors, or the Executive Council itself, bitter executive-judicial warfare erupted—notoriously stained by the **Raja Nandkumar trial (1775)** (the first execution under English forgery law in India, widely condemned as judicial murder engineered through the Hastings–Impey alliance) and the *Patna* and *Cossijurah* jurisdictional clashes.
- **Act of Settlement / Amending Act of 1781 (Bengal Judicature Act):** Enacted on Edmund Burke’s parliamentary scrutiny to terminate the 1773 executive-judicial deadlock. It **exempted the Governor-General-in-Council and EIC servants from Supreme Court jurisdiction for official acts**, completely **excluded land-revenue matters** from the Supreme Court’s purview, confined Supreme Court territorial jurisdiction to Calcutta inhabitants (mandating application of Hindu/Muslim personal laws to native defendants), and recognized the Governor-General’s ***Sadar Diwani Adalat*** as the final court of appeal from provincial *Mofussil* courts.
- **Pitt’s India Act of 1784:** Architect William Pitt the Younger separated the Company’s **commercial** and **political** functions via a **"Double Government"** in London: the **Court of Directors** retained commerce and patronage, while a newly constituted six-member Crown **Board of Control** (headed by a Cabinet minister, including the Chancellor of the Exchequer and Secretary of State) assumed supreme supervision over all civil, military, and revenue affairs, transmitting secret orders via a 3-member **Secret Committee** of Directors. For the first time, Indian territories were statutorily designated **"British possessions in India"**. The Governor-General’s Council was trimmed from **four to three** (including the Commander-in-Chief), Bombay and Madras were strictly subordinated to Bengal in war, revenue, and diplomacy, and Clause 34 formally prohibited aggressive schemes of territorial conquest (a clause soon treated as dead letter).
- **Act of 1786 (Cornwallis Amendment):** Passed explicitly to satisfy **Lord Cornwallis’s** two preconditions before sailing to India: it vested the Governor-General with statutory power to **override his Council’s majority** in extraordinary matters upon taking personal responsibility, and authorized him to concurrently serve as **Commander-in-Chief**.

### The Four Twenty-Year Charter Acts (1793, 1813, 1833, 1853)

- **Charter Act of 1793:** Renewed the EIC’s commercial monopoly for **20 years** and generalized the 1786 override veto to all future Governors-General and Presidency Governors. Crucially, it ordered that the **salaries of the Board of Control and its London staff be paid out of Indian revenues**—an extractive charge maintained until 1919 that became a classic pillar of the "Drain of Wealth". It authorized the EIC to issue "country trade" licenses to private traders (powering the China opium boom), obliged the Company to pay £500,000 annually to the British Exchequer after expenses, and institutionalized rule through codified written laws rather than personal discretion.
- **Charter Act of 1813:** Passed under intense lobbying from British industrialists shut out of Europe by Napoleon’s Continental System and inspired by Adam Smith’s *laissez-faire* economics. It **ended the EIC’s trade monopoly in India**, opening the Indian market to private British merchants, while preserving the Company’s monopoly strictly over the **tea trade and trade with China** for 20 years. It explicitly **asserted the undoubted sovereignty of the British Crown** over Company territories (while leaving Indian revenues managed by the Company under Board of Control supervision—*not* directly controlled by Parliament). Socially and fiscally, it required separate commercial and territorial accounts, legally admitted licensed **Christian missionaries** (establishing an Anglican Bishopric at Calcutta), earmarked **₹1 lakh annually** for the revival of literature and promotion of science among Indians (left unspent until 1823), and empowered local governments to impose municipal taxes.
- **Charter Act of 1833 (Saint Helena Act):** Representing the high-water mark of **Benthamite-Utilitarian centralization**, it completely **terminated ALL commercial functions of the EIC** (stripping away tea and China monopolies), transforming it into a purely administrative body holding Indian territories *"in trust for His Majesty, His heirs and successors"*. It redesignated the Governor-General of Bengal as the **Governor-General of India** (**Lord William Bentinck** becoming the first incumbent), stripped Madras and Bombay Governors of all legislative authority (centralizing law-making exclusively in Calcutta; laws henceforth were termed **Acts**, whereas earlier enactments were **Regulations**), added a fourth non-executive **Law Member** to the Council (**Lord Thomas Babington Macaulay**) solely for legislative meetings, and instituted the **First Indian Law Commission (1834, chaired by Macaulay)** to codify civil and criminal statutes. It legalized unrestricted European settlement and property ownership in India, mandated measures to abolish **slavery** (enacted via **Act V of 1843** under Ellenborough), and inserted the landmark **Section 87** declaring that no native of India could be barred from public office by reason of religion, place of birth, descent, or color—though its attempt to introduce open ICS competition was blocked by the Court of Directors.
- **Charter Act of 1853:** The last Charter Act conspicuously omitted any 20-year renewal guarantee—allowing the EIC to hold Indian territories **indefinitely at the pleasure of Parliament**, signaling imminent Crown takeover. For the first time, it **separated the legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General’s Council: making the Law Member a full executive member and expanding the legislative wing with **six new Legislative Councillors** (Chief Justice and one judge of Calcutta Supreme Court, plus four salaried official nominees from Bengal, Madras, Bombay, and the North-Western Provinces)—creating the **Indian (Central) Legislative Council** (a 12-member "mini-Parliament" featuring local provincial representation for the first time, though still containing **zero Indians**). It finally stripped the Directors of recruitment patronage by introducing **open competitive examination** for the Covenanted Civil Service (designed by the **Macaulay Committee of 1854**), shrank the Court of Directors from 24 to 18 (with 6 Crown-nominated), and authorized a separate **Lieutenant-Governor for Bengal** to relieve the Governor-General of local provincial duties.

*Constitutional Lineage Context:* Collectively, these statutes forged the centralized executive, legislative-executive separation, unified judiciary, and meritocratic civil service whose long-term constitutional evolution into the 1950 Republic is analyzed in the `Polity` hub.

## Territorial Expansion Doctrines and the Subjugation of Regional Powers (1765–1849)

### Strategic Doctrines - Ring-Fence, Subsidiary Alliance and Paramountcy

British territorial conquest across India was neither accidental nor uniformly paced; it unfolded through three evolving geopolitical doctrines calibrating imperial ambition against financial cost:

- **Policy of Ring-Fence (1765–1813, Warren Hastings):** Built around defending Company frontiers by defending neighboring buffer states—chiefly **Awadh** and the Carnatic—at the neighbors’ own financial cost. By keeping Marathas, Mysore, and Afghans away from Bengal’s borders through self-funded buffers, Hastings minimized military overhead while avoiding direct administrative annexation.
- **Subsidiary Alliance System (1798–1805, Lord Wellesley):** Arriving amid **Napoleon Bonaparte’s 1798 Egyptian expedition** and fears of a Franco-Indian alliance, Wellesley inverted Ring-Fence into aggressive **"Empire by Proxy"** (adapting Dupleix’s 1740s prototype). Any prince signing a Subsidiary Alliance accepted five interlocking shackles:
  1. Permanent stationing of a **British subsidiary military contingent** inside the state.
  2. Payment of an annual cash **subsidy**—or outright cession of fertile territory—to fund that British force.
  3. Disbandment of the ruler’s own army and acceptance of a **British Resident** at court.
  4. Total surrender of **foreign relations**—forbidding wars, negotiations with other Indian powers, or hiring non-British Europeans without Company consent.
  5. A British guarantee of protection against external invasion and internal revolt, paired with an illusory pledge of "non-interference" in internal administration.
  - **Strategic Payoff & Structural Decay:** Subsidiary Alliance enabled the EIC to maintain an immense standing army **entirely at Indian rulers’ expense**, neutralize French influence, fight future wars exclusively on allied or enemy soil, and assert British paramountcy. For Indian states, it proved fatal: stripped of sovereignty yet shielded by British bayonets from popular revolt, princes lost every incentive for good governance, while crushing subsidy demands drove states into bankruptcy and administrative decay—later handing Dalhousie the pretext for outright annexation.
  - **Chronological Sequence of Adoption:** **Hyderabad (1798)** [first formal Wellesley treaty] → **Mysore (1799)** → **Tanjore (1799)** → **Awadh (1801, ceding Rohilkhand and Lower Doab)** → **Peshwa (Treaty of Bassein, Dec 1802)** → **Bhonsle (Dec 1803)** & **Scindia (Dec 1803)** → **Holkar** and **Rajput States (Jaipur, Jodhpur, Udaipur, Bundi, Macheri, 1818 under Lord Hastings)**.
- **Policy of Paramountcy / Subordinate Isolation (1813–1823, Lord Hastings):** Discarding the diplomatic fiction of negotiating with Indian kingdoms as legal equals, Hastings crushed the Maratha Confederacy and Pindaris, formally proclaiming the Company the supreme **paramount sovereign** across the subcontinent whose rights superseded all native treaties.

### The Four Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–1799)

Mysore under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan represented the EIC’s most formidable ideological and military adversary—a centralized, European-drilled military-fiscal state blocking British control over peninsular spice commerce.

- **First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769):** Facing an anti-Mysore Triple Alliance (EIC, Marathas, Nizam), **Hyder Ali** neutralized the Marathas with cash, won over the Nizam, and launched lightning cavalry thrusts straight to the outskirts of Madras. Cornered, the Madras Council signed the humiliating **Treaty of Madras (April 1769)**—exchanging prisoners/conquests and pledging mutual defensive aid (which the British cynically breached when Marathas invaded Mysore in 1771).
- **Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784):** Provoked by the British seizure of the French Malabar port of **Mahe** (under Hyder’s protection) during the American Revolutionary War, Hyder forged a counter-alliance with the Marathas and Nizam, obliterating Colonel Baillie’s detachment at the **Battle of Pollilur (1780)** using iron-cased rockets. **Warren Hastings** detached the Nizam and Marathas diplomatically while **Sir Eyre Coote** checked Hyder at **Porto Novo (1781)**. After Hyder died of cancer (Dec 1782), **Tipu Sultan** fought British troops to a standstill, compelling the **Treaty of Mangalore (March 1784)**—mutual restoration of territories and the last treaty an Indian power dictated to the British on equal terms.
- **Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792):** Triggered when Tipu attacked British ally Travancore over disputes around Cranganore and Ayakottah forts. **Lord Cornwallis** personally took command, mobilized the **Triple Alliance (EIC + Marathas + Nizam)**, captured Bangalore, and besieged Seringapatam. Forced into the crippling **Treaty of Seringapatam (March 1792)**, Tipu surrendered **half his kingdom** (Malabar, Dindigul, and Baramahal to EIC; northern tracts to Marathas and Nizam), owed **₹3 crore war indemnity**, and handed over two of his sons as hostages until payment cleared—a classic application of Cornwallis’s maxim to *"cripple our enemy without making our friends too formidable."*
- **Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799):** Arriving with Napoleonic invasion paranoia, **Lord Wellesley** seized on Tipu’s embassies to revolutionary France, Mauritius, and Kabul and his rejection of Subsidiary Alliance. Launching converging armies under General Harris and Arthur Wellesley (future Duke of Wellington), British forces breached **Seringapatam on May 4, 1799**, where Tipu died fighting sword-in-hand at the Water Gate. The EIC annexed Kanara, Coimbatore, and Wynad (linking Madras coast-to-coast), handed marginal districts to the Nizam, and placed minor **Krishnaraja III Wodeyar** onto a shrunken Mysore throne shackled by Subsidiary Alliance (later taken under direct British Commissionership by Bentinck from 1831 to 1881).

### The Three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818)

Despite Panipat (1761), the Marathas revived dramatically under Peshwa Madhavrao I (1761–1772), restoring Shah Alam II to Delhi (1772). Their eventual downfall stemmed from chronic **confederate fratricide** exploited methodically by British diplomacy.

- **First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782):** Triggered when **Raghunathrao (Raghoba)** murdered Peshwa Narayanrao to seize Pune, only to be expelled by the ***Barabhai* regency council** headed by **Nana Phadnavis** supporting infant Peshwa Madhavrao II. Coveting Salsette and Bassein, the Bombay Presidency unilaterally signed the **Treaty of Surat (1775)** backing Raghoba; Warren Hastings’ Calcutta Council disavowed it and signed the **Treaty of Purandhar (1776)**, before London overruled Calcutta and resumed war. **Mahadji Scindia** trapped and humiliated the Bombay army at the **Battle and Convention of Wadgaon (1779)**, forcing Hastings to dispatch Bengal reinforcements under Generals Goddard and Popham (who stormed Ahmedabad and Gwalior). Both sides settled on the **Treaty of Salbai (May 1782)**, brokered by Mahadji Scindia: the EIC kept **Salsette**, dropped Raghoba on a pension, and recognized Madhavrao II. Strategically, Salbai’s **20 years of Anglo-Maratha peace** proved disastrously asymmetric—granting Britain a free hand to destroy Mysore while the Maratha chiefs fragmented internally.
- **Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805):** The death of **Nana Phadnavis (1800)** removed the last unifying statesman of the confederacy. When **Yashwantrao Holkar** routed the combined armies of Peshwa **Baji Rao II** and Daulat Rao Scindia at the **Battle of Poona (October 1802)**, the panicked Peshwa fled to British protection and signed the fatal **Treaty of Bassein (December 31, 1802)**—accepting a Subsidiary Alliance that surrendered Maratha sovereignty and gave the EIC direct military access to Pune. Outraged yet fatalistically disunited, Scindia and Bhonsle attacked without Holkar: **Arthur Wellesley** shattered them in the Deccan at **Assaye** and **Argaon (1803)**, while **Lord Lake** routed Scindia’s French-trained brigades in the north at **Laswari** and occupied **Delhi and Agra** (making Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II a British pensioner permanently). Both signed crushing subsidiary treaties—**Treaty of Deogaon (Dec 1803, Bhonsle ceding Cuttack/Orissa)** and **Treaty of Surji-Arjangaon (Dec 1803, Scindia ceding the Ganga–Yamuna Doab, Delhi–Agra, and Broach)**. Fighting alone with brilliant guerrilla cavalry, **Yashwantrao Holkar** mauled Colonel Monson’s force and resisted successfully until Wellesley’s recall for war extravagance produced the conciliatory **Treaty of Rajpurghat (Dec 1805)** restoring Holkar’s lands.
- **Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818):** **Lord Hastings’** massive encirclement campaign against the **Pindaris** (demobilized irregular horsemen attached to Maratha camps, led by Chitu, Karim Khan, and Amir Khan) deliberately violated Maratha sovereignty, triggering a desperate, poorly coordinated final Maratha uprising. British columns crushed **Peshwa Baji Rao II** at **Khadki / Kirkee (Nov 1817)**, Koregaon, and Ashti; **Appa Saheb Bhonsle** at **Sitabuldi (Nov 1817)**; and **Holkar** at **Mahidpur (Dec 1817)**. Through the **Treaty of Poona (June 1817)**, **Treaty of Gwalior (Nov 1817, binding Scindia)**, and **Treaty of Mandsaur (Jan 1818, subordinating Holkar)**, the **Maratha Confederacy was permanently dissolved**. The **Peshwaship was abolished**—Baji Rao II exiled to **Bithur** (near Kanpur) on an ₹8 lakh pension—most Pune dominions were absorbed into the expanded **Bombay Presidency**, and a small token kingdom of **Satara** was carved out for Shivaji’s descendant **Pratap Singh** to placate Maratha sentiment.

### Annexation of Sindh (1843)

Strategically positioned astride the lower **Indus River**, the Talpur Amirs of Sindh fell victim to British **"Great Game"** Russian paranoia, commercial appetite for Indus river navigation, and imperial prestige-salvaging after the First Afghan War catastrophe. Although the EIC had concluded **Treaties of Eternal Friendship (1809, 1832)** explicitly prohibiting the transport of British troops or military stores along the Indus, Lord Auckland blatantly violated these clauses during the Afghan invasion and imposed a Subsidiary Treaty (1839). Seeking an easy triumph, Governor-General **Lord Ellenborough** dispatched **Sir Charles Napier**, who ruthlessly provoked the Amirs into resistance, defeated them at **Miani** and **Dabo (1843)**, and annexed Sindh outright—confessing candidly in his own diary: *"We have no right to seize Sind, yet we shall do so, and a very advantageous, useful, humane piece of rascality it will be"* (immortalized in *Punch* magazine’s Latin pun *"Peccavi"*—"I have sinned").

### The Sikh Kingdom and Two Anglo-Sikh Wars (1809–1849)

Out of the 12 post-Mughal Sikh *Misls*, **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** of the **Sukerchakia *Misl*** forged the last great independent indigenous empire in India—capturing **Lahore (1799)**, proclaiming himself **Maharaja (1801)**, conquering Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), and Peshawar (1834), and securing the **Koh-i-Noor** from deposed Afghan monarch **Shah Shuja (1813)**.

- **Treaty of Amritsar (April 25, 1809):** Alarmed by Franco-Russian invasion scares following Napoleon’s Treaty of Tilsit (1807), **Lord Minto** dispatched **Charles Metcalfe** to Lahore. Facing British troop movements across the Sutlej, Ranjit Singh pragmatically signed the **Treaty of Amritsar**: fixing the **Sutlej River** as his permanent eastern frontier, bringing the **Cis-Sutlej Sikh states** (Patiala, Nabha, Jind) under British protection, and leaving Ranjit Singh unchallenged to expand north and west against the Afghans.
- **Ranjit Singh’s *Fauj-i-Ain* and *Fauj-i-Khas*:** Fully aware that Britain would eventually target Punjab, Ranjit Singh built India’s most modern indigenous military machine alongside a secular composite court (Foreign Minister **Fakir Azizuddin**, Finance Minister **Diwan Dina Nath**, Prime Minister **Raja Dhian Singh**). He divided his forces into the irregular feudal levies (*Fauj-i-Beqawaid*) and a salaried, European-drilled regular standing army (***Fauj-i-Ain***)—crowned by the elite Napoleonic-model brigade ***Fauj-i-Khas*** trained by European veterans **Jean-Baptiste Ventura** (infantry), **Jean-François Allard** (cavalry), **Claude Auguste Court**, and **Paolo Avitabile** (artillery).
- **First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–1846):** Ranjit Singh’s death (**June 1839**) plunged Lahore into violent succession bloodletting (Kharak Singh → Nau Nihal Singh → Sher Singh → five-year-old **Duleep Singh** under regent **Rani Jindan**), transferring real power to autonomous **Khalsa Army *Panchayats***. Provoked by British frontier military preparations under **Lord Hardinge**, the Khalsa crossed the Sutlej in Dec 1845. Despite ferocious Sikh infantry resistance, treasonous high commanders (**Prime Minister Lal Singh** and **Commander-in-Chief Tej Singh**) sabotaged operations across **Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal**, and **Sobraon (Feb 1846)**—where veteran **Sham Singh Attariwala** died rallying the line. The **Treaty of Lahore (March 9, 1846)** ceded the fertile Jalandhar Doab, slashed the Sikh army, posted a British Resident (**Henry Lawrence**), and demanded a crippling ₹1.5 crore indemnity; unable to pay, Lahore ceded **Kashmir**, which the British immediately **sold to Dogra chief Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh** via the separate **Treaty of Amritsar (March 16, 1846)**. Following unrest, the **Treaty of Bhairowal (Dec 1846)** deposed Rani Jindan and installed a British-presided Council of Regency, turning Punjab into a virtual protectorate.
- **Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849):** Sparked by **Diwan Mulraj’s** rebellion in Multan (April 1848) and escalated into a national Khalsa uprising under **Chattar Singh** and **Sher Singh Attariwala**. After a stalemate at **Ramnagar** and a bloodbath at **Chillianwala (Jan 1849)** that nearly ruined British Commander-in-Chief Lord Gough, overwhelming British heavy ordnance broke the Sikh army at the **Battle of Gujrat ("Battle of Guns", Feb 21, 1849)**. Governor-General **Lord Dalhousie** annexed Punjab outright (**March 29, 1849**), pensioned minor Duleep Singh to England (appropriating the **Koh-i-Noor** for Queen Victoria), dismantled *Fauj-i-Ain*, and administered Punjab via a three-man **Board of Administration** (Henry Lawrence, John Lawrence, Charles Mansel—replaced in 1853 by **John Lawrence** as sole Chief Commissioner).

### Frontier Buffer Wars (Nepal, Burma, Afghanistan) and Frontier Doctrine

Beyond the Gangetic-Peninsular heartland, the EIC secured the subcontinent’s natural mountain-and-maritime perimeter through three brutal frontier wars:

- **Anglo-Nepal War (1814–1816, Lord Hastings):** Expanding Gorkha control over Himalayan foothills clashed with British claims along the Terai frontier (Butwal and Sheoraj). After early British setbacks against Gorkha general **Amar Singh Thapa**, **General David Ochterlony** breached mountain defenses toward Kathmandu, compelling the **Treaty of Sagauli (March 1816)**: Nepal ceded **Garhwal and Kumaon** (giving Britain its premier hill stations—Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital), relinquished Terai claims, withdrew from **Sikkim**, accepted a British Resident at Kathmandu, and allowed **Gorkha recruitment** into the British Indian Army—while crucially preserving Nepal’s internal sovereignty as a Himalayan buffer.
- **First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826, Lord Amherst):** Aggressive Konbaung Burmese expansion into Assam, Manipur, Cachar, and Arakan (culminating in occupation of Shahpuri Island near Chittagong) triggered Britain’s costliest, most disease-ridden early campaign. Amphibious British capture of Rangoon and advance up the Irrawaddy forced the **Treaty of Yandabo (February 1826)**: Burma surrendered **Assam, Manipur, Cachar, Jaintia, Arakan, and Tenasserim**, paid ₹1 crore indemnity, and accepted a British Resident at Ava—formally pulling Northeast India into the colonial orbit. *(Note: Dalhousie annexed Lower Burma/Pegu in the Second War, 1852; Dufferin annexed Upper Burma in the Third War, 1885.)*
- **First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842) — "Forward Policy" vs. "Masterly Inactivity":** Gripped by **"Great Game" Russophobia**—fearing Tsarist expansion across Central Asia toward the Khyber Pass—Governor-General **Lord Auckland** rejected Amir **Dost Mohammad’s** pragmatic overtures (which asked for British help recovering Peshawar from Ranjit Singh) and pursued an aggressive **"Forward Policy."** Under the **Tripartite Treaty (1838)** with Ranjit Singh and deposed Afghan king **Shah Shuja**, British troops marched through Sindh and Bolan Pass to install Shah Shuja in Kabul (1839). Fierce Afghan tribal insurrection led by Dost Mohammad’s son **Akbar Khan** turned occupation into catastrophe: during the infamous **January 1842 Retreat from Kabul**, ~16,000 British-Indian troops and camp followers were annihilated in the snowbound passes, with **Dr. William Brydon** surviving as the lone European symbol reaching Jalalabad. **Lord Ellenborough** dispatched a brief punitive "Army of Retribution" to raze Kabul’s bazaar before evacuating Afghanistan and letting Dost Mohammad return to the throne.
  - **The Strategic Doctrinal Divide:** The 1842 disaster permanently defined Britain’s Northwest frontier debate between two opposing schools: the **Forward Policy** (Auckland 1838, revived disastrously by Lytton in 1878)—advocating pre-emptive diplomatic or military control beyond the Indus/Khyber to build an active anti-Russian glacis—versus **John Lawrence’s Policy of "Masterly Inactivity" (1864–1869)**, advocating strict non-interference in volatile Afghan civil wars, recognition of whichever strong Amir held Kabul, and defensive vigilance along the Indus so long as Russia stayed beyond the Oxus.

## Colonial Administrative Apparatus and Competing Governance Philosophies (1772–1849)

### The Cornwallis Code (1793) and Institutional Europeanization

Dispatched to cleanse Company governance of Hastings-era venality, **Lord Cornwallis (1786–1793)** embedded Whig constitutionalism into Bengal via the **Cornwallis Code (1793)**—anchored on three structural pillars:

- **Separation of Revenue from Justice (1787 Merger vs. 1793 Reversal):** Following Court of Directors instructions for administrative economy, Cornwallis initially **merged** revenue collection, civil justice (*Mofussil Diwani Adalat*), and magisterial powers into the District Collector under his **1787 Judicial Plan**. Realizing that concentrating such unchecked authority in one officer bred abuse and undermined impartial governance, he decisively reversed course in the **Cornwallis Code of 1793**—stripping **District Collectors** of all judicial and magisterial powers, confining them solely to revenue collection (while vesting civil justice and magistracy in a separate European District Judge-Magistrate), and crucially making Collectors **suable in civil courts** for illegal exactions. He erected a four-tier **Civil Court hierarchy** (*Munsiff/Ameen* → *Registrar* → **District *Diwani Adalat*** under a European Judge → **4 Provincial Courts of Appeal** at Calcutta, Dhaka, Murshidabad, and Patna → ***Sadar Diwani Adalat*** → King-in-Council) alongside four **European Circuit Courts** touring districts for criminal justice (replacing Indian *Faujdari* judges and culminating in *Sadar Nizamat Adalat*).
- **Formal "Rule of Law" with Racial Exception:** Cornwallis abolished barbaric mutilation punishments, codified court procedures, and asserted the sovereignty of written law over individual will—yet entrenched stark racial asymmetry by barring Indian-staffed or district criminal courts from trying **European British subjects** (who could only be tried by the Supreme Court at Calcutta).
- **Complete Racial Exclusion:** Convinced that *"every native of Hindustan is corrupt,"* Cornwallis barred Indians from every covenanted civil, judicial, and military post paying above **£500 per annum**—institutionalizing racial exclusion across the colonial state’s command heights for over a century.

### Evolution of the Covenanted Civil Services and the *Daroga* Police

- **Covenanted vs. Uncovenanted Services:** Acclaimed as the **"Father of the Indian Civil Service,"** Cornwallis converted commercial "writers" into a disciplined salaried bureaucracy by strictly prohibiting private trade and gratuities, dramatically inflating official salaries (making Bengal Collectors the world’s best-paid administrators), and enforcing promotion strictly by **seniority**. Elite British officials entering legal covenants with the London Directors formed the **Covenanted Civil Services**, whereas Indians were restricted to subordinate clerical ranks (**Uncovenanted Services**)—modestly widened under **Lord William Bentinck (1828–1835)** (who created Indian *Sadr Ameen* and *Deputy Collector* posts to curb fiscal deficits and abolished the cumbersome Provincial Courts of Appeal).
- **Training Institutions (Fort William College vs. Haileybury):** Recognizing that teenage British recruits arrived ignorant of Indian tongues and governance, **Lord Wellesley** founded **Fort William College at Calcutta (August 1800)** expressly **to train British civilians for administrative duties in India**—teaching vernaculars (Bengali, Hindustani), Persian, Sanskrit, and indigenous laws (employing scholars including William Carey). Alarmed by Wellesley’s expense and institutional autonomy, the Court of Directors did **not** endorse it—curtailing its civilian role in 1802 and founding the **East India College at Haileybury (England, 1806–1858)** to provide two years of metropolitan instruction before recruits sailed East, until the **Charter Act of 1853** and **Macaulay Committee (1854)** introduced open competitive exams.
- **The *Daroga* Police System (1791–1793):** Cornwallis stripped Bengal *zamindars* of traditional private policing authority and partitioned districts into ~20-square-mile circles (***Thanas***), each commanded by a salaried Indian police officer (***Daroga***) reporting to the European **District Magistrate**. In 1808, **Lord Minto** introduced divisional **Superintendents of Police (SP)** to coordinate anti-dacoity operations. Yet miserably underpaid *Darogas* swiftly became engines of rural extortion—colluding with the very *zamindars* they were meant to police—prompting **Bentinck (1829)** to abolish the SP post and re-concentrate revenue, magisterial, and police authority in the hands of the **Collector–Magistrate**, laying the groundwork for the 1861 Police Act overhaul (Ch 6).

### Romantic Paternalists vs. Cornwallis Legalism (Munro, Elphinstone, Metcalfe, Malcolm)

Cornwallis’s rigid, impersonal Whig-Bengal model drew fierce intellectual resistance from a remarkable cohort of early-19th-century field administrators governing newly conquered territories outside Bengal—**Sir Thomas Munro** (Madras), **Mountstuart Elphinstone** (Bombay and Deccan), **Sir Charles Metcalfe** (Delhi and North-Western Provinces), and **Sir John Malcolm** (Central India).

- **Core Tenets of Colonial Paternalism:** Viewing Cornwallis’s legalistic courts and absentee Permanent Settlement as destructive alien imports that empowered corrupt lawyers and urban moneylenders, the paternalists championed **personal, discretionary executive authority**—reuniting revenue, magisterial, and police powers in a single approachable **District Collector** modeled on an idealized benevolent Indian ruler (*Ma-Baap* governance).
- **Preservation of Indigenous Institutions:** They advocated direct peasant tenures (***Ryotwari* settlement** by Munro and Elphinstone, examined in Ch 3), revitalization of customary village ***Panchayats*** and hereditary headmen (*Patels*), and preservation of self-governing village communities—famously idealized by **Metcalfe** as India’s enduring **"little republics"** surviving dynastic storms.
- **Administrative Inclusion vs. Utilitarian Reform:** Unlike Cornwallis’s blanket racial contempt, Munro and Malcolm warned that excluding Indians from responsible public office morally degraded society and invited rebellion. By the 1830s, however, this conservative paternalism clashed directly with the **Benthamite-Utilitarian and Evangelical wave** under **Bentinck and Macaulay**, which prized standardized legal codification, Anglicist Westernization, and rapid social intervention—a three-way ideological tension (*Whig Legalism vs. Romantic Paternalism vs. Utilitarian Centralization*) that permanently defined British colonial governance.

## Embedded Early Colonial Aesthetics - Palladian Monumentality, the Picturesque and Company Painting (1770–1850)

### Neo-Classical and Palladian Civic Monumentality (Calcutta and Madras)

Once the EIC acquired territorial sovereignty, utilitarian brick trading *factories* gave way to **Imperial Neo-Classicism and English Palladianism**. Deploying Greco-Roman colonnades, triangular pediments, Corinthian porticoes, and symmetrical axial plans, Company engineers deliberately invoked classical Athens and Imperial Rome to project rational order, moral legitimacy, and civilizational permanence onto the colonial landscape. Lacking affordable local freestone, builders worked predominantly in brick coated with luminous white **Madras *chunam* (burnt shell-lime plaster)** polished to resemble marble, adapting European templates to tropical heat through deep pillared **verandas**, flat terraced roofs, louvered shutters, and lofty cross-ventilated interiors.

- **Calcutta ("City of Palaces"):**
  - **Writers’ Building (1780, designed by Thomas Lyon):** Originally constructed as an austere, repetitive Ionic-columned "civil barrack" housing 19 sets of apartments for junior EIC clerks (and later hosting classrooms for Fort William College, before being refaced in grand Greco-Roman red brick by E.J. Martin in 1880).
  - **St. John’s Church (1787, Lt. James Agg):** Modeled on James Gibbs’s St. Martin-in-the-Fields in London, inaugurating monumental stone-accented Neo-Classicism.
  - **Government House / Raj Bhavan (1799–1803, Capt. Charles Wyatt):** Commissioned by **Lord Wellesley**—who famously declared that *"India should be governed from a palace, not from a counting-house"*—and modeled faithfully on Robert Adam and James Paine’s **Kedleston Hall (Derbyshire)**. Its three-storeyed Palladian central block with Corinthian porticoes linked via four curving galleries to detached corner wings maximized monsoon cross-ventilation while anchoring Calcutta’s imperial spectacle.
  - **Calcutta Town Hall (1813, Col. John Garstin):** Classic English Palladian civic edifice approached via a monumental flight of steps rising to a stately Doric-Tuscan portico. (Earlier, Garstin also built Patna’s striking beehive masonry granary **Golghar in 1786** as an emergency famine-reserve monument after 1770.)
  - **Greek Revival Turn (1830s–40s):** Culminated in Calcutta’s **Old Silver Mint (1831, Major W.N. Forbes)**—replicating Athens’ Doric Temple of Minerva—and **Metcalfe Hall (1844)**, inspired by Athens’ Temple of the Winds.
- **Madras:** **John Goldingham** transformed **Government House (1800–1802)** with deep two-storeyed verandas and constructed the adjacent **Banqueting Hall / Rajaji Hall (1802)**—conceived explicitly like an elevated Greek Parthenon ringed by Tuscan-Doric colonnades to commemorate British triumphs at Plassey and Seringapatam—followed by **St. Andrew’s Kirk (1821)** and **Pachaiyappa’s Hall (1850)** imitating Athens’ Temple of Theseus.

### European Picturesque Landscape Art and Imperial Portraiture

Simultaneously, visiting European painters visually packaged the newly conquered subcontinent for metropolitan Britain through two influential idioms:

- **The "Picturesque" Landscape Tradition:** Originating in 18th-century British aesthetic theory, Picturesque art prized dramatic light, wild nature, and crumbling ancient ruins. **William Hodges** (in India 1780–1783, patronized by Warren Hastings) became the first professional British landscape painter in India, publishing ***Select Views in India* (1785–1788)**. He was surpassed by the uncle-and-nephew duo **Thomas and William Daniell** (touring India 1786–1793), who employed the *camera obscura* for architectural accuracy and revolutionary hand-colored **aquatint** engraving to publish the six-volume ***Oriental Scenery* (1795–1808)**. Ideologically, Picturesque vistas framed India as an ancient, static, decaying civilization visually awaiting British administrative rejuvenation.
- **Imperial Portraiture and History Painting:** Oil portraitists captured both late-18th-century Anglo-Indian hybridity and rising colonial hierarchy. **Johann Zoffany** painted intimate "conversation pieces" of British *Nabobs* alongside the celebrated ***Colonel Mordaunt’s Cock Match* (1784–86)**—capturing the vanished world of cosmopolitan camaraderie between British officers and Awadh Nawab Asaf-ud-daulah before Cornwallis’s racial distancing set in—while **Tilly Kettle** painted portraits at Faizabad and Arcot, and **Robert Home** immortalized Lord Cornwallis receiving Tipu Sultan’s hostage sons at Seringapatam, casting conquest as paternalistic benevolence.

### The Company School of Painting (*Kampani Kalam*, c. 1770–1850s)

As Mughal Delhi, Murshidabad, and regional royal courts lost fiscal patronage to EIC rule, hereditary Indian miniature painters migrated into British commercial-administrative hubs seeking survival. In the pre-photography era, Company officials, amateur naturalists, and travelers sought affordable documentary albums cataloging India’s exotic flora, fauna, festivals, monuments, and occupational castes (known as ***"Firka"* sets**—depicting *bhistis*/water-carriers, blacksmiths, fish-sellers, washermen, fakirs, and palanquin bearers).

**Hybrid Technical Grammar:** Indian ateliers skillfully blended inherited Mughal/regional miniaturist finesse with European visual tastes—exchanging opaque mineral gouache and ornate illuminated borders for **translucent European watercolors** on English watermarked paper (or translucent **mica** and **ivory** ovals), **linear perspective**, **chiaroscuro (light-and-shade modeling)**, subdued sepia/grey-brown tonalities, and stark **blank white backgrounds** emphasizing ethnographic precision.

**Four Premier Regional Schools:**

1. **Patna Kalam (Patna, Danapur, Arrah):** The supreme artistic achievement of the Company style—celebrated as the earliest independent Indian school focused almost exclusively on **common working people and everyday bazaar life** (*Firka* sets, street festivals, atmospheric night scenes in deep crimson and peacock blue) rather than monarchs or myth. Technically unique for its ***Kajli Siyahi* method**—executing the entire image **directly with a brush dipped in lampblack ink or pigment without any preliminary pencil/charcoal sketch (*khaka*)**—using hand-crafted squirrel-hair and pigeon-feather brushes and fine grain-like shading (*Java* stippling). Leading masters included **Sewak Ram** (1770–1830), **Hulas Lal** (1785–1875), **Shiv Lal**, **Shiva Dayal Lal**, noted women painters **Daksho Bibi** and **Sona Bibi**, and the tradition’s final exponent **Ishwari Prasad Verma**.
2. **Murshidabad and Calcutta:** Pioneers of exquisite natural-history illustration. Commissioned by Chief Justice Sir Elijah Impey’s wife (**Lady Mary Impey**), three **Patna-based artists** (trained in late provincial Mughal ateliers in Patna and invited to Calcutta)—**Shaikh Zain-ud-Din**, **Bhawani Das**, and **Ram Das**—produced the monumental ***Impey Album* (1777–1783)**, comprising life-sized botanical and ornithological studies prized globally for scientific precision and lyrical brushwork, paralleled by **Lord Wellesley’s** massive natural-history portfolios.
3. **Thanjavur and Trichinopoly (South India):** Rooted in Nayaka–Maratha temple painting workshops, southern Company artists painted ethnographic caste/occupation albums on paper and luminous **mica sheets**, retaining far richer color saturation, crisper contours, and denser compositional rhythm than Bengal/Bihar watercolors.
4. **Delhi and Agra (Late Mughal Company School):** Flourished under discerning patrons like **William Fraser**—whose celebrated ***Fraser Album* (c. 1815–1820)**, painted chiefly by master **Ghulam Ali Khan** and his workshop, achieved unprecedented psychological depth in naturalistic portraits of Jat villagers, Skinner’s Horse troopers, and Delhi ascetics—and Sir Thomas Metcalfe (*The Dehlie Book*), alongside jewel-like architectural miniatures of the Taj Mahal and Qutb Minar painted on **ivory**.

**Collapse of the Idiom:** The arrival of **photography in India during the 1840s–1850s** and the creation of state-run British academic art schools (Calcutta, Madras, Bombay) rapidly displaced hand-painted documentary souvenirs, extinguishing the Company School by the end of the 19th century.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Charter Act 1813 vs. Parliamentary Control of Revenues (UPSC Prelims 2019):** The 1813 Act ended EIC’s Indian trade monopoly (*except tea & China*) AND explicitly asserted **Crown sovereignty** over Company territories—but it did **NOT** place Indian revenues under direct British Parliamentary control (revenues remained managed by the EIC under Board of Control supervision until 1858).
> - **Governor-General of Bengal vs. India vs. Viceroy (UPSC Prelims 2023):** **Regulating Act 1773** → Governor-General of Bengal (**Warren Hastings**); **Charter Act 1833** → Governor-General of India (**William Bentinck**); **GOI Act 1858** → Viceroy (**Lord Canning**).
> - **1833 vs. 1853 Civil Services & Legislative Council Traps:** The **Charter Act 1833** inserted **Section 87** (removing race/religion bar in principle) and *attempted* open ICS competition, which Directors blocked; actual **open competitive ICS examination** AND the **separation of legislative and executive functions** occurred ONLY under the **Charter Act of 1853**. Even in 1853, the newly expanded Indian Legislative Council had **zero Indians** (local representation meant 4 British official nominees from provincial governments).
> - **1781 vs. 1784 vs. 1786 vs. 1793 Statutory Micro-Distinctions:** Supreme Court’s revenue jurisdiction stripped by **Act of Settlement (1781)**; **Board of Control** established by **Pitt’s India Act (1784)**; Governor-General override veto + Commander-in-Chief powers granted to Cornwallis by **Act of 1786**; **Board of Control salaries charged onto Indian revenues** by **Charter Act of 1793**.
> - **Cornwallis’s 1787 vs. 1793 Collector Flip (UPSC Prelims 2010 Anchor):** Do not conflate Cornwallis’s early and late judicial plans—in **1787** (for economy), he initially **merged** revenue, civil justice, and magisterial powers in the District Collector; in the **Cornwallis Code of 1793**, fearing concentration of unchecked power, he **stripped** Collectors of all judicial and magisterial authority.
> - **Subsidiary Alliance Core Trap (UPSC Prelims 2018):** Wellesley’s Subsidiary Alliance aimed to maintain a large standing army at others’ expense, counter Napoleonic danger, and establish British paramountcy—it was **NOT** designed *"to secure a fixed income for the Company"*. First state under Wellesley’s formal system = **Hyderabad (1798)** (Awadh’s 1765 treaty was a precursor buffer arrangement); **Yashwantrao Holkar** did NOT sign Subsidiary Alliance under Wellesley (only in 1818 under Lord Hastings via Treaty of Mandsaur).
> - **Fort William College Objective (UPSC Prelims 2020):** Wellesley founded Fort William College (1800) **to train British civilians for administrative duties in India**—NOT on orders of the Court of Directors (who opposed and curtailed it in favor of Haileybury, 1806), NOT primarily to revive Orientalism or employ William Carey.
> - **Ranjit Singh & Punjab Traps:** Seized Lahore in **1799** vs. crowned Maharaja in **1801**; acquired **Koh-i-Noor** from deposed Afghan king **Shah Shuja (1813)**, NOT from the Mughals; **Treaty of Amritsar (1809)** fixed the **Sutlej** as boundary (distinct from **Treaty of Amritsar, March 16, 1846**, whereby the British sold **Kashmir** to Dogra chief Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh); Ranjit Singh died in **1839**, whereas Punjab was annexed a decade later in **1849** under Dalhousie.
> - **Patna Kalam, *Impey Album* & Picturesque Art Distinctions:** Patna Kalam’s ***Kajli Siyahi*** technique involved painting **directly with the brush without any pencil/charcoal preliminary sketch (*khaka*)**; it focused on **ordinary commoners (*Firka* sets)** rather than courtly royalty; Lady Mary Impey’s ***Impey Album* (1777–83)** artists (**Shaikh Zain-ud-Din, Bhawani Das, Ram Das**) were recruited specifically from **Patna** to Calcutta (not Murshidabad); **William Hodges** published *Select Views in India* while **Thomas & William Daniell** produced *Oriental Scenery*; **Government House, Calcutta** (Charles Wyatt) was modeled on **Kedleston Hall (Derbyshire)**.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Statutory Time-Shift Traps:** UPSC routinely shifts landmark clauses by one 20-year Charter cycle—attributing open ICS competition or legislative-executive separation to **1833** instead of **1853**, or conflating the **1813** partial monopoly termination with **1833’s** total commercial abolition.
- **Treaty-to-War & Homonymous Treaty Traps:** Confusing identical place-names across eras—such as **Treaty of Amritsar (1809, Sutlej boundary with Ranjit Singh)** vs. **Treaty of Amritsar (1846, sale of Kashmir to Gulab Singh)**—or mispairing **Treaty of Salbai (1782)**, **Bassein (1802)**, **Sagauli (1816)**, and **Yandabo (1826)** with the wrong Governor-General.
- **Motive-Inversion Traps on Colonial Institutions:** Presenting incidental cultural by-products (such as Orientalist translation at Fort William College or fixed-income collection in Subsidiary Alliance) as the primary British motive instead of administrative utility and imperial military hegemony.
- **Administrative Philosophy Conflation Traps:** Attributing unified Collector–Magistrate–Police paternalism to **Cornwallis** (who deliberately *separated* Collector from Judge/Magistrate) rather than to **Munro, Elphinstone, and Bentinck**.
