# Chapter 9 - Anti-Caste Radicalism, Dalit Political Emancipation & Organized Women’s Movements (1829–1947)

## 1. Epistemic Break - From Upper-Caste Reform to Subaltern Emancipation

Nineteenth-century socio-religious reformers like Rammohan Roy and Ranade sought to **purify Hinduism from within**, appealing to idealized Vedic or *Bhakti* scriptures to soften caste prejudice and domestic patriarchy. By the second half of the nineteenth century, however, subaltern thinkers across Maharashtra, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu—followed by autonomous women’s pioneers—rejected that accommodationist framework altogether. Rather than treating caste and gender oppression as accidental medieval "corruptions," they indicted **Brahmanical patriarchy** (*graded inequality* fused with endogamous control over women’s sexuality) as the structural foundation of Indian society itself.

Historians like **Gail Omvedt** and **Rosalind O’Hanlon** distinguish this radical **anti-caste counter-Enlightenment** by three ruptures:
- **Rejection of Scriptural Apologetics:** Instead of debating *shastras*, radicals forged alternative counter-myths (Phule’s Kingdom of King Bali, Iyothee Thass’s Tamil Buddhism) or invoked modern universal human rights (Ambedkar’s *Liberty, Equality, Fraternity* and Periyar’s atheistic rationalism).
- **Material-Agrarian & Civic Politics:** Unlike upper-caste reform focused on domestic rituals, subaltern movements linked ritual exclusion directly to **landlessness, forced labor (*vethbegar*), bureaucratic Brahmin monopoly, colonial army/census policies, and public-space segregation** (water tanks, roads, schools, and temples).
- **Interlocking Emancipation of Women and Lower Castes:** Because caste reproduction depends structurally on strict caste **endogamy** (enforced via child marriage, enforced widowhood, and seclusion of upper-caste women alongside sexual exploitation of Dalit women via *Murali* and *Devadasi* customs), anti-caste radicals—Phule, Periyar, and Ambedkar—consistently stood at the vanguard of feminist liberation.

## 2. Western India Subaltern Vanguard - Phule to Chhatrapati Shahu

### 2.1 Mahatma Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890) and Savitribai Phule (1831–1897)
Born into the **Mali** (gardener) Shudra caste in Pune and educated at a Scottish Mission school—where he absorbed Thomas Paine’s ***Rights of Man***—**Jyotirao Govindrao Phule** experienced the raw violence of caste humiliation when expelled from a Brahmin friend’s wedding procession in 1848. Rather than seeking elite Sanskritization, Phule built the first systematic ideological and organizational offensive against caste and patriarchy in modern India.

- **Educational Revolution & Women’s Shelters:** Recognizing that Brahmanical power rested on a monopoly over literacy, Jyotirao educated his wife **Savitribai Phule** and together they opened **India’s first Indian-run school for girls at Bhide Wada, Pune on January 1, 1848** (followed by a school specifically for **Mahar and Mang untouchable children in 1851** and a night school for laborers in 1855). Evicted by Jyotirao’s father in 1849 under orthodox pressure, the couple found refuge in the Ganj Peth home of **Usman Sheikh**, whose sister **Fatima Sheikh** trained and taught alongside Savitribai—honored in Indian public memory and NCERT narratives as India’s pioneering Muslim woman educator *(while recent 2025 historical scholarship notes sparse contemporary archival records beyond Savitribai’s 1856 letter mentioning Fatima, her collaborative role in Pune’s Indigenous Library schools remains a staple cultural touchstone)*. Enduring cow dung and stones hurled by orthodox mobs, Savitribai became India’s first modern woman teacher and published radical Marathi poetry collections (***Kavyaphule*** 1854, ***Bavankashi Subodh Ratnakar*** 1892). In **1863**, the Phules opened the ***Balhatya Pratibandhak Griha*** (Infanticide Prohibition Home) in their own house to shelter pregnant Brahmin widows and prevent suicide or infanticide—adopting a widow’s son, **Yashwant**, as their own heir—and in **1868** threw open their private drinking-water tank to untouchables.
- ***Satyashodhak Samaj* (September 24, 1873):** Founded in Pune by Jyotirao Phule to liberate **Shudras and Ati-Shudras** (whom he united conceptually as the exploited ***Bahujan*** majority) from the ideological and material tyranny of the ***Shetji–Bhatji*** (moneylender–priest) alliance. The Samaj pioneered simplified, low-cost ***Satyashodhak* weddings** officiated **without Brahmin priests**, substituting oaths of mutual gender respect (*Mangalashtaka*) and cutting ruinous wedding debt. Its weekly mouthpiece ***Deenbandhu*** (launched **1877** by Phule’s associate **Krishnarao Bhalekar**, later shifted to Bombay under **Narayan Meghaji Lokhande**—the pioneer of India’s trade union movement!) carried anti-caste critique to Marathi mill-workers and peasants.
- **Ideological Works & The King Bali Counter-Myth:**
  - ***Gulamgiri*** (*Slavery*, **1873**): Dedicated explicitly to *"the good people of the United States as a token of admiration for their sublime disinterested and self-sacrificing devotion in the cause of Negro Slavery,"* Phule drew an audacious global parallel between **American chattel slavery and Indian caste bondage**. Deploying an inverted **Aryan Invasion counter-myth**, he reinterpreted the Puranic *Vamana–Bali* legend: **King Bali (*Baliraja*)** was not a demon, but the benevolent egalitarian ruler of ancient indigenous peasants (*Kshatriyas/Shudras*), defeated by deceitful Aryan-Brahmin invaders (*Vamana*, *Parashurama*) who reduced the defeated original inhabitants to servile Shudras and Ati-Shudras (*Mahars = Maha-ari*, "great enemies").
  - ***Shetkaryacha Asud*** (*Cultivator’s Whipcord*, written **1881–83**): A devastating political-economic indictment diagnosing peasant impoverishment not merely to British taxes, but to the **internal parasitic nexus** of Brahmin revenue clerks (*kulkarnis*), moneylenders (*savkars*), and British officials who taxed peasants to subsidize elite English colleges while starving primary vernacular schools—a complaint Phule formally submitted to the **Hunter Education Commission (1882)** demanding compulsory primary schooling and lower-caste teacher recruitment.
  - ***Sarvajanik Satyadharma Pustak*** (published posthumously **1891**): Outlined an egalitarian, gender-neutral civic religion celebrating *"all women and men"* (*sarva stri-purush*) as equal children of one Creator (*Nirmik*). In **1888**, Bombay’s working-class admirers conferred upon him the title ***"Mahatma"***—long before Gandhi.

### 2.2 First-Generation Dalit Vanguard - Walangkar, Kamble, and Bansode
Building directly upon Phule’s ideological foundation—and leveraging literacy gained inside the British Indian Army’s **Mahar battalions** (before the post-1857 **"Martial Races"** doctrine halted Mahar recruitment in 1892!)—three remarkable Mahar pioneers forged **independent Dalit public journalism and organizations** before Dr. Ambedkar entered public life:

- **Gopal Baba Walangkar (c. 1840–1900):** A retired Havildar of the British Indian Army from Konkan whom Ambedkar later honored as the **pioneer of the Dalit movement**. In **1886**, he founded the ***Anarya Dosh-Pariharak Mandali*** (*Society for the Removal of Wrongs of Non-Aryans*) at Dapoli (Ratnagiri). In **1888**, he launched ***Vital-Vidhvansak*** (*Destroyer of Brahmanical/Ceremonial Pollution*)—historically recognized as the **first Marathi monthly/booklet journal explicitly targeting untouchable audiences**—attacking the religious legitimacy of untouchability and contributing fiery essays to *Deenbandhu* and Agarkar’s *Sudharak*. In **1894**, Walangkar drafted a landmark **petition protesting the 1892 British ban on Mahar army recruitment**, arguing that Mahar soldiers had fought loyally from Koregaon (1818) onward and possessed equal Kshatriya lineage.
- **Shivram Janba Kamble (1875–1940):** A self-taught butler working in Pune’s Masonic Hall who mastered English and became the premier organizational voice of Deccan Mahars. He convened the historic **Saswad Conference (1903)**, founded the ***Sri Shankar Prasadik Somavanshiya Hitachintak Mitra Samaj*** (**1904**), and launched the Marathi monthly ***Somavanshiya Mitra*** (**1908**). Kamble spearheaded a moral crusade against the ***Murali* and *Jogtin*** system (dedication of Mahar girls to temple prostitution, personally presiding over a *Murali’s* marriage in 1909), petitioned the Bombay Governor and British Parliament (**1910**) for Mahar military/police recruitment and school entry, and later led the **Parvati Temple Satyagraha (Pune, 1929–30)** alongside P.N. Rajbhoj.
- **Kisan Faguji Bansode (1879–1946):** A prolific poet, labor organizer, and journalist from Nagpur (Central Provinces & Berar) who operated at the intersection of anti-caste assertion and working-class unionism. He founded the ***Sanmarg Bodhak Nirashrit Samaj*** (**1903**), opened the **Chokhamela Girls’ School (1907)** in Nagpur, and edited a succession of pioneering Dalit papers—***Nirashrit Hind Nagarik*** (1910), ***Vital Vidhvansak*** (1913), ***Majur Patrika*** (1918), and ***Chokhamela*** (1931). Unlike Ambedkar’s later break with Hinduism, Bansode—like the medieval Mahar saint Chokhamela—insisted on claiming equal spiritual worth *within* reformed Hinduism while fiercely backing Dalit labor and political rights.

### 2.3 Vitthal Ramji Shinde (1873–1944) and *Depressed Classes Mission* (1906)
Educated at Fergusson College and Manchester College, Oxford (as a Prarthana Samaj missionary), **Maharshi Vitthal Ramji Shinde** bridged upper-caste liberal humanitarianism and grassroots anti-untouchability work. On **October 18, 1906**, he established the ***Depressed Classes Mission Society of India*** in **Bombay** (with Justice N.G. Chandavarkar as President and branches across Maharashtra, Madras, and Mangalore) to provide schools, hostels, dispensaries, and vocational training for untouchables.

Shinde persuaded the **Indian National Congress (Calcutta, 1917)** to pass its **first-ever formal resolution** condemning untouchability, testified before the Southborough Committee (1919) for Depressed Class representation, and authored the authoritative sociological study ***Bharatiya Asprushyatecha Prashna*** (1933). Yet his paternalistic upper-caste stewardship soon clashed with Ambedkar’s demand for **autonomous Dalit self-leadership**—marking the structural shift from upper-caste "uplift" to Dalit self-emancipation.

### 2.4 Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj of Kolhapur (1874–1922) - Architect of Affirmative Action
Triggered by the **Vedokta Controversy (1899–1905)**—when orthodox Brahmin priests refused to chant Vedic (*Vedokta*) hymns for the Maratha royal family, insisting they were Shudras entitled only to *Puranokta* rites—**Rajarshi Shahu Maharaj** transformed his princely state of **Kolhapur** into an extraordinary laboratory of institutional social justice:
- **Historic 50% Reservation Order (July 26, 1902):** Issued a landmark **Gazette Notification** reserving **50 percent of all state administrative posts for Backward Classes** (defined broadly as all non-Brahmin communities—Marathas, artisans, untouchables, Muslims, and Jains)—the **earliest statutory affirmative-action reservation policy in modern Indian history**.
- **Institutional Demolition of Feudal Caste Power:** Abolished the hereditary Brahmin village-accountant office (***Kulkarni Watan***, 1918) and the forced untouchable village-service system (***Mahar Watan***, 1918), introduced **free and compulsory primary education (1917)**, ended separate segregated schools for untouchables (1919), legalized inter-caste and widow marriages, and established caste-inclusive student hostels (*Miss Clarke Hostel* for untouchables, 1908).
- **Patronage of Satyashodhak & Ambedkar:** Revived the ***Satyashodhak Samaj*** in Kolhapur (1911), financially funded Dr. Ambedkar’s inaugural newspaper ***Mooknayak*** (**1920**) and his London studies, and presided alongside Ambedkar at the historic **Mangaon Conference (March 1920)**—where Shahu Maharaj prophetically declared to the Depressed Classes: ***"You have found your saviour in Ambedkar; I am confident he will break your shackles."***

## 3. Kerala Social Revolutions - Challenging the "Lunatic Asylum" of Caste

Swami Vivekananda famously branded late-nineteenth-century Kerala a **"lunatic asylum"** of caste—where oppression went beyond mere untouchability (*thottukoodayma*) to grotesque **unapproachability (*theendal*)** (Ezhavas kept 24–36 feet away, Pulayas 64–96 feet away from Namboodiri Brahmins) and **unseeability**, compounded by dress-code humiliations (forbidding lower-caste women from covering their upper bodies—broken earlier by the **Channar / Upper Cloth Revolt, 1813–1859**). Between 1888 and 1936, a sequential cascade of subaltern movements dismantled this medieval order and forged the foundations of the modern "Kerala Model."

### 3.1 Sri Narayana Guru (1856–1928) and the *SNDP Yogam* (1903)
Born into the backward toddy-tapping **Ezhava** (*avarna*) caste in Travancore, **Sri Narayana Guru** mastered Sanskrit *Advaita Vedanta*, Tamil *Siddha* medicine, and Malayalam literature before turning non-dual philosophy into an instrument of quiet social dynamite.
- ***Aravippuram Prathishta* (Shivaratri, 1888):** Defying the centuries-old Brahmin monopoly over temple consecration, Narayana Guru plunged into the Neyyar river at **Aravippuram**, lifted a smooth stone, and installed it as a **Shiva *linga***. When furious Brahmin priests questioned an Ezhava’s authority, Guru replied with serene irony: ***"I have installed only an Ezhava Shiva, not a Brahmin Shiva."*** Inscribed on the shrine wall was his universal creed: *"Devoid of dividing walls of caste or race, or hatred of rival faith, we all live here in brotherhood."* Later, rejecting even image-based sectarianism, he consecrated a **mirror** (symbolizing *Tat Tvam Asi* — divinity within oneself) at Kalavamkodam (1927) and plaques inscribed *"Truth, Duty, Compassion, Love"* at Murukkumpuzha.
- **Slogan & Organization (*SNDP Yogam*, May 15, 1903):** Inspired by the **Ezhava Memorial (1896)** submitted by **Dr. Padmanabhan Palpu** (an Ezhava physician denied Travancore state service despite British medical degrees, who mobilized over 13,000 signatures demanding civil equality), Narayana Guru established the ***Sree Narayana Dharma Paripalana (SNDP) Yogam*** in **1903**—with **Guru as lifelong President**, **Dr. Palpu** as chief organizer/Vice-President, and towering Malayalam modernist poet **Kumaran Asan** (author of *Duravastha* & *Chandalabhikshuki*) as its **first General Secretary** (editing its mouthpiece *Vivekodayam*).
- **Core Philosophy:** Crystallized in his immortal maxim ***"Oru Jati, Oru Matam, Oru Daivam Manushyanu"*** (*"One Caste, One Religion, One God for Mankind"*) and *"Strengthen through organization; liberate through education,"* Guru banned expensive superstitious rituals (*talikettu kalyanam*), campaigned against liquor production and consumption, opened modern schools and cooperative industries, and established the **Sivagiri Mutt** (Varkala, 1904) and **Advaita Ashram** (Aluva, 1913)—convening Asia’s first **All-Religions Conference at Aluva (1924)**.

### 3.2 Sahodaran Ayyappan (1889–1968) - Radicalizing the Guru’s Gospel
A fiercely rationalist disciple of Narayana Guru from Cherai (Cochin), **K. Ayyappan**—revered as ***Sahodaran* (Brother) Ayyappan**—pushed anti-caste praxis beyond religious reform into **atheism, socialism, and militant inter-dining**. On **May 29, 1917**, he organized the historic ***Misra Bhojanam* (Inter-Caste Feast)** at **Cherai**, where Ezhavas sat and ate food cooked and served by Pulayas—prompting orthodox Ezhavas to ostracize him derisively as *"Pulayan Ayyappan."*

He founded the ***Sahodara Sangham*** (**1917**), edited the radical journal ***Sahodaran***, organized Cochin’s trade unions, and daringly **modified Narayana Guru’s slogan** to declare: ***"Jati Venda, Matam Venda, Daivam Venda Manushyanu"*** (*"No Caste, No Religion, No God for Man"*). Later, as a minister in Travancore-Cochin, he pioneered progressive civil marriage and secular labor legislation.

### 3.3 Mahatma Ayyankali (1863–1941) and the *SJPS* (1907)
While SNDP mobilized the relatively better-off Ezhava community, the agrarian **Pulaya** (Dalit) agricultural serfs of Travancore endured near-slavery. Unlettered yet strategically brilliant, **Ayyankali** (born in Venganoor) launched direct-action struggles for Dalit dignity:
- ***Villuvandi* (Bullock-Cart) Agitation (1893):** Dressed defiantly in upper-caste white *mundu*, vest, and turban, Ayyankali purchased a decorated two-bullock cart (*villuvandi*)—forbidden to Dalits—and drove it boldly down the prohibited public road from Venganoor to Balaramapuram, confronting upper-caste mobs with his band of trained youth (*Ayyankali Pada*) until public road access was gradually forced open (formally permitted by Travancore by 1900).
- ***Sadhu Jana Paripalana Sangham* (SJPS, 1907) & Kerala’s First Agricultural Labor Strike (1907–10):** Modeled on SNDP, Ayyankali founded the **SJPS** in **1907** to unite all depressed castes (not just Pulayas) for education, land, and civil rights. When upper-caste landlords violently blocked admission of a Pulaya girl (**Panchami**) into the Ooruttambalam government school despite a 1907 royal order, Ayyankali called a **historic indefinite strike of Pulaya agricultural laborers** across Travancore’s paddy fields—refusing to sow or harvest until their children were admitted to schools and Sunday rest-days granted! Facing famine, landlords capitulated (**1910**), proving the structural power of Dalit agrarian labor.
- **Legislative Advocacy & *Kallumala* Agitation (1915):** Nominated in **1911** as the **first Dalit member of the Sri Mulam Popular Assembly (Travancore Legislature)**, Ayyankali fought relentlessly for land grants and scholarships for Dalits. In **1915**, following violent attacks on Pulaya women trying to cover their breasts, he presided over the dramatic ***Kallumala Samaram*** (*Stone Necklace Agitation*) at Perinad (Kollam), where thousands of Pulaya women publicly snapped and discarded the degrading beaded stone necklaces (*kallumala*) forced upon them as badges of caste servitude.

### 3.4 Vaikom Satyagraha (1924–25), Guruvayur Satyagraha (1931–32), and the 1936 Proclamation
By the 1920s, Kerala’s local anti-caste struggles converged with Gandhian civil disobedience in two defining temple-road and temple-entry battles:
- **Vaikom Satyagraha (March 30, 1924 – November 23, 1925):** Launched in the **Princely State of Travancore** (Kottayam district) to win the civic right of *avarna* castes to **walk on the four public approach roads surrounding the Vaikom Mahadeva Temple** (NOT yet interior sanctum entry). Conceived by Ezhava Congress leader **T.K. Madhavan** (following the **1923 Kakinada INC resolution** prioritizing anti-untouchability), it was spearheaded by **K.P. Kesava Menon**, **K. Kelappan**, **George Joseph** (a Syrian Christian briefly leading until Gandhi advised keeping internal Hindu reform led by Hindus), and **E.V. Ramasamy ‘Periyar’**—who traveled from Tamil Nadu, was jailed twice for refusing to halt speeches, and won the enduring title ***"Vaikom Veerar"*** (*Hero of Vaikom*). Solidarity poured in nationally: Punjab’s **Akalis** established a free community *langar* at Vaikom, and Nair reformer **Mannath Padmanabhan** led a 500-strong upper-caste ***Savarna Jatha*** on foot to Trivandrum petitioning Regent **Maharani Sethu Lakshmi Bai**. Following **Mahatma Gandhi’s visit (March 1925)**—where he held a historic dialogue with **Sri Narayana Guru** at Sivagiri—a compromise negotiated with Police Commissioner W.H. Pitt opened three of the four approach roads (leaving the eastern road barred until 1936). While hailed as a moral breakthrough, Narayana Guru privately expressed dissatisfaction with the partial compromise.
- **Guruvayur Satyagraha (November 1, 1931 – October 2, 1932):** Unlike Vaikom (Travancore), Guruvayur unfolded in **British Malabar (Ponnani taluk, Madras Presidency)** and demanded **direct entry into the sanctum of the Guruvayur Sri Krishna Temple** controlled by the Zamorin of Calicut. Organized by the Kerala Provincial Congress under **K. Kelappan** (*"Kerala Gandhi"*), **Mannath Padmanabhan**, **A.K. Gopalan** (volunteer captain), **P. Krishna Pillai** (future Communist founder, who defied Brahmin privilege by ringing the sacred temple bell), and **Subramanian Tirumumpu**. When Kelappan began a fast unto death on September 21, 1932, he suspended it on **October 2, 1932** at Gandhi’s urgent plea; a subsequent poll of Ponnani caste Hindus showed over 77 percent favored temple entry.
- **Travancore Temple Entry Proclamation (November 12, 1936):** Sustained by Ezhava threats of mass conversion to Buddhism/Christianity/Sikhism and the *Nivarthana* (Abstention) movement, **Maharaja Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma** (advised by Dewan **Sir C.P. Ramaswami Iyer**) issued the epochal **Temple Entry Proclamation**, throwing open all Travancore state-managed temples to **all Hindus irrespective of caste**—a triumph Gandhi called a *"modern miracle"* (followed later by Madras legislation in 1939/1947 and Cochin in 1947–48).

## 4. Tamil Nadu and Dravidian Assertion - Iyothee Thass, Justice Party, and Periyar

In Madras Presidency, **Brahmins formed barely 3 percent of the population yet held over 70 percent of university degrees and top Indian bureaucratic, judicial, and Congress leadership posts** by the early twentieth century. This stark demographic-administrative asymmetry—intersecting with Tamil linguistic revivalism (*Maraimalai Adigal’s Pure Tamil / Thanithamizh Iyakkam*, 1916)—produced two distinct streams of anti-Brahmin politics: radical Dalit-Buddhist critique and non-Brahmin Dravidian assertion.

### 4.1 Pandit C. Iyothee Thass (1845–1914) - The Dalit-Buddhist Forerunner
Decades before both Periyar’s Dravidian movement and Dr. Ambedkar’s 1956 Buddhist conversion, **Pandit Iyothee Thass**—a Paraiyar Siddha physician, multilingual scholar (Tamil, Pali, Sanskrit, English), and radical intellectual from Madras—formulated the earliest comprehensive **Dalit-Buddhist counter-history** in India:
- **Census Subversion & *Adi Tamilar* Identity:** As early as the **1881 Census**, Thass petitioned the colonial government to record depressed classes not as "Hindus" or "Panchamas," but as ***"Adi Tamilar"*** (original Tamils) or ***"Casteless Dravidians"***—declaring in **1886** that untouchables were never Hindus, but descendants of ancient **Tamil Buddhists** (*Sakya* lineage) subjugated and degraded by invading Aryan Brahmins when Buddhism declined.
- **Organizations & *Sakya Buddhist Society* (1898):** Alongside Rettamalai Srinivasan, he formed the ***Dravida Mahajana Sabha*** (**1891**), passing ten resolutions demanding free education, land grants, and civil rights. Assisted by Theosophist **Colonel Henry Steel Olcott** (who ran free *Panchama* schools in Madras), Thass traveled to Ceylon in **1898** to take formal *Diksha* (Buddhist initiation) from Sinhalese monk **Hikkaduwe Sri Sumangala**, establishing the ***Sakya Buddhist Society* (*South India Sakya Buddhist Association*, 1898)** with vibrant branches among Dalit workers in Kolar Gold Fields (Karnataka), Madras, and Rangoon.
- ***Oru Paisa Tamizhan* (June 19, 1907):** Launched as a one-paisa Tamil weekly in Madras (renamed ***Tamilan*** in 1908) so working-class readers could afford it. Circulating across South India, Burma, Malaya, and South Africa until his death in 1914, it systematically re-read Tamil classics (*Tirukkural*, *Manimekalai*) through an anti-caste Buddhist prism.

### 4.2 The *Justice Party* (*South Indian Liberal Federation*, 1916)
Disturbed by Brahmin dominance over both the colonial bureaucracy and Annie Besant’s Home Rule League (which they viewed as a bid to replace British Raj with "Brahmin Raj"), elite non-Brahmin landlords, merchants, and professionals—**Dr. T.M. Nair**, **Sir P. Theagaraya Chetty**, and **Dr. C. Natesa Mudaliar** (who earlier founded the *Madras United League* 1912 / *Dravidian Association* 1914)—founded the ***South Indian Liberal Federation (SILF)*** on **November 20, 1916** in Madras. Popularly named the ***Justice Party*** after its English daily ***Justice*** (alongside Tamil *Dravidan* and Telugu *Andhra Prakasika*), it issued the landmark ***Non-Brahmin Manifesto*** (**December 1916**), pledging loyalty to British rule while demanding **communal representation and job reservations** for non-Brahmins.

Boycotting Gandhi’s Non-Cooperation, the Justice Party swept the **1920 Dyarchy elections** in Madras and governed for most of **1920–1937** (under A. Subbarayalu Reddiar, Raja of Panagal, P. Munuswamy Naidu, and Raja of Bobbili), leaving a colossal legislative legacy:
- **Communal Government Orders (Communal G.O.s, 1921 & 1922):** Institutionalized fixed caste-based quotas in public employment and admissions across non-Brahmins, Brahmins, Muslims, Christians, and Depressed Classes—forging the structural DNA of South Indian reservation policy.
- **Pioneering Social Legislation:** Enacted **women’s voting rights in Madras (1921)**—first province in British India!—backed **Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy** (India’s first woman legislator, 1927) to pass legislation curbing the ***Devadasi* system** (initially 1929 amendment; formally abolished 1947), created the **Staff Selection Board (1924)** (prototype of State PSCs), passed the **Hindu Religious Endowments (HRE) Act (1925)** bringing rich temple trusts under public accountability, launched **Madras Corporation’s pioneering Mid-Day Meal scheme (1920)** for poor schoolchildren, and established **Andhra University (1926)** and **Annamalai University (1929)**.
- **Class Limitation:** Despite progressive legislation, the Justice Party remained dominated by wealthy upper-Shudra *zamindars* (Vellalas, Reddis, Kammas, Chettiars), gradually alienating Dalits (*M.C. Rajah* broke away in 1923) and suffering a landslide defeat to C. Rajagopalachari’s Congress in the **1937 provincial elections**.

### 4.3 E.V. Ramasamy ‘Periyar’ (1879–1973) - *Self-Respect Movement* (1925) and *Dravidar Kazhagam* (1944)
Unlike the pro-British aristocrats of the early Justice Party, **Erode Venkatappa Ramasamy**—revered as ***Periyar*** ("Great One", conferred by Tamil women at a Madras conference in 1938!)—began his public life as a fiery Gandhian Congressman (serving as **Tamil Nadu Congress Committee President**, burning foreign cloth, picketing toddy shops, and earning glory as *Vaikom Veerar* in 1924).

His rupture with Congress came at the **Kancheepuram TNCC Conference (November 1925)** after Brahmin Congress leaders repeatedly blocked his resolution for **communal representation** and operated **segregated dining** (*pandi bhedam*) for Brahmin and non-Brahmin students at the Congress-funded **Cheranmadevi Gurukulam** run by V.V.S. Aiyar. Resigning from Congress, Periyar launched the ***Self-Respect Movement* (*Suya Mariyadhai Iyakkam*, 1925)**—assisted by S. Ramanathan—transforming anti-caste struggle into a **mass populist, atheistic, feminist cultural revolution**:
- **Radical Ideological Assault:** Rejecting God, Vedas, *Puranas*, *Manusmriti*, and the *Ramayana* (inverting Ravana as a noble Dravidian king and burning the *Manusmriti*), Periyar declared that *Swaraj* without social equality meant merely replacing white masters with Brahmin masters. His fiery vernacular print empire—***Kudi Arasu*** (*Republic*, launched **May 2, 1925**), ***Revolt*** (English, 1928), ***Puratchi*** (*Revolution*, 1933), ***Pagutharivu*** (*Rationalism*, 1934), and ***Viduthalai*** (*Liberation*, 1935)—democratized rationalism across rural Tamil districts.
- **Unmatched Feminist Radicalism:** Far ahead of contemporary nationalists, Periyar introduced ***Suya Mariyadhai Thirumanam* (Self-Respect Marriages)**—priestless, Sanskrit-free, *thali*-optional contractual unions between consenting equals (including inter-caste and widow marriages, legally validated by Tamil Nadu in 1967!). In his radical tract ***Pen Yen Adimaiyanal?*** (*Why Were Women Enslaved?*, **1942**), he demanded **women’s right to divorce, equal property inheritance, contraception/birth control** (decades before Indian family planning!), abolition of the *Devadasi* system, and women’s entry into police and military forces.
- **Anti-Hindi Agitation (1937–1940) & *Dravidar Kazhagam* (1944):** When Rajagopalachari’s Congress ministry made Hindi compulsory in Madras schools (1937), Periyar spearheaded a ferocious **Anti-Hindi Agitation**, framing Hindi imposition as Aryan-North Indian cultural imperialism and coining the slogan *"Tamil Nadu for Tamilians"* (later *"Dravida Nadu"*). Elected President of the declining Justice Party in **1938** (while imprisoned), Periyar purged it of zamindari loyalism and—at the historic **Salem Conference on August 27, 1944** (guided by the *Annadurai Resolution*)—renamed the Justice Party into the non-electoral, militant social-reform body ***Dravidar Kazhagam (DK)***, urging members to drop caste surnames and British titles (later splitting in **1949** when C.N. Annadurai founded the electoral **DMK**).

## 5. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s Political Emancipation Arc (1919–1947)

Where Phule forged the anti-caste critique and Periyar radicalized cultural rationalism, **Dr. Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (1891–1956)**—born into a Mahar military family at Mhow, armed with doctorates from **Columbia University** and the **London School of Economics** (funded by **Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda** and Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj) plus a Bar-at-Law from Gray’s Inn—elevated Dalit liberation into **constitutional jurisprudence, autonomous mass politics, and modern democratic theory**.

### 5.1 Early Constitutional Advocacy, Journalism & Institutional Base (1919–1927)
- **Southborough Franchise Committee Testimony (January 27, 1919):** Making his historic public debut, the 27-year-old Ambedkar argued before the Southborough Committee (constituted under Montagu–Chelmsford reforms) that untouchables formed a distinct civic entity excluded from *"associated life"* with caste Hindus—demanding **separate electorates**, lowered property/literacy franchise qualifications, and reserved seats proportional to population.
- ***Mooknayak* (January 31, 1920) & *Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha* (July 20, 1924):** Funded by Shahu Maharaj, Ambedkar launched the Marathi fortnightly ***Mooknayak*** (*Leader of the Voiceless*) to expose how nationalist papers like *Kesari* refused even paid advertisements for Dalit causes. On **July 20, 1924**, he established the ***Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha*** (*Depressed Classes Welfare Association*) in Bombay (with Sir Chimanlal Setalvad as President and Ambedkar as Managing Committee Chair) under the electrifying motto ***"Educate, Agitate, Organize"***—opening hostels, reading rooms, and youth clubs. Following the Mahad backlash, he launched his fortnightly ***Bahishkrit Bharat*** (**April 3, 1927**), later succeeded by ***Janata*** (1930) and ***Prabuddha Bharat*** (1956).

### 5.2 Civil-Rights Direct Action - Mahad (1927) and Kalaram (1930–35)
- **Mahad Chavdar Tank Satyagraha (March 20, 1927):** Although the Bombay Legislative Council had passed **S.K. Bole’s Resolution (1923)**—reaffirmed in 1926—opening all public water tanks and facilities to untouchables, local caste Hindus barred Dalits from the municipal **Chavdar Tank** in **Mahad (Raigad/Kolaba district)**. Leading nearly 3,000 delegates from a Depressed Classes Conference on **March 20, 1927**, Ambedkar marched to the tank, bent down, and drank water—asserting not mere thirst, but ***"the establishment of equality."*** Orthodox mobs brutally attacked returning delegates and ceremonially "purified" the tank with cow dung and urine.
- **Second Mahad Conference & Burning of *Manusmriti* (December 25, 1927):** Returning to Mahad despite an interim court injunction against drinking from the tank, Ambedkar escalated the struggle from local civil rights to an **ideological declaration of human rights** (calling Mahad the Dalit equivalent of the 1789 French National Assembly). On **December 25, 1927**—observed annually as *Manu Smriti Dahan Din*—Ambedkar (joined by his Brahmin ally **G.N. Sahasrabuddhe**) **publicly burned copies of the *Manusmriti*** as the foundational legal charter of graded caste and gender inequality. That same year he raised the ***Samata Sainik Dal*** (*Soldiers of Equality*) volunteer corps to defend Dalit gatherings. (Ten years later, in **1937**, the Bombay High Court finally upheld Dalits’ legal right to use Chavdar Tank.)
- **Kalaram Temple Satyagraha, Nashik (March 2, 1930 – 1935):** Launched alongside **Dadasaheb Bhaurao Gaikwad** with ~15,000 non-violent satyagrahis (including thousands of Dalit women) demanding entry into Nashik’s **Kalaram Temple** and the right to pull the Rama *Ratha* chariot. Five years of violent orthodox opposition convinced Ambedkar that upper-caste Hindu hearts would not melt through temple satyagrahas—culminating in his thunderous **Yeola Declaration (October 13, 1935)**: ***"Though I was born a Hindu, I solemnly assure you that I will not die as a Hindu"*** (fulfilled twenty-one years later via his mass **Navayana Buddhist conversion at Deekshabhoomi, Nagpur on October 14, 1956**).

### 5.3 Simon Commission, Rival Dalit Coalitions, RTCs, and the Poona Pact (1928–1932)
- **Simon Commission Testimony (October 1928):** While Congress boycotted the all-white Simon Commission, Ambedkar testified before it on behalf of the *Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha*. Crucially for Prelims nuance, **in 1928 Ambedkar favored universal adult franchise with JOINT electorates and reserved seats**—warning he would demand **separate electorates** *only if* universal adult franchise was denied!
- **All India Depressed Classes Congress (August 1930) vs. M.C. Rajah:** Before departing for London, Ambedkar chaired the **All India Depressed Classes Congress at Nagpur (August 8–9, 1930)** to assert independent pan-Indian Dalit representation. This created sharp friction with **Rao Bahadur M.C. Rajah** of Madras—President of the older **All India Depressed Classes Association (1926/28)** and the first nominated Dalit member of the Central Legislative Assembly—who resented British nomination of Ambedkar and **Rao Bahadur Rettamalai Srinivasan** to the Round Table Conferences. In **February 1932**, M.C. Rajah signed the ***Rajah–Moonje Pact*** with Hindu Mahasabha President **Dr. B.S. Moonje**, endorsing **joint electorates with reserved seats**—a deal Ambedkar fiercely repudiated as a surrender without Dalit political leverage.
- **Round Table Conferences (1930–32) & Clash with Gandhi:** Ambedkar was one of the few Indian leaders to attend **all three Round Table Conferences** (alongside Tej Bahadur Sapru and Rettamalai Srinivasan). At the **First RTC (1930)**, he submitted his classic *"Scheme of Political Safeguards for the Protection of the Depressed Classes"* demanding fundamental rights, civil-service quotas, and statutory ban on untouchability. At the **Second RTC (1931)**, a dramatic clash erupted: **Mahatma Gandhi** claimed Congress alone represented the Depressed Classes and opposed separate electorates as a *"vivisection of Hinduism,"* whereas **Ambedkar** countered that Congress had failed to guarantee social equality and joined the **Minorities Pact** demanding separate electorates because the British refused universal adult franchise.
- **Communal Award (August 16, 1932) & Poona Pact (September 24, 1932):** British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald’s Communal Award** recognized Depressed Classes as a distinct minority, granting them **separate electorates** in **71 Provincial Legislature seats** *(note: Ambedkar’s Writings Vol. 17 cites **78** including special allocations; legacy NCERT/Spectrum cite **71**)* for 20 years—plus a **double vote** (one vote in separate Depressed Class constituencies and a second vote in general constituencies!). Viewing separate electorates as a permanent wedge dividing Hindu society, Gandhi launched an **"epic fast unto death"** inside **Yerwada Central Jail, Poona (September 20, 1932)**. Caught between immense nationwide pressure to save Gandhi’s life and defending Dalit autonomy, Ambedkar negotiated the **Poona Pact (September 24, 1932)**—signed by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** (for Depressed Classes) and **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya** (on behalf of caste Hindus; **Gandhi himself did not sign**):
  1. **Abandoned Separate Electorates** in favor of **Joint Electorates with Reserved Seats** within the general Hindu constituency.
  2. **More than doubled Provincial reserved seats** from 71 to **148** *(later adjusted to **147** under the GoI Act 1935; dual-standard parity: remember both 148 and 147!)*.
  3. Allocated **18 percent of general seats** in the **Central Legislature** to the Depressed Classes.
  4. **Two-Tier Primary System (for 10 years):** Depressed Class voters in each reserved constituency formed an electoral college electing a **panel of four Dalit candidates** by single vote; the general joint electorate then elected the final legislator from that panel.
  5. Pledged fair representation in **public services** and earmarked educational grants.
- **Gandhi’s *Harijan Sevak Sangh* vs. Ambedkar’s Critique:** Immediately after the Pact, Gandhi founded the **All India Anti-Untouchability League (September 30, 1932)**—soon renamed ***Harijan Sevak Sangh***—with industrialist **G.D. Birla** as President and **Amritlal Vithaldas Thakkar (*Thakkar Bapa*)** as General Secretary, and launched the English weekly ***Harijan*** (**February 11, 1933**). Ambedkar initially sat on its central board alongside M.C. Rajah and R. Srinivasan, but resigned within months when Gandhi refused to transform the Sangh from an upper-caste *"penance and welfare"* charity into a militant civil-rights body run by Dalits themselves—and remained bitterly critical of the Poona Pact’s joint-electorate structure for enabling caste-Hindu majorities to pick pliable Dalit candidates.

### 5.4 *Annihilation of Caste* (1936), *ILP* (1936), and *AISCF* (1942)
- ***Annihilation of Caste* (May 1936):** Invited by the Lahore reformist body ***Jat-Pat Todak Mandal*** (led by Sant Ram B.A.) to deliver its 1936 Presidential Address, Ambedkar sent his advance manuscript—which horrified the organizers so deeply that they demanded deletions and canceled the conference. Ambedkar self-published the undelivered speech as ***Annihilation of Caste***—arguably modern India’s sharpest sociological treatise. In it, he demolished both **Marxist economic reductionism** (proving caste is *"not merely a division of labour, it is also a division of labourers"* graded by birth) and **Gandhian *Varnashrama* idealism** (arguing that caste cannot be cured by mere inter-dining or moral persuasion without **dynamiting the divine authority of the *Shastras*** and institutionalizing **inter-caste marriage**). Gandhi published a courteous rebuttal in *Harijan* (*"A Vindication of Caste"*), to which Ambedkar appended a devastating *Reply to the Mahatma* in the 1937 edition.
- **Independent Labour Party (ILP, August 15, 1936):** Breaking out of an exclusively Dalit electoral shell, Ambedkar built the **ILP** as a broad **secular, socialist, anti-caste working-class party** uniting Dalits, Kunbi-Maratha peasants, and industrial laborers. Contesting the **1937 Bombay Presidency elections**, the ILP won a stunning **14 out of 17 seats contested** (11 reserved + 3 general seats, emerging as the largest opposition party in Bombay!). Inside and outside the assembly, the ILP introduced a historic **Bill to Abolish the *Khoti* Landlord System** in Konkan (1937), marched 25,000 peasants to the Bombay Council Hall (**January 10, 1938**), led the massive **November 7, 1938 one-day general textile strike** alongside Communists against the Congress ministry’s anti-strike **Industrial Disputes Bill**, and championed maternity benefits and birth control.
- **All India Scheduled Castes Federation (AISCF, July 1942):** As wartime constitutional negotiations (Cripps Mission 1942) sidelined class politics in favor of religious/communal blocs, Ambedkar dissolved the ILP and founded the **AISCF** at **Nagpur (July 18–20, 1942)** (with **N. Sivaraj** as President) as the first **all-India political party strictly representing Scheduled Castes**—demanding recognition of SCs as a distinct political minority, separate settlements, and restoration of separate electorates. Serving concurrently as **Labour Member of the Viceroy’s Executive Council (1942–1946)**, Ambedkar instituted the **8-hour workday**, **Indian Labour Conference (Tripartite machinery)**, **Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) blueprint**, **Mines Maternity Benefit Act**, **Damodar Valley / Central Water Commission water policy**, and the **first 8.33% reservation for Scheduled Castes in Central Government services (1943)**—before founding the **People’s Education Society (1945)** (Siddharth College, Bombay; Milind College, Aurangabad) and steering India’s **Constitution Drafting Committee (1947–49)**.

## 6. Statutory Gender Milestones and Autonomous Women’s Agency (1829–1947)

Historians **Geraldine Forbes** and **Radha Kumar** trace colonial India’s women’s history across three distinct structural phases: (1) **1820s–1880s** (male-led social reform & colonial statutes focusing on upper-caste domestic abuses); (2) **1880s–1910s** (the eruption of **autonomous female feminist authorship and organizations**); and (3) **1917–1947** (all-India women’s federations fighting simultaneously for **suffrage, personal-law reform, and anti-colonial Swaraj**).

### 6.1 Statutory Gender Milestones (1795–1937)
Colonial legislation on women’s rights never sprang from altruistic British feminism; it reflected an uneasy compromise between **Indian reformist agitation**, **British civilizing-mission rhetoric**, and **colonial fear of provoking orthodox revolt** (especially after 1857):
- **Female Infanticide Regulations (1795, 1804) & Prevention Act (1870):** **Bengal Regulation XXI (1795)** and **Regulation III (1804)** declared female infanticide (prevalent among Rajput, Jat, and Khatri hypergamous clans) equivalent to murder. Because administrative enforcement lagged, the Raj enacted the **Female Infanticide Prevention Act (Act VIII of 1870)** mandating compulsory registration of births and surveillance of suspected clans in North-Western Provinces, Punjab, and Oudh.
- **Bengal Sati Regulation XVII (December 4, 1829):** Enacted by **Lord William Bentinck** following **Raja Rammohan Roy’s** relentless scriptural and journalistic campaign, declaring *Sati* illegal and punishable as culpable homicide in Bengal Presidency (extended to Madras and Bombay Presidencies in **1830**).
- **Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act (Act XV of July 26, 1856):** Drafted under Dalhousie and passed under Canning after **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar’s** campaign; legalized Hindu widow remarriage and legitimized offspring. *(Critical Legal Irony: Section 2 of the Act stripped a remarrying widow of all inherited property rights from her deceased husband—actually penalizing lower-caste widows whose customary laws previously allowed remarriage without forfeiting property!)*
- **Native Marriage Act / Civil Marriage Act (Act III of 1872):** Championed by **Keshab Chandra Sen’s Indian Reform Association**; permitted secular, monogamous, inter-caste civil marriages with minimum ages of **14 (girls) and 18 (boys)**—yet its reach remained tiny because couples had to formally declare they did **not** profess Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Parsi, or Buddhist faiths!
- **The Rukhmabai Case (1884–1888) & Age of Consent Act (Act X of March 19, 1891):** Married at age 11 to an uneducated, unemployed 19-year-old **Dadaji Bhikaji**—yet raised and educated by her progressive stepfather **Dr. Sakharam Arjun** in Bombay—**Rukhmabai Raut** courageously refused to live with Dadaji when he sued her in **1884** for **restitution of conjugal rights**. Writing scathing letters to *The Times of India* under the pseudonym ***"A Hindu Lady"*** and backed by the **Rukhmabai Defence Committee** (led by **Behramji Malabari** and **Pandita Ramabai**), she risked six months’ imprisonment rather than submit to an unconsented child marriage—winning an out-of-court settlement in **1888** (Queen Victoria overruled the prison sentence) before sailing to England to qualify as **one of India’s first practicing women physicians (1894)**! Coupled with the horrific **1889 death of 10-year-old Phulmoni Dasi** from marital rape by her 35-year-old husband in Calcutta, the Rukhmabai storm forced passage of the **Age of Consent Act, 1891** (amending IPC Section 375 to raise the age of sexual consent for girls—married or unmarried—from **10 to 12 years**). Revealing the clash between gender justice and cultural nationalism, **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** fiercely opposed the Act in *Kesari*, arguing a foreign colonial state had no right to legislate over Hindu domestic life.
- **Child Marriage Restraint Act / Sarda Act (1929):** Sponsored by Arya Samajist legislator **Rai Sahib Harbilas Sarda** (passed **September 28, 1929**; enforced **April 1, 1930** following the **Joshi Committee Report** and vigorous lobbying by WIA/AIWC). Unlike the 1891 Act (which governed consummation), the Sarda Act applied to **all communities across British India**, outlawing solemnization of marriages below **14 years for girls and 18 years for boys** (later raised to 15/18 in 1949 and **18/21 in 1978**). *(Note also two landmark 1937 statutes: the **Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act, 1937 [Deshmukh Act]**, granting Hindu widows limited life-interest in joint-family property, and the **Muslim Personal Law [Shariat] Application Act, 1937**, overriding patriarchal customary inheritance exclusions.)*

### 6.2 Pioneering Autonomous Feminist Voices - Tarabai, Ramabai, and Rokeya
By the 1880s, Indian women refused to remain silent objects of male reform treatises—speaking directly as sharp political theorists (preceded by **Rashsundari Devi’s *Amar Jiban*, 1876**, the first full-length Bengali autobiography, written by a housewife who secretly taught herself to read in her kitchen!):
- **Tarabai Shinde (1850–1910) and *Stri Purush Tulana* (1882):** A Berar Maratha woman associated with Phule’s *Satyashodhak Samaj*, **Tarabai Shinde** published ***Stri Purush Tulana*** (*A Comparison Between Women and Men*) in Marathi (**1882**)—widely celebrated by historian Rosalind O’Hanlon as **India’s first modern feminist polemic**. Provoked by the **1881 Surat trial of Vijayalakshmi**—a young Brahmin widow sentenced to death (later reduced to transportation) for killing her illegitimate infant—Tarabai launched a blistering indictment of **patriarchal double standards**, exposing how male reformers, scriptures, and newspapers blamed women’s "innate wickedness" for every vice while excusing male debauchery, polygamy, and enforced widowhood. Jyotirao Phule championed her tract vigorously in *Satsar*.
- **Pandita Ramabai Saraswati (1858–1922):** A Chitpavan Brahmin Sanskrit prodigy (taught by her heterodox father Anant Shastri Dongre) honored with the titles ***Pandita*** and ***Saraswati*** by Calcutta scholars in **1878**. Widowed young after an inter-caste marriage to Bengali lawyer Bipin Behari Medhavi, she moved to Pune and founded the ***Arya Mahila Samaj*** (**May 1882**) to combat child marriage and advocate female education—testifying boldly before the **Hunter Education Commission (1882)** for women doctors and female teacher training (contributing to the **Lady Dufferin Fund** for women’s hospitals). After converting to Christianity in England (1883) and touring America—where she published her piercing indictment ***The High-Caste Hindu Woman*** (**1887**)—she returned with American Ramabai Association funds to open ***Sharada Sadan*** (*Home of Learning*) in **Bombay (March 11, 1889)** (relocated to **Pune in 1890**) as a **secular residential school for high-caste Hindu widows** (backed initially by Ranade and Bhandarkar, though orthodox backlash over pupil conversions led her to shift later to **Kedgaon near Pune** to run her broader **Mukti Mission [1898]**, rescuing over 1,500 famine-stricken women and girls with vocational printing, weaving, and nursing trades).
- **Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932):** The towering pioneer of **Muslim women’s emancipation in Bengal**. Self-taught in Bengali and English with her brother’s and husband’s support, Rokeya published the brilliant feminist science-fiction satire ***Sultana’s Dream*** (**1905**, in Madras’s *The Indian Ladies’ Magazine*)—imagining **"Ladyland,"** a serene, solar-powered utopia ruled by scientific women where aggressive men are locked indoors in *mardana* seclusion! In **1911**, she established the **Sakhawat Memorial Girls’ School in Calcutta** (initially with eight pupils), going door-to-door to persuade conservative families to educate daughters, and in **1916** founded the ***Anjuman-i-Khawatin-i-Islam* (Muslim Women’s Association)** to organize slum literacy, widow aid, and employment. Her devastating essays (*Motichur*, *Padmarag*, *Abarodhbasini*) exposed *purdah* seclusion not as religious virtue, but as a patriarchal cage denying women rationality and economic self-reliance.

### 6.3 Pan-Indian Organized Women’s Associations (1910–1947)
In the early twentieth century, women moved from localized clubs (*Ramabai Ranade’s Ladies Social Conference / Bharat Mahila Parishad, 1904* — the women’s wing of the Indian National Social Conference) to **independent, national feminist federations**:
- ***Bharat Stree Mahamandal* (1910):** Founded in **Allahabad (1910)** by **Sarala Devi Chaudhurani** (Rabindranath Tagore’s niece, nationalist editor of *Bharati*, and *Swadeshi* activist)—recognized as the **first major all-India women’s organization convened and led strictly by an Indian woman**. Convinced that male reformers still treated women as subordinates *"in the shade of men,"* Sarala Devi established branches in Lahore, Allahabad, Delhi, Karachi, Amritsar, Hyderabad, Kanpur, Bankura, Hazaribagh, Midnapur, and Calcutta to promote **female education (including zenana/home tuition), abolition of *purdah*, and women’s socioeconomic uplift** across caste and class lines.
- **Women’s Indian Association (WIA, May 8, 1917):** Founded at **Adyar, Madras** by Irish feminist suffragette **Margaret Cousins**, **Annie Besant** (first President), and **Dorothy Jinarajadasa**, alongside **Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy**, **Sarojini Naidu**, and **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** (organ: ***Stri Dharma***). As India’s first truly **multi-ethnic, politically oriented feminist association**, WIA led the historic **December 1917 All-India Women’s Deputation to Edwin Montagu** (led by Sarojini Naidu) demanding **equal women’s suffrage**—successfully pressuring provincial legislatures to enfranchise women starting with **Madras (1921)** and **Bombay (1921/22)** (with **Travancore** pioneering princely suffrage in 1920!).
- **National Council of Women in India (NCWI, 1925):** Established in **1925** as a national branch of the **International Council of Women (ICW)**, spearheaded by **Mehribai Tata** (Lady Dorab Tata), **Cornelia Sorabji** (India’s first female barrister), **Tarabai Premchand**, and **Shaffi Tyabji**. Heavily anchored among aristocratic, Parsi, and pro-British philanthropic circles, NCWI focused on legislative lobbying (Sarda Act, labor/maternity protection, anti-trafficking) and welfare, though its elite membership and official deference limited its mass nationalist appeal.
- **All India Women’s Conference (AIWC, January 1927):** Initiated by **Margaret Cousins** and inaugurated at **Fergusson College, Poona (January 5–8, 1927)** with **Maharani Chimnabai Gaekwad of Baroda** as its first President (backed by the **Begum of Bhopal**, **Rani Sahiba of Sangli**, and **Lady Dorab Tata**) and **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** as its dynamic **first Organizing Secretary**—soon led by **Sarojini Naidu**, **Rajkumari Amrit Kaur**, **Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy**, **Hansa Mehta**, **Rameshwari Nehru**, **Begum Shareefa Hamid Ali**, and **Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit**.
  - **Evolution & Agenda:** Initially launched to reform women’s education (founding **Lady Irwin College, Delhi in 1932**), AIWC swiftly expanded into India’s premier **secular, egalitarian feminist federation** (organ: *Roshni*). It fought for the **Sarda Act (1929)**, equal **Hindu personal-law codification** (shaping the Rau Committee 1941 and post-1947 Hindu Code Bills), equal pay, **universal adult franchise**, and a **Uniform Civil Code**.
  - **The Suffrage & Reservation Debate (1931–1935):** Crucially, when British authorities proposed **communal/reserved seats for women** under the 1932 Communal Award and GoI Act 1935, **AIWC, WIA, and NCWI jointly rejected separate electorates and reserved seats**—demanding ***"equality and no privileges"*** via universal adult franchise, refusing to let colonial divide-and-rule fracture Indian womanhood along religious lines.
  - **Structural Critique (Bridge to Mains GS1):** While AIWC and WIA won landmark constitutional guarantees (15 women sat in the Constituent Assembly, enshrining gender equality in Articles 14–16), feminist historians highlight a persistent **class-caste blind spot**: dominated by upper-caste, English-educated urban women, these pre-1947 federations framed reform around marriage, property, and franchise—rarely confronting the agrarian wages, caste-based sexual violence, or landlessness of Dalit, Adivasi, and working-class women (an exclusion later challenged by autonomous post-1975 women’s movements covered in Chapter 18).

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Rukhmabai Case (1884) Scope Trap (UPSC Prelims 2020):** The historic *Dadaji Bhikaji v. Rukhmabai* legal dispute centered specifically on **(2) Age of consent** and **(3) Restitution of conjugal rights**—NOT directly on *(1) Women’s right to gain education* (even though Rukhmabai later studied medicine in London!).
> - **Signatories & Seat Tallies of the Poona Pact (Sept 24, 1932):** **Mahatma Gandhi did NOT sign the Poona Pact**—it was signed by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** on behalf of the Depressed Classes and **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya** (plus 20+ leaders including C. Rajagopalachari, Sapru, Jayakar, M.C. Rajah) on behalf of caste Hindus. **Dual-Standard Seat Parity:** Communal Award granted **71** provincial seats *(78 in Ambedkar’s Vol. 17 counting special seats)* → Poona Pact raised this to **148** *(later adjusted to **147** under GoI Act 1935)* + **18%** of Central Legislature general seats.
> - **Ambedkar’s Position Before the Simon Commission (1928) vs. RTCs (1930–32):** Before the **Simon Commission (1928)**, Ambedkar actually advocated **joint electorates with reserved seats PROVIDED universal adult franchise was granted**—he pivoted to **separate electorates** at the RTCs only after universal adult franchise was rejected! Also do **NOT** confuse Ambedkar’s **All India Depressed Classes Congress (Aug 1930)** with **M.C. Rajah’s All India Depressed Classes Association** (which signed the joint-electorate **Rajah–Moonje Pact, Feb 1932**).
> - **Vaikom (1924–25) vs. Guruvayur (1931–32) Geographic & Objective Inversion:** **Vaikom** took place in the **Princely State of Travancore** strictly for the right to use **public roads surrounding the temple** (NOT interior entry yet), where **Periyar** earned the title *Vaikom Veerar*. **Guruvayur** took place in **British Malabar (Madras Presidency)** specifically for **direct interior entry** into the Guruvayur Sri Krishna temple (led by K. Kelappan, A.K. Gopalan, and P. Krishna Pillai).
> - **Sri Narayana Guru vs. Sahodaran Ayyappan Slogan Inversion:** **Sri Narayana Guru’s** slogan was ***"One Caste, One Religion, One God for Mankind."*** It was his radical disciple **Sahodaran Ayyappan** (founder of *Sahodara Sangham* and *Misra Bhojanam*, 1917) who deliberately altered it into ***"No Caste, No Religion, No God for Man."***
> - **Founders of Women’s Organizations (High-Frequency Swaps):**
>   - **First all-India women’s org founded by an Indian woman:** ***Bharat Stree Mahamandal* (1910, Allahabad)** = **Sarala Devi Chaudhurani** *(NOT Mehribai Tata or Annie Besant!)*.
>   - **Women’s Indian Association (WIA, 1917, Adyar):** **Margaret Cousins, Annie Besant, Dorothy Jinarajadasa**.
>   - **National Council of Women in India (NCWI, 1925):** **Mehribai Tata** (Lady Dorab Tata).
>   - **All India Women’s Conference (AIWC, 1927, Poona):** Initiated by **Margaret Cousins**; **First President = Maharani Chimnabai Gaekwad of Baroda**; **First Organizing Secretary = Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** *(NOT Sarojini Naidu or Kamala Nehru as first founder!)*.
> - **AIWC / WIA Stand on Reserved Seats (1930s):** Contrary to intuitive assumptions, **AIWC, WIA, and NCWI explicitly OPPOSED separate electorates and reserved seats for women** during the 1930s constitutional debates, demanding universal adult franchise without communal quotas.
> - **Two Journals Named *Vital-Vidhvansak*:** **Gopal Baba Walangkar** published the famous original ***Vital-Vidhvansak* (1888)** (UPSC Prelims 2020 answer!); **Kisan Faguji Bansode** later revived the title for a Nagpur paper in **1913**.
> - **First Province to Grant Women’s Voting Rights:** Among British Indian provinces, **Madras Presidency (1921)** under the **Justice Party** was first (followed closely by Bombay 1921/22), while among Princely States, **Travancore (1920)** led even earlier.

> [!tip] Whole-UPSC Spillover
> - **GS2 (Polity — Affirmative Action, Constitutional Morality & Articles 15–17, 25, 330–335):** Every pillar of India’s constitutional social-justice architecture traces its direct genealogy to this chapter: **Chhatrapati Shahu’s 1902 order** and the **Justice Party’s 1921–22 Communal G.O.s** seeded Articles 15(4) & 16(4) reservations; **Vaikom, Guruvayur, and the 1936 Travancore Proclamation** directly inspired **Article 25(2)(b)** (throwing open Hindu religious institutions to all classes); **Ambedkar’s Poona Pact (1932)** engineered **Articles 330 & 332** (joint-electorate reserved SC constituencies); and the **AIWC/WIA suffrage campaign** paved the road from universal adult franchise (Article 326) to the **106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023 (*Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*)**.
> - **GS1 (Indian Society — Caste Persistence & Intersectional Feminism):** Phule’s *Shetji–Bhatji* thesis, Ambedkar’s *Annihilation of Caste* distinction between *"division of labour"* and *"division of labourers,"* Tarabai Shinde’s critique of Brahmanical patriarchy, and the class-caste limits of pre-1947 urban women’s bodies directly power GS1 Mains answers on urban caste persistence (2026), inter-caste vs. inter-religious marriages (2024), and intersectional Dalit feminism (2018).
> - **GS4 (Ethics) & Essay:** Contrasting **Gandhi’s ethical-penance approach** (moral persuasion, *Harijan* self-purification) with **Ambedkar’s rights-based constitutional radicalism** (structural law, self-respect, annihilation of caste), or **Periyar’s rationalist dissent** and **Savitribai Phule’s fearless civic empathy**, offers elite conceptual nuance for philosophical essays on justice, dignity, and social transformation.

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature
1. **Intra-Regional Leader & Slogan Swapping:** Swapping **Sri Narayana Guru** (*One Caste, One Religion, One God*) with **Sahodaran Ayyappan** (*No Caste, No Religion, No God*), or mixing **Vaikom** (Travancore; temple roads; Periyar) with **Guruvayur** (British Malabar; interior entry; K. Kelappan / P. Krishna Pillai).
2. **Dalit Representation Nuance Traps:** Assuming Gandhi personally signed the Poona Pact (signatory for caste Hindus was **Madan Mohan Malaviya**), conflating Ambedkar’s **1930 AIDCC** with **M.C. Rajah’s AIDCA**, or missing that Ambedkar accepted joint electorates with universal adult franchise before the **1928 Simon Commission**.
3. **Women’s Organization Founder & Stance Inversions:** Assigning *Bharat Stree Mahamandal* (1910) to Mehribai Tata instead of **Sarala Devi Chaudhurani**, claiming **AIWC (1927)** was India’s first women’s association (preceded by 1910 BSM, 1917 WIA, 1925 NCWI), or asserting AIWC/WIA demanded reserved seats for women in the 1930s (they demanded universal suffrage without reservations).
4. **Statutory Reach & Age-Threshold Traps:** Mixing up the **Native Marriage Act (1872)** (limited to non-orthodox declarants; ages 14/18), the **Age of Consent Act (1891)** (consummation age raised from 10 to 12; opposed by Tilak), and the **Sarda Act (1929)** (all-community marriage age 14/18; enforced 1930).
