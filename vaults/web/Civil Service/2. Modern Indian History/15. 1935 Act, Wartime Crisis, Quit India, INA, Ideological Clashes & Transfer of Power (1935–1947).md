# 1935 Act, Wartime Crisis, Quit India, INA, Ideological Clashes & Transfer of Power (1935–1947)

The final twelve years of British rule compressed constitutional engineering, global total war, rural insurrection, armed exile armies, and communal partition into an unprecedented crisis of imperial survival. What began in 1935 as London's most elaborate statutory attempt to perpetuate colonial control through federal devolution ended in August 1947 in an accelerated British withdrawal amid fractured sovereignty.

Beneath the rapid succession of missions, pacts, and mutinies, this decade also forced India's founding thinkers to debate what freedom itself ought to produce. The clashes between **Mahatma Gandhi** and **Ambedkar, Nehru, Tagore, Bose, and Savarkar** were not marginal personal disputes—they established the foundational philosophical coordinates of modern India's Constitution, economy, education, and civic identity.

## 1. The Government of India Act, 1935 and Late-1930s Politics (1935–1939)

### Structural Architecture of the Government of India Act, 1935

Enacted by the British Parliament following the Third Round Table Conference, the March 1933 White Paper, and Lord Linlithgow's Joint Select Committee report, the **Government of India Act, 1935** (321 sections and 10 schedules) was the longest statute Westminster had ever passed. Its political logic was strategic containment: cede limited administrative patronage at the provincial periphery while fortifying British control over finance, defence, and central power.

- **Proposed All-India Federation (Never Operationalized):** Envisaged a union of the 11 British Indian Governors' Provinces (mandatory accession), 6 Chief Commissioners' Provinces, and the Princely States (**purely voluntary** via an Instrument of Accession). Because federation required accession by princely rulers controlling at least **half the princely seats in the Council of States** and **half the aggregate princely population**—a threshold the princes never met—the federal clause remained a dead letter, leaving the Centre operating under the **1919 Act** until 1947.
- **Dyarchy at the Centre:** Transferred Dyarchy from provinces up to the proposed Federal Executive. **Reserved subjects**—**Defence, External Affairs, Ecclesiastical Affairs, and Tribal Areas**—were retained strictly under the **Governor-General** assisted by up to three appointed counsellors (not accountable to the Federal Legislature), while **Transferred subjects** went to responsible ministers subject to the Governor-General's sweeping "special responsibilities."
- **Three-List Legislative Division:** Divided powers into a **Federal List (59 items)**, **Provincial List (54 items)**, and **Concurrent List (36 items)**. Crucially for Prelims, **residuary powers were vested exclusively in the Governor-General**—neither in the Federal Legislature nor in the provinces.
- **Provincial Autonomy (Enforced April 1, 1937):** Abolished Provincial Dyarchy, replacing it with elected ministries responsible to provincial legislatures—though Governors retained discretionary vetoes and **Section 93** powers to suspend ministries and assume direct rule. Introduced **bicameral legislatures in 6 of 11 provinces** (Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam, and United Provinces), expanded the franchise to roughly **14% of British India's population (~30 million voters)**, and extended separate communal electorates to Depressed Classes (modified per Poona Pact), women, and labor.
- **Institutional Blueprint:** Established a **Federal Court** (inaugurated October 1, 1937, with **Sir Maurice Gwyer** as first Chief Justice), provided for the **Reserve Bank of India** (operationalized April 1935), created Federal, Provincial, and Joint Public Service Commissions, abolished the Secretary of State's **Council of India** (replacing it with advisers), separated **Burma** and **Aden** from India, and carved out **Sind** (from Bombay) and **Orissa** (from Bihar & Orissa) as new provinces.

Nationalist leaders condemned its autocratic safeguards—Jawaharlal Nehru famously dismissed the Act as a **"charter of slavery"** and *"a machine with strong brakes but no engine."* Yet operationally, over two-thirds of the 1950 Indian Constitution's administrative and federal scaffolding drew directly upon its text and the governance experience gained under it.

### The 1937 Elections and the 28-Month Congress Ministries (1937–1939)

At the **Lucknow (April 1936)** and **Faizpur (December 1936 — first rural INC session)** Congresses, both presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru, Congress resolved to contest the provincial elections to wreck the Act from within and demonstrate mass hegemony. In the **February 1937 elections**, Congress achieved a sweeping triumph—winning **711 (rising to 716) of 1,585 seats**, securing absolute majorities in **5 provinces** (Madras, Bihar, Orissa, Central Provinces, and United Provinces), emerging as the single largest party in **Bombay, Assam, and NWFP**, and ultimately forming ministries across **8 of 11 provinces** (missing only **Bengal** under Fazlul Haq's Krishak Praja Party–League coalition, **Punjab** under Sikandar Hayat Khan's Unionist Party, and **Sind**).

Across their **28 months in office (July 1937 – October 1939)**, Congress ministries demonstrated indigenous administrative capacity:

- **Civil Liberties & Agrarian Relief:** Repealed emergency laws, lifted bans on nationalist newspapers and political organizations (except the centrally banned CPI), released thousands of political prisoners, restored lands confiscated during CDM, enacted tenancy-security and debt-relief legislation (UP, Bihar, Madras), expanded primary schooling and *khadi*, and slashed ministerial salaries to ₹500 per month.
- **Structural Contradictions:** Operating inside a colonial fiscal framework, right-wing ministers collided sharply with peasant and labor radicals—using police against Bihar's *Bakasht* satyagrahis, passing the anti-strike **Bombay Industrial Disputes Act (1938)**, and prosecuting socialist agitators (Yusuf Meherally, S.S. Batliwala).
- **Communal Turning Point:** Having won barely **4.8% of the total Muslim vote** in 1937 and failing to form a government even in Muslim-majority provinces, M.A. Jinnah sought coalition entry in the United Provinces. When Congress insisted UP League legislators dissolve their separate parliamentary group to join the ministry, Jinnah pivoted decisively to mass communal mobilization ("Islam in danger"), circulating three atrocity dossiers alleging systematic bias against Muslims—the **Pirpur Report (November 1938, Raja Syed Muhammad Mehdi of Pirpur)**, the **Shareef Report (March 1939, Bihar)**, and Fazlul Haq's ***Muslim Sufferings Under Congress Rule* (December 1939)**.

### Haripura Planning and the Tripuri Crisis (1938–1939)

At the **Haripura Congress Session (February 1938)**, **Subhas Chandra Bose** was unanimously elected INC President. Demonstrating bold technocratic foresight, Bose launched the **National Planning Committee (NPC, October 1938)**—appointing **Jawaharlal Nehru as Chairman** alongside scientist Meghnad Saha, engineer M. Visvesvaraya, socialists, and industrialists—laying the institutional seedbed for post-1947 Five-Year Plans.

Strategic harmony ruptured in early 1939 when Bose stood for a rare second term against Gandhi's nominee, **Pattabhi Sitaramayya**. Bose won the **January 1939 presidential ballot (1,580 votes to 1,377)**—a stunning upset prompting Gandhi to declare *"Pattabhi's defeat is more mine than his."* The clash reflected a deep strategic divide: anticipating European war, Bose demanded issuing Britain a **six-month ultimatum** for *Purna Swaraj* followed by immediate mass struggle, while Gandhi judged Congress organizationally riven by ministry corruption and Hindu–Muslim tension, unprepared for disciplined satyagraha.

At the **Tripuri Congress Session (March 1939, near Jabalpur)**—where an ailing Bose presided from a stretcher—Gandhi's loyalist **Govind Ballabh Pant** moved the decisive **Pant Resolution**, expressing full confidence in the outgoing Working Committee and instructing Bose to nominate his new CWC **"in accordance with the wishes of Gandhiji."**

Crucially for Prelims analysis, Bose's isolation stemmed from a **fractured Congress Left**: while the **Communists supported Bose**, **Congress Socialist Party leaders (notably Jayaprakash Narayan)** and **M.N. Roy's followers** abstained from opposing the Pant Resolution, prioritizing Congress unity under Gandhi over a split. When Gandhi refused to suggest names, Bose resigned the presidency (**April 1939**; replaced by **Rajendra Prasad**), founded the **Forward Bloc on May 3, 1939** inside Congress to rally radical elements, and was disqualified from holding elective Congress office for three years after protesting an AICC ban on unauthorized civil disobedience (**August 1939**).

## 2. Wartime Rupture, Quit India, and the 1943 Bengal Famine (1939–1944)

### World War II Outbreak, the Lahore Resolution, and August Offer (1939–1940)

On **September 3, 1939**, Viceroy **Lord Linlithgow** unilaterally declared India belligerent in World War II without consulting a single Indian legislature or ministry. At **Wardha (September 1939)**, the Congress Working Committee condemned Nazi aggression yet refused participation in an imperialist war unless Britain declared democratic war aims and granted immediate responsible government. When Linlithgow stalled, **Congress provincial ministries resigned en masse (October–November 1939)**—an exit Jinnah's Muslim League (joined by B.R. Ambedkar) celebrated as **"Day of Deliverance" on December 22, 1939**.

With Congress out of office, Britain pivoted sharply toward the Muslim League as a wartime counterweight. At the **Lahore Session of the All-India Muslim League (March 22–24, 1940)**, presided over by Jinnah, Bengal Premier **A.K. Fazlul Haq** formally moved—and **Choudhry Khaliquzzaman** seconded—the historic **Lahore Resolution (March 23, 1940)** (initially drafted by Punjab Premier **Sikandar Hayat Khan** and stiffened by the Subject Committee). It demanded that geographically contiguous Muslim-majority areas in the North-Western and Eastern zones be grouped to constitute **"Independent States"** with autonomous, sovereign constituent units. *(Critical Prelims nuance: the word **"Pakistan"** did NOT appear anywhere in the Lahore Resolution; the Indian press promptly coined the label "Pakistan Resolution.")*

Following the Fall of France (June 1940), Linlithgow unveiled the **August Offer (August 8, 1940)** to woo Indian war cooperation. It promised **Dominion Status** as Britain's ultimate goal, immediate expansion of the Viceroy's Executive Council with an Indian majority, a War Advisory Council, and—for the **first time in British policy**—explicitly recognized Indians' right to frame their own constitution via a post-war **Constituent Assembly composed mainly of Indians**. Yet a poison pill doomed it: Britain pledged that **no future constitution would be adopted without the consent of minorities**, handing Jinnah a permanent constitutional veto. Congress rejected it outright (Nehru: *"Dominion status is dead as a doornail"*), while the League welcomed the veto.

To register anti-war moral dissent without sabotaging Britain's struggle against fascism, Gandhi launched **Individual Satyagraha (*Delhi Chalo Movement*, October 17, 1940 – December 1941)**—sending vetted individuals village-by-village to deliver anti-war speeches asserting free expression. **Acharya Vinoba Bhave** was the **first satyagrahi** (Paunar, Maharashtra), **Jawaharlal Nehru** the **second**, and **Brahma Dutt** the **third**; over 25,000 courted arrest before Japan's entry into the war prompted suspension.

### The Cripps Mission (March–April 1942)

Japan's lightning capture of Singapore (February 15, 1942) and Rangoon (March 7, 1942) brought Axis armies to India's eastern border. Pressured by US President Franklin Roosevelt, Chinese leader Chiang Kai-shek, and British Labour ministers, Prime Minister Winston Churchill dispatched **Sir Stafford Cripps** (a socialist sympathetic to Indian nationalists) to Delhi on **March 22, 1942** with a Draft Declaration:

- **Dominion Union:** Promised a post-war **Indian Union with full Dominion Status**, explicitly free to secede from the British Commonwealth and join any international body.
- **Solely Indian Constituent Assembly:** After the war, a constitution-making body would be formed with members **indirectly elected by Provincial Assemblies** (via proportional representation) and **nominated by the Princely States** (upgrading the August Offer's *"mainly"* Indian formula to *"solely"* Indian).
- **Provincial Opt-Out ("Local Option"):** Any British Indian Province unwilling to accept the new Constitution could retain its existing constitutional status or **sign a separate agreement with Britain** enjoying equal Dominion sovereignty—constituting the **first formal British blueprint conceding Partition**.
- **Interim Defence Reservation:** During the war, defence and executive veto remained wholly under British control.

Official Congress negotiators **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** objected fiercely to three clauses: Dominion Status instead of immediate independence, princely nomination of state delegates, and the provincial opt-out legitimizing Balkanization; Gandhi famously dismissed the offer as *"a post-dated cheque [on a crashing bank]."* Simultaneously, Jinnah rejected Cripps because it stopped short of an explicit, guaranteed Pakistan. Talks collapsed on **April 11, 1942**.

### The Quit India Movement / August Kranti (1942–1944)

The Cripps breakdown, wartime price inflation, racial discrimination during the chaotic evacuation of Indians from Burma, and fears of British scorched-earth retreat before Japan pushed Gandhi to abandon restraint. Adopting the **Wardha CWC Resolution (July 14, 1942)**, the AICC convened at **Gowalia Tank Maidan (August Kranti Maidan), Bombay on August 8, 1942**, ratifying the **Quit India Resolution** demanding immediate British withdrawal.

In his electrifying August 8 address, Gandhi gave India its starkest mantra—***"Do or Die"*** (*Karenge ya Marenge*)—alongside targeted directives to every stratum: soldiers were told not to leave the army yet refuse to fire on compatriots; civil servants to declare allegiance to Congress; princes to accept popular sovereignty; students to walk out if resolute; and peasants to pay rent only to anti-government landlords.

Before dawn on **August 9, 1942** (**Operation Zero Hour**), colonial police arrested the entire national leadership in one sweep—confining Gandhi, Kasturba, Sarojini Naidu, and Mahadev Desai at the **Aga Khan Palace (Pune)**, Nehru, Patel, Azad, and Pant at **Ahmednagar Fort**, and Rajendra Prasad at **Bankipur Jail (Patna)**. Defying tear gas and barricades, young socialist **Aruna Asaf Ali** hoisted the Tricolour at Gowalia Tank, unleashing the largest, most violent, and most decentralized mass revolt since 1857.

- **Leaderless Mass Insurrection:** Stripped of senior moderating leaders, urban strikes (including a 13-day shutdown at Tata's Jamshedpur steelworks and three-month textile closures in Ahmedabad) quickly gave way to sweeping rural uprisings across Eastern UP, Bihar, Bengal, Maharashtra, Odisha, and Karnataka—tearing up railway tracks, cutting telegraph lines, and seizing post offices and police stations. Colonial troops unleashed 57 army battalions and even RAF machine-gunning from the air, arresting over 90,000 and killing thousands.
- **Underground Directorate & Congress Radio:** Sustained nationwide coordination passed to a daring underground network dominated by **CSP socialists and radical Gandhians**—**Jayaprakash Narayan** (who scaled the walls of Hazaribagh Central Jail in November 1942 and organized the guerrilla ***Azad Dasta*** in the Nepal Terai), **Ram Manohar Lohia**, **Aruna Asaf Ali**, **Sucheta Kripalani**, **Achyut Patwardhan**, **Biju Patnaik**, **Chhotubhai Puranik**, and **R.P. Goenka**. Operating from shifting Bombay rooftops (August–November 1942), **Usha Mehta** (alongside Babubhai Khakar, Vithaldas Jhaveri, and Chandrakant Jhaveri) ran the clandestine **Congress Radio** (42.34 meters), broadcasting news and Lohia's directives across India.
- **Four Iconic Parallel Governments (*Jatiya / Prati Sarkars*):**
  1. **Ballia (Eastern UP, August 1942 — ~1 week):** Led by veteran Gandhian **Chittu Pandey** (*Sher-e-Ballia*), who liberated jail inmates and assumed district governance before troops retook the town.
  2. **Tamluk / Midnapore (Bengal, Dec 1942 – Sept 1944):** The ***Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar***, organized by **Satish Chandra Samanta**, **Sushil Kumar Dhara**, and **Ajoy Mukherjee**, ran people's arbitration courts, cyclone relief, grain redistribution, and the armed volunteer corps ***Vidyut Vahini***. Earlier, on September 29, 1942, 73-year-old peasant widow **Matangini Hazra** (*Gandhi Buri*) died marching into police bullets clutching the Tricolour at Tamluk police station.
  3. **Satara (Maharashtra, Aug 1943 – May 1946 — longest-lived):** The ***Prati Sarkar*** directed by **Krantisinh Nana Patil**, **Y.B. Chavan**, and **Achyut Patwardhan** established *Nyayadan Mandals* (people's courts), *Toofan Sena* youth squads, prohibition, rural libraries, and austere "Gandhi marriages" across hundreds of villages.
  4. **Talcher (Odisha, 1942):** **Pabitra Mohan Pradhan** declared a peasant-worker *Chasi-Maulia Raj* (crushed by RAF aerial strafing), while tribal leader **Lakshman Naik** led Koraput's revolt before his execution in March 1943.
- **Groups Staying Aloof (High-Yield Prelims Check):** The **Muslim League**, **Communist Party of India** (pursuing its WWII *"People's War"* line), **Hindu Mahasabha**, **RSS**, and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** (serving as Labour Member in the Viceroy's Executive Council) did **not** support Quit India. When Viceroy Linlithgow demanded Gandhi condemn underground sabotage, Gandhi embarked on a **21-day fast (February 1943)** in Aga Khan Palace—blaming the state's *"leonine violence"* for provoking popular fury.

Though militarily suppressed by 1944, Quit India achieved an irreversible political victory: it proved that Britain could govern India only through permanent wartime military occupation, placing immediate total independence as the non-negotiable floor of all subsequent talks.

### The Great Bengal Famine of 1943

Running tragically alongside Quit India's aftermath was the **Great Bengal Famine (1943–44)**, which claimed between **2.1 and 3 million lives** through starvation and ensuing malaria/cholera epidemics. As Nobel laureate **Amartya Sen** later proved in *Poverty and Famines*, Bengal suffered **no catastrophic aggregate food-supply collapse** relative to 1941—instead, it endured a deadly **entitlement failure** manufactured by colonial wartime priorities:

- **Inflationary Sterling War-Financing:** London financed Allied purchases in India by crediting blocked "Sterling Balances" in London while forcing the Reserve Bank of India to print massive rupee notes domestically—triggering **300–500% rice price inflation** that wiped out the purchasing power of landless laborers, fishermen, barbers, and rural artisans whose wages stayed stagnant.
- **Military Denial Policies & Burma Collapse:** Japan's capture of Burma (1942) halted ~15% of Bengal's rice imports, followed by an October 1942 Midnapore cyclone and fungal crop blight. Simultaneously, anticipating a Japanese landing, British military authorities enforced scorched-earth **"Boat Denial" and "Rice Denial" policies (1942)**—confiscating or destroying over 46,000 river boats and coastal paddy surpluses across southern Bengal, paralyzing rural fishing and intra-delta grain transport.
- **Whitehall Obstruction:** Despite desperate cables from Viceroy Lord Wavell and Secretary of State Leo Amery pleading for emergency wheat shipments, **Winston Churchill's War Cabinet** repeatedly refused shipping allocations, prioritizing European military stockpiles and civilian reserves while inter-provincial Indian trade barriers trapped Bengal in isolation.

Appointed in July 1944, the **Famine Inquiry Commission (Woodhead Commission; Report May 1945)**—chaired by **Sir John Woodhead** (with S.V. Ramamurty, Manilal Nanavati, M. Afzal Husain, and nutritionist W.R. Aykroyd)—conservatively logged ~1.5 million deaths, faulted provincial mismanagement and private speculative hoarding, yet diplomatically shielded Whitehall's refusal to divert shipping.

## 3. Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army (1941–1945)

### Escape from India, the First INA, and Singapore Revival (1941–1943)

Convinced that Britain's wartime peril offered India an external military opening, **Subhas Chandra Bose** escaped house arrest from his Elgin Road residence in Calcutta in **January 1941** disguised as Pathan insurance agent *Mohammad Ziauddin*. Crossing through Peshawar, Kabul, and Moscow, he reached **Berlin (April 1941)**, where he established the **Free India Centre**, launched **Azad Hind Radio** (where he was first hailed **"Netaji"** and popularized the secular greeting ***"Jai Hind"***), and raised the 3,000-strong **Indian Legion (*Indische Legion*)** from Indian POWs captured in North Africa.

Meanwhile, in Southeast Asia, the first **Indian National Army (INA / *Azad Hind Fauj*)** took shape following Britain's humiliating surrender of Malaya and Singapore (February 1942). **Captain Mohan Singh** of the 1/14th Punjab Regiment, working with Japanese intelligence liaison Major Iwaichi Fujiwara, recruited Indian prisoners of war into a liberation army. Veteran revolutionary exile **Rash Behari Bose** convened the **Tokyo Conference (March 1942)** and **Bangkok Conference (June 1942)**, integrating the civilian **Indian Independence League (IIL)** with the formally constituted **INA (September 1, 1942)**. However, bitter friction over Japanese attempts to keep the INA a subservient propaganda tool led to Mohan Singh's arrest in December 1942 and the dissolution of the first INA.

Realizing Hitler's Soviet invasion foreclosed an overland European corridor to India, Subhas Bose undertook a hazardous 90-day voyage aboard German submarine **U-180** and Japanese submarine **I-29**, reaching Tokyo before arriving in **Singapore on July 2, 1943**. Rash Behari Bose formally transferred supreme command of the IIL and INA to Netaji on **July 4–5, 1943**.

### The Provisional Government, Rani of Jhansi Regiment, and Imphal–Kohima (1943–1945)

Under Netaji's electrifying leadership, the INA surged to roughly **45,000–50,000 personnel**, drawing not only POWs but also tens of thousands of civilian Tamil, Malayali, and Punjabi plantation laborers and merchants across Malaya and Burma—forging a fiercely secular military ethos with shared kitchens, Hindu–Muslim–Sikh officer comradeship, and Hindustani commands.

- **Provisional Government of Free India (*Arzi Hukumat-e-Azad Hind*):** Proclaimed in **Singapore on October 21, 1943** (later shifting headquarters to **Rangoon in January 1944**), recognized diplomatically by nine states (Japan, Germany, Italy, Burma, Croatia, Manchukuo, Nanking China, Philippines, and Siam).
- **Symbolic Territory & Regiments:** On **December 30, 1943**, Bose raised the Tricolour at Port Blair after Japan formally handed over administrative control of the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**, renaming them ***Shaheed Dweep*** and ***Swaraj Dweep***. Alongside four infantry brigades named after **Gandhi, Nehru, Azad, and Subhas**, Bose established the pathbreaking all-women combat **Rani of Jhansi Regiment**, commanded by **Captain Dr. Lakshmi Swaminathan (Sahgal)**.
- **Bridging the Ideological Gulf:** On **July 6, 1944**, broadcasting over Azad Hind Radio from Singapore, Bose became the **first leader to publicly address Mahatma Gandhi as "Father of the Nation"**, seeking his blessings for *"India's last war of independence."*
- **Imphal–Kohima Campaign (March–July 1944):** Joining Japan's offensive (*Operation U-Go*) into northeastern India, INA units under **Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal, and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon** crossed the Indo-Burma frontier; on **April 14, 1944**, **Colonel Shaukat Malik** of the Bahadur Group hoisted the Azad Hind flag on liberated Indian soil at **Moirang (Manipur)**. Yet superior Allied air dominance, collapsing Japanese logistics, torrential monsoons, and virulent malaria forced a devastating retreat through Burma.

Following Japan's surrender (August 15, 1945), Subhas Chandra Bose reportedly perished in an aircraft crash at **Taihoku (Taipei), Taiwan on August 18, 1945**. Militarily, the INA failed to breach the Brahmaputra; politically, its post-war trials detonated the British Indian Army's loyalty from inside.

## 4. Constitutional Endgame, Post-War Upsurges, and the Transfer of Power (1944–1947)

### Formulae, Parity, and the Simla Deadlock (1944–1945)

Following Gandhi's medical release (May 1944), three constitutional probes attempted to bridge the Congress–League chasm before the war ended:

- **C.R. Formula (Rajagopalachari Formula, March/July 1944):** Endorsed by Gandhi, it invited the Muslim League to back immediate independence and join an interim national government; *after the war*, a commission would demarcate contiguous Muslim-majority districts in the North-West and East, where a **plebiscite of ALL inhabitants** would decide separation, accompanied by mutual treaties on defence, commerce, and communications if partitioned. During the **Gandhi–Jinnah Talks (September 1944, Bombay)**, Jinnah rejected the plan as offering a *"maimed, mutilated, and moth-eaten Pakistan,"* demanding all six provinces intact, voting restricted solely to Muslims, and prior recognition of the Two-Nation Theory.
- **Desai–Liaquat Pact (January 1945):** Informal blueprint drafted by Congress legislative leader **Bhulabhai Desai** and League deputy **Liaquat Ali Khan**, proposing **equal (40%–40%) Congress–League parity** in an interim central executive (with 20% reserved for minorities). Though officially disowned by both high commands, it fatally normalized the political precedent of **parity between a national party and a communal party**.
- **Wavell Plan & Simla Conference (June 14 – July 14, 1945):** Seeking to break the wartime deadlock ahead of Britain's general election, Viceroy **Lord Wavell** proposed reconstituting the Viceroy's Executive Council with entirely Indian members **except the Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief**, granting **equal representation to Caste Hindus and Muslims**, while keeping Viceregal veto intact under the 1935 Act. The Simla Conference collapsed when **Jinnah insisted the Muslim League alone possessed the exclusive right to nominate ALL Muslim members**—demanding Congress drop its own President, **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, and barring Punjab's Unionist Muslims. By letting Jinnah's veto scuttle the summit, Wavell elevated the League's prestige ahead of the crucial winter elections.

### Three Post-War Mass Upsurges - INA Trials and the RIN Mutiny (Nov 1945 – Feb 1946)

With Labour Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** taking office in July 1945, London scheduled winter elections—only to ignite an explosive tri-service nationalist tempest through a monumental administrative blunder:

- **Red Fort INA Trials (November 1945 – May 1946):** Putting captured INA officers on public court-martial for treason at Delhi's symbolic Red Fort, the Raj chose an unwittingly unifying trio for the opening trial—**Major General Shah Nawaz Khan (Muslim)**, **Colonel Prem Kumar Sahgal (Hindu)**, and **Colonel Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon (Sikh)**. Overnight, anti-colonial solidarity surged across communal boundaries: Congress assembled an iconic **INA Defence Committee** led by **Bhulabhai Desai**, **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Kailash Nath Katju**, **Asaf Ali**, and **Jawaharlal Nehru** (who donned his barrister's gown after twenty-five years). Street protests—including explosive Calcutta strikes (November 1945 and February 1946 *Rashid Ali Day*) uniting Congress, League, and CPI flags—compelled Commander-in-Chief **Sir Claude Auchinleck** to remit their life sentences.
- **Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (February 18–23, 1946):** Triggered aboard signal training ship ***HMIS Talwar*** in Bombay over foul food, racial insults by Commander F.W. King, slow demobilization, and INA trials, the strike spread within 48 hours to **78 warships, 20 shore establishments, and ~20,000 ratings** across Bombay, Karachi (*HMIS Hindustan*), Calcutta, and Madras. Ratings formed a **Naval Central Strike Committee** led by **M.S. Khan**, hoisted **Congress, Muslim League, and CPI Red flags tied together** on mastheads, and drew parallel mutinous strikes in the **Royal Indian Air Force (RIAF)** and **Indian Signal Corps (Jabalpur)**, plus a fierce Bombay working-class hartal (February 22, where British troops killed over 200 civilians). Concerned that armed mutiny would descend into uncontrolled chaos on the eve of transfer negotiations, **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and M.A. Jinnah** jointly persuaded the ratings to surrender on February 23. Yet the imperial Rubicon was crossed: Britain could no longer rely on Indian guns to hold India.

### The 1945–46 Elections, Cabinet Mission, and Direct Action (1945–1946)

Held under the narrow 1935 property franchise and separate electorates, the **Winter 1945–46 Elections** exposed stark communal polarization. Congress swept **91% of non-Muslim votes** (57 of 102 Central seats and ministries in 8 provinces, including Muslim-majority NWFP and Assam), while the **Muslim League swept 86.6% of Muslim votes**—winning all **30 of 30 Muslim Central seats** and 442 of 509 provincial Muslim seats (forming ministries in Bengal and Sind, while a Congress–Unionist–Akali coalition under Khizr Hayat Tiwana blocked it in Punjab).

Dispatching his three-man **Cabinet Mission**—**Lord Pethick-Lawrence** (Secretary of State, Chair), **Sir Stafford Cripps**, and **A.V. Alexander**—who arrived on **March 24, 1946**, Attlee made Britain's final attempt to leave behind a **united India**. Unveiled on **May 16, 1946**, the **Cabinet Mission Plan** proposed:

- **Explicit Rejection of Sovereign Pakistan:** Deemed a separate Pakistan militarily, economically, and demographically unviable (leaving 38% non-Muslims in western Pakistan and 48% in Bengal–Assam, plus splitting the Indian Army).
- **Three-Tier Loose Federal Architecture:**
  1. **Weak Union Centre:** Restricted strictly to **three subjects—Foreign Affairs, Defence, and Communications** (with power to raise necessary finance); **all other subjects and residuary powers vested in the Provinces**, while Princely States retained unceded powers.
  2. **Provincial Grouping (Middle Tier):** Provinces were divided into three Sections to frame group constitutions—**Section A** (Madras, Bombay, UP, Bihar, CP, Orissa — Hindu-majority), **Section B** (Punjab, NWFP, Sind — NW Muslim-majority), and **Section C** (Bengal, Assam — East Muslim-majority).
  3. **Provincial Opt-Out & Reconsideration:** Any province could opt out of its Group *only after* the first general election under the new constitution, and reconsider Union/Group terms at **ten-year intervals**.
- **Constituent Assembly (389 Seats):** Indirectly elected by Provincial Assemblies via proportional representation (292 British Indian provinces + 4 Chief Commissioners' provinces + 93 Princely State nominees, apportioned across General, Muslim, and Sikh communities at ~1 seat per million).

Initially accepted by both parties in June 1946, the Plan collapsed over an irreconcilable ambiguity: **Jinnah insisted Grouping was compulsory** (providing a de facto autonomous Pakistan inside a weak shell), whereas **Congress insisted Grouping was optional** (refusing to coerce Congress-ruled NWFP and Hindu-majority Assam into Sections B and C). When **Jawaharlal Nehru** declared at a Bombay press conference (**July 10, 1946**) that Congress entered the Constituent Assembly sovereign and unfettered—predicting Grouping would likely dissolve—Jinnah revoked acceptance (July 29) and called **"Direct Action Day" on August 16, 1946**.

Direct Action detonated the **Great Calcutta Killings** (4,000–5,000 dead in seventy-two hours under Suhrawardy's League ministry), triggering a catastrophic cycle of retaliatory communal massacres across **Noakhali (October 1946)**, **Bihar (October–November 1946)**, **Garhmukteshwar (UP)**, and **Rawalpindi/Punjab (March 1947)**.

Meanwhile, an **Interim Government** led by **Jawaharlal Nehru** (Vice-President of the Executive Council) took office on **September 2, 1946** (Patel holding Home, Rajendra Prasad Food & Agriculture, Baldev Singh Defence). Hoping to contain violence, Wavell inducted the **Muslim League on October 26, 1946** even though it refused to renounce Direct Action or enter the Constituent Assembly (which opened **December 9, 1946**). Handed the **Finance portfolio**, **Liaquat Ali Khan** weaponized his obstructionist *"Poor Man's Budget"* (1947) and vetoed ministerial approvals from within—convincing hardened pragmatists like Patel and Nehru that an orderly Partition with a **strong centralized Union** was preferable to permanent communal paralysis and civil war.

### Attlee's Deadline, Plan Balkan, Mountbatten Plan, and the 1947 Act

On **February 20, 1947**, Prime Minister **Clement Attlee** shocked Westminster by announcing an irrevocable British withdrawal deadline—**no later than June 30, 1948**—warning that if a fully representative Constituent Assembly failed to materialize, London would transfer power either to a central government or to **existing provincial governments**, while replacing Wavell with **Lord Louis Mountbatten** (who arrived March 22, 1947).

Mountbatten's staff (Lord Ismay and George Abell) initially drafted the notorious **"Dickie Bird Plan" / Plan Balkan (April–May 1947)**, proposing to transfer sovereignty directly to **each individual British Indian Province** (with Punjab and Bengal voting on internal partition) and letting provinces and princely states freely choose whether to join a Union or stand independent. When Mountbatten showed the draft privately to Nehru at Simla on **May 10, 1947**, Nehru reacted with blistering fury, warning it would trigger violent **Balkanization** and civil war.

Scrapping Plan Balkan overnight, Mountbatten turned to Reforms Commissioner **V.P. Menon**, whose redrafted blueprint—approved by Patel, Nehru, Jinnah, and Baldev Singh—was unveiled as the **Mountbatten Plan (June 3 Plan) on June 3, 1947**:

- **Two Sovereign Dominions:** Partition of British India into the Dominions of **India** and **Pakistan**, with Dominion Status granted swiftly (satisfying Congress's desire for a strong central continuity and Britain's desire to retain both states inside the Commonwealth).
- **Provincial Partition Mechanics:** Legislative assemblies of **Punjab and Bengal** would meet in two communal sections (Muslim-majority vs. non-Muslim-majority districts); a simple majority vote in *either* section triggered partition of that province. **Sind's** assembly voted independently; **referendums** were mandated in **NWFP** (boycotted by Ghaffar Khan's *Khudai Khidmatgars*) and **Sylhet district of Assam**.
- **Boundary Commissions & Princely States:** Two Boundary Commissions under **Sir Cyril Radcliffe** were tasked with demarcating Punjab and Bengal borders. British paramountcy over the ~565 Princely States would **lapse**, yet Mountbatten explicitly ruled out independence as a viable third Dominion, pressuring princes to accede to India or Pakistan based on geographical contiguity and demographics.
- **Accelerated Exit Date:** Mountbatten advanced British departure by ten months—to **August 15, 1947** (the second anniversary of Japan's WWII surrender)—leaving barely **72 days** to divide armies, treasuries, railways, and borders.

Westminster enacted the **Indian Independence Act, 1947** (Royal Assent **July 18, 1947**), legally terminating British sovereignty, abolishing the Secretary of State for India, creating Pakistan (August 14) and India (August 15), and transforming both Constituent Assemblies into **fully sovereign legislatures** empowered to repeal any British statute, including the Independence Act itself.

### Historiography - Why Did Britain Leave India in 1947?

Two competing historical frameworks explain Britain's abrupt departure—and an AIR-1 Mains answer synthesizes both cleanly:

- **Imperial Exhaustion & Global Realignment (Revisionist / Cambridge–Economic School — John Gallagher, Anil Seal, B.R. Tomlinson):** World War II bankrupted Britain, flipping India from a colonial debtor into Britain's **largest wartime creditor** (accumulating ~£1.3 billion in blocked Sterling Balances). India's declining value as a captive market for Lancashire textiles, US Cold War anti-colonial pressure, and the fiscal impossibility of funding post-war British welfare alongside imperial garrisons rendered the Raj a depreciating liability.
- **Collapse of Colonial Hegemony & Coercive Pillars (Nationalist / Marxist School — Bipan Chandra, Sumit Sarkar):** Economic exhaustion alone never forced rapid retreat elsewhere—Britain fought brutal counter-insurgencies to retain Malaya and Kenya deep into the 1950s. In India, decades of Gandhian mass mobilization hollowed out moral consent, **Quit India** shattered rural administrative confidence, British recruitment to the **ICS collapsed** (leaving fewer than 600 British officers by 1946), and—decisively—the **INA Trials and 1946 RIN/RIAF/Army unrest** demonstrated that the **Indian Armed Forces** would no longer shoot countrymen to prolong colonial rule. As Clement Attlee later acknowledged candidly to Calcutta Chief Justice P.B. Chakraborty, the erosion of loyalty among Indian troops made Britain's military position untenable, forcing a strategic retreat to salvage Commonwealth goodwill.

## 6. Foundational Ideological Clashes - Competing Visions of Modern India (GS1 / GS4 / Essay Core)

India's independence struggle never marched under a monolithic doctrinal flag. Four towering intellectual confrontations—each pitting **Mahatma Gandhi** against a formidable rival vision—shaped the moral, constitutional, economic, and cultural architecture of the republic.

### 1. Gandhi vs. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar - Caste Emancipation and the Indian Village

Both leaders shared an uncompromising commitment to ending the degradation of India's untouchables, yet diagnosed its root cause and cure from opposite poles. For Gandhi, untouchability was a moral sin—an *"excrescence"* distorting an originally functional, non-hierarchical **Varna** order of hereditary duty; liberation required a **change of heart** among caste Hindus through penance, temple entry, and decentralized village republics (*Gram Swaraj*).

For Ambedkar—arguing forcefully in ***Annihilation of Caste* (1936)**—caste was not a benign division of labor but a graded **division of laborers** structurally sanctified by the *Shastras* themselves. Moral persuasion without destroying scripture-backed endogamy was futile. Where Gandhi romanticized the self-sufficient Indian village, Ambedkar famously denounced the Indian village on the Constituent Assembly floor as *"a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism"*—demanding **urbanization, industrialization, secular constitutional rights, statutory reservations, and state-led economic empowerment** as the true engines of Dalit emancipation.

### 2. Gandhi vs. Jawaharlal Nehru - Modernity, Industrialization, and the State

The Gandhi–Nehru partnership combined deep personal affection with profound philosophical disagreement laid bare in their famous **October 1945 exchange of letters** revisiting ***Hind Swaraj* (1909)**. Gandhi distrusted the centralized modern state (calling it *"violence in a concentrated and organized form"*) and large-scale capitalist machinery, fearing both bred unemployment, ecological ruin, and alienation; his ideal economy rested on decentralized **village industries, *khadi*, voluntary limitation of wants, and *Trusteeship***.

Nehru—an avowed rationalist inspired by European scientific humanism and Soviet planning—retorted candidly that a backward agrarian village could never defend national sovereignty or abolish mass poverty. Independent India required **modern heavy industry, large hydroelectric dams, scientific temper, centralized Five-Year Planning, and an active welfare state**. Yet their underlying convergence on **inclusive secularism, parliamentary democracy, anti-fascist internationalism, and humane egalitarianism** enabled Nehru to inherit Gandhi's mantle while steering India toward industrial modernity.

### 3. Gandhi vs. Rabindranath Tagore - Nationalism, *Swadeshi*, and Education

Conducted publicly in the pages of *Modern Review* and *Young India* (1921, 1925, and 1934), the **Gandhi–Tagore debate** remains one of the highest intellectual exchanges of the twentieth century—a dialogue between two close friends who called each other *Mahatma* and *Gurudev*.

- **Nationalism vs. Cosmopolitan Universalism:** While Gandhi viewed Indian nationalism as a necessary moral stepping-stone to international brotherhood (*"one cannot be an internationalist without being a nationalist"*), Tagore warned that fierce, exclusive **Nationalism** was a Western mechanical idol—a *"geographical demon" (Bhougolik Apadevata)* breeding chauvinism and blind conformity. Tagore sharply criticized Gandhi's **1921 bonfires of foreign cloth** (calling destruction of fabric while millions went naked morally cruel and economically wasteful) and questioned the coercive herd mentality of mandatory daily *charkha* spinning.
- **Pedagogy & Science:** Both rejected Macaulay's sterile colonial exam-factory and championed mother-tongue instruction, yet diverged sharply on purpose. Gandhi's ***Nai Talim* / Wardha Scheme (1937)** centered on **vocational craft-based basic education** fostering self-reliance and rural dignity (and he backed Non-Cooperation boycotts of colonial schools), whereas Tagore's **Santiniketan and *Visva-Bharati* (1921)** championed **unfettered aesthetic creativity, harmony with nature, global East–West synthesis, and open scientific inquiry**—leading Tagore to condemn student school-boycotts as negative withdrawal and to publicly rebuke Gandhi in **1934** when Gandhi attributed the catastrophic **Bihar Earthquake** to divine punishment for untouchability (Tagore insisting physical calamities obey natural laws, not moral retribution).

### 4. Gandhi vs. Subhas Chandra Bose and V.D. Savarkar - Means, Ends, and Nationhood

- **Gandhi vs. Subhas Chandra Bose (Means vs. Ends & Political Economy):** Both sought complete independence (*Purna Swaraj*), strict secular communal harmony, and women's empowerment—evident in Bose addressing Gandhi as **"Father of the Nation"** (1944) and Gandhi calling Bose **"the Prince among Patriots."** Yet their political philosophies diverged decisively: for Gandhi, **means and ends were convertible terms**—freedom won through violence or Axis collaboration would corrupt the republic itself. For Bose, **ends justified tactical realpolitik** (*"my enemy's enemy is my friend"*), justifying armed exile liberation via Germany and Japan. Economically and politically, Gandhi's decentralized *Gram Swaraj* clashed with Bose's vision (*Samyavada*) of rapid **state-directed heavy industrialization, radical land redistribution, and a temporary authoritarian-disciplined state** during post-independence reconstruction.
- **Gandhi vs. V.D. Savarkar (Civic–Pluralist vs. Cultural–Civilizational Nationalism):** In ***Essentials of Hindutva* (1923)**, Vinayak Damodar Savarkar defined Indian nationhood not through civic territorial residency alone, but through shared ancestry (*Rashtra*), culture (*Sanskriti*), and the dual test of ***Pitribhumi* (Fatherland) and *Punyabhumi* (Holyland)**—treating Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, and Jains as organically bound to India while viewing Muslims and Christians as holding sacred loyalties outside it. Furthermore, Savarkar championed realpolitik and the **militarization of Hindu society** (actively encouraging wartime recruitment into the British Indian Army during WWII to gain military training), rejecting Gandhian *Ahimsa* as enfeebling. Gandhi directly repudiated the Two-Nation/cultural-exclusivist premise in *Hind Swaraj* and across his life: for Gandhi, **India was a composite territorial-civic civilization** where religions were distinct ethical paths toward the same Truth, and genuine *Swaraj* demanded fearless moral non-violence (*Satyagraha*) rather than militarized majoritarianism.

> - **GOI Act 1935 Residuary & Reserved Traps (2018 & 2024 Prelims):** Residuary powers under the 1935 Act were vested exclusively in the **Governor-General** (NOT Federal Legislature or Provinces). Moreover, **Defence and Foreign Affairs** were **Reserved subjects** under the Governor-General's executive counsellors—NOT under the control of the Federal Legislature! Finally, the All-India Federation and Central Dyarchy **never came into operation** because Princely States did not accede.
> - **Forward Bloc Formation Factors (2026 Prelims Trap):** Subhas Bose formed the Forward Bloc (May 1939) **inside Congress** due to losing Gandhi's confidence, a disunited Congress Left, and **CSP (JP) + M.N. Royists prioritizing Congress unity** during the Pant Resolution—whereas the **Communists actually supported Bose** (eliminate statements claiming Communists opposed him at Tripuri!).
> - **Lahore Resolution (1940) Textual Precision:** Moved by **A.K. Fazlul Haq** (not Jinnah!), drafted initially by **Sikandar Hayat Khan**; demanded *"Independent States"* (plural)—the word **"Pakistan"** does **not** appear anywhere in the text.
> - **August Offer (1940) vs. Cripps Mission (1942) Constituent Assembly Nuance (2022 Prelims):** August Offer accepted a post-war constitution-making body *"mainly"* of Indians; Cripps upgraded it to *"solely"* Indians—with members **ELECTED by Provincial Assemblies** and **NOMINATED by Princely States** (UPSC traps aspirants by claiming Provincial Assemblies *nominated* members!).
> - **Individual Satyagraha Sequence (1940):** 1st **Acharya Vinoba Bhave**, 2nd **Jawaharlal Nehru**, 3rd **Brahma Dutt** (never confuse Brahma Dutt with Vallabhbhai Patel or Rajendra Prasad).
> - **Quit India Non-Participants & Parallel Governments:** **Muslim League, CPI, Hindu Mahasabha, RSS, and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** abstained from Quit India. Chavan — longest running), **Talcher** (Pabitra Mohan Pradhan).
> - **INA Chronology & Officers (2021 Prelims):** INA was first conceived by **Captain Mohan Singh** (1941–42) and organized politically by **Rash Behari Bose** (Bangkok 1942) *before* Subhas Bose took command in Singapore (July 1943). **Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal, and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon** were the Hindu–Muslim–Sikh INA officer trio tried together at the **Red Fort (Nov 1945)**—not Interim Government ministers or Constituent Assembly framers.
> - **Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) Exact Features (2015 Prelims):** It **explicitly rejected Pakistan**, recommended a **three-tier weak Federal Government** (Centre holding **only Defence, Foreign Affairs, and Communications**; residuary powers to **Provinces**), and did **NOT** enlarge Indian court powers or expand Indian recruitment into the ICS.
> - **Plan Balkan vs. June 3 Plan:** **Plan Balkan (Dickie Bird Plan, May 1947)** proposed transferring power to **individual provinces** (vehemently rejected by Nehru at Simla); the **Mountbatten / June 3 Plan (redrafted by V.P. Menon)** replaced it with clean transfer to **two sovereign Dominions** (India and Pakistan).
> - **1934 Bihar Earthquake & "Father of the Nation" Origins:** **Rabindranath Tagore** publicly criticized Gandhi's moral-sin explanation of the 1934 Bihar Earthquake; **Subhas Chandra Bose** was the first to publicly address Gandhi as **"Father of the Nation"** (July 6, 1944, Azad Hind Radio, Singapore).

> - **GS2 Polity (Constitutional Lineage):** The **GOI Act, 1935** supplied India's three-list division, Federal Court precursor, RBI, PSCs, and emergency/gubernatorial template (Article 356 echoing Section 93), yet independent India deliberately flipped two 1935/1946 choices—vesting **residuary powers in Parliament (Article 248)** rather than the Governor-General or Provinces, and rejecting the Cabinet Mission's weak Centre after Partition removed the compulsion to appease the League.
> - **GS3 Economy & Disaster Governance:** The **1938 National Planning Committee** (Bose–Nehru) directly seeded independent India's Planning Commission, while Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis of the **1943 Bengal Famine** remains the foundational Mains benchmark for India's Public Distribution System (PDS), buffer stocks, and food-security jurisprudence.
> - **GS4 Ethics & Essay (Means–Ends & Competing Visions):** The **Gandhi–Ambedkar, Gandhi–Tagore, Gandhi–Nehru, and Gandhi–Bose** dialogues supply supreme, nuanced ethical frameworks for GS4 case studies (deontological purity of means vs. utilitarian outcomes; moral persuasion vs. structural legal justice) and philosophical Essays on nationalism, modernity, and education.
