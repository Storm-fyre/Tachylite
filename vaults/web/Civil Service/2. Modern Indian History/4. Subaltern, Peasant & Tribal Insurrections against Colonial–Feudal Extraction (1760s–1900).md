# Chapter 4 - Subaltern, Peasant & Tribal Insurrections against Colonial–Feudal Extraction (1760s–1900)

Between the acquisition of the Bengal *Diwani* in 1765 and the dawn of the twentieth century, British rule in India never enjoyed a single decade of uncontested rural peace. Long before urban elites assembled in Bombay in 1885 to draft constitutional petitions, peasants, forest-dwelling *Adivasis*, disbanded militias, and dispossessed landed magnates waged over a hundred localized wars against the East India Company and the Crown.

Conventional colonial archives dismissed these ruptures as "fanaticism," "plunder," or law-and-order "disturbances." Modern historiography reveals a far sharper structural reality: these insurrections were rational, organized counter-attacks against the systematic dismantling of India’s customary agrarian and ecological order.

## Architecture of Subaltern Insurgency - The Sarkar-Sahukar-Zamindar Triad

### Competing Historiographical Lenses

Four distinct analytical frameworks compete to decode nineteenth-century rural resistance, each exposing a different layer of causal mechanics:

- **Colonialist / Administrative School:** Framed uprisings as "pre-political" spasms of primitive criminality, tribal irrationality, or religious fanaticism, deliberately severing violence from revenue extraction to justify coercive pacification.
- **Nationalist School:** Treated pre-1857 revolts as heroic, proto-nationalist rehearsals culminating in 1857 and later Gandhi's mass politics—an approach that honors rebel courage but often retrofits modern nation-state consciousness onto fiercely localized territorial loyalties.
- **Marxist School (A.R. Desai, Kathleen Gough, Sumit Sarkar):** Located the primary motor in agrarian class contradiction triggered by capitalist-colonial commercialization, cash-rent rigidity, and landlord-moneylender expropriation.
- **Subaltern Studies School (Ranajit Guha):** In *Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India* (1983), Ranajit Guha demolished Eric Hobsbawm’s thesis that peasant rebels were "pre-political." Guha demonstrated that subaltern insurgency operated with an autonomous, disciplined political consciousness entirely independent of elite nationalist leadership.

### Guha’s Structural Grammar and the Extractive Triad

To the nineteenth-century cultivator or forest dweller, colonial power did not arrive as an abstract constitutional theory; it materialized as an interlocking triangle of domination—the ***Sarkar–Sahukar–Zamindar*** triad (morphing into the ***Sarkar–Diku*** nexus in tribal heartlands). The **Sarkar** (colonial magistrate, police *daroga*, and civil court) enforced absolute private property and inflexible cash revenue.

The **Zamindar** (or revenue farmer/*thikadar*) extracted rack-rents and illegal cesses (*abwabs*), while the **Sahukar** (*mahajan*/moneylender) captured peasant surpluses through usurious compound debt backed by colonial debt-recovery decrees. Because each node reinforced the other two, attacking a moneylender's ledger or a zamindar's *cutcherry* was functionally an attack on the colonial state itself.

Guha mapped this insurgent logic across **six elementary structural codes**:
1. **Negation:** Rebels defined their identity inversely by smashing the status markers of their oppressors (seizing palanquins, burning land deeds, humiliating police *darogas*).
2. **Ambiguity:** Colonial law criminalized collective political revolt as mere "dacoity" or "riot," masking intentional political insurrection behind crime statistics.
3. **Modality:** Uprisings were conspicuously public, communal, and ritualized—preceded by collective assemblies, drumbeats (*nagada*), and deliberate destruction rather than stealthy theft.
4. **Solidarity:** Kinship, caste, clan, and sectarian bonds operated as horizontal mobilization grids, frequently enforcing strict social boycotts against collaborators.
5. **Transmission:** Insurgency spread rapidly through non-literate visual and acoustic signals—circulating *Sal* tree branches, chapatis, lotus flowers, drums, and itinerant preachers.
6. **Territoriality:** Mobilization remained anchored in ancestral ethnic-ecological domains (*Damin-i-Koh*, *Kolhan*, *Jungle Mahals*), giving rebellions extraordinary local intensity while capping their ability to coordinate pan-Indian campaigns.

### The 1857 Structural Inflection Point

A sharp tactical discontinuity divides pre-1857 and post-1857 rural resistance. Before 1857, revolts were largely **restorative, armed, and frequently led by displaced feudal elites or millenarian prophets** seeking to resurrect the pre-colonial moral economy.

After 1858, Queen Victoria’s Proclamation forged a conservative alliance co-opting princely rulers, zamindars, and *taluqdars* as imperial pillars. Stripped of feudal leadership, **settled peasants emerged as autonomous protagonists** in the Indigo, Pabna, Deccan, and Assam movements—pivoting toward disciplined rent strikes, legal litigation funds, targeted debt-bond destruction, and early tactical convergence with urban middle-class intellectuals. Tribal communities, however, remained locked in armed millenarian warfare through 1900 because colonial forest laws (1865 and 1878) accelerated ecological dispossession precisely in the post-1857 decades.

## Early Civil, Feudal and Deposed-Military Resistances

Between 1763 and 1856, the Company’s rapid fiscal militarism dismantled pre-colonial agrarian power-sharing. Revenue demands escalated sharply in cash, hereditary tax-free service lands held by local militias (*Paiks*, *Poligars*, *Ghatwals*, *Gadkaris*, *Ramosis*) were resumed, and traditional chiefs lost estates to Calcutta and Madras auction speculators. The resulting civil rebellions fused dispossessed gentry with impoverished peasantry.

### Sanyasi–Fakir Rebellion (Bengal & Bihar, 1763–1800)

The earliest prolonged challenge to Company governance erupted in the wake of the 1765 *Diwani* grant and the catastrophic **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**. Bands of armed Hindu ***Dasnami Naga* sanyasis** and Muslim ***Madari* fakirs**—itinerant mendicants who historically collected alms from Bengal zamindars, engaged in silk/money trade, and served as mercenary soldiers—found their customary pilgrimage routes taxed and their patron zamindars bankrupted by Company extraction.

Displaced peasants, unemployed soldiers from dismantled Nawabi armies, and starving artisans joined their ranks across Rangpur, Dinajpur, Bogra, Dacca, and Mymensingh. Prominent leaders included **Majnu Shah** (the central Fakir strategist until 1786), **Musa Shah**, **Chirag Ali**, **Bhawani Pathak**, and **Devi Chaudhurani**—highlighting both Hindu–Muslim tactical solidarity and female combatant leadership.

Rebels systematically raided Company revenue treasuries, grain kothis, and factories using guerrilla mobility. Governor-General **Warren Hastings** deployed sustained military columns over decades to suppress the insurgency by 1800. A century later, **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** immortalized (and selectively Hinduized) the revolt in his landmark novel ***Anandamath* (1882)**—source of *Vande Mataram*—and in ***Devi Chaudhurani* (1884)**.

### Chuar Rebellion or Jungle Mahal Uprisings (Bengal–Jharkhand Border, 1766–1816; Coda 1832–33)

Colonial officials used the derogatory Bengali epithet *"Chuar"* ("barbaric" or "uncivilized robber") to label the **Bhumij** tribal cultivators, hunters, and hereditary village guards (*Paiks* and *Ghatwals*) inhabiting the dense **Jungle Mahals** spanning Midnapore, Bankura, Manbhum, Barabhum, and Dhalbhum. Modern scholarship increasingly designates this protracted six-decade conflict as the **Jungle Mahal Revolt**.

Triggered by famine, exorbitant post-1765 revenue demands, forced auctions of ancestral jungle estates, and the **resumption of rent-free *Paikan* and *Ghatwali* service lands** following Cornwallis’s 1793 police reforms, the revolt unfolded in rolling waves:
- **First Wave (1766–1772):** Sparked in Dhalbhum/Ghatsila by **Raja Jagannath Singh (Jagannath Dhal)** and backed by Chuar sardars **Subal Singh** (Kaliapal) and **Shyam Ganjam** (Dhadka), forcing temporary Company capitulation in Midnapore.
- **Peak Wave (1798–1799):** Dispossessed Raipur zamindar **Durjan Singh** mobilized over 1,500 armed *Paiks* to halt estate auctions, establishing rebel control over 30 villages while **Rani Shiromani of Karnagarh** turned her estate into the rebellion's financial and strategic nerve center until her arrest in April 1799.
- **Institutional Aftermath and Coda:** Intense resistance forced the British to carve out a separate **Jungle Mahals District (1805)** and temporarily restore *Paikan* tenures, though grievances reignited in the **Bhumij Revolt (*Ganga Narayan Hungama*, 1832–33)** under **Ganga Narayan Singh**, prompting the reorganization of the tract under the South-West Frontier Agency.

### Paika Rebellion of Khurda (Odisha, 1817)

The ***Paikas*** ("foot soldiers") were a hereditary landed peasant-militia recruited since the sixteenth century by Odisha’s Gajapati kings from diverse social groups in exchange for rent-free military tenures (***nish-kar jagirs***). Following the British conquest of Odisha from the Marathas in 1803 and the dethronement of Raja **Mukunda Deva II** of Khurda (after his minister Jayee Rajguru’s 1804 resistance and execution), the Company abolished *Paika* estates and imposed ruinous short-term revenue settlements.

Three acute economic shocks welded the *Paikas* to the wider Odia peasantry and tribal Khonds of Ghumusar: (1) extortionate revenue assessments auctioning estates to absentee Bengali speculators; (2) the **colonial salt monopoly**, driving up prices of an essential coastal commodity; and (3) the **abolition of indigenous cowrie shell currency** in favor of scarce silver *Sicca* rupees for tax payments.

In March 1817, **Bakshi Jagabandhu Bidyadhar Mahapatra**—the dispossessed hereditary military commander (*Bakshi*) of Khurda—rallied *Paikas*, Khonds, and ryots under the unifying cultural emblem of **Lord Jagannath**. Rebels torched the Banpur police station and treasury, captured Khurda and Puri, and fought pitched engagements before superior British reinforcements crushed the rising by 1818; Jagabandhu waged guerrilla warfare until surrendering under negotiated terms in 1825.

> **Epistemic & Contemporary Status Note:** Following the bicentenary (2017), Odisha demanded that the Paika *Bidroha* be designated India’s "First War of Independence." In December 2021, the Union Ministry of Culture clarified in Parliament—citing ICHR advice—that while it cannot replace 1857 as the First War of Independence, it stands recognized as a pioneering popular anti-colonial uprising. A renewed public controversy erupted in July 2025 over its omission from the revised NCERT Class 8 History textbook.

### Tamil Poligar Wars (*Palayakkarar* Revolt, 1795–1805) and Pazhassi Raja

In Tamil country, ***Palayakkarars* (Poligars)** were autonomous military chieftains originally instituted under Vijayanagara and Madurai Nayaka governance, holding sovereign taxation, judicial, and village-policing (*Kaval*) rights. When the Nawab of Arcot assigned Tinnevelly and Carnatic revenue collection to the East India Company, the Poligars rejected the Company's claim to sovereignty as illegitimate usurpation.

- **Early Pioneer:** **Puli Thevar** of Nerkattumseval led the first anti-Company Poligar confederacy (1755–1767) before the decisive turn-of-the-century wars.
- **First Poligar War (1799):** **Veerapandiya Kattabomman**, Poligar of **Panchalankurichi**, defied Collector Jackson's humiliating demands, fought Major Bannerman’s siege, and—after betrayal by the Raja of Pudukkottai—was publicly hanged at Kayathar in October 1799.
- **Second Poligar War (1800–1801):** Far wider in geographic ambition, this pan-South Indian confederacy united **Marudu Pandiyar Brothers** (Periya Marudu and Chinna Marudu of Sivaganga), Kattabomman’s deaf-mute brother **Oomaithurai**, Gopala Nayak of Dindigul, and Dhoondaji Wagh of Mysore. In June 1801, the Marudu brothers issued the historic **Tiruchirappalli Proclamation**, pasting manifestos on the Srirangam temple and Nawab's palace urging Hindus, Muslims, and castes across South India to unite against European rule.
- **Outcome:** Brutal British suppression culminated in the execution of the Marudu brothers (October 1801), demolition of Poligar forts, and the **Carnatic Treaty (1801)** transferring direct control to the Company. Disbanded Poligar soldiers and exiled royal families concentrated at Vellore directly fueled the **Vellore Mutiny of 1806**.

Across the Western Ghats in Malabar, **Kerala Varma Pazhassi Raja** of Kottayam simultaneously waged the protracted **Cotiote War (1797–1805)** from the Wayanad jungles—mobilizing Nair warriors and tribal **Kurichiyar** archers against British revenue extortion and pepper monopolies until dying in combat in November 1805.

### Velu Thampi Dalawa’s Revolt (Travancore, 1808–1809)

In 1805, Lord Wellesley coerced Travancore into a harsh revision of its **Subsidiary Alliance**, doubling subsidy demands while Resident **Colonel Colin Macaulay** meddled aggressively in internal state appointments. Prime Minister (*Dalawa*) **Velu Thampi**, originally pro-British, broke with the Company when arrears mounted and Macaulay demanded his dismissal.

Forging a military alliance with **Paliath Achan**, Prime Minister of Cochin, Velu Thampi launched armed strikes on the British Residency at Cochin and issued the celebrated **Kundara Proclamation (January 11, 1809)**. Addressing subjects in fiery Malayalam prose, he warned that British rule would strip Travancore of its land, salt, trade, and religious traditions, summoning every able-bodied subject to armed resistance. Outgunned by British reinforcements marching through the Aramboly Pass, Velu Thampi retreated to the Mannadi Bhagavathy temple and committed suicide in March 1809 to evade capture.

### Kittur Rebellion - Rani Chennamma and Sangolli Rayanna (Karnataka, 1824–1830)

More than two decades before Lord Dalhousie formalized the Doctrine of Lapse, the Bombay Presidency deployed its logic against the small Lingayat principality of **Kittur** (Belagavi district). When Raja Mallasarja died leaving no biological son, the Company refused to recognize his adopted heir **Shivalingappa** and moved to confiscate the treasury.

**Rani Chennamma** rejected annexation and routed the initial Company assault in October 1824, killing Dharwad Collector **St. John Thackeray** and capturing British officers. A massive second expedition breached Kittur fort in December 1824; Chennamma was imprisoned in Bailhongal fort where she died in 1829.

Resistance did not end with royal defeat. Her subaltern military lieutenant **Sangolli Rayanna**—a village *Shet-sanadi* (watchman/militia leader) from the Kuruba community—rebuilt a peasant guerrilla force between 1829 and 1830, torching government offices, raiding treasuries, and rallying landless tenants until betrayal led to his hanging at Nandagad in January 1831.

### Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy (Rayalaseema, Andhra, 1846–1847)

After acquiring the Ceded Districts (Rayalaseema) from the Nizam in 1800, Thomas Munro systematically abolished the hereditary military-fiscal powers of local *Poligars* while promising subsistence pensions. By the 1840s, the Company resumed ancestral *Inam* (tax-free) lands and denied hereditary pension transfers to **Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy**, descendant of the Nossam Poligar lineage in Kurnool district.

In July 1846, Narasimha Reddy mobilized nearly 5,000 dispossessed *Kattubadis* (village militia), Boya and Chenchu forest fighters, and distressed peasants, storming the **Koilkuntla treasury** and fighting British detachments across the Nallamala hills. Captured through local treachery, he was publicly hanged at Koilkuntla in February 1847—his head displayed in an iron cage for thirty years to deter revolt.

### Ramosi, Gadkari and Sawantwadi Uprisings (Western India, 1822–1879)

The collapse of the Maratha Peshwa in 1818 triggered acute unemployment among hill-fort militias and mountain communities across the Sahyadris:

- **Ramosi Uprisings (Satara & Poona):** The **Ramosis**, hill tribes serving as fort guards and night-watchmen under the Marathas, rose in **1822 under Chittur Singh**, plundering the Satara countryside. Renewed waves broke out in **1825–26 and 1829** under **Umaji Naik** and **Bapu Trimbakji Sawant** during severe famine; the British pacified them through land grants and recruitment into hill police. Half a century later, in **1879**, Chitpavan Brahmin military-accounts clerk **Vasudev Balwant Phadke** organized the ***Ramosi Krishak Jatha*** to fund an armed anti-British uprising following the devastating 1876–78 Deccan famine—serving as the critical historical bridge between traditional hill insurgency and modern revolutionary nationalism (Phadke was deported to Aden, dying on hunger strike in 1883).
- **Gadkari Revolt (Kolhapur, 1844):** The **Gadkaris** were hereditary garrison soldiers holding rent-free lands attached to Maratha hill forts. When British-installed administrator **Daji Krishna Pandit** disbanded the garrisons and placed their lands under direct *Mamlatdar* supervision, the Gadkaris seized the forts of **Samangarh** and **Bhudargarh**, holding out against British columns for months.
- **Sawantwadi Revolt (1844):** Simultaneously in coastal Konkan, Maratha noble **Phond Sawant** and his son **Anna Saheb** rallied local *Desais* and peasants against the British deposition of their ruler and heavy administrative exactions, requiring martial law to restore Company control.

### Ahom Revolt (Assam, 1828–1830)

During the First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–26), the East India Company pledged to withdraw from Assam after expelling Burmese invaders. Following the **Treaty of Yandabo (1826)**, however, the Company retained control of Lower Assam and moved to incorporate Upper Assam into Bengal’s revenue orbit, dismantling the traditional Ahom nobility and *Paik* aristocracy.

In **1828**, Ahom prince **Gomdhar Konwar**—backed by **Dhanjoy Borgohain** and former nobles—staged a formal enthronement near Jorhat and marched toward Rangpur before Captain Rutherford dispersed the poorly armed rebels. A second conspiracy in **1830** under **Kumar Rupchand** and **Piyali Phukan** sought alliances with Khasi, Singpho, and Khampti chiefs. Alarmed by relentless frontier instability, the Company adopted a conciliatory pivot in **1833**, restoring Upper Assam to **Maharaja Purandar Singha** as a tributary state before annexing it permanently in 1838 on pretexts of tribute default.

## Socio-Religious Peasant Movements - Millenarian Faith as Agrarian Protest

In early and mid-nineteenth-century rural India, secular trade unions and political parties did not exist. When colonial land tenures shattered peasant livelihoods, **religious revivalism provided the only available grammar of mass solidarity, moral outrage, and trans-village organization**. Though phrased in puritanical or millenarian theology, these movements attacked concrete material enemies: European planters, rack-renting zamindars, and colonial revenue machinery.

### Wahabi Movement (*Tariqah-i-Muhammadiyah*, 1820s–1870s)

Inspired by the eighteenth-century Delhi theologian **Shah Waliullah** and his son **Shah Abdul Aziz** (alongside Arabian Wahabi puritanism), **Syed Ahmad of Rae Bareli** launched India’s most organized underground socio-religious resistance in the 1820s. Declaring British-ruled India ***Dar-ul-Harb*** (abode of war/unbelief) that had to be restored to ***Dar-ul-Islam***, Syed Ahmad sought to purify Islam of syncretic accretions while building an armed political network.

His movement operated through a sophisticated two-node architecture: a covert recruitment, ideological, and financial **headquarters at Patna**—brilliantly managed after his death by brothers **Vilayat Ali and Inayat Ali**—linked via secret couriers to a militant frontier operational base at **Sithana** in the North-West Frontier. Initially directing its *jihad* against Sikh rule in Punjab, Syed Ahmad was killed fighting Ranjit Singh’s forces at the **Battle of Balakot (1831)**.

Following the British annexation of Punjab in 1849, the Wahabis turned their entire apparatus exclusively against British colonial rule, fighting frontier campaigns throughout the 1850s and 1860s. Colonial intelligence dismantled the pan-Indian courier grid only through the sweeping **Ambala Trial (1864)** and **Patna Trial (1865)**, sentencing leaders to transportation to the Andamans. So deep was colonial anxiety that Viceroy Mayo commissioned W.W. Hunter to write *The Indian Musalmans* (1871), catalyzing the imperial shift toward wooing conservative Muslim elites.

### Faraizi Movement (East Bengal, 1818–1862)

Founded in **Faridpur** (East Bengal) by **Haji Shariatullah** in 1818, the **Faraizi** movement initially demanded strict adherence to ***Faraiz*** (obligatory Islamic duties) and renunciation of un-Islamic rural customs. Declaring British Bengal *Dar-ul-Harb*, Shariatullah suspended congregational Friday (*Jumu'ah*) and Eid prayers—a potent symbolic denial of colonial political legitimacy.

Under his son **Muhsinuddin Ahmad**, popularly known as **Dudu Miyan (1840–1862)**, the movement underwent a decisive agrarian-revolutionary transformation. Dudu Miyan proclaimed an egalitarian theology asserting that **"all land belongs to God"** (*Jami Allah-er*), rendering zamindari rent claims and illegal cesses (*abwabs*—including contributions to Durga Puja) religiously illegitimate.

He divided East Bengal (Faridpur, Dacca, Bakarganj, Tippera) into administrative circles (*Halkas*) under local ***Khalifas***, establishing parallel village panchayats to boycott British courts and organizing armed *lathiyal* clubs against oppressive Hindu zamindars and European indigo planters. Repeatedly imprisoned without lasting conviction until his death in 1862, Dudu Miyan left a disciplined peasant base that largely merged into the Wahabi stream and foreshadowed the Pabna leagues.

### Titu Mir’s Movement (Barasat / 24 Parganas, West Bengal, 1831)

**Mir Nithar Ali (Titu Mir)**, a former wrestler and disciple of Syed Ahmad Barelvi, fused Wahabi reformism with sharp agrarian protest among impoverished Muslim peasants and weavers across **Barasat and Nadia** (24 Parganas, West Bengal). His followers challenged both European indigo planters and local zamindars—most infamously **Krishna Deb Ray of Purwa**, who attempted to break the movement by levying a humiliating **₹2.50 beard tax** on every bearded follower.

When petitions to Company magistrates failed, Titu Mir declared Company rule ended and constructed an iconic bamboo fortification—***Bansher Kella***—at **Narkelberia**, collecting revenue directly and repelling zamindari militias. In November 1831, British troops under Colonel Stewart blasted the bamboo fort with heavy artillery, killing Titu Mir and dozens of peasant defenders. Though colonial officers labeled it a communal riot, the rebellion targeted structural agrarian exploiters—sparing poor Hindu ryots while attacking both oppressive zamindars and European indigo factories.

### Pagal Panthis (Mymensingh / Sherpur, East Bengal, 1825–1833)

In the northern borderlands of Mymensingh district (Sherpur pargana), a semi-religious, syncretic mystic named **Karam Shah** founded the **Pagal Panthi** sect, preaching universal brotherhood, truth, and equality primarily among marginalized **Garo, Hajong, and Hadi** tribal-peasant cultivators.

When local zamindars imposed crippling rent enhancements and arbitrary *abwabs* after the First Anglo-Burmese War, Karam Shah’s son **Tipu Shah** turned the sect into a militant tax-resistance army. Declaring a ceiling on lawful rent, Tipu’s followers captured Sherpur in 1825, drove out zamindars, appointed their own judges, and established a short-lived egalitarian administration before British military columns crushed the sect by 1833.

### Kuka / Namdhari Movement (Western Punjab, 1840–1872)

Founded in 1840 in western Punjab (Hazro) by **Bhagat Jawahar Mal (Sian Saheb)** and expanded by **Baba Balak Singh**, the **Namdhari (Kuka)** movement began as a puritanical Sikh social-reform wave: abolishing caste hierarchies, child marriage, female infanticide, dowry, and alcohol/meat consumption while championing widow remarriage and simple *Anand Karaj* weddings.

After the British annexation of Punjab (1849), the movement acquired a fiercely anti-colonial political program under **Baba Ram Singh** (who founded the Namdhari center at **Bhaini Sahib**, Ludhiana, in 1857). Remarkably predating Gandhian politics by half a century, Baba Ram Singh enforced strict ***Swadeshi* and Non-Cooperation**—boycotting British courts, government schools, railways, postal services, and imported Manchester textiles in favor of handspun white *khaddar*, while running parallel village judicial *Subas* and private courier networks.

Militant Kukas also targeted colonial-licensed cattle slaughterhouses as symbols of imperial disrespect at Amritsar, Raikot, and **Malerkotla (January 1872)**. Following a clash at Malerkotla, Ludhiana Deputy Commissioner **L. Cowan**—acting without trial or higher sanction—ordered 49 Kuka prisoners blown from the mouths of cannons (followed by 16 more under Commissioner Forsyth), while **Baba Ram Singh** was deported without trial to Rangoon, where he died in 1885.

## Tribal (Adivasi) Insurrections - Forest Enclosure, Beth Begari and Diku Intrusion

Tribal uprisings across Chotanagpur, the Eastern and Western Ghats, Central India, and the North-East differed fundamentally from settled peasant agitations. As UPSC Prelims 2011 pinpointed, their common underlying catalyst was the **"complete disruption of the old agrarian order of the tribal communities"**—an assault spanning five interlocking colonial vectors:

1. **Alienation of Communal Land Tenures:** Colonial law demolished collective clan ownership (*Khuntkatti* among Mundas, *Manki-Munda* estates among Hos, customary hill rights among Pahariyas), substituting alienable individual zamindari titles.
2. ***Diku* Intrusion:** Revenue monetization flooded previously insulated tribal belts with non-tribal outsiders—termed ***Dikus***—comprising moneylenders (*mahajans*), traders, liquor contractors (*abkari* vendors), revenue farmers (*thikadars*), and corrupt police-court intermediaries.
3. ***Beth Begari* (Forced Unpaid Labor):** Zamindars, *thikadars*, and colonial road and railway engineers coerced indebted Adivasis into unpaid corvée labor.
4. **Ecological Expropriation & Forest Enclosure:** Establishing the Imperial Forest Department (1864) and enacting the **Indian Forest Acts of 1865 and 1878** converted ancestral commons into state Reserved Forests for railway sleepers—criminalizing shifting cultivation (*Jhum*, *Podu*, *Bewar*), hunting, grazing, and minor forest produce collection, while imposing excise taxes on traditional brews (*Hadiya*).
5. **Cultural & Missionary Friction:** The visible overlap between European Christian missionaries and colonial magistrates provoked deep defensive anxiety over spiritual and customary autonomy.

### Pahariya Rebellion (1778) and Tilka Manjhi (1784–1785)

The martial **Mal Pahariyas** of the **Rajmahal Hills** (eastern Bihar/Jharkhand) historically lived autonomously off forest foraging, *Jhum* cultivation, and transit tolls from adjacent plains. When post-1765 Company expansion and plains zamindars encroached into the foothills, the Pahariyas launched fierce raids under **Raja Jagannath (1778)**.

Recognizing that military expeditions failed in dense hill terrain, Bhagalpur Collector **Augustus Cleveland** pioneered an indirect "pacification" policy—granting stipends to Pahariya *Sardars*, creating a tribal Hill Assembly, and raising a **Bhagalpur Hill Rangers** militia—setting up the administrative precursor to *Damin-i-Koh*.

Intertwined with this early Rajmahal frontier is the celebrated tribal hero **Tilka Manjhi (c. 1750–1785)**, also identified in regional tradition as **Jabra Pahariya**. In official commemoration and regional Adivasi lore (honored today by Tilka Manjhi Bhagalpur University), Tilka mobilized hill tribals after the 1770 famine—circulating *Sal* leaves as secret rallying tokens—looted Company treasuries for the starving, wounded Collector **Augustus Cleveland** with a poisoned arrow, and was dragged behind horses and hanged from a banyan tree at Bhagalpur in January 1785.

> **Epistemic Hygiene Caveat:** Archival historians (notably Dinesh Narayan Verma) point out that contemporary eighteenth-century colonial records attribute Cleveland's death in January 1784 aboard ship near the Hooghly mouth to illness and contain no pre-1970 documentary reference to the name "Tilka Manjhi," whose written biography crystallized through twentieth-century oral retrieval and literary works like Mahasweta Devi’s *Shalgirah Ki Pukar Par*. Both the official commemorative narrative and the archival caveat belong in rigorous historical memory.

### Kol Insurrection (Chotanagpur, 1831–1832)

Covering Ranchi, Singhbhum, Hazaribagh, Palamau, and western Manbhum, the **Kol Mutiny** united Munda, Oraon, Ho, and Bhumij communities (grouped loosely by colonial officers as "Kols"). The immediate flashpoint was the Raja of Chotanagpur leasing tribal villages—previously governed by hereditary tribal headmen (*Mankis* and *Mundas*)—to outsider **Sikh and Muslim revenue farmers (*Thikadars*)**, who imposed arbitrary rents, *Beth Begari*, opium levies, and personal humiliation (including the abduction of tribal women belonging to Singrai Manki).

In December 1831, insurgents rallied under **Buddho Bhagat** (of Silligaon, who fell fighting troops alongside his family), **Joa Bhagat**, **Madara Mahato**, and **Singrai and Bindrai Manki**. Displaying calculated political discernment, the Kols burned *Diku* estates, police *thanas*, and revenue offices across Chotanagpur while deliberately sparing low-caste indigenous artisans (blacksmiths, potters, milkmen) integral to tribal village life.

**Institutional Outcome:** Crushed with heavy artillery from Calcutta and Danapur by March 1832, the uprising exposed the total failure of Bengal’s general Regulations in tribal belts. The Company enacted **Regulation XIII of 1833**, abolishing the Ramgarh administrative structure and carving out the non-regulation **South-West Frontier Agency** under **Captain Thomas Wilkinson**.

### Ho and Munda Resistance in Kolhan (Singhbhum, 1820–1837)

The fierce *Larka* ("fighting") **Hos** of **Singhbhum (*Kolhan*)** had never submitted to Mughals or Marathas. When the Raja of Parahat sought British military backing in 1820 to enforce revenue claims over them, the Hos resisted British columns through 1820–1827 and later joined the 1831–32 Kol insurrection with devastating effect.

Recognizing that indirect feudal intermediaries provoked permanent warfare, Thomas Wilkinson detached Kolhan from local rajas in **1837** and promulgated **Wilkinson’s Rules**—placing the tract under direct British administration while legally preserving the traditional Ho ***Manki–Munda*** village self-governance system (a rare customary concession surviving in parts of Jharkhand’s Kolhan division even today).

### Khond Uprising (Odisha & Ghumusar Hills, 1837–1856)

In the rugged hill tracts of **Ghumusar, Kalahandi, Patna, and Boudh** (Odisha–Andhra border), the **Khond (Kondh)** tribes rose persistently against three colonial intrusions: (1) British campaigns to suppress their ritual ***Meriah*** (human sacrifice) practice; (2) imposition of new taxes and census surveillance; and (3) infiltration of Oriya zamindars and moneylenders following the Company’s deposition of the Raja of Ghumusar.

Led initially by **Dora Bisoi** (1837) and from 1846 onward by his charismatic nephew **Chakra Bisoi**—who forged tactical alliances with Savara tribal leader **Radha Krishna Dandasena** (1856–57)—the Khonds fought British expeditions across the Eastern Ghats for nearly two decades. The movement subsided only after Chakra Bisoi vanished into the forests in 1856 and Dandasena was hanged.

### Santhal *Hool* (Rajmahal Hills / Damin-i-Koh, 1855–1856)

The **Santhal *Hool*** ("Rebellion") stands as the largest, most intensely organized pre-1857 tribal war against colonial rule. In 1832, the Company had demarcated a fertile foothill perimeter around the Rajmahal Hills—from Bhagalpur to Rajmahal—named ***Damin-i-Koh*** ("skirts of the hills"), inviting industrious Santhal cultivators to clear forests and expand agricultural revenue.

Within two decades, the *Damin* became an inferno of *Diku* predation. Bengali and Bihari ***mahajans*** trapped Santhals in hereditary debt bondage (*Kamioti*) at 50–500% annual interest, ***zamindars*** encroached upon newly cleared lands, European railway contractors abused Santhal laborers on the East Indian Railway line, and corrupt police ***darogas*** and courts rubber-stamped fraudulent decrees.

- **Leadership & Mobilization:** On **June 30, 1855** (commemorated annually as ***Hul Diwas***), over 10,000 Santhals assembled at **Bhognadih** under four Murmu brothers—**Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand, and Bhairav**—powerfully supported by their sisters **Phulo and Jhano Murmu**, who famously infiltrated an enemy encampment under cover of night to slay over twenty sepoys.
- **Ideology & Course:** Sidhu and Kanhu announced a divine mandate from *Thakur* (Supreme God) ordering the expulsion of all *Dikus* and the end of *Sarkar* rule. Over 60,000 Santhals—aided by non-tribal lower castes like blacksmiths, potters, weavers (*Jolahas*), and milkmen (*Gwalas*)—swept the Bhagalpur–Rajmahal–Birbhum corridor, cutting postal and railway lines and routing an initial British contingent under Major Burroughs.
- **Suppression:** Martial law was declared in November 1855. Armed only with bows, poisoned arrows, and axes against British muskets and artillery, ~15,000–20,000 Santhals died fighting; colonial civil servant W.W. Hunter recorded in *Annals of Rural Bengal* that *"they did not understand yielding; as long as their national drum (nagada) beat, the whole party would stand, and allow themselves to be shot down"*. Sidhu was killed in battle (1855) and Kanhu was captured and executed (February 1856).
- **Statutory Outcomes (High-Yield Prelims Chain):**
  1. **Act XXXVII of 1855:** Carved out the **Santhal Parganas** as a separate **Non-Regulation District** exempt from general Bengal civil and revenue codes.
  2. **Santhal Parganas Settlement Regulation III of 1872:** Section 27 established an explicit statutory bar making it **illegal for a Santhal to transfer *raiyati* land to a non-Santhal** by sale, mortgage, or private contract—later consolidated post-independence into the **Santhal Parganas Tenancy (Supplementary Provisions) Act, 1949**.

### Bhil Uprisings (1817–1846) and Govind Guru’s Mangarh Massacre (1913)

Inhabiting the strategic mountain passes of **Khandesh** (Satpura–Western Ghats belt), the **Bhils** resisted the British annexation of Khandesh in 1818 after losing traditional pass-guarding tolls (*Rakhwali*) alongside famine and agrarian disruption. Major outbreaks erupted in **1817–19**, **1825** (under **Sewaram**), **1831**, and **1846**. Combining military force with co-optation, **James Outram** raised the **Khandesh Bhil Corps (1825)**, gradually integrating Bhils into colonial policing while curbing forest autonomy.

A profound socio-religious and anti-feudal resurgence later emerged across the **Rajasthan–Gujarat–Madhya Pradesh borderlands** (Dungarpur, Banswara, Santrampur) under **Govind Guru** (born in a Banjara family and influenced by Swami Dayananda Saraswati). Founding the ***Samp Sabha* (1883)** to forge tribal unity and the puritanical ***Bhagat* Movement (1908)** centered on sacred fire-hearths (*Dhunis*), Govind Guru campaigned against liquor, feudal forced labor (*Begar*), and exorbitant princely taxes, submitting a 33-point charter of grievances in 1910.

When hundreds of thousands of Bhils gathered peacefully at **Mangarh Hill (Banswara, Rajasthan)** in November 1913 demanding agrarian justice and Bhil autonomy, British Indian Army units and princely forces encircled the plateau on **November 17, 1913** and opened indiscriminate fire, massacring roughly **1,500 Adivasis**—an atrocity remembered as the **"Adivasi Jallianwala"**. Govind Guru was captured, his death sentence commuted to life imprisonment, and later released under restrictions in Gujarat.

> **Contemporary Heritage Update (2026):** Despite sustained regional demands following the 2022 *Mangarh Dham ki Gaurav Gatha* commemoration, the Central Government formally clarified in **August 2026** that Mangarh Dham does not meet the statutory criteria under the AMASR Act, 1958 for declaration as a "Monument of National Importance," favoring joint cultural development by Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra instead.

### Koli Uprisings (1829–1848) and Koya–Rampa Rebellions (1803–1886)

- **Koli Uprisings (Western Ghats, Maharashtra):** Neighbors of the Bhils across Ahmednagar, Poona, and Nasik, the **Kolis** lost fort-garrison employment after 1818. They rose in **1829, 1839**, and most fiercely during **1844–1848** under **Raghu Bhangria** and **Bapu Bhangria**, raiding treasury posts and moneylenders before military suppression.
- **Koya and Rampa Rebellions (Eastern Ghats / Godavari Agency, Andhra):** The tribal **Koyas** and hill Reddis of the **Rampa** tract revolted repeatedly (**1803, 1840s, 1862**) against outsider ***Muttadars*** (intermediary estate holders), oppressive toddy/excise duties, colonial prohibitions on ***Podu*** (shifting cultivation), and forest access curbs.
  - **1879–1880 Peak:** Led by **Tomma Sora**, Koya and Konda Sara rebels overran police stations across the Godavari Agency and Malkangiri, proclaiming an autonomous tribal regime before Tomma Sora was shot dead in July 1880.
  - **1886 Resurgence:** **Raja Anantayyar** organized a millenarian band (*Rama Dandu* — "Army of Rama") claiming divine legitimacy before rapid suppression. *(Crucial Prelims Distinction: Keep Tomma Sora’s 1879–80 Koya–Rampa revolt strictly separate from **Alluri Sitarama Raju’s 1922–24 Rampa / Manyam Rebellion** linked to Non-Cooperation.)*

### North-East Frontier Insurrections - Khasi, Singpho, Jaintia and Naga

Colonial penetration into the Brahmaputra Valley after 1826 triggered decades of fierce frontier warfare as British engineers, tea planters, and survey columns pierced previously sovereign highland polities:

- **Khasi Uprising (1829–1833):** To link newly acquired Assam with Sylhet (Bengal), British Agent David Scott secured consent for a strategic hill road through Nongkhlaw. When the project brought forced labor, foreign Bengali/British personnel, and threats to Khasi sovereignty, **Tirot Sing** (*Syiem* of Nongkhlaw) rallied Khasi chiefs alongside **Garos, Khamptis, and Singphos** in April 1829, ambushing the Nongkhlaw garrison. Tirot Sing waged four years of punishing guerrilla warfare across the Khasi Hills before surrendering under truce in January 1833; he died a political prisoner in Dacca jail in 1835.
- **Singpho Rebellions (1830, 1843):** Straddling Upper Assam and the Arunachal borderlands, the **Singphos** resisted British encroachment for tea gardens, abolition of traditional tributary rights, and forced corvée. After an 1830 clash, chief **Nirang Phidu** launched a major offensive in **1843**, attacking the British military garrison at Beesa before colonial reinforcements broke the rising.
- **Jaintia Uprising (1860–1862):** Following the 1835 annexation of the Jaintia Kingdom, British imposition of a **house tax (1860)** and **income tax (1861)** provoked a sustained highland revolt under **U Kiang Nangbah** (publicly hanged at Jowai in December 1862).
- **Naga Resistance (1832–1879):** From the first British survey columns in 1832 through the establishment of the Naga Hills district (1866), **Angami Nagas** fiercely defended village sovereignty against punitive expeditions and house-tax collection—culminating in the killing of Political Officer G.H. Damant and the heroic **Siege of Khonoma (1879)** before superior artillery forced submission.

### Birsa Munda’s *Ulgulan* ("The Great Tumult", 1899–1900)

The culminating peak of nineteenth-century Adivasi insurgency erupted across the Ranchi–Khunti–Singhbhum plateau of Chotanagpur. Over the nineteenth century, the Munda community's ancient ***Khuntkatti*** tenure (joint collective ownership by descendants of the original forest-clearing lineages) had been systematically cannibalized by *Zamindars*, *Thikadars*, and *Dikus*, compounded by relentless *Beth Begari* and colonial forest laws.

For nearly forty years prior (**1858–1895**), Munda elders (*Sardars*) had waged the ***Sardari Larai***—a peaceful constitutional campaign filing petitions with Calcutta courts and colonial governors for restoration of *Khuntkatti* lands. Its utter failure convinced the youth that legal appeals to an extractive state were futile.

- **Birsa’s Vision & *Birsait* Faith:** Born on **November 15, 1875** at Ulihatu (Khunti), **Birsa Munda** received brief education at a German Lutheran mission school before absorbing Vaishnava ascetic ideas under Anand Panre. Synthesizing indigenous cosmology with puritanical moral reform, Birsa preached the ***Birsait* faith**: devotion to one supreme god (***Singbonga***), rejection of liquor, witchcraft, and superstitious sacrifices, and radical internal self-purification. Revered as ***Dharti Aba*** ("Father of the Earth"), he explicitly welded spiritual renewal to anti-colonial political liberation.
- **Course of the *Ulgulan*:** Arrested and jailed from 1895 to 1897 on sedition charges, Birsa spent 1898–99 organizing secret night assemblies across Chotanagpur’s sacred groves. On **Christmas Eve, 1899**, he launched the ***Ulgulan*** ("The Great Tumult") under the ringing political slogan: ***"Abua Raj ete jana, Maharani Raj tundu jana"*** ("Let our own kingdom be established, let the Queen's kingdom end"). Rebels armed with bows, arrows, and axes targeted police stations, *Diku* estates, contractors, and churches symbolizing colonial collusion.
- **Suppression & Martyrdom:** British forces massacred armed Munda defenders—including women and children—at **Dombari Buru (Sail Rakab Hill)** on January 9, 1900. Betrayed for a ₹500 bounty, Birsa was captured in the Jamkopai forest of Chakradharpur (February 1900) and died officially of cholera in Ranchi jail on **June 9, 1900**, at just twenty-five years of age.
- **Statutory Outcome (High-Yield Prelims):** Terrified by the scale of Munda solidarity, the colonial administration launched comprehensive land survey and settlement operations (1902–1910), legally **abolished *Beth Begari***, recognized surviving *Khuntkatti* estates, and enacted the landmark **Chotanagpur Tenancy (CNT) Act, 1908**—imposing strict legal prohibitions on the sale or transfer of tribal land to non-tribals.

> **Contemporary Institutional Legacy (2024–2026):** In 2021, the Union Government declared November 15 as ***Janjatiya Gaurav Divas***. Building on the nationwide ***Janjatiya Gaurav Varsh* (2024–25)** celebrating Birsa Munda’s 150th birth anniversary—and the 151st anniversary observances in November 2026—the state anchored flagship tribal welfare missions directly in his legacy, notably **PM-JANMAN** and the **Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (DAJGUA)**.

## Post-1857 Peasant Leagues - Legalism, Rent Strikes and Intelligentsia Convergence

Following the 1857 watershed, agrarian insurgency across India’s settled plains underwent a profound structural metamorphosis. Because displaced princes and zamindars had been neutralized or co-opted into the Raj’s post-1858 conservative coalition, **cultivating peasants emerged as the primary, autonomous force**. Rather than seeking to topple the British Crown through millenarian war, post-1857 leagues fought **specific, secular economic grievances**—coercive crop contracts, arbitrary rent hikes, and usurious debt—deploying rent strikes, collective legal defense funds, social boycotts, and strategic appeals to colonial statutes and the urban nationalist press.

### Indigo Revolt (*Nil Bidroha*, Bengal, 1859–1860)

Triggered in September 1859 at **Govindpur village in Nadia district** and rapidly sweeping Jessore, Pabna, Murshidabad, Rajshahi, and Malda, the **Indigo Revolt** represented the sharpest triumph of disciplined nineteenth-century peasant resistance.

- **Mechanism of Coercion:** European indigo planters preferred the ***Ryoti* system** over cultivating their own leased lands (***Nij*** system). Planters forced Bengali peasants to accept minuscule fraudulent cash advances (***Dadon***), trapping them into binding contracts to sow indigo on their most fertile rice lands at fixed prices far below market cost. Refusal met brutal private retaliation—kidnapping, flogging, cattle seizure, and arson by planters' mercenaries (***Lathiyals***)—temporarily abetted by the draconian **Act XI of 1860**, which made breach of indigo contract a criminal offense.
- **Leadership & Cross-Communal Solidarity:** Former planter agents **Digambar Biswas** and **Bishnu Charan Biswas** led the initial strike in Nadia, quickly joined by local leaders such as **Rafiq Mondal** (Malda) and **Kader Molla** (Pabna). Peasants refused advances, boycotted planters’ servants socially, beat back *lathiyals* collectively (with women hurling household utensils), and waged relentless courtroom battles—maintaining unbroken **Hindu–Muslim peasant unity**.
- **Historic Intelligentsia Convergence:** For the first time in colonial history, the urban Bengali *bhadralok* championed an agrarian insurgency. **Harish Chandra Mukherjee** turned ***The Hindu Patriot*** into an investigative megaphone exposing planter violence; **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** dispatched field reports; and **Dinabandhu Mitra** published the incendiary Bengali drama ***Nil Darpan* (1860)**. Translated into English by **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** and circulated by sympathetic missionary **Rev. James Long** (who was fined ₹1,000—paid on the spot by Kaliprasanna Singha—and jailed for one month for libel), the play galvanized liberal opinion in both Calcutta and London.
- **Outcome & Economic Epilogue:** Alarmed by widespread rural defiance so soon after 1857, the government appointed the **Indigo Commission (1860)** under **W.S. Seton-Karr**. Magistrate E.W.L. Tower famously testified that *"not a chest of indigo reached England without being stained with human blood."* In November 1860, the government issued a notification affirming that **ryots could not be compelled to cultivate indigo**. Planters abandoned Bengal by 1863, relocating westward to Bihar’s *Tinkathia* estates (laying the exact groundwork for **Champaran, 1917**). However, as **UPSC Prelims 2020** emphasized, the ultimate terminal collapse of natural indigo across India by the early twentieth century stemmed not from peasant revolt alone, but from its **unprofitability in global markets following the invention of cheaper German synthetic aniline dyes**.

### Pabna Agrarian Leagues (East Bengal, 1873–1885)

In May 1873, peasants of **Yusufshahi Pargana in Pabna district** (East Bengal) formed a formal **Agrarian League** to halt systematic landlord sabotage of **Rent Act X of 1859**—the statute granting occupancy rights to tenants cultivating a holding continuously for twelve years. Zamindars evaded Act X through arbitrary rent enhancements, illegal *abwabs*, forced short-term leases, and fraudulent shrinkage of land-measurement units.

- **Leadership & Constitutional Tactics:** Led by **Ishan Chandra Roy** (a petty landholder hailed as *"Bidrohi Raja"*), **Shambhunath Pal**, and **Khoodi Mullah**, the League pioneered **strictly legalistic mass mobilization**. Peasants collected subscriptions to hire Calcutta lawyers, organized peaceful rent strikes depositing lawful rents directly into district courts, blew buffalo horns to assemble villagers against evictions, and neutralized colonial repression by declaring: ***"We want to be the ryots of Queen Victoria and of her only."***
- **Class Fault-Lines in Early Nationalism:** Although over two-thirds of Pabna peasants were Muslim and most major zamindars were Hindu, the movement remained staunchly **agrarian and non-communal**, led jointly by Hindu and Muslim organizers. Yet it exposed a sharp ideological split within the Bengali intelligentsia: zamindar-aligned nationalist newspapers like *Amrita Bazar Patrika* and *Hindoo Patriot* initially attacked the League as "anti-zamindar," whereas **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, R.C. Dutt, and Surendranath Banerjea’s Indian Association** vigorously defended tenant security.
- **Statutory Outcome:** Over a decade of persistent agrarian pressure across East Bengal forced the colonial state to pass the landmark **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885**, which secured occupancy tenure (*settled ryots*) and regulated rent enhancements across Bengal.

### Deccan Riots (Maharashtra, 1875)

Erupting on May 12, 1875 in **Supa village (Poona district)** and spreading rapidly across 33 villages in Poona and Ahmednagar, the **Deccan Riots** exposed the lethal debt architecture of the **Ryotwari** settlement.

- **Global-to-Local Economic Trigger:** During the **American Civil War (1861–65)**, Lancashire mills scrambled for Indian cotton, driving Deccan cotton prices skyward and tempting Kunbi peasants into heavy borrowing. When American supply resumed post-1865, cotton prices crashed just as the Bombay government imposed a steep **50% upward land-revenue revision (1867)** alongside poor harvests. Caught between rigid revenue demands and collapsing prices, peasants surrendered lands to outsider **Marwari and Gujarati *Sahukars*** (moneylenders), who exploited British civil courts and the **Limitation Law (1859)** (mandating bond renewal every three years, rolling unpaid interest directly into principal).
- **Surgical Non-Lethal Tactics:** Kunbi cultivators first launched an airtight **social boycott**—forbidding village barbers, washermen, shoemakers, and laborers from serving *Sahukars*. When boycotts failed to secure relief, peasants stormed moneylenders’ houses and shops with remarkable discipline: **seizing and publicly burning debt bonds (*Khatas / Bahis*), mortgage deeds, and court decrees** while largely avoiding physical violence or theft.
- **Intelligentsia Support & Statutory Outcome:** **M.G. Ranade** and the **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha** championed the ryots' cause in petitions and public memoranda. The government appointed the **Deccan Riots Commission (1878)**, culminating in the **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879**, which abolished imprisonment for non-payment of debt, empowered civil courts to go behind fraudulent bonds to inspect original principal, and restricted forced land transfers to moneylenders.

### Malabar Moplah (*Mappila*) Outbreaks (Kerala, 1836–1896)

South Malabar—specifically **Ernad and Walluvanad taluks**—experienced between twenty-two and thirty localized, fiercely violent outbreaks from **1836 to 1854** and again from **1873 to 1896**.

- **Structural Agrarian Root:** Customary pre-colonial Malabar operated a shared three-tier tenure between ***Jenmis*** (nominal birthright holders, mostly upper-caste Namboodiri Brahmins and Nairs), ***Kanamdars*** (mortgage-tenure holders), and ***Verumpattamdars*** (actual cultivators, heavily Muslim ***Mappilas***). British courts fundamentally misread *Jenmam* through English freehold law, transforming *Jenmis* into absolute landlords with unrestricted power to levy steep renewal fees (*Melcharth*), rack-rent, and evict *Mappila* tenants at will.
- **Ideology & Colonial Diagnosis:** Lacking institutional redress, impoverished *Mappila* peasants attacked exploitative *Jenmis* and state agents under the religious idiom of *Shahid* (martyrdom). Rejecting the colonial stereotype of innate "fanaticism," Special Commissioner **William Logan’s landmark Report (1881)** squarely diagnosed agrarian tenancy insecurity as the root cause. Yet colonial fear of alienating *Jenmi* allies yielded only the weak **Malabar Compensation for Tenants' Improvements Act, 1887** (amended 1900)—leaving the core contradiction unresolved until it detonated during the **1921 Khilafat–Moplah Rebellion**.

### Assam *Raij-Mels* - Phulaguri Dhawa (1861) and Patharughat (1894)

In Assam’s Brahmaputra Valley, British annexation replaced the pre-colonial non-monetized *Paik* labor system with direct **Ryotwari cash land revenue**—forcing cash-starved peasants into the grip of immigrant Marwari *Keya* moneylenders. Assamese cultivators countered extraction through ***Raij-Mels*** ("people's assemblies")—traditional, deeply democratic village-cluster parliaments led by local *Gossains*, *Dolis*, and *Gaonburas* that united Assamese Hindus, Muslims, and plains tribes (Tiwas/Lalungs, Kacharis, Bodos) across social boundaries.

- **Phulaguri Dhawa (October 18, 1861, Nagaon District):** Assam’s first organized peasant uprising broke out among Tiwa (Lalung) and Assamese peasants after the Company **banned private poppy (*kani*) cultivation in 1861** (to enforce a lucrative government opium monopoly) while rumors spread of impending taxes on betel nut (*tamul*) and betel leaf (*pan*). When Assistant Commissioner **Lt. G.B. Singer** arrived at a five-day Phulaguri *Raij-Mel* and ordered police to forcibly seize peasants’ bamboo sticks, furious cultivators beat him to death. Brutal reprisals followed, including executions of Lalung leaders like **Songbor Lalung**.
- **The 1893–1894 No-Tax Rebellion and Patharughat Massacre (January 28, 1894):** Following steep land-revenue enhancements of **70–80%** imposed by Chief Commissioner William Ward in 1892–93, massive *no-revenue* *Raij-Mels* erupted across Kamrup and Darrang—notably at **Rangia (December 1893)** and **Lachima (January 21, 1894)**. On **January 26–28, 1894**, thousands of unarmed Hindu and Muslim peasants gathered at **Patharughat** (Mangaldoi circle, Darrang district) to petition Deputy Commissioner **J.D. Anderson** against the ruinous hike. When Anderson rejected their demand and ordered armed police to disperse the assembly, peasants retaliated with clods of earth and sticks; police opened rapid volley fire, killing roughly **140 peasants** (colonial records understated the toll at 15) and wounding over 150. Immortalized in the folk epic ***Dalipuran***, the **Patharughat Uprising**—preceding Amritsar by twenty-five years—endures in Indian history as **"Assam's Jallianwala Bagh"**.

| **Gadkari & Sawantwadi** | 1844 | Kolhapur & Sawantwadi (Maharashtra) | Disbanded Gadkaris; Phond Sawant & Anna Saheb | Disbanding hereditary fort garrisons (Daji Krishna Pandit); ruler deposition | Samangarh/Bhudargarh recaptured; martial law in Sawantwadi |
| **Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy** | 1846–1847 | Kurnool / Rayalaseema (Andhra Pradesh) | **Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy** | Resumption of ancestral Poligar pensions & *Inam* lands; Koilkuntla treasury raid | Hanged publicly at Koilkuntla (Feb 1847) |
| **Santhal *Hool*** | 1855–1856 | *Damin-i-Koh* (Rajmahal Hills — Bhagalpur to Rajmahal) | **Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand, Bhairav, Phulo & Jhano Murmu** | *Diku* usury (50–500%), zamindari dispossession, railway abuse, corrupt *darogas* | **Act XXXVII of 1855** (Santhal Parganas Non-Regulation District) + **Reg III of 1872** (land-transfer ban) |

Singer killed (1861); **Patharughat firing (Jan 28, 1894)** killing ~140 ("Assam's Jallianwala Bagh") |

| **Malabar Moplah Outbreaks** | 1836–54; 1873–96 | Ernad & Walluvanad, South Malabar (Kerala) | Localized *Mappila* tenant bands | Absolute *Jenmi* property rights under British courts -> rack-renting & eviction | Logan Report (1881); weak **Tenants' Improvements Act, 1887**; erupted again in 1921 |

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Two Distinct Rampa Rebellions:** **1879–80 (Tomma Sora)** and **1886 (Raja Anantayyar)** were pre-nationalist Koya–Rampa forest/toddy revolts in Godavari Agency; **1922–24 (Alluri Sitarama Raju)** was the Gandhian-era Manyam/Rampa guerrilla war inspired by Non-Cooperation (yet armed). Never conflate their leaders or centuries.
> - **Indigo Revolt (1859–60) vs. Synthetic Dye Collapse (Prelims 2020 Trap):** The 1859–60 Bengal Indigo Revolt ended forced indigo cultivation *in Bengal* (pushing planters to Bihar's *Tinkathia* belt), but the **subcontinental decline of indigo cultivation by the early 20th century** was driven primarily by **German synthetic aniline dyes** making natural indigo unprofitable globally. Also note: *Tinkathia* (3/20th of holding) was the **Bihar/Champaran** system, whereas Bengal operated primarily via ***Nij* and *Ryoti*** (*Dadon*) contracts!
> - ***Nil Darpan* (1860–61) Translator vs. Publisher Dual-Standard Rule:** Dinabandhu Mitra’s Bengali drama *Nil Darpan* (1860) was translated into English anonymously by **Michael Madhusudan Dutt**, while **Rev. James Long** supervised its publication/circulation (1861) and was convicted of libel. In single-choice exams, always mark **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** as the English translator when both names appear; select **Rev. James Long** if asked who published/circulated it (or if Dutt is omitted from the options).
> - **Santhal Parganas Statutory Precision:** Do **not** fall for the coaching myth of a "Santhal Parganas Tenancy Act of 1876." The exact colonial legislative sequence is: **(1) Act XXXVII of 1855** (creating the Non-Regulation District) → **(2) Santhal Parganas Settlement Regulation III of 1872** (Section 27 barring Santhal-to-non-Santhal land transfer) → **(3) Santhal Parganas Tenancy (Supplementary Provisions) Act, 1949**.
> - **Deccan Riots (1875) Leadership Trap:** **Vasudev Balwant Phadke did NOT lead the 1875 Deccan Riots**. The 1875 riots were a spontaneous Kunbi peasant boycott/bond-burning movement (intellectually backed by M.G. Ranade’s Poona Sarvajanik Sabha); Phadke organized the armed *Ramosi Krishak Jatha* four years later in **1879**.
> - **Kuka Swadeshi Precedent:** Swadeshi, handspun *khaddar*, and institutional boycott of British courts, schools, and postal services were practiced systematically by **Baba Ram Singh’s Kuka (Namdhari) Movement (1857–1872)**—decades before the 1905 Bengal Swadeshi or 1920 Non-Cooperation movements.
> - **Pabna Press Split:** Contrary to assumptions of automatic nationalist-peasant harmony, *Amrita Bazar Patrika* initially opposed the Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873) due to its zamindari sympathy, while Bankim Chandra, R.C. Dutt, and Surendranath Banerjea’s Indian Association backed tenant occupancy legislation.
> - **Early Pre-Dalhousie Lapse:** The 1824 British refusal to recognize **Rani Chennamma’s** adopted heir in **Kittur** predated Dalhousie’s formal Doctrine of Lapse (1848) by 24 years—a classic chronological elimination trap.
> - **Mangarh Dham 2026 Status:** Remember Mangarh Massacre occurred on **November 17, 1913** (Rajasthan–Gujarat border, Bhils under Govind Guru)—six years *before* Jallianwala Bagh (1919)—and as of **August 2026**, the Centre declined ASI "Monument of National Importance" designation under AMASR Act, 1958.

> [!tip] Whole-UPSC Spillover
> - **GS2 (Polity & Constitutional Safeguards):** Nineteenth-century tribal rebellions directly birthed colonial **"Non-Regulation / Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas"** (1833 SWFA, 1855 Santhal Parganas, 1874 Scheduled Districts Act), which evolved into the **Fifth and Sixth Schedules** of the Constitution of India, **PESA (1996)**, and contemporary **CNT (1908) / SPT (1949)** land-alienation protections.
> - **GS3 (Internal Security & Environment):** The colonial triad of *forest enclosure + land alienation + outsider extraction* maps directly onto the structural roots of **Left-Wing Extremism (LWE)** across the Chotanagpur–Dandakaranya–Eastern Ghats corridor, the **Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006** attempting to undo the 1878 Indian Forest Act’s "historical injustice," and modern ecological assertions like the **Dongria Kondh Niyamgiri movement** and Jharkhand’s **Pathalgadi** mobilization.
> - **GS1 Society & Essay:** The shift from localized *Raj–Zamindar–Sahukar* violence to **Pabna’s legalism** and **Assam’s *Raij-Mels*** illustrates how subaltern actors develop institutional literacy, turning colonial legality itself into an instrument of counter-hegemony—rich fodder for Essay themes on *dissent, law vs. justice, and indigenous ecological sovereignty*.

### Verified UPSC Mains PYQs (2014–2026) & Prelims Anchors

- **CSE Mains 2023 (GS1, 15 Marks):** *"How did the colonial rule affect the tribals in India and what was the tribal response to the colonial oppression?"*
- **CSE Mains 2019 (GS1, 10 Marks):** *"The 1857 Uprising was the culmination of the recurrent big and small local rebellions that had occurred in the preceding hundred years of British rule. Elucidate."*
- **Verified Prelims Focal Anchors (2011–2020):**
  - **CSE Prelims 2020:** Identification of ***"Ulgulan" or the Great Tumult*** with Birsa Munda’s rebellion of 1899–1900.
  - **CSE Prelims 2020:** Root cause of the early 20th-century **decline of indigo cultivation in India** (unprofitability in world markets due to new inventions—synthetic German dyes—rather than peasant revolt alone).
  - **CSE Prelims 2018:** Dual post-revolt colonial measures following the **Santhal Uprising** (creation of *Santhal Parganas* + prohibition on Santhal-to-non-Santhal land transfer).
  - **CSE Prelims 2011:** Identification of the overarching common cause of 19th-century **tribal insurrections** (complete disruption of the old agrarian order of tribal communities).

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Statutory Inversion:** UPSC swaps specific post-revolt enactments across uprisings—pairing the **CNT Act (1908)** with Santhals instead of Birsa Munda, or pairing **Act X of 1859** with the Deccan Riots instead of Pabna/Bengal occupancy rights.
- **Chronological Anchoring Around 1857:** Favorite elimination sets interleave **Santhal *Hool* (1855–56)**, **1857 Revolt**, **Indigo Revolt (1859–60)**, **Pabna (1873)**, **Deccan Riots (1875)**, and ***Ulgulan* (1899–1900)**—catching candidates who mistakenly place Indigo *before* 1857 or Santhal *after* 1857.
- **Tactical & Ideological Misattribution:** Traps frequently describe the **Deccan Riots (1875)** or **Pabna Leagues (1873)** as "anti-British armed insurrections seeking overthrow of Crown rule," whereas both operated strictly against intermediaries (*Sahukars*/*Zamindars*) while invoking legal redress or royal loyalty.
- **Regional-Leader Cross-Matching:** High-risk swaps target lesser-studied frontier and peninsular revolts—mixing **Tirot Sing (Khasi)** with **Gomdhar Konwar (Ahom)**, **Chakra Bisoi (Khond)** with **Buddho Bhagat (Kol)**, or **Bakshi Jagabandhu (Paika)** with **Velu Thampi (Travancore)**.
