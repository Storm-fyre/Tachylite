# Chapter 7 - Print Capitalism, Colonial Education Policies & Bazaar Visual Modernity (1780–1944)

## 1. The Structural Triad - Print, Pedagogy, and Visual Modernity

Colonial domination in nineteenth-century India did not rest solely on revenue extraction and coercive policing; it operated through an engineered **epistemic architecture**. By remaking how Indians read, studied, and visualized their social world, the British Raj sought to manufacture consent and assemble a cheap, loyal bureaucratic intermediary class. Yet the three instruments deployed to stabilize imperial rule—the printing press, Westernized schooling, and mechanical image reproduction—escaped official custody.

Borrowing Benedict Anderson’s concept of **print capitalism**, commercial presses in Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Poona, and Banaras transformed localized speech communities into horizontally linked reading publics. Even where individual literacy remained below 10 percent, collective public reading (*path-sabhas* at village tea shops, libraries, and bazaars) multiplied the reach of a single vernacular newspaper tenfold. A paper bought by one subscriber routinely reached twenty or thirty listeners.

Simultaneously, the colonial state’s refusal to invest in mass primary schooling—while subsidizing English-medium higher education to staff subordinate courts and offices—produced a politically restless **bilingual intelligentsia**. Trained in Burke, Mill, and constitutional jurisprudence, yet barred from the upper echelons of the Indian Civil Service, this stratum weaponized print against the Raj. Alongside them, urban artisans and entrepreneurial lithographers forged a **bazaar visual modernity**—through Kalighat watercolours, Battala woodcuts, and Raja Ravi Varma’s oleographs—that gave the nascent nation a shared, reproducible iconography.

## 2. Rise of the Vernacular and English Public Sphere (1780–1900)

### 2.1 From European Scandal-Sheets to the Reformist Press
Indian journalism began not as an anti-colonial enterprise, but as an intra-European feud against the East India Company’s commercial monopoly and official corruption. On **January 29, 1780**, **James Augustus Hicky** launched ***The Bengal Gazette*** (also titled *Calcutta General Advertiser*), India’s first printed newspaper. Describing itself as *"a weekly political and commercial paper open to all parties, but influenced by none,"* Hicky’s sheet ruthlessly lampooned Governor-General Warren Hastings, Lady Hastings, and Chief Justice Elijah Impey.

Hastings retaliated by denying postal privileges, launching libel prosecutions, and seizing Hicky’s printing types in **1782**. Early competitors—*India Gazette* (1780), *Calcutta Gazette* (1784, enjoying official patronage), *Madras Courier* (1785), and *Bombay Herald* (1789)—generally stayed within acceptable Anglo-Indian boundaries. The truly combative English-language pioneer was **James Silk Buckingham**, whose ***Calcutta Journal*** (1818) systematically exposed official misconduct and demanded press liberty until Acting Governor-General John Adams deported him in 1823.

The decisive transition to an Indian-owned public sphere unfolded in two parallel streams in Bengal and Bombay:
- **Serampore Missionary & Pioneer Vernacular Presses:** Baptist missionaries at Danish-controlled Serampore (William Carey, Joshua Marshman, William Ward) issued Bengal’s earliest vernacular periodicals in **1818**—the monthly ***Digdarshan*** and the weekly ***Samachar Darpan***. Earlier still, **Gangadhar Bhattacharya** launched the short-lived ***Bengal Gazette*** (1816/1818), the first Indian-owned English newspaper. In Bombay, Parsi scholar **Fardunjee Marzban** founded ***Bombay Samachar*** (**1822**), Asia’s oldest continuously published newspaper, while in Calcutta **Jugal Kishore Shukla** launched ***Udant Martand*** (**May 30, 1826**), India’s first Hindi-language weekly (commemorated annually as Hindi Journalism Day).
- **Raja Rammohan Roy and the Reformist Counter-Public:** Rammohan treated the press as a rationalist tribunal against both orthodox dogma and Company despotism. He launched the Bengali weekly ***Sambad Kaumudi*** (**1821**) to campaign against *Sati* and advocate modern science, and the Persian weekly ***Mirat-ul-Akbar*** (**1822**) to address the pan-Indian Persianate administrative elite on constitutional rights, jury trials, and international liberty (such as Irish and Neapolitan struggles). He also briefly supported the English ***Brahmanical Magazine*** (1821) to rebut Serampore missionary polemics and co-sponsored the multilingual ***Bangaduta*** (1829).

### 2.2 The Nationalist Press - Architects, Idioms, and Regional Reach
After the 1857 Uprising ruptured Anglo-Indian racial trust, the European-owned press (*The Pioneer* of Allahabad, *The Statesman* in its early phase, *The Englishman* of Calcutta, *Civil and Military Gazette* of Lahore) generally closed ranks behind imperial prestige. Indian-owned journalism stepped into the breach—not as a commercial profit machine, since most nationalist papers operated on shoe-string budgets and personal sacrifice, but as the **primary opposition parliament** of a disenfranchised society. By 1875, over 400 Indian newspapers were circulating across British India.

- **Harish Chandra Mukherjee and *The Hindu Patriot* (1853):** Originally founded in Calcutta by Madhusudan Ray with **Girish Chandra Ghosh** as its first editor, *The Hindu Patriot* achieved national stature under **Harish Chandra Mukherjee** (editor 1855–1861). During the **Indigo Revolt (1859–60)**, Mukherjee turned the paper into an investigative clearinghouse, printing peasant testimonies from Nadia and Jessore, funding legal defence, and forcing the appointment of the Indigo Commission (1860). After Mukherjee’s premature death, **Kristo Das Pal** steered the paper toward a more moderate zamindari voice (*British Indian Association* organ).
- **Dwarakanath Vidyabhushan and *Somprakash* (1858):** Conceived with **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar’s** intellectual backing, *Somprakash* became Bengal’s most trenchant Bengali political weekly. It pioneered sustained commentary on peasant grievances, indigo planter brutality, and Lytton’s Afghan misadventures, earning the distinction of being the primary target when the Vernacular Press Act struck in 1878.
- **Dadabhai Naoroji (*Rast Goftar* & *Voice of India*):** In Bombay, Naoroji and Kharshedji Cama launched the Gujarati fortnightly ***Rast Goftar*** ("Truth Teller", **1851**) to drive Parsi social reform and attack ecclesiastical orthodoxy. In **1883**, Naoroji launched ***The Voice of India*** (in collaboration with Behramji Malabari and William Wedderburn), an innovative English monthly that curated and synthesized editorials from regional Indian newspapers so British parliamentarians could read a consolidated "Indian public opinion."
- **Sisir Kumar Ghosh and *Amrita Bazar Patrika* (1868):** Started by **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** and **Motilal Ghosh** as a Bengali weekly in Jessore (moving to Calcutta in 1871 and adopting a bilingual Bengali-English format), *Amrita Bazar Patrika* combined caustic satirical wit with populist rural reportage. When Lord Lytton’s **Vernacular Press Act** was enacted on **March 14, 1878**, the Ghosh brothers executed journalism’s most celebrated legal manoeuvre: within a single week (March 21, 1878), they converted the paper completely into an **English-language weekly**, bypassing the Act’s vernacular-only dragnet and humiliating the Viceroy.
- **Surendranath Banerjea and *The Bengalee* (1862 / 1879):** Founded in **1862** by Girish Chandra Ghosh, *The Bengalee* was purchased and transformed into the flagship voice of moderate constitutional nationalism by **Surendranath Banerjea** in **1879**. In **1883**, Banerjea became the **first Indian journalist to be imprisoned**—receiving a two-month jail term for contempt of court after criticizing Calcutta High Court Justice J.F. Norris for ordering a *Saligram* (Hindu sacred stone) to be produced in court as evidence.
- **G. Subramania Iyer (*The Hindu* & *Swadesamitran*):** Reacting against the Anglo-Indian press’s racist campaign opposing T. Muthuswami Iyer’s appointment as the first Indian judge of the Madras High Court, six young intellectuals ("the Triplicane Six," led by **G. Subramania Iyer** and **M. Veeraraghavachariar**) founded ***The Hindu*** in **1878** as an English weekly (turning daily in 1889). Recognizing that English excluded Tamil-speaking towns and villages, Subramania Iyer launched ***Swadesamitran*** in **1882** (daily by 1899), which trained sub-editors like Subramania Bharati and built the lexicon of modern Tamil political prose.
- **Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Gopal Ganesh Agarkar (*Kesari*, *The Mahratta*, & *Sudharak*):** In January **1881**, Poona’s New English School circle—Vishnushastri Chiplunkar, Tilak, Agarkar, M.B. Namjoshi, and V.S. Apte—launched twin weeklies: ***The Mahratta*** (English, Jan 2, 1881) and ***Kesari*** (Marathi, Jan 4, 1881). Crucially for Prelims precision, **Agarkar was the first editor of *Kesari* (1881–87)** while **Tilak edited *The Mahratta***. When ideological friction broke open over whether social reform (*Agarkar’s rationalist priority*) or political mobilization (*Tilak’s cultural-nationalist priority*) came first, Agarkar resigned in October 1887. Tilak assumed direct editorship of *Kesari*, while Agarkar launched the fiercely rationalist bilingual weekly ***Sudharak*** in **1888**—with **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** editing its English columns.
- **Bharatendu Harishchandra and the Modern Hindi Public Sphere:** Centered in Banaras, **Bharatendu Harishchandra** (1850–1885; honored with the title *Bharatendu* by Kashi scholars in 1880) standardized **Khari Boli** Hindi prose as a vehicle for economic critique, social satire, and cultural assertion. Through his trio of journals—***Kavi Vachan Sudha*** (1868), ***Harishchandra Magazine*** (1873, renamed *Harishchandra Chandrika*), and the pioneering women’s journal ***Bala Bodhini*** (1874)—alongside biting plays like *Bharat Durdasha* and *Andher Nagari*, he linked North Indian merchant and literate classes to the Bengal Renaissance and anti-drain economic nationalism.

## 3. The Colonial Press Censorship Grid (1799–1931)

### 3.1 The Logic of Colonial Press Regulation
Unlike metropolitan Britain—where press liberty evolved through parliamentary struggle and jury trial—the colonial state in India operated on a **doctrine of perpetual political exception**. Officials drew a sharp racial and linguistic line between the Anglo-Indian press (which they feared might expose military vulnerability to French or Russian rivals, or embarrass the Governor-General in London) and the vernacular press (which they feared would incite "disaffection" among sepoys and peasants).

Consequently, press policy oscillated between brief periods of utilitarian liberalization (Hastings in 1818, Bentinck, Metcalfe in 1835, Ripon in 1882) and swift statutory crackdowns whenever imperial hegemony faced crisis (1799 Mysore/French threat, 1857 Revolt, 1878 Famine/Afghan War, 1908–10 Swadeshi/Revolutionary upsurge, and 1930–31 Civil Disobedience).

### 3.3 Section 124A IPC (Sedition) and Landmark Press Trials
Even after Ripon repealed the Vernacular Press Act in 1882, the colonial state possessed a far deadlier, language-neutral weapon inside criminal law: **Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code**. Although Thomas Babington Macaulay had originally drafted a sedition clause (Clause 113) in his 1837 draft Penal Code, it was inexplicably omitted when the IPC was enacted in 1860. Alarmed by the **Wahabi movement’s** anti-British preachings, Law Member **James Fitzjames Stephen** inserted **Section 124A** via **Act XXVII of 1870** to criminalize exciting "disaffection" against the Government established by law.

Four landmark prosecutions chart how the Raj expanded Section 124A into a gag on political journalism:
1. ***Queen-Empress v. Jogendra Chunder Bose* (1891 — The *Bangobasi* Case):** India’s **first sedition trial** under Section 124A took place at the Calcutta High Court, ironically over orthodox Bengali weekly *Bangobasi*’s blistering attack on the **Age of Consent Act, 1891** as "forced Europeanization". Chief Justice W. Comer Petheram drew a sharp doctrinal line between lawful **"disapprobation"** (criticism of measures) and punishable **"disaffection"** (exciting ill-will or resistance to authority). The jury failed to reach a unanimous verdict, and proceedings were dropped after *Bangobasi* tendered an apology.
2. **Tilak’s First Sedition Trial (1897):** Following the Chapekar brothers’ assassination of Plague Commissioner W.C. Rand in Poona, Tilak was prosecuted for *Kesari* articles invoking Shivaji’s killing of Afzal Khan. Justice **Arthur Strachey** controversially defined "disaffection" not as active incitement to violence, but simply as **"the absence of affection"**—meaning any expression of disloyalty or enmity toward British rule became criminal. Tilak received 18 months’ rigorous imprisonment; London immediately codified Strachey’s expansive reading via the **IPC Amendment Act of 1898**, explicitly adding **"hatred or contempt"** alongside disaffection and inserting **Section 153A** (promoting enmity between classes).
3. **Tilak’s Second & Third Trials (1908 & 1916):** In **1908**, after *Kesari* analyzed the political roots of the Muzaffarpur bomb thrown by Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki, Justice Davar sentenced Tilak to **six years’ transportation to Mandalay (Burma)**, sparking a historic six-day political strike by Bombay’s textile workers. During his third sedition prosecution in **1916** (over Home Rule speeches), **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** defended Tilak before the Bombay High Court and secured a landmark acquittal, establishing that criticizing the bureaucracy did not constitute sedition against the Crown.
4. **Mahatma Gandhi’s "Great Trial" (March 1922):** Charged under Section 124A alongside *Young India* publisher Shankarlal Banker for three articles (*Tampering with Loyalty*, *The Puzzle and its Solution*, *Shaking the Manes*), Gandhi pleaded guilty before Sessions Judge C.N. Broomfield in Ahmedabad. Calling Section 124A **"the prince among the political sections of the Indian Penal Code designed to suppress the liberty of the citizen,"** Gandhi turned the courtroom into a moral indictment of colonial legality and accepted a six-year prison sentence.

*(Epistemic Continuity Note: In post-Independence India, the Supreme Court upheld Section 124A in *Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* (1962) only by narrowing it to speech with a direct tendency to incite violence or public disorder. On **May 11, 2022** [*S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India*], the Supreme Court placed Section 124A in abeyance. The **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**—in force from **July 1, 2024**—omits the word "sedition" and replaces the statutory space with **Section 152** ["Act endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India"], whose constitutional validity remains under Supreme Court review following notice issued on August 8, 2025.)*

## 4. Colonial Education Policies - From Orientalist Patronage to the Sargent Plan (1781–1944)

### 4.1 Indigenous Learning and the Orientalist Phase (1781–1813)
Before British intervention, India was neither an educational void nor purely clerical. **William Adam’s Reports (1835–1838)** on Bengal and Bihar—commissioned by Bentinck—documented an extraordinarily dense, decentralized web of over 100,000 village ***pathshalas*** (Hindu elementary schools) and ***maktabs*** (Muslim elementary schools), alongside *tols* and *madrasas* for higher classical learning. These institutions featured flexible agricultural calendars, zero rigid fee structures, local community funding, and surprisingly high lower-caste attendance in practical numeracy and accounting—traits that colonial inspection regimes and fixed tuition later destroyed.

During the first decades of Company rule, however, the British followed an **Orientalist policy** rooted in pragmatic statecraft: to govern through Hindu and Muslim personal laws without provoking social revolt, Company officials needed native law officers (*pandits* and *maulvis*) and classical translations.
- **Calcutta Madrasa (1781):** Founded by **Warren Hastings** to teach Arabic, Persian, and Islamic jurisprudence to train Muslim law officers for Company courts.
- **Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784):** Founded in Calcutta by Supreme Court judge **Sir William Jones** (alongside Charles Wilkins and H.T. Colebrooke) to study Indian philology, history, and antiquities, launching the journal *Asiatic Researches*.
- **Sanskrit College at Benaras (1791):** Established by British Resident **Jonathan Duncan** to study Hindu law, philosophy, and Sanskrit literature.
- **Fort William College, Calcutta (1800):** Founded by Governor-General **Lord Richard Wellesley** (never confuse with his brother Arthur Wellesley!) to train newly arrived British civil servants in Indian vernaculars, laws, and customs. The Court of Directors disapproved of its expense and autonomy, closing its civil-servant training wing in **1802** (shifting training to East India College, Haileybury in England in 1806), though Fort William survived as a language-translation hub that catalyzed modern Bengali and Urdu prose.

### 4.2 The Charter Act of 1813, Indian Agency, and the Orientalist–Anglicist Clash
Pressure from two metropolitan lobbies—**Evangelicals** (Charles Grant, William Wilberforce) seeking missionary access and **Utilitarians** (James Mill) demanding Westernized administrative reform—cracked the Company’s non-interference policy in the **Charter Act of 1813**. Section 43 of the Act directed the Company to set aside **₹1 lakh annually** *"for the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of the learned natives of India, and for the introduction and promotion of a knowledge of the sciences"*.

Crucially, **Indian reformers did not wait for British initiative**. In **1817**, enlightened Bengalis led by **Raja Rammohan Roy**, Scottish watchmaker **David Hare**, Chief Justice **Sir Edward Hyde East**, and even orthodox notables like **Radhakanta Deb** established **Hindu College, Calcutta** (later **Presidency College, 1855**) specifically to impart secular Western science, English literature, and European history. Rammohan famously wrote to Lord Amherst in **December 1823**, protesting the government’s proposal to establish a new Sanskrit College in Calcutta and urging instead *"a more liberal and enlightened system of instruction, embracing mathematics, natural philosophy, chemistry, anatomy, with other useful sciences."*

Meanwhile, the ₹1 lakh grant sat largely unspent until **1823**, when the government constituted a ten-member **General Committee of Public Instruction (GCPI)**. The Committee immediately deadlocked into the **Orientalist–Anglicist Controversy**:
- **Orientalists** (led by **H.H. Wilson** and **H.T. Prinsep**): argued that statutory funds should support Sanskrit and Arabic colleges, grafting Western science gradually onto indigenous classical traditions to avoid alienating Indian elites.
- **Anglicists** (led by **Charles Trevelyan**, Alexander Duff, and later **Thomas Babington Macaulay**): split internally over whether English or vernaculars should carry modern knowledge, but united in dismissing classical Oriental learning as obsolete.

### 4.3 Macaulay’s Minute (1835) and the Architecture of Cultural Hegemony
Appointed Law Member and President of the GCPI, **Thomas Babington Macaulay** broke the stalemate with his infamous **Minute on Indian Education (February 2, 1835)**, ratified by Governor-General **Lord William Bentinck’s Resolution (March 7, 1835)**. With supreme cultural arrogance—claiming that *"a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia"*—Macaulay mandated that public funds be redirected strictly toward **Western literature and sciences taught through the English medium**.

Macaulay’s objective was openly instrumental and ideological: to manufacture *"a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes, in opinions, in morals and in intellect."* As literary historian **Gauri Viswanathan (*Masks of Conquest*)** demonstrated, English literature was institutionalized as an academic discipline in colonial India **before** it entered Oxford or Cambridge—serving as a secular substitute for Christian evangelism to inculcate British moral superiority without violating official religious neutrality.

To rationalize spending minuscule sums on elite urban institutions, Macaulay and Lord Auckland (1839) adopted the **Downward Filtration Theory**: educating a thin upper stratum of Indians in English, trusting that modern knowledge would organically "filter down" to the masses in vernacular tongues. In reality, filtration failed completely. Instead of diffusing mass literacy, it entrenched a deep class-linguistic chasm, starved indigenous *pathshalas*, and was reinforced by **Lord Hardinge’s 1844 Resolution** granting preference in government employment to English-educated candidates.

Two vital counter-currents before 1854 deserve atomic recall: **James Thomason**, Lieutenant-Governor of the North-Western Provinces (1843–53), rejected Macaulayite elitism to pioneer a **vernacular village-school (*Halkabandi*) system** funded by a land-revenue cess alongside the **Thomason College of Civil Engineering at Roorkee (1847)** (India’s first engineering college, today IIT Roorkee). In Calcutta, **J.E.D. Bethune** founded the **Bethune School (May 1849)**—with **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar** as its Secretary—marking the institutional launch of secular female education.

Diagnosed that improving universities required reforming secondary schools: (1) proposed a **12-year school course**; (2) separated the **Intermediate stage** from universities into autonomous Intermediate Colleges regulated by a **Board of Secondary and Intermediate Education**; (3) recommended a **3-year Honours/Degree course** after Intermediate (origin of the **10+2+3** blueprint); (4) recommended a **unitary, residential, teaching university at Dacca** (est. 1921); (5) advocated less bureaucratic rigidity, **female education** (purdah schools & special board), teacher training, and applied science/technology. | Inspired establishment of **seven new universities (1916–21)** (Mysore, Patna, Banaras, Aligarh, Dacca, Lucknow, Osmania). Under **GoI Act 1919 (Dyarchy)**, education became a **Transferred Subject** under Indian ministers—who were then starved of funds by British-controlled Reserved Finance departments. |
| **1929** | **Hartog Committee** | Sir Philip Hartog *(Auxiliary Committee of the **Simon Commission**)* / Lord Irwin | Alarmed by declining quality amid rapid post-1919 quantitative expansion of schools. Coined the classic twin diagnosis of **"wastage"** (premature withdrawal before permanent literacy) and **"stagnation"** (retention in the same grade for >1 year) in primary education. Recommended a policy of **consolidation and improvement over hasty expansion**; opposed immediate compulsory primary education; advocated **diverting average students after middle school** into industrial/commercial streams and enforcing **selective admissions** to stop overcrowding in universities. | Widely criticized by Indian nationalists as an elitist brake on mass literacy; led to revival of the **Central Advisory Board of Education (CABE)** in 1935 (originally set up in 1920, abolished in 1923 as an economy measure). |
| **1937** | **Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (*Nai Talim*)** | **Zakir Hussain Committee** *(based on Mahatma Gandhi’s *Harijan* articles; All-India National Education Conference, Wardha, Oct 1937)* | Counter-colonial pedagogical revolution anchored in **"learning through activity/productive craft"** (*takli*/spinning, weaving, woodwork, agriculture, pottery) correlating academic subjects to lived manual labor. Mandated **7-year free and compulsory nationwide education** (ages 7–14) in the **mother tongue**; **Hindi** compulsory from Classes 2–7 (English excluded till Class 8); **no religious instruction**; envisioned schools as **self-supporting** via sale of students’ craft produce covering teacher salaries. | Endorsed at the **1938 Haripura Congress** and piloted by Congress provincial ministries (1937–39) through *Hindustani Talimi Sangh*; stalled after Oct 1939 ministry resignations during WWII, though its ethos influenced NEP 2020’s mother-tongue and vocational integration. |
| **1944** | **Sargent Plan of Education** | Sir John Sargent (Educational Adviser to GoI) / **CABE** under Lord Wavell | First official colonial plan targeting universal national education matching contemporary England’s standard over **40 years (1944–1984)**. Proposed: (1) **Pre-primary** schools (3–6 yrs); (2) **Universal, free, compulsory elementary education** (6–14 yrs) divided into **Junior Basic (6–11)** and **Senior Basic (11–14)**; (3) **6-year selective High School** (11–17 yrs) split into **Academic** and **Technical/Vocational** streams; (4) **abolition of the Intermediate course**; (5) **3-year university degree**; (6) liquidation of **adult illiteracy in 20 years**; (7) dedicated teacher training, school meals, and handicapped education. | Nationalists dismissed the 40-year horizon as unimaginably timid and questioned transplanting an English model into impoverished India; post-Independence **B.G. Kher Committee (1948)** compressed the target to **16 years**. |

### 4.5 Structural Balance Sheet of Colonial Education
By 1947, after nearly a century of Wood’s Despatch and seven major commissions, **Indian literacy stood at roughly 12–16 percent** (and barely 7–8 percent among women). Colonial expenditure remained chronically regressive—rarely exceeding 1–2 percent of total revenues, with massive skew toward military budgets.

Three structural distortions endured beyond 1947:
- **Anti-Industrial Literary Bias:** Excluding a handful of engineering/medical hubs (Roorkee 1847, Calcutta Medical College 1835, Sibpur, Poona, Guindy), the colonial system deliberately neglected **scientific, technical, and agricultural education** to prevent Indian industrial competition against Britain.
- **Urban–Rural & Gender Asymmetry:** Educational infrastructure clustered in the three Presidency capitals and upper-caste male urban strata, widening social disparities between English-educated professional elites and vernacular rural masses.
- **The Unintended Boomerang:** Yet the colonial classroom failed to create Macaulay’s docile collaborators. By providing a **common pan-Indian link language (English)**, exposing students to modern constitutionalism and economic statistics, and concentrating frustrated graduates inside university hostels and bar associations, colonial education inadvertently forged the organizational spine of Indian nationalism.

## 5. Bazaar Visual Modernity - Kalighat, Battala, Art Schools, and Raja Ravi Varma

Print capitalism did not merely circulate words; it triggered a **visual revolution** that democratized who could own, see, and interpret sacred and satirical imagery. Across nineteenth-century Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, three overlapping arenas—urban bazaar workshops, colonial art schools, and steam-powered chromolithography—redefined Indian visual culture long before the nationalist Bengal School emerged in 1905.

### 5.1 Kalighat Paintings (Calcutta, c. 1830–1930)
As Calcutta swelled into the imperial capital and the **Kalighat Kali Temple** (rebuilt 1809) became a booming pilgrimage node, rural folk scroll-painters (***patuas*** or *chitrakars*) migrated from **Midnapore, Birbhum, Bankura, and 24 Parganas** to settle around the temple precinct. Facing urban pilgrims who had neither the time nor the cash to commission epic narrative cloth scrolls (*pata*), the *patuas* reinvented their craft for mass souvenir production.

- **Technical Transformation:** Abandoning slow natural pigments on long cloth scrolls, Kalighat artists switched to cheap, machine-made **European mill paper** and quick **watercolours** (later synthetic aniline dyes), using large brushes to execute **bold, sweeping, uninterrupted black outlines** over minimal monochrome backgrounds. Incorporating selective British watercolour shading, they gave human limbs a distinctive **tubular, cylindrical volume** while omitting linear perspective. Family workshops operated on an assembly-line model—one member drew outlines, another filled washes—producing prints sold for a few *paise*.
- **From Sacred Icons to Urban Satire:** Early Kalighat sheets depicted Kali, Durga, Shiva, Krishna, and Islamic shrines. By the 1860s–80s, however, Kalighat artists emerged as Calcutta’s sharpest **subaltern social cartoonists**. Their secular paintings mercilessly mocked the Westernized Bengali ***Babu*** (dandies with Albert haircuts, oiled curls, and buckled shoes), tyrannical wives leading henpecked husbands on leashes (*Ghor Kali* / inverted social order), hypocritical Vaishnava priests (symbolized by a pious cat clutching a prawn in its mouth), British magistrates, and urban courtesans (*Elokeshi*, *Shyamakanta* wrestling tigers).
- **The Tarakeshwar Scandal (1873):** Kalighat’s media power peaked during the sensational **Mahant–Elokeshi affair (1873)**, in which a young housewife, Elokeshi, was seduced by the corrupt Mahant (head priest) of the Tarakeshwar Shiva temple and subsequently decapitated by her enraged husband Nobin Chandra Banerjee. Kalighat painters produced serialized "breaking-news" visual episodes—from the seduction and murder to the courtroom trial and the Mahant breaking stones in jail—creating an early graphic tabloid culture.
- **Decline and Modernist Afterlife:** By the 1910s–20s, Kalighat watercolours succumbed to cheaper, glossy German and Bombay chromolithographs. Yet their bold calligraphic economy directly inspired **Jamini Roy’s** modernist folk revival in the 1920s–30s, and European curators famously noted affinities between Kalighat’s rhythmic simplifications and twentieth-century modernist masters like Fernand Léger and Henri Matisse.

### 5.2 Battala Woodcut Prints (North Calcutta)
Just north of Kalighat, around a giant banyan tree (*bata-tala*) in North Calcutta’s **Chitpur–Shobhabazar–Garanhata** district, exploded **Battala**—nineteenth-century South Asia’s largest vernacular print-bazaar. Beginning in the 1810s–20s with pioneer printers like **Biswanath Deb**, hundreds of small Indian-owned wooden presses churned out affordable Bengali chapbooks, almanacs (*panjikas*), mythological tales (*Vidyasundar*), sensational crime pamphlets, devotional manuals, and racy social farces (*prahasanas*) satirizing *bhadralok* hypocrisy, widow remarriage debates, and colonial courts.

To illustrate these cheap booklets and standalone devotional broadsides (*Dasha Mahavidya*, *Chandi*, *Tarakeshwar* scenes), Battala printers enlisted traditional Bengali metalworking and engraving artisan castes—primarily ***Karmakars*** (blacksmiths) and ***Swarnakars*** (goldsmiths), such as **Panchanan Karmakar** (who earlier cut Bengali movable type for Charles Wilkins and the Serampore press) and later engravers like **Nrityalal Datta** and **Hiralal Karmakar**.

- **Visual Signature:** Unlike Kalighat’s fluid brushwork, Battala **woodcuts** and **metal-plate engravings** relied on sharp cross-hatching, intricate decorative floral borders, dense background patterning, and theatrical staging. Often hand-tinted with vibrant washes, Battala prints shared Kalighat’s irreverent urban gaze while reaching a far wider literate-and-semi-literate readership.
- **Cultural Politics:** As historian **Anindita Ghosh (*Power in Print*)** established, high-caste *bhadralok* reformers sneered at Battala as "vulgar" or "obscene" low-life literature; yet Battala democratized the Bengali public sphere, sustained colloquial idioms (*chalit bhasha*), and gave voice to women’s and artisans’ everyday anxieties outside elite Brahmo print circles.

### 5.3 Colonial Art Schools - Craft Preservation vs. Academic Naturalism
Following the **1851 Great Exhibition at Crystal Palace, London**—where Indian textiles, metalwork, and carpets stunned British industrial designers—the colonial state established a network of **Government Art Schools** modeled on London’s **South Kensington system**:
- **Madras School of Art (1850):** Founded privately by surgeon **Alexander Hunter** (taken over by government in 1852); India’s oldest modern art institution, focusing heavily on industrial arts, pottery, metalwork, and drawing.
- **Government School of Art, Calcutta (1854):** Originated as the School of Industrial Art under the Industrial Art Society (supported by Rajendralal Mitra and Jatindramohan Tagore); taken over by government in 1864 under **Henry Hoover Locke** (who trained Indian students in academic drawing, lithography, and wood engraving—producing the **Calcutta Art Studio** lithographers of 1878, famous for mythological prints!) and later radically transformed by **E.B. Havell** (Principal from 1896).
- **Sir Jamsetjee Jeejebhoy (J.J.) School of Art, Bombay (1857):** Funded by Parsi philanthropist Jamsetjee Jeejebhoy; under **John Lockwood Kipling** (Rudyard Kipling’s father) and **John Griffiths** (who famously led students to copy the **Ajanta cave murals** between 1872 and 1885), it became the premier bastion of **Victorian academic realism** and architectural sculpture in western India.
- **Mayo School of Industrial Arts, Lahore (1875):** Founded with **John Lockwood Kipling** as its first Principal to codify and commercialize Punjabi woodcarving, metalwork, and handicrafts.

As art historians **Partha Mitter** and **Tapati Guha-Thakurta** have analyzed, colonial art pedagogy rested on a patronizing **epistemic binary**: British officials praised India’s **"decorative crafts"** (textiles, carvings, brassware) while denying that India possessed any indigenous tradition of **"fine art"** (high sculpture or naturalistic oil painting). Consequently, art schools simultaneously tried to rescue traditional crafts for export markets while forcing Indian fine-art students to copy Greco-Roman plaster casts, master **single-point linear perspective**, and practice oil portraiture—inadvertently equipping Indian painters to contest European monopoly over the "modern" gaze.

### 5.4 Raja Ravi Varma (1848–1906) and the 1894 Lithographic Revolution
No single artist reshaped the modern Indian visual imagination more decisively than **Raja Ravi Varma** of Kilimanoor, Travancore. Never formally enrolled in a British art school, Ravi Varma mastered **European academic oil painting**—chiaroscuro (light-and-shadow modeling), anatomical realism, linear perspective, and theatrical drapery—through relentless self-study at the Travancore court and close observation of visiting Dutch portraitist **Theodore Jensen** (1868).

- **The Aesthetic Synthesis:** Winning the Governor’s Gold Medal at the Madras Fine Arts Exhibition (1873) for *Nair Lady Adorning Her Hair* and international acclaim at Vienna (1873) and Chicago’s World’s Columbian Exposition (1893), Ravi Varma secured elite royal commissions from **Travancore, Mysore, and Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III of Baroda** (whose **Laxmi Vilas Palace** houses his greatest canvases). His masterstroke was fusing **Victorian academic oil realism** with **Sanskritic Puranic and epic iconography**—painting ***Shakuntala***, ***Damayanti and the Swan (Hamsa Damayanti)***, ***Jatayu Vadha***, ***Draupadi Vastraharan***, and the ethnographic ***Galaxy of Musicians***. He rendered Hindu deities and epic heroines not as stylized flat temple icons, but as flesh-and-blood Indian women draped in Maharashtrian or Malayali saris, framed against lush Indian landscapes.
- **The 1894 Ravi Varma Fine Art Lithographic Press:** Acting on the advice of former Travancore Dewan **Sir T. Madhava Rao**—who urged reproducing Ravi Varma’s epics cheaply so ordinary Indian homes could replace imported German kitsch—Ravi Varma and his younger brother **C. Raja Raja Varma** (in financial partnership with Bombay textile magnate **Govardhandas Khatau Makhanji**) launched the **Ravi Varma Fine Art Lithographic Press in 1894** in **Girgaum, Bombay**. Importing steam-driven presses from Germany and hiring master German chromolithographers **Fritz Schleicher** and **B. Gerhardt**, the press printed up to **800 impressions per hour**, using between **7 and 30 hand-drawn lithographic stones per image** to produce rich, oil-like glazed colour prints called **oleographs**.
- **Mass Cultural Democratization & Commercial Fate:** The press’s inaugural oleograph, ***The Birth of Shakuntala*** (**July 12, 1894**, priced at ₹6), was rapidly followed by affordable ₹2 prints of ***Lakshmi*** and ***Saraswati***. Ravaged by the Bombay plague, the press relocated to **Ghatkopar (1898)** and then to **Malavli near Lonavala (1899)**; weighed down by debts and rampant piracy, the brothers sold the press and copyrights in **1901** to **Fritz Schleicher**, who ran it profitably for decades. Notably, young **Dadasaheb Phalke** worked at the press before pioneering Indian mythological cinema (*Raja Harishchandra*, 1913)—carrying Ravi Varma’s visual grammar directly onto the silver screen.
- **The Counter-Colonial Critique (Bridge to Chapter 10):** Ravi Varma’s oleographs democratized worship—allowing Dalit and non-temple-entry households to install sacred deities in domestic shrines—and forged a **pan-Indian calendar-art canon** that still defines popular Hindu iconography. Yet by the early 1900s, Swadeshi ideologues—**E.B. Havell, Abanindranath Tagore, Sister Nivedita, and Ananda Coomaraswamy**—attacked Ravi Varma’s academic naturalism as "soulless," "theatrical," and overly Westernized, sparking the counter-colonial **Bengal School of Art** (covered in Chapter 10).

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature
1. **Scope-Swapping across Education Commissions:** UPSC routinely swaps the jurisdiction of **Hunter (1882)** (primary/secondary only), **Raleigh (1902)** (universities only), **Sadler (1917)** (secondary-to-university 10+2+3 bridge), and **Hartog (1929)** (consolidation vs. wastage/stagnation—never an expansionist universal-compulsion plan like Sargent 1944).
2. **Absolute-Quantifier Inversion on Language Medium:** Claims that Macaulay’s Minute funded vernacular mass schooling, that Wood’s Despatch mandated English *"at all levels,"* or that the Wardha Scheme introduced English from Class 1 are classic UPSC traps.
3. **Statute-to-Viceroy & Repeal Displacement:** Pairing the Vernacular Press Act (1878) or Indian Press Act (1910) with liberal Viceroys (Ripon/Hardinge), confusing the **1867 Registration Act** (regulatory, repealed only in 2023/2024) with repressive licensing laws, or attributing the **1921 Press Committee** repeal to Simon instead of **Tej Bahadur Sapru**.
4. **Founder / Initial-Editor / Location Swaps:** Attributing *Kesari*’s initial editorship solely to Tilak (ignoring Agarkar), *Hindu Patriot*’s founding to Harish Chandra Mukherjee, Sanskrit College Benaras to William Jones, or placing Raja Ravi Varma’s 1894 Lithographic Press in Travancore instead of Bombay/Malavli.
