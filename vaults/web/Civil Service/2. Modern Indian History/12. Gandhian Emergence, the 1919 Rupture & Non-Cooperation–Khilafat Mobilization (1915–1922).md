# Gandhian Emergence, the 1919 Rupture & Non-Cooperation–Khilafat Mobilization (1915–1922)

Between 1915 and 1922, Indian nationalism crossed its most decisive sociological threshold: from an elite urban constitutional discourse into a pan-Indian mass insurgency (*Jan Andolan*). Before Mahatma Gandhi's intervention, neither Moderate petitioning nor Extremist revivalism nor secret armed cells had found a scalable grammar capable of bringing crores of peasants, artisans, workers, students, and women simultaneously into sustained anti-colonial defiance.

Gandhi resolved this structural impasse by fusing an ethical philosophy forged in South Africa with an acute reading of colonial power. When Britain met wartime sacrifices with the contradictory **1919 duality**—hollow provincial concessions under the Montagu–Chelmsford Act paired with police-state repression under the Rowlatt Act and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre—Gandhi turned the Indian National Congress into a disciplined, village-level mass machine that permanently broke the colonial state's moral authority.

## The South African Crucible — Laboratory of *Satyagraha* (1893–1914)

Arriving in Natal in **May 1893** as a shy 23-year-old barrister hired by Gujarati merchant firm Dada Abdulla & Co., Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi confronted legalized white supremacy head-on—catalyzed by his eviction from a first-class train compartment at **Pietermaritzburg (June 7, 1893)**. Over twenty-one years in South Africa, Gandhi forged India's future mass politics in miniature by uniting three socially fractured groups—wealthy Muslim Gujarati traders, impoverished Tamil and Bihari indentured laborers (*girmitiyas*), and South Africa-born Christians—across caste, language, and religious lines.

### Constitutional Apprenticeship and Ashram Experiments (1894–1906)

During his first twelve years, Gandhi operated squarely within Moderate constitutional methods—petitions, press columns, and appeals to British imperial citizenship:

* **Natal Indian Congress (1894):** Founded to challenge the Natal Franchise Disqualification Bill and the oppressive **£3 annual poll tax** levied on ex-indentured Indians refusing re-indenture, establishing Gandhi's lifelong habit of meticulous accounting and transparent public fundraising.
* ***Indian Opinion* (1903):** Launched as a multilingual weekly (English, Gujarati, Tamil, and Hindi) to knit scattered Indian migrants into a single political public sphere and educate readers in civic rights and ethical discipline.
* **Phoenix Settlement (1904, near Durban):** Established after reading **John Ruskin’s *Unto This Last*** on an overnight train—which Gandhi later paraphrased into Gujarati as ***Sarvodaya*** ("welfare of all"). Phoenix operationalized three radical egalitarian principles: individual good resides in the collective good, a lawyer's work holds identical moral value to a barber's, and a life of manual physical labor is the highest vocation.

### The Birth of *Satyagraha*, *Hind Swaraj* (1909) and the Great March (1906–1914)

When petitions failed to stop the Transvaal government's **Asiatic Registration Act ("Black Act", 1906)**—requiring all Indians over age eight to carry fingerprinted registration certificates—Gandhi crossed into active non-violent defiance. At the Empire Theatre in Johannesburg (**September 11, 1906**), delegates took a solemn oath to court jail rather than submit, publicly burning registration passes in August 1908.

* **From "Passive Resistance" to *Satyagraha*:** Dissatisfied with the English term "passive resistance"—which implied helplessness and permitted hatred or tactical violence—Gandhi invited readers of *Indian Opinion* to coin an indigenous phrase. Refining Maganlal Gandhi's *Sadagraha* ("firmness in a good cause"), he coined ***Satyagraha*** ("insistence on truth" / soul-force), nourished intellectually by **Leo Tolstoy’s *The Kingdom of God Is Within You***, **Henry David Thoreau’s *Civil Disobedience***, Jain *anekantavada* (non-absolutism), Vaishnava *ahimsa*, and the *Bhagavad Gita*.
* **Tolstoy Farm (1910, near Johannesburg):** Built alongside German-Jewish architect **Hermann Kallenbach** to shelter, educate, and sustain the families of imprisoned *satyagrahis* through collective farming, carpentry, and sandal-making—demonstrating that sustained civil resistance required an economic support ecosystem.
* ***Hind Swaraj* (November 1909):** Drafted rapidly in Gujarati aboard the steamer *S.S. Kildonan Castle* returning from London in direct debate with *India House* militants, *Hind Swaraj* laid out Gandhi's foundational civilizational critique through a dialogue between *Editor* and *Reader*:
  1. **Colonialism rested on Indian consent:** Britain did not conquer India by superior swords alone; *"the English have not taken India; we have given it to them"* through commercial collaboration, legal participation, and cultural awe. Withdrawing cooperation would collapse the Raj peacefully.
  2. **Critique of Industrial Modernity:** Gandhi indicted modern Western civilization (railways, machine factories, parliamentary majoritarianism, commercial medicine, and adversarial courts) as a "nine-day wonder" enslaving moral conscience to bodily appetite.
  3. **Inseparability of Means and Ends:** Challenging revolutionary consequentialism, Gandhi insisted *"there is just the same inviolable connection between the means and the end as there is between the seed and the tree."* Achieving British departure through armed terror would merely produce *"English rule without the Englishman"*—retaining the tiger's nature without the tiger. True ***Swaraj*** demanded moral self-rule (*Atma-shasan*) alongside political independence.
* **The 1913–14 Final Breakthrough:** When a 1913 Cape Supreme Court verdict invalidated all non-Christian Indian marriages—effectively branding Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi wives as concubines—and General Smuts broke his promise to repeal the £3 tax, **women led by Kasturba Gandhi** entered the movement alongside striking Indian coal miners in Newcastle. Leading the **Great March of over 2,000 miners into Transvaal (November 1913)**, Gandhi forced Smuts into the **Smuts–Gandhi Agreement / Indian Relief Act (June 1914)**—abolishing the £3 poll tax, recognizing Indian marriages, and conceding domicile certificates.

### Strategic Architecture of Gandhian Politics

Modern historiography (Bipan Chandra's Gramscian framework) highlights why Gandhian *Satyagraha* proved strategically lethal to British rule in India—far beyond simple moral idealism:

* **Dismantling Colonial Hegemony (*War of Position*):** The British Raj governed 300 million Indians with a tiny European bureaucracy and army primarily through **hegemonic consent**—the dual myths of British moral benevolence and invincible military power. Mass non-violent defiance trapped the colonial state in a **"Catch-22"**: arresting peaceful protesters exposed British rule as naked tyranny (eroding its legitimacy in India and Westminster), whereas tolerating defiance shattered its aura of invincibility.
* **Low-Barrier Mass Inclusion:** Violent underground cells inevitably restricted recruitment to tiny urban youth elites; *ahimsa*, spinning, fasting, and peaceful marches lowered entry barriers so unarmed women, elderly villagers, and poor peasants could confront empire directly.
* **Controlled Escalation (*Struggle–Truce–Struggle*):** Recognizing that ordinary masses cannot sustain peak revolutionary sacrifice indefinitely, Gandhi structured nationalism as alternating waves of intense open confrontation (*Struggle*) followed by phases of organizational repair, constructive work, and moral consolidation (*Truce*).

## Return to India and Localized Satyagraha Breakthroughs (1915–1918)

Landing in Bombay on **January 9, 1915** *(commemorated annually as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas)*, Gandhi accepted his political mentor **Gopal Krishna Gokhale’s** sage counsel to travel across British India for a full year with *"ears open and mouth shut"* before taking public positions. Establishing his ashram at **Kochrab (May 1915)**—relocated to the banks of the **Sabarmati River (June 1917)**—Gandhi announced his disruptive arrival at the **Banaras Hindu University (BHU) inauguration (February 1916)**, shocking bejeweled princes and nationalist elites by declaring that salvation lay not in ornate speeches or aristocratic patronage, but in *"the farmer"*.

Between April 1917 and June 1918, three surgical local interventions—remembered cleanly in strict chronological order as **CAK (Champaran → Ahmedabad → Kheda)**—tested and validated *Satyagraha* on Indian soil.

### Champaran Satyagraha (April 1917) — First Civil Disobedience

* **Agrarian Exploitation:** In Champaran district (North Bihar), European planters enforced the coercive ***Tinkathia* system**, compelling tenant cultivators (*ryots*) to grow indigo on **3/20ths (three *kathas* per *bigha*)** of their best land at unremunerative rates. When German synthetic aniline dyes collapsed natural indigo prices after 1900, planters sought to shift their losses onto peasants—demanding extortionate rent hikes (***sharahbeshi***) and heavy lump-sum compensation payments (***tawan***) in exchange for releasing peasants from indigo contracts.
* **Mobilization and Civil Defiance:** Relentlessly pursued by local indigo cultivator **Rajkumar Shukla** at the December 1916 Lucknow Congress, Gandhi reached Motihari in April 1917 joined by a remarkable young team of Bihari and Gujarati professionals—**Rajendra Prasad, J.B. Kripalani, Mahadev Desai, Mazhar-ul-Haq, Narhari Parikh, and Brajkishore Prasad**. Served with an official Section 144 order to leave the district immediately, Gandhi politely declined, prepared to accept imprisonment for obeying the *"higher law of conscience"*—marking his **first act of Civil Disobedience in India**.
* **Settlement and Impact:** Cornered by Gandhi's calm resolve and terrified of creating a martyr, the Bihar Lieutenant-Governor withdrew the prosecution and appointed the **Champaran Agrarian Inquiry Committee (1917)** (chaired by F.G. Sly, with **Gandhi serving as a member**). Backed by ~8,000 meticulously recorded peasant depositions, the committee's findings were enacted as the **Champaran Agrarian Act (1918)**, abolishing the *Tinkathia* system outright and ordering planters to refund **25%** of illegal *tawan* exactions. Critics questioned accepting a quarter refund, yet Gandhi judged accurately that breaking the planters' aura of absolute impunity mattered more than the cash percentage—within a decade, European planters deserted Champaran altogether. Above all, Champaran achieved a watershed breakthrough: **joining localized peasant unrest directly to the Indian National Movement**.

### Ahmedabad Mill Strike (February–March 1918) — First Hunger Strike

* **The Industrial Impasse:** Following the 1917 plague, Ahmedabad's Indian textile mill owners—led by prominent industrialist **Ambalal Sarabhai**—decided to terminate the special "Plague Bonus" despite steep World War I price inflation. Workers demanded a **50% wage hike** to survive; owners countered with **20%** and declared a lockout. Approached by Ambalal's own sister, social worker **Anasuya Sarabhai** (and the British Collector), Gandhi audited mill profit books, determined **35%** was morally and economically just, and advised workers to strike peacefully while pledging neither violence nor scabbing.
* **Moral Pressure and Institutional Legacy:** After two weeks, hunger began fracturing worker solidarity. On **March 15, 1918**, Gandhi undertook his **first fast unto death (hunger strike) in India**—simultaneously steeling workers' discipline and exerting immense moral coercion on mill owners who revered him personally. Within three days, owners yielded to an arbitration tribunal that awarded the full **35% wage hike**. Out of this campaign grew the **Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association (*Majoor Mahajan Sangh*, 1920)**—co-founded by Gandhi with **Anasuya Sarabhai** as lifelong president. India's oldest textile trade union operationalized Gandhi's principle of **Trusteeship and peaceful industrial arbitration** rather than Marxist class warfare.

### Kheda Satyagraha (March–June 1918) — First Non-Cooperation

* **Revenue Code Violation:** Excessive rain and pest blight ruined the 1917–18 Kharif harvest across Kheda district (Gujarat). Under Clause 11 of the **Bombay Revenue Code**, whenever crop output dropped below **one-fourth (25%) of normal yield**, farmers were legally entitled to **total remission of land revenue**. Colonial revenue collectors refused remission, inflating crop estimates and threatening attachment of cattle and lands.
* **Grassroots Leadership & Outcome:** Serving as President of the **Gujarat Sabha** (whose field inquiries alongside Servants of India Society's Vithalbhai Patel and N.M. Joshi verified crop failure), Gandhi launched his **first Non-Cooperation campaign** urging peasants to withhold revenue collectively. The movement became the political crucible of **Vallabhbhai Patel**—who abandoned his flourishing Ahmedabad legal practice and western attire to tour Kheda villages alongside **Indulal Yagnik**, Narhari Parikh, and Mohanlal Pandya (nicknamed *"Dungli Chor"* after symbolically harvesting onions from confiscated fields). Facing iron peasant discipline, the government issued confidential instructions to collect revenue strictly from affluent farmers capable of paying while suspending recovery from distressed ryots. Gandhi gracefully withdrew the agitation in June 1918, having built an enduring rural fortress in Gujarat.

## The 1919 Constitutional–Coercive Duality — Montford Reforms, Rowlatt and Jallianwala Bagh

With World War I ending in November 1918, Indian expectations ran high following Edwin Montagu's August 1917 promise of "responsible government". Instead, British policy reverted to its time-tested **"Carrot and Stick"** calculus: pairing a deeply flawed constitutional concession (**Government of India Act, 1919**) with permanent wartime police-state powers (**Rowlatt Act, March 1919**).

### Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms)

Framed on the July 1918 Montagu–Chelmsford Report and brought into operation in 1921, the Act reshaped colonial administrative architecture while keeping real sovereign power firmly in British hands:

* **Central–Provincial Jurisdiction Demarcation:** Through the **Devolution Rules**, the Act formally bifurcated administrative and legislative jurisdiction into **Central Subjects** (defence, foreign affairs, currency, railways, customs, posts & telegraphs) and **Provincial Subjects** (police, justice, land revenue, irrigation, education, health, agriculture, local self-government), separating **provincial budgets from the Central budget** for the first time and empowering provinces to enact their own budgets.
* **Provincial Dyarchy ("Double Government"):** Operating in eight major Governors' provinces (Madras, Bombay, Bengal, UP, Punjab, Bihar & Orissa, CP, and Assam; later Burma), provincial governance was split into two radically unequal halves:
  * **Reserved Subjects** *(controlled by the Governor and his Executive Council, completely unaccountable to the legislature)*: **Finance, Land Revenue, Law and Order, Police, Administration of Justice, Irrigation, Press Control, and Famine Relief**.
  * **Transferred Subjects** *(handled by the Governor acting on the advice of Indian Ministers chosen from elected provincial legislators, answerable to the Council)*: **Local Self-Government, Education, Public Health & Sanitation, Agriculture, Public Works, Excise, and Cooperative Societies**.
  * *Why Dyarchy was inherently unworkable:* Indian ministers inherited cash-hungry development departments while the British **Finance Member** (Reserved) controlled the purse strings; civil servants (ICS) remained under the Secretary of State rather than ministers; and subjects were absurdly split (e.g., Agriculture was Transferred while Irrigation and Land Revenue were Reserved). Governors could override ministers at will and certify rejected bills.
* **Central Bicameralism without Central Responsibility:** At the Centre, **no Dyarchy or responsible government existed**—the Governor-General remained answerable solely to London, armed with sweeping veto, ordinance, and **certification powers** to enact bills rejected by legislators (though **three of six members** of the Viceroy's Executive Council, excluding the Commander-in-Chief, were now required to be Indians). The Act replaced the unicameral Imperial Council with a **bicameral Indian Legislature**:
  * **Council of State (Upper House):** 60 members (34 elected, 26 nominated; 5-year term; male electorate initially).
  * **Central Legislative Assembly (Lower House):** 145 members (104 elected, 41 nominated; 3-year term).
  * **Direct Elections & Restricted Franchise:** Introduced direct elections for the first time, yet property, tax, and education thresholds restricted the vote to barely ~1–3% of the population. Crucially, the Act did **NOT** recommend universal voting rights for women above 21—it merely empowered individual provincial legislatures to decide whether to enfranchise property-qualified women (Madras and Bombay became the first to do so in 1921).
* **Expansion of Communal Electorates:** Retaining separate electorates for Muslims, the Act **extended separate communal electorates to Sikhs (in Punjab), Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans**—alongside reserved non-Brahmin seats in Madras (enabling the Justice Party to form ministries and issue the 1921 Communal G.O.). Far from healing divisions, these structural incentives actively fostered community-based political alliances designed to counterbalance unified Indian nationalism.
* **Allied Institutional Innovations:** Created the office of **High Commissioner for India in London** (assuming agency/procurement duties from the Secretary of State, whose salary finally shifted onto the British Exchequer), provided for establishing a **Central Public Service Commission** (set up in 1926 on the Lee Commission's advice), inaugurated the advisory **Chamber of Princes (*Narendra Mandal*, 1921)**, and mandated a **Statutory Commission after ten years** to review India's fitness for further advance (later accelerated into the 1927 Simon Commission).
* **The Muddiman Committee / Reforms Enquiry Committee (1924):** Facing relentless legislative deadlocks mounted by the Swaraj Party demanding immediate Dominion Status, the Government appointed a nine-member committee under Home Member **Sir Alexander Muddiman** (including prominent Indian non-officials **Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru, M.A. Jinnah, Sir P.S. Sivaswami Iyer, and Dr. R.P. Paranjpye**) to assess Dyarchy. Submitting a sharply divided report (Dec 1924 / published 1925), the British official **Majority Report** claimed Dyarchy had not received a fair trial and suggested minor administrative fixes (notably recommending the **separation of Accounts from Audit** as a financial reform), whereas the non-official Indian **Minority Report** declared the 1919 constitution inherently broken and urged immediate abolition of Dyarchy alongside a permanent constitution providing automatic progress toward responsible self-government.

### The Rowlatt Act (March 1919) and Rowlatt Satyagraha

Even before Montford became law, the Raj bared its coercive teeth. Acting on the **Sedition Committee (1918)** headed by British judge **Sir Sidney Rowlatt** (investigating wartime revolutionary plots), the government rushed through the **Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act (March 1919)** over the unanimous opposition of **every single elected Indian member** of the Imperial Legislative Council—prompting **M.A. Jinnah, Madan Mohan Malaviya, and Mazhar-ul-Haq** to resign their seats in disgust.

Denounced across India as the **"Black Act"** and **"Na Dalil, Na Vakil, Na Appeal"** (*no argument, no lawyer, no appeal*), the statute perpetuated wartime emergency repression in peacetime: authorizing detention of political suspects without trial for up to two years, warrantless searches, suspension of *habeas corpus*, and secret trials before three High Court judges with **no jury and no right of appeal**.

Calling the Act a betrayal of wartime loyalty, Gandhi launched his **first all-India mass Satyagraha (Rowlatt Satyagraha)**. Forming a ***Satyagraha Sabha*** in Bombay, he shrewdly mobilized three overlapping constituencies: the nationwide cadre of the **Home Rule Leagues**, younger Pan-Islamist Muslim networks, and urban artisanal/merchant classes. A nationwide *hartal* of fasting, prayer, and peaceful defiance—originally fixed for **March 30** (observed in Delhi under Arya Samajist **Swami Shraddhanand**, who was famously invited to address crowds from the pulpit of Jama Masjid in an extraordinary display of Hindu–Muslim unity) and shifted to **April 6, 1919** nationwide—paralyzed cities across British India. When police arrested Gandhi at Palwal (April 9) en route to Punjab and escorted him back to Bombay, leaderless crowds erupted into violent riots across Delhi, Ahmedabad, Viramgam, and Punjab.

### Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919), Tagore and Competing Inquiries

Nowhere did colonial panic turn more homicidal than in Punjab, governed by authoritarian Lieutenant-Governor **Sir Michael O’Dwyer**. On **April 10, 1919**, authorities arbitrarily arrested and deported two wildly popular Hindu–Muslim unity leaders in Amritsar—**Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew** and **Dr. Satyapal**. When peaceful protesters marching to the Deputy Commissioner's residence demanding their release were fired upon (leaving several dead), furious mobs retaliated by burning banks and railway offices, killing five Europeans and assaulting English missionary **Marcella Sherwood**. O’Dwyer handed Amritsar over to **Brigadier-General Reginald E.H. Dyer**.

* **The Massacre (Baisakhi, April 13, 1919):** Despite a poorly circulated curfew proclamation, between 15,000 and 20,000 unarmed men, women, and children—largely rural Baisakhi pilgrims alongside citizens protesting Kitchlew and Satyapal's arrest—assembled peacefully inside **Jallianwala Bagh**, an enclosed plot bounded by high walls with a single narrow exit lane. Arriving with 50 riflemen (armored machine-gun cars proved too wide for the lane), Dyer blocked the sole exit and ordered rapid fire **without issuing a single warning to disperse**. Troops fired **1,650 rounds** continuously for ~10 minutes straight into the densest pockets of fleeing civilians, halting only when ammunition ran nearly dry. While colonial figures admitted **379 dead**, the Indian National Congress inquiry and modern historians place fatalities at **over 1,000 dead** and 1,500+ wounded.
* **Martial Law Reign of Terror:** Dyer followed the slaughter with sadistic martial law across Amritsar, Lahore, Gujranwala (bombed from the air), and Kasur—enforcing public floggings, forced salutes to Europeans, arbitrary arrests, and the notorious **"Crawling Order"**, forcing every Indian passing along Kucha Kurichhan lane (where Marcella Sherwood was attacked) to crawl on their bellies.
* **Moral Rupture and Renunciations:** Shocked by the violence during Rowlatt protests, Gandhi suspended Rowlatt Satyagraha on **April 18, 1919**, candidly confessing a ***"Himalayan Miscalculation"*** in unleashing civil disobedience before training masses in *ahimsa*. Once censorship lifted and Punjab's horrors reached the nation, **Rabindranath Tagore renounced his Knighthood on May 30, 1919**, writing to Viceroy Chelmsford that *"the time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation."* Gandhi returned his **Kaiser-i-Hind** gold medal (awarded for South African ambulance service) in August 1920, and Sikh revulsion at Golden Temple priests (*mahants*) honoring Dyer with a *siropa* directly ignited the **Akali / Gurdwara Reform Movement (1920–25)**. *(Twenty-one years later, on **March 13, 1940** at **Caxton Hall, London**, Punjabi revolutionary **Shaheed Udham Singh** assassinated former Punjab Lt.-Governor **Sir Michael O’Dwyer**—Dyer himself having died of natural causes in 1927—before attaining martyrdom at Pentonville Prison on July 31, 1940.)*
* **Hunter Committee vs. Congress Inquiry:** Under mounting outcry, Edwin Montagu appointed the **Disorders Inquiry Committee (October 14, 1919)** chaired by **Lord William Hunter**—comprising five British members and **three Indian members: Sir Chimanlal Setalvad, Pandit Jagat Narayan, and Sardar Sahibzada Sultan Ahmad Khan**. Before hearings even concluded, the Raj shielded officials through an **Indemnity Act** (condemned as the "Whitewashing Bill"). During Setalvad's razor-sharp cross-examination, Dyer brazenly confessed his firing was premeditated to *"produce a moral effect"* across Punjab and that he would have used machine guns had the lane been wider. Yet the Hunter Majority Report (March 1920) merely censured Dyer for a *"mistaken conception of duty"* (while the three Indian members' Minority Note condemned his conduct as *"inhuman"*). Relieved of command without criminal prosecution, Dyer was hailed as the *"Saviour of Punjab"* by the British House of Lords and gifted a £26,000 purse raised by the *Morning Post*.
* **Congress Punjab Sub-Committee (Tahqiqat Committee):** Boycotting Hunter, the INC convened its own non-official inquiry featuring **Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das, Mahatma Gandhi, Abbas Tyabji, and M.R. Jayakar** (supported by Madan Mohan Malaviya's Punjab relief work). Its March 1920 report documented systematic state atrocities, rejecting the imposition of martial law outright. Watching Britain applaud Dyer shattered Gandhi's last shred of faith in British constitutional justice—completing his metamorphosis from a loyal Empire reformer into a revolutionary non-cooperator.

## Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Mobilization (1920–1922)

By mid-1920, three explosive grievances converged simultaneously: wartime economic inflation crushing artisans and workers, bitter humiliation over the **Punjab Wrong** (Jallianwala Bagh and Hunter whitewash), and intense Muslim anguish over the **Khilafat Wrong**. Gandhi fused all three into the **Non-Cooperation–Khilafat Movement**, launching India's first truly nationwide mass upheaval.

### The Khilafat Catalyst and the Treaty of Sèvres (1919–1920)

During World War I, British Prime Minister Lloyd George had promised Indian Muslims respectful treatment of the Ottoman Empire to ensure loyal military recruitment. Following Germany and Turkey's defeat, Allied powers shattered that pledge through the harsh **Treaty of Sèvres (August 10, 1920)**—dismembering Ottoman territories, stripping the Turkish Sultan (revered by Sunni Muslims worldwide as the ***Khalifa*** or spiritual-temporal Caliph) of authority over Arab lands and sacred Muslim shrines (*Jazirat-ul-Arab*).

In early 1919 (Bombay), prominent Muslim leaders formed the **All-India Khilafat Committee**, spearheaded by the militant **Ali Brothers (Maulana Mohammad Ali and Maulana Shaukat Ali)**, **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, **Maulana Hasrat Mohani**, and merchant Seth Chhotani. Elected **President of the All-India Khilafat Conference (Delhi, November 1919)**, Gandhi threw his full moral weight behind Muslim demands—declaring it *"an opportunity of uniting Hindus and Mohammedans as would not arise in a hundred years."*

Critics (both secular liberals like Jinnah and later historians) noted the long-term risk of injecting a religious pan-Islamic grievance into secular nationalism. Yet Gandhi recognized that the Khilafat leadership had already adopted strictly anti-imperialist non-cooperation and brought millions of Muslim peasants, artisans, and *ulama* (*Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind*) into joint street solidarity with Hindus for the first time since 1857.

### Calcutta Special and the Nagpur Organizational Revolution (1920)

The Khilafat Committee formally inaugurated Non-Cooperation on **August 1, 1920**—the very morning **Bal Gangadhar Tilak passed away** in Bombay, transferring undisputed nationalist leadership onto Gandhi's shoulders.

* **Calcutta Special Session (September 1920, President: Lala Lajpat Rai):** Overcoming steep skepticism from senior Congress stalwarts (C.R. Das, Bipin Chandra Pal, Annie Besant, and M.A. Jinnah, who opposed boycotting the upcoming November 1920 Montford council elections), Gandhi secured endorsement for a **staged Non-Cooperation programme**: beginning with surrender of British titles and boycott of government schools, law courts, legislative councils, and foreign goods, while explicitly holding **mass civil disobedience and non-payment of taxes in reserve for the final stage** if *Swaraj* did not arrive within a year and the government resorted to repression.
* **Nagpur Annual Session (December 1920, President: C. Vijayaraghavachariar):** Marking Gandhi's total triumph, former skeptic **C.R. Das** himself moved the main Non-Cooperation resolution. Beyond ratifying the movement and Gandhi's electrifying pledge of ***"Swaraj within a year"***, Nagpur executed a **permanent constitutional transformation of the INC**:
  1. **Ideological Creed Shift:** Replaced the old Moderate goal (*"self-government within the Empire through constitutional means"*) with **"attainment of *Swaraj* through peaceful and legitimate means"**—sanctioning extra-constitutional mass struggle (prompting constitutionalists **M.A. Jinnah, Annie Besant, B.C. Pal, and G.S. Khaparde** to resign from Congress).
  2. **Continuous Executive Leadership:** Established a **15-member Congress Working Committee (CWC)** to direct operations year-round instead of meeting merely three days each December.
  3. **Linguistic Federalization:** Reorganized **Provincial Congress Committees (PCCs) on a linguistic basis** (e.g., Andhra, Karnataka, Gujarat, Maharashtra), bypassing colonial Presidency borders and unlocking vernacular rural mobilization.
  4. **Grassroots Democratization:** Built tiered village, taluka, and district committees backed by a 350-member AICC, slashing annual membership dues to a symbolic **4 annas (25 paise)** so landless peasants and factory workers could join Congress directly.

### Boycott, Constructive Institutions and the Tilak Swaraj Fund (1920–1921)

Throughout 1921, Non-Cooperation swept British India with unprecedented civic discipline—fusing negative **Boycott** with positive **Constructive nation-building**:

* **Economic and Institutional Boycott:** Foreign cloth bonfires blazed across towns, slashing British cotton piece-goods imports nearly in half—from **₹102 crore (1920–21) to ₹57 crore (1921–22)**. Legendary barristers—**Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajendra Prasad, C. Rajagopalachari, Tanguturi Prakasam, Asaf Ali, and Saifuddin Kitchlew**—renounced lucrative court practices; nearly **90,000 students** walked out of government-aided colleges and schools. When the **Prince of Wales landed in Bombay on November 17, 1921**, empty streets and nationwide *hartals* greeted the royal visitor (marred by four days of violent street clashes in Bombay between boycott volunteers and pro-government loyalists, prompting Gandhi to fast for five days).
* **National *Vidyapiths* and Counter-Hegemonic Education:** To absorb boycotting youth, nationalists erected over 800 indigenous educational institutions free from colonial grants—most notably **Gujarat Vidyapith** (Ahmedabad), **Kashi Vidyapith** (Banaras), **Bihar Vidyapith** (Patna), **Tilak Maharashtra Vidyapith** (Poona), **Bengal National College** (headed briefly by young ICS resigner **Subhas Chandra Bose**), and **Jamia Millia Islamia** (founded **October 29, 1920** at Aligarh by Maulana Mohammad Ali, Hakim Ajmal Khan, Maulana Azad, and Zakir Husain before shifting to Delhi in 1925).
* ***Khadi*, Social Reform and the Tilak Swaraj Fund:** At the **March 1921 Bezwada (Vijayawada) AICC session**, Congress launched three concrete constructive targets: enrolling one crore members, distributing 20 lakh spinning wheels (*charkhas*), and raising **₹1 crore for the Tilak Swaraj Fund**—a target astonishingly **oversubscribed within six months**, proving Indians could finance mass politics without princely or British patronage. Equally vital, for the first time in its history, Congress formally integrated the **eradication of untouchability**, anti-liquor temperance, village arbitration *panchayats*, and Hindu–Muslim fraternization directly into its official political agenda.

### Regional and Subaltern Spillovers — Reinterpreting "Gandhi Raj" from Below

Subaltern historiography (Ranajit Guha, Shahid Amin, Sumit Sarkar) underscores a decisive paradox of 1920–22: once news of *Gandhi Maharaj* reached villages and forest hills, local peasants, workers, and tribals translated *Swaraj* into their own urgent struggles against **landlords, forest guards, and oppressive municipal taxes**—frequently outstripping the cautious boundaries set by Congress headquarters:

* **Malabar / Moplah Rebellion (August 1921–1922, Kerala):** In Eranad and Valluvanad taluks of South Malabar, impoverished Muslim *Mappila* (Moplah) tenants had suffered generations of rack-renting and arbitrary eviction under British-protected upper-caste Hindu ***jenmi*** landlords. Energized by Khilafat–Tenancy committees, Mappilas erupted into armed insurgency after colonial police raided the Tirurangadi mosque (August 20, 1921) arresting popular Khilafat preachers. Led by **Ali Musliyar** and **Variankunnath Kunjahammad Haji** (with initial participation from nationalist figures like M.P. Narayana Menon), rebels initially targeted colonial courts, treasuries, and railways; however, once harsh martial law cut off Congress–Khilafat moderating leadership, the agrarian-anti-colonial rising tragically degenerated into **brutal communal violence, murders, and forced conversions against local Hindus**, severely jolting national Hindu–Muslim unity. Colonial troops crushed the revolt brutally (~2,300 rebels killed, 45,000 jailed), climaxing in the horrific **Wagon Tragedy (November 20, 1921)**, wherein **67 of ~90 Mappila prisoners suffocated to death** sealed inside an unventilated iron luggage wagon en route from Tirur to Podanur jail.
* **Chirala–Perala Civic Exodus (1921, Coastal Andhra):** When the Madras Presidency merged the prosperous twin weaver villages of Chirala and Perala into a single municipality—threatening a tenfold surge in annual taxes (from ₹4,000 to ₹40,000)—flamboyant Telugu leader **Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya (*Andhra Ratna*)** organized a disciplined saffron-robed volunteer corps (***Rama Dandu***). Following Gandhi's advice at Bezwada to render the municipality meaningless through non-violent withdrawal, Gopalakrishnayya led **~13,500 out of 15,000 residents** in a dramatic **mass civic exodus (April 1921)** beyond town boundaries into a self-built thatched township named **Ramanagar**, governed for eleven months via its own popular assembly and arbitration court before Gopalakrishnayya's arrest broke the commune.
* **Pedanandipadu No-Tax Campaign (January–February 1922, Guntur, Andhra):** Led by **Parvataneni Veeraiah Chowdary** (*Andhra Shivaji*), village revenue officials (*karnams* and *munsifs*) resigned collectively and peasants across nearly 100 villages withheld over 95% of land revenue without a single act of violence—parallel to the **Palnadu Forest Satyagraha** (under Kanneganti Hanumanthu) defying grazing levies—demonstrating Andhra's extraordinary readiness for civil disobedience until Gandhi halted tax refusal after Chauri Chaura.
* **Alluri Sitarama Raju’s Rampa / Manyam Rebellion (August 1922–May 1924, Eastern Ghats, Andhra):** Deep in the Gudem–Rampa forest hills, the colonial **Madras Forest Act (1882)** prohibited tribals' traditional ***podu*** (shifting) cultivation, barred collection of minor forest produce, and subjected Koya and Konda Reddi Adivasis to unpaid forced road labor (*vetti*) under tyrannical Tahsildar Bastian. **Alluri Sitarama Raju (*Manyam Veerudu*)**—a charismatic non-Adivasi ascetic deeply inspired by Gandhi's call for *khadi*, temperance, and indigenous *panchayats*—concluded nevertheless that colonial violence could only be met by arms. Launching guerrilla warfare in **August 1922**, Raju raided police stations (Chintapalli, Krishnadevipeta, Rajavommangi) to capture modern rifles and outmaneuvered colonial patrols for nearly two years until Assam Rifles cornered and executed him summarily on **May 7, 1924**.
* **Tana Bhagat Movement (Chotanagpur, Jharkhand):** Initiated in **April 1914** among Oraon tribals by young prophet **Jatra Oraon (Jatra Bhagat)** (later guided by Sibu Oraon) as a non-violent monotheistic socio-religious purification drive—rejecting witchcraft, animal sacrifice, liquor, *begar* forced labor, and colonial *chowkidari* tax—the Tana Bhagats enthusiastically **joined Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement in 1921**. Embracing white *khadi*, *Gandhi Baba* songs, and persistent non-violent refusal to pay land rent to colonial *dikus* (even participating actively through Quit India 1942), they bridged autonomous Adivasi reform seamlessly into Gandhian nationalism.
* **Allied Regional Upsurges:** Across Bengal and Assam, **Birendranath Sasmal** led a victorious anti-Union Board no-tax campaign in Midnapore, Assam tea-garden *coolies* staged historic exodus strikes at Chargola and Chandpur (assisted by C.F. Andrews and J.M. Sengupta), Awadh peasants organized under **Baba Ramchandra** and the **Eka Movement** (detailed in Chapter 14), and Sikh peasants launched the **Akali / Gurdwara Reform Movement** (detailed in Chapter 8).

### Chauri Chaura (February 4/5, 1922) and the Bardoli Withdrawal

By late 1921, British jails overflowed with over 30,000 political prisoners—including C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Lajpat Rai, and the Ali Brothers (jailed following the July 1921 Karachi Khilafat resolution declaring military service under Britain religiously unlawful). At the **Ahmedabad Congress (December 1921, Acting President: Hakim Ajmal Khan**, since President-elect C.R. Das was imprisoned), Gandhi was appointed sole executive authority to escalate into Civil Disobedience. On **February 1, 1922**, Gandhi served a seven-day ultimatum to Viceroy Lord Reading threatening **mass Civil Disobedience and non-payment of taxes in Bardoli taluka (Surat, Gujarat)** unless political prisoners were freed and press censorship lifted.

Three days later, disaster erupted in eastern United Provinces:

* **The Chauri Chaura Clash (February 4, 1922 — Dual-Standard Date Parity):** Archival court records (reconstructed in Shahid Amin's *Event, Metaphor, Memory*) and official **Government of India / PIB Centenary commemorations** date the clash precisely to **February 4, 1922** *(while several legacy textbooks print **February 5, 1922**, when news telegraphed across India)*. In the market town of **Chauri Chaura** (Gorakhpur district), local police had severely beaten volunteer leader **Bhagwan Ahir** (a demobilized WWI sepoy) picketing liquor sales and inflated meat/fish prices. When ~2,000–3,000 peasant volunteers marched peacefully past the police station (*thana*) chanting *"Gandhi Maharaj ki Jai"*, armed constables opened fire, killing **three civilians**. Infuriated, the crowd forced constables back inside the *thana* and torched it, killing **22 policemen**. *(In the ensuing trial, 172 peasants were sentenced to death by the Gorakhpur Sessions Court before **Madan Mohan Malaviya** stepped in at Allahabad High Court, winning acquittal for 38 and cutting executions down to 19.)*
* **The Bardoli Resolution (February 12, 1922) & Aftermath:** Stunned by the breach of *ahimsa*, Gandhi convened the CWC at Bardoli, passing the **Bardoli Resolution** calling off Non-Cooperation immediately, instructing peasants to resume legal rent payments to *zamindars*, and pivoting strictly to constructive social work. On **March 10, 1922**, the Raj arrested Gandhi for seditious *Young India* articles; in a majestic trial before Ahmedabad Sessions **Judge C.N. Broomfield**, Gandhi pleaded guilty to disaffection toward an *"unnatural"* colonial system and accepted a **six-year prison term** (released unconditionally in February 1924 after an emergency appendectomy). Simultaneously, the Khilafat rationale collapsed externally when Turkish nationalist **Mustafa Kemal Atatürk** abolished the Ottoman Sultanate (November 1922), proclaimed a secular Republic (1923), and **abolished the Caliphate itself in March 1924**.

#### Competing Historiographical Verdicts on the 1922 Withdrawal

* **Radical Nationalist & Marxist Critique:** Leaders jailed at peak agitation—**Subhas Chandra Bose** (calling it a *"national calamity"*), **Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, C.R. Das, and Lala Lajpat Rai**—questioned punishing the entire nation for one Gorakhpur village's lapse. Marxist historian **R.P. Dutt** went sharper, pointing directly to the **Bardoli Resolution's clauses safeguarding zamindars' legal rent rights** to argue that Gandhi halted the movement out of bourgeois-landlord terror that peasant militancy (Awadh, Malabar, Chauri Chaura) was turning into a class revolution against propertied Indian allies.
* **Nationalist Strategic Defense (Bipan Chandra):** Counter-argues that by February 1922 the urban boycott was already losing steam; no unarmed mass movement can hold peak confrontation indefinitely. Had Gandhi ignored Chauri Chaura and pressed into Bardoli tax revolt amidst spreading violence, the colonial military—eager for a pretext—would have crushed the movement with lethal force dwarfing Jallianwala Bagh and shattered the prestige of non-violence. Strategic withdrawal preserved the Congress's moral legitimacy and grassroots cadres intact (**Struggle–Truce–Struggle**).
* **Subaltern Synthesis (Shahid Amin):** Moves beyond the "betrayal vs. genius" binary to demonstrate that Chauri Chaura's peasants were neither mindless mobs nor docile Congress soldiers; they appropriated *"Gandhi Maharaj"* to validate their own autonomous peasant moral economy against abusive local police—confirming that mass nationalism permanently exceeded any single leader's script.

UPSC routinely swaps Ahmedabad and Kheda!
> * ***Majoor Mahajan Sangh* (1920) Founder Trap:** The **Ahmedabad Textile Labour Association (*Majoor Mahajan Sangh*)** was founded by **Mahatma Gandhi** and **Anasuya Sarabhai** (NOT N.M. Joshi or Lala Lajpat Rai, who founded **AITUC** in the same year, 1920).
> * **GOI Act, 1919 — Four Lethal Elimination Traps (Prelims 2015, 2021, 2022, 2026):**
>   1. **Reserved vs. Transferred Subjects:** **Finance, Land Revenue, Police, and Administration of Justice** were **Reserved** (Governor + Executive Council); **Local Self-Government, Public Health, Education, and Agriculture** were **Transferred** (Indian Ministers).
>   2. **Dyarchy Location:** Introduced **ONLY in Provinces**, **NOT at the Centre** (Central Dyarchy was proposed only under the GOI Act, 1935!).
>   3. **Women's Suffrage Trap (Prelims 2021):** Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (1919) did **NOT** grant voting rights to *all* women above 21—they left women's limited property-based enfranchisement to provincial councils' discretion.
>   4. **Separate Electorates Expansion (Prelims 2026):** Extended beyond Muslims to **Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans**—NOT yet to Depressed Classes (that arrived with the 1932 Communal Award).
> * **Two Different "Hunter" Bodies:** **Hunter Commission (1882–83)** under W.W. Hunter reviewed **primary and secondary education**; **Hunter Committee / Disorders Inquiry Committee (Oct 1919)** under **Lord William Hunter** (with Indian members **Sir Chimanlal Setalvad, Pandit Jagat Narayan, and Sultan Ahmad Khan**) probed the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre & Punjab disturbances**.
> * **Udham Singh Target Trap:** On **March 13, 1940** (Caxton Hall, London), **Shaheed Udham Singh** assassinated former Punjab Lieutenant-Governor **Sir Michael O’Dwyer**—**NOT** Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer (who had already died in England in 1927).
> * **Calcutta Special (Sept 1920) vs. Nagpur (Dec 1920) INC Sessions:** **Calcutta Special** (President: **Lala Lajpat Rai**) approved Non-Cooperation *initially* with staged boycott; **Nagpur Regular** (President: **C. Vijayaraghavachariar**) ratified it unanimously, shifted Congress's creed to ***Swaraj* by peaceful and legitimate means**, created the **15-member CWC**, formed **linguistic PCCs**, and introduced the **4-anna fee**.
> * **Chauri Chaura Dual-Date Parity:** Archival records & **PIB / GoI Centenary commemorations** date Chauri Chaura precisely to **February 4, 1922** (22 policemen & 3 civilians killed); several legacy textbooks print **February 5, 1922**. Recognize both so neither option format trips elimination.
> * **Alluri Sitarama Raju Nuance:** Though revered as *Manyam Veerudu* leading Koya–Konda Reddi tribals in the 1922–24 Rampa Rebellion against the **Madras Forest Act (1882)**, Raju was **non-Adivasi by birth** and **admired Gandhi's khadi & temperance** while **explicitly rejecting non-violence** in favor of armed guerrilla raids.

> [!tip] Whole-UPSC Spillover
> * **GS2 (Polity — Federalism, Bicameralism & Institutional Lineage):** The **Government of India Act, 1919** planted four direct institutional roots of India's 1950 Constitution: explicit statutory demarcation of Central vs. Provincial jurisdictions (*Devolution Rules* → Seventh Schedule), a **bicameral national legislature** (*Council of State* & *Legislative Assembly* → Rajya Sabha & Lok Sabha), the **Central Public Service Commission** (→ UPSC), and the **Muddiman Committee's (1924)** recommendation to separate **Accounts from Audit** (finally operationalized at the Union level in 1976). Equally consequential, Congress's **1920 Nagpur linguistic PCCs** committed the future Republic politically to the **1956 linguistic reorganization of states**.
> * **GS4 (Ethics — Means–Ends Unity, Trusteeship & Moral Accountability):** *Hind Swaraj* (1909) anchors India's deepest deontological public-ethics framework: rejecting consequentialist shortcuts (*"as the seed, so the tree"*), redefining leadership as moral self-discipline (*Satyagraha* vs. weaponized coercion), advocating **Trusteeship** in industrial governance (*Ahmedabad Mill Strike*), and modeling rare public **moral accountability**—Gandhi openly confessing his *"Himalayan Miscalculation"* (1919) and accepting responsibility at his 1922 sedition trial rather than evading culpability.
> * **Essay & GS1 Society (Constructive Institution-Building vs. Agitation):** Gandhi's Non-Cooperation blueprint proves that enduring transformation never succeeds on negative protest alone—boycotting colonial courts, cloth, and schools worked only because Congress simultaneously built **national *Vidyapiths*, *khadi* livelihoods, arbitration *panchayats*, and anti-untouchability platforms**. Likewise, the contrasting arcs of Malabar (1921), Chauri Chaura (1922), and Tana Bhagat (1921) illuminate the timeless democratic tension between centralized ethical discipline and autonomous grassroots aspiration.

  * **2025:** **Non-Cooperation Movement** — Congress declaring *Swaraj* by all legitimate and peaceful means (Nagpur 1920) and implementing the movement in stages, keeping civil disobedience and non-payment of taxes for the next stage if *Swaraj* did not arrive within a year and repression intensified.
  * **2022:** **GOI Act, 1919** — distinguishing Provincial **Reserved subjects** (Administration of Justice, Land Revenue, Police) from **Transferred subjects** (Local Self-Government).
  * **2021:** **Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (1919)** — trap testing that 1919 did **NOT** recommend granting voting rights to all women above 21 (contrasted with 1935 Act giving women reserved seats).

### Prelims Trap Signature

* **Constitutional Feature Swapping (1909 vs. 1919 vs. 1935):** UPSC systematically swaps **Provincial Dyarchy** (1919) with **Central Dyarchy / Provincial Autonomy** (1935), **Central Bicameralism** (1919) with **Provincial Bicameralism** (1935), and **Separate Electorates** (1909 Muslims → 1919 Sikhs/Christians/Anglo-Indians/Europeans → 1932/35 Depressed Classes/Women/Labor).
* **Reserved vs. Transferred Functional Logic:** Use the imperial power heuristic—anything touching **money, coercion, or land** (*Finance, Police, Justice, Land Revenue, Irrigation*) stayed **Reserved**; low-budget welfare (*Local Self-Government, Health, Education, Agriculture, Public Works*) went **Transferred**.
* **Early Gandhian Satyagraha Attribute Inversion:** Swapping *First Civil Disobedience* (**Champaran 1917**), *First Hunger Strike* (**Ahmedabad 1918**), *First Non-Cooperation* (**Kheda 1918**), and *First All-India Mass Satyagraha* (**Rowlatt 1919**)—or confusing **Rajkumar Shukla** (Champaran) with **Anasuya Sarabhai** (Ahmedabad) and **Indulal Yagnik** (Kheda).
* **Regional Non-Cooperation Micro-Distinctions:** Differentiating **non-violent civic/no-tax movements** (**Chirala–Perala** under Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya, **Pedanandipadu** under Parvataneni Veeraiah Chowdary, **Tana Bhagats** under Jatra Oraon) from **armed regional rebellions** (**Moplah 1921** under Ali Musliyar/Variankunnath Haji; **Rampa 1922–24** under Alluri Sitarama Raju).
