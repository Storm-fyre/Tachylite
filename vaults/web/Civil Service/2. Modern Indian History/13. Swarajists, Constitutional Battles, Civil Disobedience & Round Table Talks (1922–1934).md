# Swarajists, Constitutional Battles, Civil Disobedience & Round Table Talks (1922–1934)

The twelve years between the Bardoli withdrawal of February 1922 and the suspension of Civil Disobedience in April 1934 operated as an irreversible political ratchet. Whenever the colonial state narrowed the constitutional corridor, Indian nationalism hardened its threshold demand and escalated to the streets; whenever mass defiance strained imperial administration, London convened negotiating tables and deployed communal fragmentation as its counter-lever.

Across this arc, the freedom struggle completed three structural transformations: it institutionalized dual-track politics (combining parliamentary obstruction with rural constructive work), crossed the Rubicon from **Dominion Status** to ***Purna Swaraj***, and articulated in the **Karachi Resolution (1931)** the earliest social-democratic charter of independent India.

---

## 1. The Post-Bardoli Interregnum - Swarajists vs. No-Changers (1922–1927)

### Strategic Vacuum and the Gaya Split (December 1922)

The abrupt suspension of the Non-Cooperation Movement after Chauri Chaura (February 1922) left the national movement disoriented. In March 1922, Mahatma Gandhi was arrested and sentenced to six years' imprisonment by **Justice C.N. Broomfield** for three seditious articles in *Young India* (before medical release in February 1924).

With the Khilafat alliance fracturing after Kemal Atatürk's secular reforms in Turkey and communal riots erupting across North India, Congress leadership split over how to sustain anti-colonial pressure during a non-agitational trough. The disagreement was strictly tactical rather than ideological—both factions accepted Gandhi's leadership and non-violence, differing solely on participation in the legislative councils created under the **Government of India Act, 1919**.

- **Pro-Changers (Swarajists):** Led by **Chittaranjan Das (C.R. Das)**, **Motilal Nehru**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan**, and **Vitthalbhai Patel**, they argued that boycotting the 1923 council elections would hand legislative platforms to colonial loyalists and communalists. Their stated strategy was **"ending or mending"** the legislatures from within through *"uniform, continuous, and consistent obstruction"*, transforming sterile chambers into theatres of nationalist propaganda.
- **No-Changers:** Led by **Vallabhbhai Patel**, **Rajendra Prasad**, **C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji)**, and **Dr. M.A. Ansari**, they contended that council entry would breed parliamentary corruption, dilute mass revolutionary fervor, and betray constructive boycotts. They advocated quiet grassroots consolidation—*khadi* spinning, temperance, national schools, Hindu–Muslim reconciliation, and anti-untouchability work—to prepare the peasantry for the next mass offensive.

At the **Gaya Congress Session (December 1922)**, presided over by C.R. Das himself, the No-Changers defeated the council-entry resolution by 1,740 votes to 890. Das resigned the Congress presidency and, on **January 1, 1923**, founded the **Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party** (popularly the **Swaraj Party**), with **C.R. Das as President** and **Motilal Nehru as Secretary**.

### Averting a Second Surat - Delhi (1923) and Belgaum (1924)

Both wings remembered the disastrous **Surat Split (1907)**, which had paralyzed the movement for nearly a decade. At the **Delhi Special Session (September 1923)**, presided over by **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, Congress brokered a pragmatic compromise permitting Congressmen to contest the November 1923 elections while retaining faith in the constructive programme.

Following Gandhi's release in February 1924, initial friction gave way to institutional accommodation. Recognizing that the Swarajists had emerged as a disciplined anti-colonial phalanx inside the councils—and facing brutal British repression against Bengal revolutionaries—Gandhi concluded the **Gandhi–Das–Nehru Pact (November 1924)**, formally ratified at the **Belgaum Congress Session (December 1924)**, the **only INC session ever presided over by Mahatma Gandhi**.

Under this settlement, council work was entrusted exclusively to the Swaraj Party as an **autonomous legislative wing of the Congress**, while the parent organization concentrated on constructive mobilization via the **All India Spinners' Association (AISA, 1925)**. Unity held because both camps recognized their structural interdependence.

### Legislative Warfare and Institutional Gains (1923–1928)

Despite having barely weeks to campaign under a narrowly restricted property franchise, the Swarajists scored major victories in the **November 1923 elections**:

- **Central Legislative Assembly:** Won **42 of 101 elected seats** (becoming the largest bloc) and forged a working coalition ("Nationalist Party") with independents led by **M.A. Jinnah** and Liberals led by **Madan Mohan Malaviya**.
- **Provincial Councils:** Captured an absolute majority in the **Central Provinces**, emerged as the single largest party in **Bengal**, and registered formidable tallies in Bombay and the United Provinces.
- **Municipal Strongholds:** Congressmen simultaneously seized urban civic bodies—**C.R. Das** became Mayor of Calcutta (appointing Subhas Chandra Bose as Chief Executive Officer), **Vitthalbhai Patel** chaired the Bombay Corporation, **Vallabhbhai Patel** ran the Ahmedabad Municipality, **Jawaharlal Nehru** led Allahabad, and **Rajendra Prasad** headed Patna.

Inside the Central Legislative Assembly, the Swarajists dismantled the British claim that the 1919 reforms functioned democratically. Their core institutional breakthroughs included:

- **Speaker's Chair (August 1925):** **Vitthalbhai Patel** (elder brother of Vallabhbhai) defeated the government nominee to become the **first Indian President (Speaker) of the Central Legislative Assembly**, establishing strict parliamentary neutrality and winning an independent Assembly Secretariat separated from executive control.
- **Exposing Dyarchy:** Swarajist pressure forced the Raj to appoint the **Muddiman Committee (Reforms Enquiry Committee, 1924)** under Sir Alexander Muddiman; its minority report (signed by Tej Bahadur Sapru, Jinnah, and Sivaswami Aiyer) indicted Provincial Dyarchy as structurally unworkable.
- **Budgetary and Statutory Defiance:** They repeatedly threw out the Finance Bill and Demands for Grants, compelling the Viceroy to invoke autocratic **certification powers**, thereby stripping away the constitutional veneer of the Raj. They also pushed through resolutions demanding the release of political prisoners, repeal of repressive statutes (Bengal Regulation III of 1818), and convening of a Round Table Conference for full responsible government.
- **Defeat of the Public Safety Bill (1928–29):** Designed to empower the Viceroy to deport foreign socialists and communists (notably British trade unionists Philip Spratt and Ben Bradley) without trial, the bill was defeated in **September 1928 (62–61)** via **Vitthalbhai Patel's historic casting vote**. When the Raj reintroduced it alongside the **Trade Disputes Bill** in **April 1929**, Patel ruled it out of order because the ongoing Meerut Conspiracy Trial rendered free debate *sub judice*—forcing Lord Irwin to issue it by autocratic ordinance (the immediate trigger for Bhagat Singh and B.K. Dutt's Central Assembly bombing on April 8, 1929).

### Erosion, the Responsivist Split, and Historiographical Appraisal

Parliamentary obstruction hit hard structural ceilings after 1925. The premature death of **C.R. Das (June 1925)** removed the party's most magnetic tactician, while the lure of ministerial patronage, rising Hindu–Muslim communal violence (1924–26), and inability to convert parliamentary votes into executive power bred internal fatigue.

By 1925–26, the Swarajists fractured into two warring camps:

- **Responsivists:** Led by **Lala Lajpat Rai**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya**, **N.C. Kelkar**, **M.R. Jayakar**, and **B.S. Moonje**, they argued that unyielding obstruction injured Hindu political interests and advocated **"responsive cooperation"**—accepting executive offices and committee seats whenever the government showed willingness to work with them.
- **Non-Responsivists:** Led by **Motilal Nehru**, they denounced ministerial cooperation as capitulation, staged a mass walkout from the Central Assembly in **March 1926**, and suffered severe electoral setbacks in the **November 1926 elections** (winning seats mainly in Madras while losing ground across North India to communalized Responsivist and Muslim blocs). All remaining Swarajist legislators resigned after the Lahore Congress (December 1929).

**Historiographical Lens:** Older colonial accounts dismissed the Swarajists as self-serving constitutionalists, while early Marxist critiques viewed council entry as a bourgeois diversion from mass militancy. **Bipan Chandra's Struggle–Truce–Struggle (S-T-S)** framework reframes the 1922–27 phase as a Gramscian *war of position*: mass movements cannot remain permanently at fever pitch without exhausting popular endurance. The Swarajists kept nationalist hegemony visibly alive in the press during the political ebb, while the No-Changers quietly built the ashram networks and peasant cadres (especially in Gujarat's Bardoli taluka) that powered the 1930 explosion.

## 2. Constitutional Deadlocks and the Shift to Purna Swaraj (1927–1929)

### The All-White Simon Commission (1927–1930)

Section 84A of the Government of India Act, 1919 mandated a statutory review commission after ten years (1929). Anticipating electoral defeat by the Labour Party, British Prime Minister **Stanley Baldwin's** Conservative government advanced the appointment by two years, announcing the **Indian Statutory Commission** on **November 8, 1927**.

Chaired by Liberal lawyer **Sir John Simon**, the seven-member body was **exclusively British (all-white)**—including future Prime Minister **Clement Attlee**—on the patronizing premise that Indians lacked the neutrality to judge their own fitness for self-government. This racial slight instantly reignited pan-Indian political solidarity across fractured parties.

At the **Madras Congress Session (December 1927)**, presided over by **Dr. M.A. Ansari**, Congress resolved to boycott the Commission **"at every stage and in every form"** (simultaneously passing Jawaharlal Nehru's snap resolution declaring complete independence the ultimate goal). The boycott united Congress with the **Liberal Federation (Tej Bahadur Sapru)**, the **Hindu Mahasabha**, and **M.A. Jinnah's Calcutta faction of the Muslim League**.

Yet the boycott was not universal—a critical Prelims distinction. Groups that **cooperated with the Simon Commission** included:

- **Unionist Party** in Punjab (Sir Fazl-i-Husain).
- **Justice Party** in Madras Presidency.
- **Sir Muhammad Shafi's Lahore wing** of the Muslim League (supported initially by Allama Iqbal, though Iqbal resigned over Shafi's memorandum in June 1928).
- **Central Sikh Sangh** and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, who submitted a landmark memorandum on behalf of the *Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha* demanding universal adult suffrage and political safeguards for the Depressed Classes.

When the Commission landed in Bombay on **February 3, 1928**, it encountered nationwide hartals, black flags, and **Yusuf Meherally's** electrifying slogan **"Simon Go Back"**. Police brutality peaked in **Lahore on October 30, 1928**, where Superintendent **James Scott** ordered a lathi-charge against peaceful protesters led by **Lala Lajpat Rai** (*Sher-e-Punjab*), who succumbed to his injuries on **November 17, 1928**—directly precipitating the HSRA assassination of John Saunders a month later. In Lucknow, Jawaharlal Nehru and Govind Ballabh Pant endured severe police beatings.

When the **Simon Commission Report** was finally published in **May 1930**, history had already outrun it. It recommended **abolishing Provincial Dyarchy** in favor of responsible provincial ministries (hedged by sweeping gubernatorial veto powers), retaining **separate communal electorates**, denying responsible government at the Centre (proposing only a weak "Council of Greater India" with princely states), **separating Burma from India**, granting local legislatures to NWFP and Baluchistan, and studying Sind's separation.

### The Butler Committee and Princely Paramountcy (1927–1929)

Running parallel to the Simon Commission was an imperial maneuver aimed at shielding the 560-odd Princely States from nationalist contagion. Appointed in **December 1927** by Lord Irwin and reporting in **1929**, the three-member **Indian States Committee (Butler Committee)**—comprising **Sir Harcourt Butler**, **Prof. W.S. Holdsworth**, and **S.C. Peel**—examined the legal and fiscal relationship between the British Crown and the Princely States.

Its 1929 Report laid down two doctrines that shaped constitutional deadlocks until 1947:

- **"Paramountcy Must Remain Paramount":** Rejecting the princes' plea to freeze Crown intervention within narrow treaty text, the committee declared paramountcy a living, evolving power bound to expand with changing imperial necessities.
- **The Consent Veto:** Crucially, it recommended that **Princely States must NOT be transferred without their own consent** to any future British Indian government responsible to an Indian legislature.

By separating the Crown's authority (exercised via the Viceroy as Crown Representative) from British Indian ministries, London erected a legal firewall that converted conservative princes into an imperial veto block inside future federal proposals.

### Delhi Proposals, the Nehru Report, and the "Parting of the Ways" (1927–1929)

Nettled by the Simon boycott, Secretary of State **Lord Birkenhead** taunted Indian leaders in the House of Lords to draft a constitution acceptable to all Indian communities—betting that communal divisions would make consensus impossible. Indian parties accepted the gauntlet through an **All-Parties Conference** (convened February–May 1928 under Dr. M.A. Ansari).

An earlier diplomatic opening had briefly appeared in the **Delhi Proposals**, drafted at a conference of 30 Muslim leaders convened by **M.A. Jinnah** in Delhi on **March 20, 1927** (and formally endorsed by the Madras Congress in **December 1927**; *legacy summaries often anchor the event to December 1927*). For the first time since 1906, Jinnah's wing agreed to **surrender separate electorates** in favor of **joint electorates with reserved seats** if four conditions were met:

1. Separation of **Sind** from the Bombay Presidency.
2. Introduction of full constitutional reforms in **NWFP and Baluchistan**.
3. Guaranteed **one-third (33%) Muslim representation** in the Central Legislature.
4. Muslim representation in **Punjab and Bengal strictly proportional to population**.

Tasked with drafting the actual constitution, the **Motilal Nehru Committee**—chaired by **Motilal Nehru** with **Jawaharlal Nehru** as Secretary, and members **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **Ali Imam**, **Shuaib Qureshi**, **M.S. Aney**, **Sardar Mangal Singh**, **Subhas Chandra Bose**, and **G.R. Pradhan**—submitted the **Nehru Report in August 1928** (approved at Lucknow on August 28, 1928). As the first major Indian-authored constitutional blueprint, it proposed:

- **Dominion Status:** Immediate self-governing Dominion Status on parity with Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and the Irish Free State.
- **19 Fundamental Rights:** Including **universal adult franchise**, complete **equal rights for women**, freedom of speech and association, right to form unions, and complete **dissociation of the state from religion** (secular citizenship).
- **Parliamentary Federal-Unitary Design:** Bicameral central parliament with an executive responsible to the legislature; **linguistic reorganization of provinces**; separation of Sind (subject to financial viability) and full provincial status for NWFP; a **Supreme Court**; and **residuary powers vested in the Centre**.
- **Electoral Compromise (The Fatal Fracture):** Rejected separate electorates everywhere, proposing **joint electorates** with seat reservations for Muslims *only* at the Centre and in Muslim-minority provinces (for ten years)—**explicitly denying seat reservations in Muslim-majority Punjab and Bengal** under intense pressure from the Hindu Mahasabha and Sikh representatives.

At the **Calcutta All-Parties Convention (December 1928)**, Jinnah moved **three minimal amendments** to salvage the compromise: one-third Muslim seats at the Centre, population-based reservation in Punjab and Bengal for ten years (until adult suffrage became operational), and **residuary powers vested in the provinces**. When Hindu Mahasabha spokesman **M.R. Jayakar** blocked every amendment and Congress failed to override Mahasabha pressure, an embittered Jinnah departed declaring it **"the parting of the ways"**.

Reuniting with the conservative Shafi faction, Jinnah unveiled his **Fourteen Points (March 1929)**—which served as the Muslim League's irreducible constitutional charter for the next decade. Its core pillars demanded a **federal constitution with residuary powers in the provinces**, uniform provincial autonomy, continuation of **separate electorates** (abandonable only by voluntary community choice), **one-third Muslim representation in the Central Legislature and all cabinets**, separation of Sind, reforms in NWFP and Baluchistan, constitutional safeguards for Muslim culture/personal law, no territorial change disturbing Muslim majorities in Punjab, Bengal, or NWFP, and a **three-fourths community veto** blocking any legislative bill opposed by three-quarters of a community's legislators.

### Youth Radicalization, Irwin's Declaration, and the Lahore Watershed (1928–1929)

The Nehru Report's acceptance of Dominion Status simultaneously provoked a revolt from Congress's younger radical wing. In **November 1928**, **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** (as General Secretaries), alongside **S. Srinivasa Iyengar** (President), established the **Independence for India League** to campaign for complete independence and socialist restructuring.

At the **Calcutta Congress Session (December 1928)**, presided over by **Motilal Nehru**, Gandhi mediated between the older constitutionalists and the impatient youth. Congress formally approved the Nehru Report but served Britain a **one-year ultimatum** (cut down from two years under Nehru–Bose pressure) to grant Dominion Status—declaring that failure by December 31, 1929 would commit Congress to ***Purna Swaraj*** and mass Civil Disobedience.

Meanwhile, **Ramsay MacDonald's Labour Party** took office in Britain (May 1929). Seeking to preempt confrontation, Viceroy **Lord Irwin** issued the **Diwali Declaration (October 31, 1929)**, stating that *"the natural issue of India's constitutional progress"* was the attainment of **Dominion Status** and promising a **Round Table Conference (RTC)** once the Simon Report appeared. Because the declaration offered **no timetable** and faced fierce backlash from British Conservatives, Indian leaders responded with the **Delhi Manifesto (November 2, 1929)**—demanding that the RTC act as a Constituent Assembly to draft a Dominion constitution immediately, guarantee majority Congress representation, and proclaim a general political amnesty.

On **December 23, 1929** (the very morning HSRA militants bombed Irwin's Viceregal train near Delhi), Irwin met Gandhi and Motilal Nehru and bluntly refused the Delhi Manifesto's terms. The constitutional door slammed shut.

Days later, the historic **Lahore Congress Session (December 1929)** convened under **Jawaharlal Nehru** (chosen at Gandhi's insistence to harness surging youth radicalism). Its epochal resolutions crossed the point of no return:

- Declared the **Nehru Report lapsed** and proclaimed ***Purna Swaraj* (Complete Independence)** the sole objective of the Congress.
- Announced a total **boycott of the Round Table Conference** and directed all Congress legislators to **resign from central and provincial legislatures**.
- Authorized the Congress Working Committee (CWC) to launch **Civil Disobedience**, including non-payment of taxes, at a time and manner chosen by Gandhi.

At **midnight on December 31, 1929**, Jawaharlal Nehru unfurled the newly adopted Tricolour on the banks of the **Ravi River** amidst cries of *Inquilab Zindabad*. Congress designated **January 26, 1930** as the first **Independence Day (*Swarajya Diwas*)**, when millions took the Gandhian Independence Pledge asserting the inalienable right to abolish an exploitative government—a date permanently commemorated twenty years later by enacting the Indian Constitution on January 26, 1950.

## 3. The Civil Disobedience Movement - Salt, Mass Agency, and Regional Theaters (1930–1934)

### Gandhi's Eleven Demands and the Semiotics of Salt

Before crossing into open illegality, Gandhi presented Viceroy Irwin with a carefully calibrated **Eleven-Point Ultimatum (January 31, 1930)** carrying a deadline of March 11. Rather than vague slogans, the eleven points engineered a **multi-class anti-imperial coalition**:

- **General Nationalist Interests:** 50% reduction in military expenditure; 50% cut in upper civil service salaries; total prohibition; reform of the Criminal Investigation Department (CID); amendments to the Arms Act permitting citizen firearms licensing; release of all political prisoners; and acceptance of the **Postal Reservation Bill**.
- **Indian Bourgeois / Capitalist Demands:** Revaluation of the rupee–sterling exchange ratio downward from the artificially inflated **1s 6d back to 1s 4d**; protective tariffs for indigenous textiles; and **reservation of coastal shipping for Indian enterprises**.
- **Peasant & Subaltern Demands:** 50% reduction in land revenue; and total **abolition of the Salt Tax and the British government's salt monopoly**.

When Irwin ignored the ultimatum, Gandhi selected the **Salt Tax** as the opening detonator—a choice that initially baffled both Nehru's socialists and British intelligence officers (Irwin remarked dismissively that the prospect of a salt campaign did not keep him awake at night). Strategically, salt was an act of supreme political genius:

- **Universal yet Non-Divisive:** Salt was a physiological necessity taxed regressively by a state monopoly (which Gandhi termed a *"fourfold curse"* injuring health, rural income, indigenous craft, and moral dignity). Unlike rent strikes (which split landlords from tenants) or religious issues (which risked communal polarization), salt affected every Indian equally regardless of caste, class, or creed.
- **Decentralized Mass Direct Action:** Anyone living near a shoreline, salt pan, or saline inland deposit could instantly manufacture illegal salt using a simple clay pot—democratizing law-breaking from elite lawyers down to illiterate villagers.

### The Dandi March and the Rhetoric of Moral Sedition (March–April 1930)

On **March 12, 1930**, Mahatma Gandhi departed **Sabarmati Ashram (Ahmedabad)** accompanied by **78 chosen satyagrahis** (79 marchers in total) representing regions and communities across India. Marching **240 miles (~385 km)** across rural Gujarat in **24 days** to the coastal village of **Dandi (Navsari district)**, Gandhi converted every evening stop into a serialized national theatre covered live by global wire services—prompting hundreds of village *patels* (headmen) to resign government posts in his wake.

Arriving on April 5, Gandhi walked down to the Arabian Sea on the morning of **April 6, 1930**, picked up a fistful of natural salt encrusted on the shore, and formally inaugurated the Civil Disobedience Movement. In his defining Dandi indictment of British imperialism, he proclaimed: **"The British rule in India has brought about moral, material, cultural and spiritual ruination of this great country... Sedition has become my religion"**—inverting colonial criminal law into sacred civic *dharma*.

*Gender Nuance (High Prelims Yield):* Gandhi initially excluded women from the original 78 Dandi marchers (despite vigorous lobbying by **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay** and **Sarojini Naidu**), arguing that British police would not strike women and that their moral authority was best deployed picketing liquor and foreign-cloth shops. Kamaladevi pressed Gandhi to lift the restriction for the broader movement, and Sarojini Naidu joined him directly at Dandi—unlocking the single largest public breakthrough of Indian women in modern history.

### Regional Theaters, Subaltern Innovations, and Women's Emancipation

Once Gandhi broke the salt law, Civil Disobedience cascaded across British India in sharply localized forms tailored to each province's ecology and agrarian grievance:

- **Massive Mobilization of Women:** For tens of thousands of women—from aristocratic urban homes to peasant courtyards—CDM shattered *purdah* and domestic confinement. Leaders such as **Sarojini Naidu**, **Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay**, **Kasturba Gandhi**, **Avantikabai Gokhale**, **Hansa Mehta**, **Mithuben Petit**, **Ambabai** (Karnataka), **Rama Devi** (Odisha), and **Prabhavati Devi** (Bihar) organized salt pans, led picketing lines outside liquor and foreign-cloth outlets, edited underground bulletins, and filled British jails.
- **Tamil Nadu — Vedaranyam Salt March (April 1930):** **C. Rajagopalachari (Rajaji)** led roughly 100 disciplined volunteers on a 150-mile march from **Tiruchirappalli to Vedaranyam** on the Tanjore coast, marching to **Namakkal Ramalingam Pillai's** iconic Tamil hymn *Kathi Indri Ratham Indri* ("A war without sword or blood").
- **Malabar / Kerala — Payyanur March (April 1930):** **K. Kelappan** (*Kerala Gandhi*, veteran of the Vaikom Satyagraha) led salt marchers from **Calicut (Kozhikode) to Payyanur**; on Calicut beach, **P. Krishna Pillai** famously shielded the national flag against brutal police lathi blows.
- **Andhra:** Coastal districts (Guntur, Krishna, Godavari) established disciplined volunteer military-style camps (**Sibirams**) coordinated by **Konda Venkatappayya** and **T. Prakasam**.
- **Odisha:** **Gopabandhu Choudhury** and **Acharya Harihar Das** led mass salt manufacture at **Inchudi** (Balasore district)—recognized as the second most prominent salt satyagraha site in India after Dandi—alongside Cuttack and Puri.
- **Gujarat — Dharasana Salt Works Raid (May 21, 1930):** After Gandhi's midnight arrest at Karadi (May 4–5) and the arrest of veteran judge **Abbas Tyabji**, **Sarojini Naidu**, **Imam Saheb**, and **Manilal Gandhi** led 2,500 unarmed volunteers in disciplined waves against police armed with steel-tipped lathis. American United Press correspondent **Webb Miller's** graphic eyewitness cable—describing satyagrahis refusing to raise even a hand to ward off skull-crushing blows—triggered global revulsion and destroyed Britain's moral legitimacy abroad. Parallel raids struck **Wadala (Bombay)** and **Sanikatta (Karnataka)**.
- **NWFP — Peshawar and the Garhwal Mutiny (April 23, 1930):** In the fiercely martial Pathan frontier, **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** (*Badshah Khan* / *Frontier Gandhi*)—editor of the first Pashto political monthly ***Pukhtoon***—had built the ***Khudai Khidmatgars*** ("Servants of God"), nicknamed **Red Shirts (*Surkh Posh*)** for their brick-red dyed uniforms, pledged to strict Gandhian non-violence. Following Ghaffar Khan's arrest on **April 23, 1930**, unarmed protesters massed at Peshawar's **Qissa Khwani Bazaar**. When British officers ordered a Hindu platoon of the **2/18th Royal Garhwal Rifles** under **Havildar-Major Chandra Singh Garhwali** to fire on the Muslim Pathan crowd, Garhwali refused—proclaiming they would not shoot unarmed brothers. Though British armoured cars still massacred over 200 demonstrators and Garhwali was court-martialed to life imprisonment, the Raj's fundamental axiom—that "martial" Indian troops would reliably fire across communal lines—had cracked.
- **Manipur & Nagaland — Rani Gaidinliu's *Heraka* Revolt:** Among the Zeliangrong Nagas, spiritual reformer **Haipou Jadonang** linked socio-religious revitalization (the ***Heraka*** movement) with anti-colonial refusal of house taxes and forced porterage (*pothang*). After British authorities hanged Jadonang (August 1931), his teenage cousin **Rani Gaidinliu** assumed guerrilla-spiritual leadership, explicitly invoking Gandhi's *Swaraj* until her capture in 1932 (jailed until 1947; Jawaharlal Nehru bestowed the title *"Rani"* in 1937).
- **Bihar & Bengal — Anti-Chaukidari Tax:** In landlocked Bihar, symbolic salt extraction at **Nakhas Pond (Patna)** and Saran rapidly evolved into a formidable mass refusal to pay the hated **Chaukidari tax** (levied to maintain village watchmen viewed as colonial spies). Bengal districts—especially **Midnapore, Arambagh, and Bankura**—combined salt defiance with anti-Chaukidari and anti-Union Board tax boycotts (alongside revolutionary resurgence marked by Surya Sen's April 1930 Chittagong Armoury Raid).
- **Central Provinces, Maharashtra & Karnataka — Forest Satyagrahas:** Peasant and *Adivasi* communities defied colonial Forest Act grazing and timber bans (notably **Ganjan Singh Korku's** Jungle Satyagraha in Betul/Seoni, Bilasi in Maharashtra, and Sirsi/Siddapur in Karnataka).
- **Assam — Anti-Cunningham Circular Agitation (May 1930):** When **J.R. Cunningham** (Director of Public Instruction) mandated that students and guardians sign good-behavior undertakings forfeiting political activity, Assam erupted into a massive student strike that birthed indigenous schools like the **Kamrup Academy**.
- **Maharashtra — Sholapur Textile Uprising (May 1930):** News of Gandhi's arrest ignited a militant general strike by Sholapur textile workers, who burned liquor shops, police posts, and railway stations and administered a **virtual parallel government** for several days until martial law crushed them (four leaders—Mallappa Dhanshetti, Jagannath Shinde, Qurban Hussain, and Srikrishna Sarda—were hanged in January 1931).
- **United Provinces — No-Revenue, No-Rent:** Triggered by collapsing Great Depression crop prices, Congress urged zamindars to withhold revenue from the Raj and tenants to withhold rent from loyalist *taluqdars*—producing a radical rural ferment that alarmed conservative landlords.
- **Grassroots Political Media:** Across towns and villages, activists innovated *Prabhat Pheris* (dawn devotional-nationalist singing marches), *Vanar Senas* (boys' brigades) and *Manjari Senas* (girls' brigades), clandestine cyclostyled *Patrikas*, and magic-lantern slides.

### Social Coalition, Great Depression Economics, and Structural Limits (CDM vs. NCM)

Civil Disobedience coincided with the onset of the **Great Depression (1929)**, which devastated Indian agrarian export prices while colonial land revenue demands remained rigidly fixed in cash. Rich and middle peasant strata (Gujarat's Patidars, UP's Jats, Midnapore's Mahishyas) surged into CDM to stave off debt foreclosures.

Simultaneously, Indian industrialists—led by **G.D. Birla**, **Purshottamdas Thakurdas**, and **FICCI**—provided substantial covert financing and moral backing during 1930, seeking tariff autonomy, coastal reservation, and relief from the overvalued 1s 6d rupee. Yet as **Sumit Sarkar** highlights, this business coalition turned deeply anxious by late 1930 when no-rent campaigns, Sholapur militancy, and collapsing commerce threatened property order—putting pressure on Congress to seek a negotiated truce.

Measured against the **Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22)**, Civil Disobedience revealed four decisive structural shifts:

- **Escalated Objective and Method:** NCM demanded an undefined *Swaraj* plus Khilafat/Punjab redressal through passive institutional boycott; CDM demanded ***Purna Swaraj*** through **active, deliberate violation of colonial laws** from Day 1.
- **Unprecedented Scale & Economic Impact:** Between **90,000 and 120,000 satyagrahis** were imprisoned (nearly triple NCM's tally); British cloth imports plunged sharply, liquor excise collapsed, and provincial revenue collections faltered.
- **Declining Muslim Participation:** Unlike the Khilafat-energized NCM, **Muslim participation dropped sharply** across most of British India due to post-Nehru Report alienation, active Muslim League abstention, and British communal patronage. Major exceptions were the massive Pashtun mobilization in **NWFP** (*Khudai Khidmatgars*), Bihar's weaver communities (*Momins*), and pockets of East Bengal (Senhatta, Tripura, Dacca).
- **Localized Working-Class Role:** Apart from Sholapur, Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras dock/textile pockets, organized industrial labor stayed largely aloof—its communist vanguard decapitated by the **Meerut Conspiracy Case arrests (March 1929)** and constrained by the Comintern's sectarian "Third Period" directive attacking Congress as a "bourgeois-reformist" party.

## 4. Round Table Diplomacy, the Karachi Vision, and the Communal Award (1930–1934)

### First Round Table Conference (November 12, 1930 – January 19, 1931)

Even as jails overflowed in India, British Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** opened the **First Round Table Conference** in London—the **first constitutional summit in imperial history where British and Indian leaders met as nominal equals**.

With the **Indian National Congress boycotting** and its leadership behind bars, 89 delegates attended: **16 British party leaders**, **16 Princely State delegates**, and **57–58 British Indian representatives** spanning every non-Congress interest—the **Muslim League** (Aga Khan III, M.A. Jinnah, Maulana Muhammad Ali, Fazlul Haq), **Liberals** (Tej Bahadur Sapru, V.S. Srinivasa Sastri, C.Y. Chintamani), **Hindu Mahasabha** (M.R. Jayakar, B.S. Moonje), **Depressed Classes** (**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, Rettamalai Srinivasan), **Sikhs** (Sardar Ujjal Singh), **Women** (Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz, Radhabai Subbarayan), **Indian Christians** (K.T. Paul), Justice Party, Anglo-Indians, and Indian business (Homi Mody).

The conference produced a surprise constitutional pivot: **Tej Bahadur Sapru** proposed an **All-India Federation** uniting British India and the Princely States, which the princes unexpectedly accepted in principle (calculating that princely nominees in a federal legislature would dilute Congress democracy and curtail arbitrary Viceregal paramountcy). MacDonald conceded provincial autonomy and limited central responsibility hedged with British "safeguards" over defence and finance. Yet everyone recognized that negotiating India's future without Congress was staging *Hamlet* without the Prince of Denmark.

### The Gandhi–Irwin (Delhi) Pact (March 5, 1931)

Determined to bring Congress to London, Lord Irwin unconditionally released Gandhi and the CWC on **January 25, 1931**. Mediated by Liberal statesmen **Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **M.R. Jayakar**, and **V.S. Srinivasa Sastri**, fifteen days of direct negotiations between Gandhi and Irwin culminated in the **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (Delhi Pact)** on **March 5, 1931**.

Symbolically, the Pact placed the Indian National Congress on **equal diplomatic footing** with the British Empire—provoking **Winston Churchill's** furious lament over the *"nauseating and humiliating spectacle of this one-time Inner Temple lawyer, now seditious fakir, striding half-naked up the steps of the Viceroy's palace"* to parley on equal terms with the King-Emperor's representative. Substantively, the terms were sharply bounded:

- **What the British Government Conceded:**
  1. Immediate release of all political prisoners **not convicted of violence**.
  2. Remission of all fines not yet collected and **return of confiscated lands not yet sold to third parties**.
  3. Lenient treatment for government servants who had resigned during CDM.
  4. Right to **collect or manufacture salt in coastal villages for personal consumption** (explicitly **not for commercial sale**).
  5. Right to **peaceful and non-aggressive picketing** of liquor and foreign-cloth shops.
  6. Withdrawal of all emergency ordinances promulgated during CDM.
- **Two Crucial Demands Irwin REFUSED:**
  1. Rejected Gandhi's demand for a **public inquiry into police excesses**.
  2. Refused to **commute the death sentences of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru** to life imprisonment (though Gandhi pressed Irwin personally, he refused to make commutation a deal-breaker for the Pact; the three revolutionaries were hanged in Lahore on **March 23, 1931**).
- **What Congress Conceded:** Suspended the Civil Disobedience Movement and agreed to participate in the **Second Round Table Conference**.

*Analytical Assessment:* Left-wing critics (Subhas Bose, Jawaharlal Nehru briefly) viewed the Pact as a premature retreat that left peasants' already-auctioned lands unrecovered and failed to save Bhagat Singh. Conversely, Bipan Chandra notes that mass movements face diminishing marginal stamina—after a year of brutal repression and peasant exhaustion, securing a dignified truce without surrendering *Purna Swaraj* preserved organizational capacity for the next round.

### The Karachi Congress and the Social-Democratic Blueprint (March 29–31, 1931)

Convened barely six days after the execution of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru, the **special Karachi Congress Session**—presided over by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**—opened amid intense youth grief. Volunteers of the *Naujawan Bharat Sabha* greeted Gandhi with black flags and cries of *"Down with Gandhism"*. Congress passed a balanced resolution admiring the bravery and supreme sacrifice of the three martyrs while dissociating itself from political violence, formally endorsed the **Gandhi–Irwin Pact**, and reiterated ***Purna Swaraj***.

Karachi's enduring constitutional legacy was its landmark **Resolution on Fundamental Rights and National Economic Programme**—drafted chiefly by **Jawaharlal Nehru** and refined with Gandhi. For the first time, Congress defined precisely what *Swaraj* meant for ordinary citizens, directly prefiguring Parts III and IV of India's 1950 Constitution:

- **Fundamental Civil & Political Rights:** Freedom of speech, press, assembly, and association; **equality before the law** irrespective of caste, creed, or sex; **religious neutrality of the state**; **universal adult franchise**; free and compulsory primary education; protection of minority cultures, scripts, and languages; and equal access to public wells, roads, and schools.
- **National Economic Programme:** Living wage for industrial workers, capped working hours, healthy workplace conditions, maternity benefits, prohibition of child labor, right to form unions, substantial reduction in land revenue and rent, exemption of uneconomic holdings, relief from rural indebtedness, progressive income tax, and **state ownership or control of key industries, mineral resources, railways, waterways, shipping, and other public transport**.

### Second Round Table Conference and Deadlock (September 7 – December 1, 1931)

The **Second Round Table Conference** in London was the **only RTC attended by the Indian National Congress**. Rather than fielding a delegation, Congress nominated **Mahatma Gandhi as its sole official representative** (accompanied by Sarojini Naidu representing Indian women, Madan Mohan Malaviya in a personal capacity, G.D. Birla, Mirabehn, Mahadev Desai, and Pyarelal; alongside Ambedkar, Jinnah, Muhammad Iqbal, Aga Khan, and Sapru).

By autumn 1931, however, Britain's political ground had shifted sharply rightward. Economic crisis had toppled Labour; Ramsay MacDonald now presided over a Conservative-dominated **National Government**, reactionary Tory **Sir Samuel Hoare** had become Secretary of State, and hardliner **Lord Willingdon** had replaced the conciliatory Irwin as Viceroy.

Proceedings rapidly deadlocked in the **Minorities Committee**:

- Gandhi insisted that Congress alone spoke for over 85% of India—including the Depressed Classes—and vehemently opposed **separate electorates for Untouchables**, arguing it would permanently fracture Hindu society without dismantling caste stigma.
- Rejecting Congress's monopoly claim, representatives of Muslims, Depressed Classes (**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**), Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans forged a joint **Minorities Pact** demanding separate electorates across communities.

With imperial Tories refusing immediate responsible government at the Centre, MacDonald closed the session on **December 1, 1931** with three unilateral announcements: (1) creation of **two Muslim-majority provinces—NWFP and Sind**; (2) appointment of three expert committees—**Lothian Committee** (Franchise), **Percy Committee** (Federal Finance), and **Davidson Committee** (Indian States); and (3) a blunt warning that London would impose a unilateral **Communal Award** if Indian parties failed to agree.

### Renewed Repression, the Communal Award, and the Poona Pact (1932)

Returning to Bombay empty-handed on **December 28, 1931**, Gandhi discovered that Viceroy **Lord Willingdon** had already launched a pre-emptive war ("Civil Martial Law") against Congress in Bengal, UP, and NWFP (arresting Nehru and Ghaffar Khan). When Willingdon refused even to grant Gandhi an interview, the CWC resumed the **Second Phase of Civil Disobedience (January 1932 – April 1934)**.

Willingdon struck with ruthless bureaucratic precision: Gandhi was arrested on **January 4, 1932**, Congress organizations were outlawed nationwide, ashram properties and funds were confiscated, nationalist newspapers were gagged, and between **80,000 and 120,000 activists** were jailed. Though Congress defiantly held outlawed annual sessions in Delhi (1932) and Calcutta (1933, chaired by **Nellie Sengupta**), relentless coercion steadily sapped street momentum.

On **August 16, 1932**, Prime Minister **Ramsay MacDonald** dropped Britain's sharpest political wedge: the **Communal Award**, drawn from the **Lothian Franchise Committee's** findings. Extending the 1909 and 1919 divide-and-rule framework, it retained separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans, added special constituencies for **Women** (3% reserved in all provinces except NWFP), **Marathas** (select seats in Bombay), labor, landlords, commerce, and universities—and, most explosively, **recognized the Depressed Classes as a distinct minority entitled to separate electorates (71 provincial seats) PLUS a double vote** (one vote in a separate Dalit constituency, one in the general constituency) for twenty years.

While Congress adopted a neutral posture ("neither accept nor reject") toward Muslim and Sikh awards to avoid alienating minorities, Gandhi regarded separating the Depressed Classes as an existential vivisection of Hindu society. From **Yerwada Central Jail (Pune)**, he commenced an **indefinite fast unto death on September 20, 1932**. *(The deeper philosophical clash over caste emancipation is examined in Chapter 9; the constitutional mechanics sit squarely within the 1932–34 arc.)*

Under immense emotional pressure as Gandhi's health deteriorated, intense negotiations led to the **Poona Pact**, signed at Yerwada Jail on **September 24, 1932** by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** (representing the Depressed Classes) and **Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya** (representing caste Hindus, alongside 21 other signatories including Sapru, Jayakar, Rajaji, Rajendra Prasad, M.C. Rajah, and G.D. Birla—**Gandhi himself did not formally sign the document**):

- **Abandonment of Separate Electorates:** Replaced separate electorates for the Depressed Classes with **joint electorates and reserved seats**.
- **Expanded Provincial Reservation:** Raised reserved provincial seats from **71** (under MacDonald's Award) to **148** per the primary signed text (Madras 30, Bombay with Sind 15, Punjab 8, Bihar & Orissa 18, Central Provinces 20, Assam 7, Bengal 30, United Provinces 20). *(Dual-standard parity: several Indian textbooks and coaching digests print **147** due to a legacy transcription of Bombay with Sind as 14.)*
- **Central Legislature Reservation:** Reserved **18% of the seats allotted to the general electorate for British India** in the Central Legislature (exact primary Clause 4 text; *occasionally misprinted as 19% in secondary compilations*).
- **Two-Tier Primary System:** Registered Depressed Class voters in each reserved constituency formed an electoral college electing a **panel of four candidates** (by single vote), from whom the general joint electorate selected the final legislator. This primary system was set to lapse after **ten years** unless ended sooner by mutual agreement, while seat reservations continued until terminated by mutual consent.
- **Civic & Educational Safeguards:** Mandated fair representation in public services and earmarked shares of provincial educational grants for Depressed Class upliftment.

The British Cabinet formally accepted the Poona Pact on **September 26, 1932** as an amendment to the Communal Award, whereupon Gandhi broke his fast. Immediately afterward, Gandhi pivoted his political energy toward anti-untouchability reconstruction: he founded the **All India Anti-Untouchability League (September 1932)**—renamed **Harijan Sevak Sangh** with **G.D. Birla** as its first President—launched the weekly journal ***Harijan*** (early 1933), and undertook an arduous 12,500-mile **Harijan Tour (November 1933 – July 1934)**.

### Third Round Table Conference and the Winding Down of CDM (1932–1934)

The **Third Round Table Conference (November 17 – December 24, 1932)**—derided as the *"Pocket Conference"*—was the smallest and least representative of the three, attended by just **46 delegates**. Crucially, it was **boycotted by both the Indian National Congress and the British Labour Party**, while **M.A. Jinnah** was neither invited nor present (living in self-imposed legal exile in London).

Notable Indian leaders who participated across **all three Round Table Conferences** included **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, **Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru**, **M.R. Jayakar**, **N.M. Joshi**, **Aga Khan III**, and **Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz**. *(Shortly after this session, in January 1933, Cambridge student **Choudhary Rahmat Ali** circulated his pamphlet "Now or Never", coining the acronym **PAKSTAN**.)*

Though politically hollow, the Third RTC finalized the technical architecture published in Britain's **White Paper on Indian Constitutional Reforms (March 1933)**. Scrutinized by a **Joint Parliamentary Committee** chaired by **Lord Linlithgow (1933–34)**, these recommendations were enacted directly into the **Government of India Act, 1935**.

Back in India, Gandhi suspended mass Civil Disobedience in **May 1933** (shifting to Individual Civil Disobedience in August 1933) and formally **withdrew the movement unconditionally in April 1934** (ratified by the Patna AICC in May 1934). At the **Bombay Congress Session (October 1934)**, presided over by **Rajendra Prasad**, Gandhi **resigned from the primary membership of the INC** to devote himself to rural self-reliance via the **All India Village Industries Association (AIVIA, Wardha)**—while remaining the movement's supreme moral arbiter. The tactical pause immediately opened two new arenas: the emergence of the **Congress Socialist Party (May 1934)** and a revived Swarajist-style push toward contesting the 1934 Central Assembly elections.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Belgaum Session (1924) Singularity:** Belgaum was the **only** INC annual session ever presided over by Mahatma Gandhi—and it ratified the Gandhi–Das–Nehru Pact making the Swaraj Party an integral legislative wing of Congress, *not* a split.
> - **Simon Commission Cooperators vs. Boycotters:** Never assume all Indian organizations boycotted Simon. The **Unionist Party (Punjab)**, **Justice Party (Madras)**, **Shafi wing of the Muslim League**, and **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** (presenting his *Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha* memorandum) interacted with the Commission.
> - **Nehru Report (1928) Traps:** It demanded **Dominion Status** (NOT *Purna Swaraj*), **joint electorates** (NOT separate electorates), **central residuary powers** (NOT provincial), and **denied seat reservations to Muslims in Punjab and Bengal**—the exact clause that broke consensus with Jinnah's Delhi Proposals.
> - **Butler Committee (1927) Mandate:** Frequently confused with Centre–Province financial or legislative division; its exclusive mandate was the **relationship between the British Paramount Power (Crown) and the Indian Princely States**.
> - **Gandhi's Dandi Quote & Gender Detail:** *"Sedition has become my religion"* was declared while **violating the Salt Law at Dandi (April 1930)**, not at Champaran or Quit India. Furthermore, **no women were included among the original 78 Dandi marchers** departing Sabarmati, though Sarojini Naidu joined at Dandi and led the Dharasana raid.
> - **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931) Exclusion Trap:** The Pact did **NOT** grant a public inquiry into police excesses, did **NOT** release prisoners convicted of violent offences, did **NOT** commute Bhagat Singh's death sentence, did **NOT** restore lands already sold to third parties, and permitted salt-making in coastal villages strictly for **personal consumption (not sale)**.
> - **Karachi Session (1931) President vs. Drafter:** **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** presided over the Karachi Session, whereas **Jawaharlal Nehru** primarily drafted the *Resolution on Fundamental Rights and National Economic Programme*.
> - **Round Table Attendance Matrix:** **INC and Gandhi** attended **ONLY the 2nd RTC**; **M.A. Jinnah** attended the **1st and 2nd** (absent from the 3rd); **British Labour Party** boycotted the **3rd RTC**; **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and **Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru** (alongside M.R. Jayakar, N.M. Joshi, Aga Khan III, and Begum Jahanara Shahnawaz) attended **all three RTCs**.
> - **Poona Pact (1932) Signatory & Numeric Parity:** Signed by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and **Madan Mohan Malaviya** (Gandhi did *not* formally sign the document). Exact signed primary figures are **148 provincial seats** (vs. **71** in the Communal Award; legacy coaching texts occasionally print **147**) and **18%** of general British-Indian seats in the **Central Legislature** (not provincial legislatures, and not 19%).
> - **Harijan Campaign Timing:** Gandhi's formal institutionalization of Harijan upliftment (*All India Anti-Untouchability League / Harijan Sevak Sangh* and *Harijan* weekly) followed immediately **after the Poona Pact (September 1932)**—not after the Gandhi–Irwin Pact or GOI Act 1935.

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature

- **Clause-Swapping Across Constitutional Milestones:** UPSC routinely swaps provisions between the **Nehru Report (1928)**, **Karachi Resolution (1931)**, **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931)**, and **Poona Pact (1932)**—for example, attributing *Purna Swaraj* or provincial residuary powers to the Nehru Report, or assigning 18% Depressed Classes reservation to provincial rather than central legislatures.
- **False Chronological Compression:** Examiners test tight micro-sequences inside 1927–1932—especially **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (Mar 5, 1931) → Execution of Bhagat Singh (Mar 23, 1931) → Karachi Session (Mar 29–31, 1931)** and **Second RTC (late 1931) → Communal Award (Aug 1932) → Poona Pact (Sept 1932) → Third RTC (Nov–Dec 1932)**.
- **Monolithic Participation Fallacy:** Any absolute statement claiming "universal boycott" of the Simon Commission, "complete attendance" by Congress at all RTCs, or "uniform Muslim/working-class surge" during CDM is an immediate elimination trigger.
- **Regional Leader–Site Inversions:** Watch for deliberate swaps among regional CDM hubs—Rajagopalachari (Vedaranyam/Tanjore), K. Kelappan (Payyanur/Malabar), Gopabandhu Choudhury (Inchudi/Odisha), Sarojini Naidu (Dharasana/Gujarat), Chandra Singh Garhwali (Peshawar/2/18th Garhwal Rifles), Rani Gaidinliu (Manipur–Nagaland *Heraka*), and Cunningham Circular (Assam).
