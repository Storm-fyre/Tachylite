# Chapter 5 - Fiscal Architecture, Income-Tax Act 2025, Next-Gen GST 2.0 & Public Debt Sustainability

Fiscal policy is the macroeconomic steering wheel through which a sovereign allocates resources, redistributes income, and stabilizes business cycles via taxation, expenditure, and borrowing. Between September 2025 and April 2026, India executed a simultaneous three-pillar redesign of its entire fiscal operating system: **Next-Gen GST 2.0** (effective September 22, 2025), the ***Income-Tax Act, 2025*** (effective April 1, 2026), and the **post-FY26 shift to a Central Government Debt-to-GDP anchor** targeting **50 ± 1% by March 2031**. Mastering this chapter demands holding accounting identities, statutory transitions, and macroeconomic transmission mechanisms in a single coherent frame.

## Budgetary Mechanics, Capex Multipliers & Deficit Arithmetic

### Constitutional Spine & the Revenue–Capital Boundary

India's budgetary architecture rests on four constitutional guardrails: **Article 265** forbids levying or collecting any tax without statutory authority, **Article 112** mandates laying the *Annual Financial Statement* before Parliament, **Article 114** prohibits withdrawing money from the **Consolidated Fund of India (Article 266(1))** without an Appropriation Act, and **Article 266(2)** separates the **Public Account**—where the Union acts merely as a banker for Provident Funds and Small Savings without requiring parliamentary appropriation. Following the **2017–18 structural reforms**, the Union merged the Railway Budget into the General Budget, advanced presentation to February 1, and abolished the archaic Plan vs. Non-Plan distinction in favour of the analytically sharper **Revenue vs. Capital** classification.

Every single budgetary transaction is sorted by one strict **stock-vs-flow accounting test**: *does the receipt or outflow alter the government's physical/financial assets or debt liabilities?*

- **Revenue Receipts:** Recurring inflows that neither create a repayment liability nor reduce a sovereign asset—comprising **Tax Revenue** (net to Centre after Finance Commission devolution) and **Non-Tax Revenue** (interest receipts, RBI and CPSE dividends, spectrum fees, fines, and external grants).
- **Revenue Expenditure:** Consumption and maintenance outflows that neither create a physical/financial asset for the Union nor extinguish a liability—covering **interest payments** (₹14.04 lakh crore in BE 2026–27, devouring **26.3% of total expenditure** and **~40% of revenue receipts**), salaries, pensions, subsidies, defence revenue, and **all grants-in-aid to States**. Crucially, even a grant expressly earmarked for a State to build a highway is booked as *Revenue Expenditure* in the Union Budget because legal ownership of the resulting asset vests with the State, not the Centre.
- **Capital Receipts:** Inflows that either create a future liability (**Debt-Creating Capital Receipts**: dated G-Secs, Treasury Bills, external sovereign loans, NSSF/Small Savings net inflows) or shrink an existing asset (**Non-Debt Capital Receipts**: disinvestment proceeds and recovery of past loans).
- **Capital Expenditure (Capex):** Outflows that create durable assets (highways, railways, defence hardware, equity infusion into CPSEs, and sovereign loans extended to States/PSUs) or reduce sovereign liabilities (**principal repayment of past debt**, whereas interest on that same debt sits on the Revenue Account).

### Capex "Crowding-In" Multipliers vs. Financial "Crowding-Out"

The macroeconomic case for India's post-2021 Capex pivot rests on asymmetric **fiscal multipliers**—the cumulative rupee increase in GDP triggered by a ₹1 rise in public spending. Empirical estimates by the RBI and NIPFP place India's **Revenue Expenditure multiplier below unity (~0.45–0.98)** and front-loaded in Year 1, whereas the **Capital Expenditure multiplier ranges between ~2.45 and 3.25** across 2–4 years because transport corridors, power grids, and ports raise the marginal productivity of private capital.

This generates a structural tension between two opposing forces:
1. **Physical Crowding-In:** Public infrastructure absorbs high upfront gestation risk, lowers logistics costs, and lifts expected returns on private plants—pulling dormant corporate investment off the sidelines.
2. **Financial Crowding-Out:** Because public Capex is largely debt-financed (Gross Market Borrowing of **₹17.2 lakh crore** and Net Market Borrowing of **₹11.7 lakh crore** in BE 2026–27), heavy sovereign bond issuance competes for finite domestic household financial savings. If sovereign borrowing outstrips liquidity growth, G-Sec yields rise, lifting corporate borrowing spreads and squeezing rate-sensitive private investment.

Union Budget 2026–27 calibrates this trade-off by holding core Capex at **₹12.22 lakh crore** (~23% of total expenditure of ₹53.47 lakh crore) while lifting **Effective Capital Expenditure** (Union Capex + Grants-in-Aid to States for Creation of Capital Assets) to **₹17.1 lakh crore**, supported by **₹1.5 lakh crore** in 50-year interest-free Capex loans to States under SASCI. By simultaneously compressing the fiscal deficit, the Centre seeks physical crowding-in without triggering yield-curve crowding-out.

### Deficit Taxonomy & the February 2026 Dual-Base Parity Shock

A raw deficit figure reveals nothing about whether the state is borrowing to build freight corridors or merely to pay pensions and old interest. Five distinct deficit metrics dissect that quality:

1. **Revenue Deficit ($\text{RD} = \text{Revenue Expenditure} - \text{Revenue Receipts}$):** Measures sovereign **dissaving**. A positive RD means the government is borrowing solely to fund routine consumption, shrinking national savings without adding productive capacity.
2. **Effective Revenue Deficit ($\text{ERD} = \text{RD} - \text{Grants for Creation of Capital Assets}$):** Introduced in FY 2011–12 and codified via the 2012 FRBM amendment to correct the accounting distortion whereby asset-creating transfers to States inflate the Union's Revenue Deficit. ERD isolates pure consumption dissaving.
3. **Gross Fiscal Deficit ($\text{GFD} = \text{Total Expenditure} - [\text{Revenue Receipts} + \text{Non-Debt Capital Receipts}]$):** Identically equal to the government's **total net borrowing requirement** during the fiscal year. Subtracting net lending (loans advanced minus recoveries) yields the **Net Fiscal Deficit (NFD)**. Note that *no official "Capital Deficit" metric* exists in India's *Budget at a Glance*.
4. **Gross Primary Deficit ($\text{GPD} = \text{GFD} - \text{Interest Payments}$):** Strips out inherited debt-servicing obligations to reveal the **current year's discretionary fiscal stance**. A zero Primary Deficit implies the government is borrowing strictly to pay interest on past debt; a positive GPD (0.7%–0.77% of GDP in FY27 BE) means fresh borrowing still exceeds the interest bill.
5. **Monetized Deficit:** The net increase in **RBI credit to the Central Government** (sum of RBI's net holdings of Treasury Bills/dated securities plus drawdown of government cash balances with RBI). Until 1997, fiscal deficits were automatically monetized via **91-day ad-hoc Treasury Bills**, expanding reserve money ($M_0$) and igniting chronic inflation. The landmark **1994 and March 1997 agreements** between RBI Governor C. Rangarajan and Finance Minister P. Chidambaram permanently abolished ad-hoc T-Bills effective April 1, 1997; subsequently, **Section 5 of the FRBM Act, 2003** barred RBI from subscribing to primary G-Sec issuances except under tightly defined escape-clause conditions.

> **Dual-Standard Numeric Parity Alert (Feb 1 vs. Feb 27, 2026):** Union Budget 2026–27 was presented on **February 1, 2026** using the legacy `2011–12` GDP series. Twenty-six days later, on **February 27, 2026**, MoSPI released the new `2022–23` GDP base series, revising FY26 nominal GDP downward by ~₹11.67 lakh crore (~3.3%, from ₹357.14 lakh crore to ₹345.47 lakh crore). Without a single extra rupee being spent, every deficit-to-GDP and debt-to-GDP ratio shifted mechanically upward—for instance, FY27 BE Fiscal Deficit sits at **4.3% of GDP** on the Budget-Day `2011–12` base but **4.46% of GDP** on the post-revision `2022–23` base (Ministry of Finance *Monthly Economic Review*, Feb 2026). ### Modern Budgeting Frameworks

India supplements line-item incremental budgeting with three analytical disciplines:
- **Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB):** Forces every ministry to justify every rupee of outlay afresh from a zero baseline rather than rolling forward the prior year's appropriation plus an incremental mark-up. Applied selectively in India during scheme sunset reviews and expenditure rationalization exercises.
- **Outcome Budgeting:** Shifts administrative accountability from financial **outlays** (rupees spent) to physical **outputs** (classrooms built) and measurable **outcomes** (learning gains). Since 2017–18, the Union presents a unified Output–Outcome Monitoring Framework (OOMF) drafted jointly by the Department of Expenditure and NITI Aayog covering Central Sector and Centrally Sponsored Schemes.
- **Gender Budgeting:** Operationalized since FY 2005–06 via **Budget Statement 13** (Gender Budget Statement) to audit how fiscal policy addresses structural gender gaps. It disaggregates outlays into **Part A** (100% women-specific schemes), **Part B** (30%–99% pro-women allocations), and **Part C** (sub-30% allocations introduced to broaden visibility across infrastructure and general welfare schemes).

### Liquidity Bridges, Sovereign Green Bonds & Public Asset Recycling

- **Ways and Means Advances (WMA):** Constituted under **Section 17(5) of the RBI Act, 1934** on April 1, 1997 to replace ad-hoc Treasury Bills. WMA is strictly a **temporary cash-flow bridge** for mismatches between tax receipts and spending—not a permanent source of deficit financing. Advances must be vacated within **90 days** and carry interest at the prevailing **RBI Policy Repo Rate**; breaches beyond mutually agreed half-yearly limits trigger **Overdraft** priced at **Repo Rate + 2%** (permissible for up to 10 consecutive working days). WMA covers the Union and all States **except Sikkim** (which has no banking agreement with RBI).
- **Sovereign Green Bonds (SGrBs):** Anchored in the November 2022 Sovereign Green Bond Framework, SGrBs ring-fence borrowed proceeds exclusively for public-sector low-carbon projects (clean transit, renewables, green hydrogen, energy efficiency), explicitly **excluding** nuclear power, fossil-fuel extraction/generation, and hydropower plants >25 MW. Issued via RBI auctions, India's SGrBs command a modest intermittent **"greenium"** (0–6 bps lower yield than conventional G-Secs). In **June 2026**, RBI significantly deepened foreign demand by designating all new **5-, 7-, 10-, 15-, 30-, and 40-year SGrBs** as **Fully Accessible Route (FAR)** securities—removing quantitative FPI ceilings—alongside dedicated IFSC trading and settlement.
- **DIPAM & Public Asset Value Maximization:** The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (Ministry of Finance) has pivoted away from distress equity sales aimed solely at plugging fiscal gaps toward a **holistic asset-management strategy** combining calibrated strategic disinvestment, steady CPSE dividends, debt ETFs (**Bharat Bond ETF**), and balance-sheet unlocking. Listed CPSE market capitalization surged roughly **3.5x** from ₹12.10 lakh crore (2021) to **₹42.76 lakh crore** (early 2026), while FY27 BE targets **₹80,000 crore** in miscellaneous capital receipts.
- **National Monetisation Pipeline 2.0 (NMP 2.0):** Prepared by NITI Aayog in consultation with infrastructure ministries for **FY 2025–26 to FY 2029–30**, NMP 2.0 scales India's brownfield asset-recycling ambition to a **Total Monetisation Value of ₹16.72 lakh crore**—over 2.6x NMP 1.0's ₹6 lakh crore target (of which **₹5.3 lakh crore, or ~89%**, was realized during FY22–FY25). The expanded envelope includes **₹5.8 lakh crore** in private-sector investment across **12 sectors**, heavily concentrated (~87–88%) in five core verticals: **Highways/MMLPs/Ropeways (₹4.42L Cr)**, **Power (₹2.77L Cr)**, **Ports (₹2.64L Cr)**, **Railways (₹2.62L Cr)**, and **Coal (₹2.16L Cr)**. Crucially, asset monetization recycles cash flows from de-risked operational brownfield assets via ToT concessions, securitization, and InvITs while **retaining ultimate sovereign ownership**—distinct from equity privatization.
- **Budget 2026–27 Dedicated CPSE REITs:** To accelerate NMP 2.0 without fire-selling sovereign land, Union Budget 2026–27 announced **dedicated Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)** to pool and monetize vast, underutilized income-generating commercial real estate and land parcels held by CPSEs across railways, ports, power transmission, and telecom. Paired with RBI's move enabling calibrated bank lending to eligible listed REITs, CPSE REITs convert illiquid sovereign realty into transparent, SEBI-regulated, yield-bearing capital-market instruments accessible to both institutional and retail investors.

## Direct Tax Architecture & the *Income-Tax Act, 2025*

### Tax Mechanics - Buoyancy, Elasticity, Laffer Curve & Tax Expenditure

Direct taxes (personal income tax and corporate tax) satisfy the **ability-to-pay principle** because statutory liability and economic incidence fall on the same person, making them inherently **progressive** (average tax rate rises with income)—unlike indirect taxes, which are structurally **regressive** relative to household income. Four analytical ratios govern direct-tax performance:

- **Tax Buoyancy ($\frac{\%\Delta \text{Tax Revenue}}{\%\Delta \text{Nominal GDP}}$):** Measures total responsiveness of tax receipts to GDP growth, capturing *both* automatic economic expansion *and* discretionary policy changes (rate hikes, base widening, compliance drives). Buoyancy > 1 raises the Tax-to-GDP ratio automatically.
- **Tax Elasticity:** Isolates the **built-in automatic responsiveness** of tax revenue to GDP growth while holding statutory tax rates, exemptions, and laws strictly constant. High buoyancy with low elasticity signals fragile revenue gains reliant on repeated rate hikes rather than organic economic formalization.
- **Laffer Curve:** Maps an inverted-U relationship between marginal tax rates and aggregate tax revenue. Beyond an optimum rate $t^*$, higher marginal rates trigger a dominant **substitution effect** (discouraging formal work/investment and incentivizing evasion or jurisdictional arbitrage) over the revenue effect, shrinking collections. India's shift toward lower, exemption-free slabs paired with digital data triangulation (AIS, SFT, GSTN-CBDT API sync) seeks to harvest the upward-sloping compliance dividend of the Laffer curve.
- **Tax Expenditure:** Revenue consciously foregone by the sovereign through exemptions, deductions, accelerated depreciation, and tax holidays (reported annually in the Budget's *Statement of Revenue Impact of Tax Incentives*). While targeted incentives—such as Budget 2026–27's **tax holiday until 2047** for foreign cloud providers operating via Indian data centres and the expansion of **IFSC/GIFT City tax holidays from 10 to 20 years** (followed by a 15% flat rate)—can seed strategic clusters, unchecked tax expenditures narrow the base and breed litigation.

### The *Income-Tax Act, 2025* (Effective April 1, 2026)

Over six decades, the *Income-Tax Act, 1961* accumulated more than **800 sections, 4,000+ amendments**, tangled provisos, and redundant sunset clauses that drove litigation backlogs and compliance friction. Following the July 2024 review, the Lok Sabha referred the February 2025 Bill to a **31-member Parliamentary Select Committee chaired by Baijayant Panda**; Parliament passed the revised Bill in August 2025, receiving Presidential assent on **August 21, 2025 (Act No. 30 of 2025)**. The statute took effect on **April 1, 2026**, accompanied by the simplified ***Income-Tax Rules, 2026***, formally repealing the 1961 Act.

Key structural overhauls under the new code:
- **Lean Statutory Architecture:** Condenses direct-tax law into **536 sections across 23 chapters and 16 schedules**, replacing convoluted prose with plain language, mathematical formulas, consolidated tabular withholding (TDS/TCS) schedules, and code-based reporting. Crucially, the statute modernizes *form and clarity* without imposing surprise new taxes or overturning settled substantive jurisprudence.
- **Unified "Tax Year" (Eliminating Previous Year vs. Assessment Year):** Under the 1961 Act, income earned in *Previous Year* 2025–26 was taxed in *Assessment Year* 2026–27—creating perennial portal mismatches and challan errors. The 2025 Act abolishes the Assessment Year entirely, installing a single **Tax Year** aligned with the 12-month financial year (or a shorter period for newly set-up businesses/income sources). Income earned between April 1, 2026 and March 31, 2027 is **Tax Year 2026–27**, though returns continue to be filed and assessed *after* the close of that Tax Year. During transitional calendar year 2026, returns filed for FY 2025–26 (**AY 2026–27**) remain governed strictly by the repealed 1961 Act, whereas live TDS and advance tax for **Tax Year 2026–27** run under the 2025 Act.
- **New vs. Old Personal Tax Regime Continuity:** **Section 202** of the 2025 Act (replacing Section 115BAC) cements the **New Tax Regime** as the statutory default for individuals and HUFs across all age brackets. Union Budget 2026–27 retained the progressive FY25–26 slabs (Nil up to ₹4L; 5% for ₹4–8L; 10% for ₹8–12L; 15% for ₹12–16L; 20% for ₹16–20L; 25% for ₹20–24L; 30% above ₹24L). Under **Section 156** (replacing Section 87A), resident individuals with total normal income up to **₹12 lakh** receive a rebate up to ₹60,000—making tax liability zero (**₹12.75 lakh** gross for salaried individuals after the ₹75,000 standard deduction under Section 19), cushioned by marginal relief. Crucially, this rebate is **unavailable to NRIs** and **cannot offset special-rate incomes** (such as equity LTCG/STCG or VDAs). The **Old Regime** survives purely via opt-out for taxpayers with heavy deductions (Schedule XV / Section 123 investments, HRA, home-loan interest).
- **Accounting Convergence & TCS Relief (Budget 2026–27):** A Joint MCA–CBDT Committee is integrating Income Computation and Disclosure Standards (ICDS) into **IndAS**, abolishing dual tax-accounting books from **Tax Year 2027–28**. Simultaneously, TCS on overseas remittances under RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) for education and medical treatment was cut from **5% to 2%**.

### Corporate Taxation, MAT Overhaul & International Tax Alignment

- **MAT Cut (`15% → 14%`) & Credit Lock-In (Budget 2026–27):** Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT) was created to prevent profitable "zero-tax" companies from using accelerated depreciation and profit-linked exemptions under the Old Corporate Regime to wipe out tax liability, forcing them to pay tax on *book profits*. Union Budget 2026–27 reduced MAT from **15% to 14%** and converted it into a **final tax** (ending fresh MAT credit generation). Brought-forward MAT credits accumulated up to March 31, 2026 remain usable for set-off—capped at **25% of annual tax liability**—**exclusively when the company shifts to the concessional New Corporate Tax Regime** (base rate 22%), accelerating corporate migration toward an exemption-free tax code.
- **OECD 15% Global Minimum Tax (BEPS 2.0 Pillar Two):** Designed by the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework to halt the global corporate tax "race to the bottom", Pillar Two mandates a **15% effective minimum tax** on multinational enterprises (MNEs) with consolidated global revenues **≥ €750 million**, enforced via interlocking rules: the **Income Inclusion Rule (IIR)** (parent jurisdiction collects top-up tax), the **Undertaxed Profits Rule (UTPR)** (backstop collection by affiliate jurisdictions), and the **Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-Up Tax (QDMTT)** (host country collects the top-up tax first so it does not surrender revenue abroad). As of late 2026, India has not yet enacted Pillar Two into domestic statute. Policymakers confront a sharp strategic trade-off: enacting a QDMTT preserves revenue if an MNE's Indian effective tax falls below 15%, yet blunt adoption could neutralize India's industrial tax incentives (including IFSC and 2047 cloud data-centre holidays), especially after Pillar One stalled and the **January 5, 2026 OECD side-by-side accommodation** reshaped US–global Pillar Two interplay.
- **Equalization Levy Abolition & Significant Economic Presence (SEP):** Traditional bilateral tax treaties (DTAAs) award business-profit taxing rights to a market country only when a foreign firm has a physical **Permanent Establishment (PE)**—an obsolete rule for borderless digital giants. India pioneered two unilateral workarounds: the **Equalization Levy (EL)** (a gross-receipts levy outside the Income-Tax Act, hence bypassing DTAA shields) and domestic **Significant Economic Presence (SEP)** rules. Facing US Section 301 tariff friction and seeking alignment with multilateral negotiations, India dismantled the EL in two clean strokes: the **2% EL on non-resident e-commerce supplies** (introduced 2020) was abolished effective **August 1, 2024**, and the original **6% EL on online digital advertising** ("Google Tax", introduced 2016) was abolished effective **April 1, 2025**. This leaves domestic SEP rules—whose enforcement against treaty partners still requires bilateral DTAA renegotiation—as India's residual digital-nexus lever.
- **Angel Tax Abolition:** Enacted in 2012 under **Section 56(2)(viib)** of the 1961 Act to curb money laundering, the "Angel Tax" taxed unlisted startups on share capital raised at valuations exceeding Fair Market Value, penalizing genuine venture funding based on intangible growth potential. Following persistent startup-ecosystem pushback, Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 **abolished Angel Tax for all classes of domestic and foreign investors** effective FY 2025–26 onwards, and the provision is completely absent from the *Income-Tax Act, 2025*.

### Capital Gains, STT, VDAs & FAST-DS 2026

- **Capital Gains Harmonization & Buyback Arbitrage Fix:** Building on the July 23, 2024 structural overhaul carried intact into the 2025 Act, **Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG)** on STT-paid listed equity/equity mutual funds stand at **20%** (up from 15%), while **Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG)** apply at a uniform **12.5%** across financial and non-financial assets—with an annual **₹1.25 lakh exemption** for listed equities and **removal of indexation** (retaining a grandfathering option of 20% with indexation vs. 12.5% without for resident individuals/HUFs on immovable property acquired before July 23, 2024). **Union Budget 2026–27** closed two major arbitrage windows:
  1. **Share Buybacks:** Reclassified buyback proceeds as **Capital Gains for all shareholders** (instead of dividend income), while imposing an **additional buyback tax on promoters** to yield an effective tax rate of **22% for corporate promoters** and **30% for non-corporate promoters**.
  2. **Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs):** Restricted the capital-gains exemption on SGB maturity redemption strictly to **original subscribers holding continuously until maturity**, denying the tax break to secondary-market buyers.
- **Securities Transaction Tax (STT):** A direct transaction tax levied since 2004 on exchange-traded equities, equity mutual funds, and derivatives, collected automatically by recognized stock exchanges. Alarmed by retail speculation in high-frequency Futures & Options (F&O), **Union Budget 2026–27** hiked derivatives STT effective **April 1, 2026**: sale of options rose from **0.10% → 0.15%** (of option premium), exercised options from **0.125% → 0.15%** (of intrinsic value), and sale of futures from **0.02% → 0.05%** (of traded price).
- **Virtual Digital Assets (VDAs):** Governed by a deliberately deterrent ring-fenced tax regime (originally Sections 115BBH/194S of the 1961 Act, carried forward into ITA 2025): a **flat 30% tax** on gains with zero expense deductions except cost of acquisition, **zero set-off or carry-forward of VDA losses** against any income (even other VDA gains), and a **1% TDS** on transfers exceeding ₹10,000 (₹50,000 for specified persons) to map every transaction trail. **Budget 2026–27** tightened enforcement under **Sections 446 and 509** of ITA 2025 by inserting explicit penalties of **₹200 per day** for non-furnishing of crypto-asset transaction statements and **₹50,000** for furnishing inaccurate information.
- **FAST-DS 2026 (*Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers – Disclosure Scheme, 2026*):** Enacted via Chapter IV (Sections 130–144) of the Finance Act, 2026 and operational from **August 16 to December 31, 2026** (with March 31, 2026 as the asset valuation date), FAST-DS offers returning NRIs, ESOP/RSU holders, and small taxpayers a one-time compliance exit just as CBDT began populating **CRS/FATCA Automatic Exchange of Information (AEOI)** foreign-account feeds directly into taxpayers' AIS portals. It operates two distinct windows:
  - **Category I (Undisclosed Foreign Asset/Income up to ₹1 crore):** Covers untaxed overseas income or assets; settled by paying **30% tax + 30% additional levy (= 60% effective)**—versus the punitive 120% tax-plus-penalty under the Black Money Act, 2015.
  - **Category II (Undeclared Foreign Asset up to ₹5 crore):** Covers assets acquired from already-taxed funds or during non-resident years but inadvertently omitted from Schedule FA; settled by paying a flat **₹1 lakh fee**.
  - Valid declarations confer statutory immunity from penalty and prosecution under the Black Money Act (excluding money-laundering or completed assessment cases), complementing Finance Act 2026's retrospective **decriminalization of minor non-immovable foreign holdings up to ₹20 lakh** from October 1, 2024.

## Indirect Taxes, Customs Architecture & *Next-Gen GST 2.0*

### Customs Duties & the Inverted Duty Structure (IDS)

Indirect taxes fall on goods and services rather than income, allowing businesses to shift economic incidence onto final consumers. At the border, **Customs Duty**—comprising Basic Customs Duty (BCD), Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC), Social Welfare Surcharge (SWS), and Integrated GST (IGST) on imports—functions simultaneously as a revenue earner and an industrial-policy shield.

A chronic pathology in both Customs and domestic GST is the **Inverted Duty Structure (IDS)**—a distortion where raw materials or intermediate inputs attract a **higher tax rate** than the finished output. Under Customs, IDS makes importing finished goods cheaper than manufacturing them domestically, hollowing out domestic value addition. Under GST, IDS leaves manufacturers with trapped **accumulated Input Tax Credit (ITC)**—draining working capital—especially because **Section 54(3) of the CGST Act** allows IDS refunds strictly on *inputs*, denying refunds on *input services* and *capital goods*. Union Budget 2026–27 targeted both fronts by simplifying tariff lines, extending BCD exemptions for aircraft MRO and lithium-ion Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) capital goods, rolling out the AI-assisted **Customs Integrated System (CIS)**, and recasting honest-taxpayer penalties under Section 28(5) of the Customs Act as a stigma-free additional charge.

### Core GST Architecture - Destination Principle, ITC & Digital Compliance

Enacted via the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** and rolled out on July 1, 2017, GST subsumed 17 central and state indirect levies (Central Excise, Service Tax, CVD/SAD, State VAT, CST, Entry Tax, Luxury Tax) into a single national market. Its constitutional spine rests on **Article 246A** (simultaneous Union–State legislative competence), **Article 269A** (exclusive Union levy of IGST on inter-state supplies and imports, apportioned to the destination State), and **Article 279A** (the federal GST Council).

Four operational pillars define how GST works in practice:
1. **Destination-Based Consumption Principle:** Unlike the pre-2017 origin-based Central Sales Tax (CST)—which let manufacturing States export taxes to poorer consuming States—GST accrues to the **jurisdiction where consumption occurs**. Exports are **zero-rated** (output taxed at 0% with full refund of input taxes), whereas imports bear full IGST to ensure domestic neutrality.
2. **Dual Structure & Seamless ITC Chain:** Intra-state supplies attract equal **CGST + SGST/UTGST**; inter-state supplies attract **IGST**. Every registered business offsets taxes paid on inputs (**Input Tax Credit**) against output tax liability, restricting net tax incidence strictly to *value added* at each stage and eliminating cascading ("tax on tax"). Cross-utilization follows a strict statutory hierarchy: IGST credit must be exhausted completely first (against IGST, then CGST/SGST in any order), while **CGST credit and SGST credit can never offset each other**.
3. **Digital Enforcement Spine:** The **e-Way Bill** (mandatory electronic transit permit for consignments exceeding ₹50,000, integrated with FASTag and RFID) tracks physical cargo, while **e-Invoicing** (mandatory real-time Invoice Reference Number generation on the IRP portal for businesses with turnover ≥ ₹5 crore) auto-populates supplier GSTR-1 and buyer GSTR-2B returns, squeezing out fake-invoice credit fraud.
4. **GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT) & Structural Exclusions:** For eight years after rollout, the absence of GSTAT—the statutory second appellate tier between First Appellate Authorities and High Courts—clogged writ dockets across High Courts. Implementing the 56th GST Council roadmap, **GSTAT became operational** to accept appeals by late September 2025 and commence hearings by late December 2025 (setting a June 30, 2026 limitation window for legacy backlog appeals), with its **Principal Bench designated as the National Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling (NAAAR)** to end contradictory State advance rulings. Meanwhile, **five petroleum products** (crude oil, petrol, diesel, ATF, natural gas) remain constitutionally inside GST awaiting a Council trigger date, while **alcohol for human consumption** (constitutionally outside GST), **electricity duty**, and **real-estate stamp duty** remain outside the ITC chain—leaving residual cascading in Indian manufacturing costs.

### *Next-Gen GST 2.0* (56th GST Council — Effective September 22, 2025 & February 1, 2026)

India's original GST launched with an unwieldy four-slab main grid (`5%`, `12%`, `18%`, `28%`) plus `0%`, `3%` bullion, `0.25%` rough diamonds, and an overlapping **GST Compensation Cess**—spawning endless classification litigation (*caramelized vs. salted popcorn; paratha vs. roti*) and acute inverted duties. Following the Prime Minister's August 15, 2025 announcement, the landmark **56th GST Council** (September 3, 2025) executed **Next-Gen GST 2.0**, implemented via CBIC notifications effective **September 22, 2025**:

- **Slab Collapse (`4 Core Slabs → 2 Core Slabs + 1 Special De-merit Slab`):** Abolished the contentious **12% and 28% slabs** for virtually all mainstream goods and services. The restructured grid comprises a **5% Merit Rate** (everyday essentials, packaged foods, butter/ghee/cheese, soaps, shampoos, toothpaste, bicycles, farm equipment, footwear ≤₹2,500), an **18% Standard Rate** (default bracket absorbing most ex-28% consumer durables like ACs, refrigerators, TVs >32 inches, cement, small cars, and motorcycles ≤350cc), and a special **40% De-merit/Sin & Luxury Rate** (aerated/sweetened beverages, luxury vehicles, yachts, private aircraft, casinos, and online gaming), alongside retained **0% Exempt** and **3% precious-metals** bands.
- **Household & Healthcare Relief:** Granted **full 0% GST exemption** to **individual life and health insurance policies** (including family floaters and senior-citizen covers, down from 18%), 36 life-saving/cancer/rare-disease drugs, pre-packaged paneer, UHT milk, and student stationery (pencils, notebooks).
- **Sunset of Compensation Cess & February 1, 2026 Sin-Goods Overhaul:** Because GST Compensation Cess collections were legally committed to repaying COVID-era back-to-back compensation loans, pan masala and tobacco remained temporarily on the old `28% + Cess` structure until loan discharge. Upon completing debt servicing, the Ministry of Finance terminated the **GST Compensation Cess effective February 1, 2026**, replacing it with a tough, multi-layered public-health regime:
  - Cigarettes, pan masala, gutkha, chewing tobacco/zarda, and manufactured tobacco shifted to **40% GST** (bidis placed at **18%**), with valuation switched from transaction value to mandatory **Retail Sale Price (RSP)** to curb under-invoicing.
  - Over and above GST, the ***Central Excise (Amendment) Act, 2025*** imposed steep specific and ad-valorem additional excise duties on tobacco (₹2,050–₹8,500 per 1,000 cigarette sticks depending on length/filter; 91% on gutkha; 82% on zarda).
  - Concurrently, the ***Health Security se National Security (HSNS) Cess Act, 2025*** (enacted under Article 271) introduced a **capacity-based cess** on pan masala manufacturers—levied on the number and rated speed of installed pouch-packing machines rather than self-reported sales—earmarking revenues for healthcare infrastructure and national security.
- **Trust-Based Trade & Compliance Reforms:** Deliberately declined to revive intrusive anti-profiteering bureaucracy; extended **90% risk-based provisional refunds** to Inverted Duty Structure claims (matching zero-rated exports); amended **Section 13(8) of the IGST Act** so the place of supply for **intermediary services** shifts from supplier's location to recipient's location (resolving long-running litigation that denied export zero-rating to Indian GCC/BPO intermediaries); and launched simplified registration for small/low-risk firms and small e-commerce sellers.

## Public Debt Sustainability, FRBM Evolution & the Post-FY26 Debt Anchor

### Sovereign Debt Composition & the Domar Stability Condition

Sovereign solvency turns not on the absolute rupee stock of debt, but on its trajectory relative to nominal GDP ($d_t = \frac{\text{Debt}_t}{\text{GDP}_t}$) and its currency/maturity composition. India's **Central Government debt** (55.6% of GDP in FY27 BE on the pre-revision base; ~57.5% on the revised `2022–23` base; General Government combined Centre + States debt ~81% of GDP) possesses three structural stabilizers: over **90% is internal and rupee-denominated** (shielding India from external sovereign rollover crises or "original sin"), external sovereign debt is overwhelmingly long-term and concessional (multilateral/bilateral), and weighted average maturity of dated G-Secs exceeds 12 years, muting acute refinancing spikes.

The mathematical engine of debt sustainability is the **Domar Identity**:

$$\Delta d_t = \left(\frac{r - g}{1 + g}\right) d_{t-1} + pb_t$$

Here, $r$ is the nominal effective interest rate on public debt, $g$ is the **nominal GDP growth rate** (real growth + GDP deflator inflation), and $pb_t$ is the **Primary Deficit-to-GDP ratio** (positive when spending excluding interest exceeds non-debt receipts). Two crisp laws fall out immediately:
1. **The Growth–Interest Differential ($r - g$):** Whenever **$g > r$**, the snowball coefficient $\frac{r - g}{1 + g}$ is **negative**—nominal economic growth outpaces debt-interest compounding, passively eroding Debt-to-GDP each year. India's structural advantage rests on maintaining **~10% nominal GDP growth** against a **~6.8%–7.2% weighted sovereign borrowing cost**, generating a ~300 bps favourable differential.
2. **The Vulnerability Trigger:** If nominal growth slumps (say due to disinflation or a real shock) so that $g \le r$, or if the **Primary Deficit ($pb_t$)** remains persistently large, debt spirals upward regardless of intent. Even while $g > r$ protects solvency, a high debt stock inflicts **fiscal preemption**: interest payments consuming ~40% of Union revenue receipts crowd out health, education, and R&D spending and raise sovereign risk premia.

### FRBM Act, 2003, N.K. Singh Escape Clauses & Off-Budget Cleanup

Enacted in the wake of late-1990s fiscal stress, the ***Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003*** sought statutory fiscal discipline and monetary–fiscal separation. Its original mandate aimed to eliminate the Revenue Deficit completely and cap Fiscal Deficit at 3% of GDP by FY 2008–09, barred RBI from primary G-Sec purchases (Section 5), mandated annual parliamentary statements (*Macroeconomic Framework, Medium-Term Fiscal Policy, Fiscal Policy Strategy*, later adding *Medium-Term Expenditure Framework*), and empowered CAG compliance reviews (2012 amendment).

After repeated target deferrals post-2008, the **N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee (2017)** redesigned the architecture, largely codified through the **Finance Act, 2018**:
- **Debt Ceiling:** Set a medium-term ceiling of **60% General Government Debt-to-GDP** (**40% Centre + 20% States**), paired with a 3% Fiscal Deficit operational target while quietly dropping the rigid statutory Revenue Deficit elimination rule.
- **Section 4(2) Escape Clause:** Permitted annual Fiscal Deficit deviation up to **0.5% of GDP**—and allowed RBI primary-market G-Sec subscription under exceptional conditions—strictly upon five codified triggers: **(i)** national security or act of war, **(ii)** national calamity, **(iii)** collapse of agriculture severely hitting output and farm incomes, **(iv)** far-reaching structural reforms with unanticipated fiscal implications (invoked in FY 2019–20 after the corporate tax cut), and **(v)** a sharp decline in real output growth of at least 3 percentage points below the four-quarter average.
- **Unfinished Institutional Reform:** The N.K. Singh Committee's recommendation to establish an independent statutory **Fiscal Council**—to audit macro projections, verify escape-clause invocations, and curb optimistic revenue forecasting—remains unimplemented.

A parallel integrity fault line was **Off-Budget Borrowings (Extra-Budgetary Resources — EBRs)**: directing state-owned agencies (FCI borrowing from NSSF to finance unpaid food subsidies, or NHAI/PFC/IRFC raising "fully serviced bonds" repaid out of future budgets) to fund sovereign outlays without recording them in the headline Fiscal Deficit. Between FY21 and FY23, the Union Government commendably pulled FCI's massive NSSF arrears back **on-budget**, sharply boosting transparency. However, sub-national off-budget SPV borrowing persists; consequently, both the **16th Finance Commission (tabled February 1, 2026)** and NITI Aayog's **Fiscal Health Index (FHI)** have pressed to ban off-budget vehicles and publish comprehensive, consolidated public-sector borrowing disclosures.

### The Post-FY26 Shift to a Debt-to-GDP Anchor (`50 ± 1%` by March 2031)

During the post-pandemic consolidation window (FY22–FY26), the Centre anchored credibility on a rigid annual flow target—driving the Fiscal Deficit from 9.2% (FY21) below **4.5% of GDP by FY26** (achieved at **4.4% RE** on the pre-revision base / **4.51%** post-revision). Beginning **FY 2026–27**, India transitioned to a next-generation fiscal rule: making **Central Government Debt-to-GDP** the primary operational anchor, targeting **50 ± 1% by March 31, 2031** (declining steadily from 56.1% RE FY26 to **55.6% BE FY27** on the pre-revision series, or **~58.0% → ~57.5%** on the revised Feb 27, 2026 GDP base).

Why does a **stock anchor (Debt/GDP)** outperform a rigid **annual flow cap (3% FD)** for an emerging economy?
- **Counter-Cyclical Flexibility:** Rigid annual FD ceilings force governments to slash Capex precisely during downturns (pro-cyclical austerity) to hit an arbitrary percentage. A medium-term Debt/GDP corridor lets the annual Fiscal Deficit flex modestly around cyclical shocks while forcing consolidation during booms, preserving the multi-year Capex pipeline.
- **Direct Link to Sovereign Risk & Yields:** Bond markets and credit rating agencies price sovereign risk off long-run debt trajectories and interest-to-revenue ratios rather than 10 bps single-year deficit swings. Anchoring Debt/GDP at 50 ± 1% signals steady erosion of interest preemption—freeing fiscal room for Viksit Bharat investments.
- **Federal Synergy:** The **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)** reinforced this debt-centric discipline by recommending that States strictly observe a **3% of GSDP fiscal deficit ceiling** (with zero off-budget loopholes) while projecting a complementary Union Fiscal Deficit path trending toward **3.5% of GDP by FY31**. Note, however, that the February 27, 2026 GDP base revision adds ~1.8 percentage points to the starting Central Debt-to-GDP ratio (~57.5% vs. 55.6%), demanding either faster nominal growth or tighter primary-deficit compression over FY28–FY31 to stay inside the 50 ± 1% band.

## Federal Fiscal Interlinkages - Devolution, Cesses & Combined Borrowing Space

Union fiscal policy cannot operate in macroeconomic isolation because States execute over **60% of combined general-government developmental and social-sector spending** and carry roughly **~25%–27% of GDP** in sub-national debt. When **General Government debt hovers near ~81% of GDP**, sovereign bond markets evaluate Centre and State borrowing jointly—especially since both draw from the same domestic pool of household savings and commercial bank SLR demand. The **16th Finance Commission (2026–31)**, tabled alongside Union Budget 2026–27 on February 1, 2026, retained vertical tax devolution at **41% of the divisible pool** while discontinuing traditional Revenue Deficit Grants and introducing a GDP-contribution weight—pivoting transfers toward fiscal prudence and performance.

Simultaneously, two live fiscal frictions bind Union budgets to State solvency: **(i)** the expanding share of **non-divisible cesses and surcharges** (such as AIDC, Health & Education Cess, and the new **HSNS Cess** under Article 271), which sit outside the 41% divisible pool and shrink effective State receipts, and **(ii)** short-run revenue compression for States following **Next-Gen GST 2.0's** sweeping rate cuts from 12%/28% to 5%/18% without a renewed compensation guarantee. For the complete constitutional architecture of Article 280, horizontal devolution weights, GST Council voting mechanics, and the Supreme Court's *Mohit Minerals* doctrine, cross-refer directly to [[14. Federalism II - Fiscal Federalism, GST & 16th Finance Commission#16th Finance Commission Formula and Horizontal Devolution|16th Finance Commission Formula]].

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Grants for Capital Assets vs. Loans to States:** A Union *grant* to a State to build a metro line is **Revenue Expenditure** (asset owned by the State; deducted only when computing *Effective Revenue Deficit*). A 50-year interest-free *loan* to a State for the exact same metro line is **Capital Expenditure** (creates a financial claim for the Union).
> - **Interest vs. Principal Repayment:** Paying interest on sovereign debt is **Revenue Expenditure**; repaying the principal on maturity is **Capital Expenditure** (extinguishes a liability).
> - **The "Capital Deficit" Mirage & Non-Debt Capital Receipts Distractor:** India's *Budget at a Glance* publishes RD, ERD, FD, and PD—**not** "Capital Deficit". Furthermore, if a Prelims numerical gives Fiscal Deficit, Non-Debt Capital Receipts, and Interest Payments, **do NOT subtract NDCR again** to find Primary Deficit ($\text{GPD} = \text{GFD} - \text{Interest}$ directly; NDCR was already netted inside GFD!).
> - **Tax Year Transition Trap:** Income earned in **FY 2025–26** (filed during calendar year 2026 as **AY 2026–27**) is still governed by the *Income-Tax Act, 1961*. The *Income-Tax Act, 2025* and its unified **"Tax Year"** govern income earned from **April 1, 2026 onwards** (Tax Year 2026–27).
> - **₹12 Lakh Tax-Free Rebate Exclusions:** The **Section 156** rebate (earlier Sec 87A) under the default New Regime applies strictly to **resident individuals** and strictly against **slab-rate normal income**—it cannot waive tax on special-rate **equity LTCG/STCG, VDAs, or NRI income**.
> - **MAT 14% Credit Conditionality:** Following Budget 2026–27, brought-forward MAT credit can be set off (up to 25% of tax liability) **only** if the company opts into the **New Corporate Tax Regime**, not if it stays in the Old Regime.
> - **Equalization Levy Status:** Neither the 2% e-commerce EL (repealed Aug 2024) nor the 6% online-ad EL (repealed Apr 2025) exists anymore. Any MCQ claiming India currently levies a 6% "Google Tax" is outdated.
> - **GST 2.0 & Sin-Goods Nuances:** Mainstream GST 2.0 slabs took effect on **September 22, 2025**, whereas the **40% GST on tobacco/pan masala**, the end of **GST Compensation Cess**, and the launch of the **HSNS Cess** (capacity-based on pan masala, *not* cigarettes) took effect on **February 1, 2026**—and **bidis sit at 18%**, not 40%.
> - **WMA Atomic Checks:** Ways and Means Advances are **short-term (≤90 days) liquidity bridges** at the **Repo Rate** (Overdraft at Repo + 2%), **not** long-term deficit financing, and apply to the Union plus all States **except Sikkim**.
> - **NMP 2.0 vs. Disinvestment:** Asset monetization under NMP 2.0 (₹16.72L Cr) leases brownfield cash-flow rights while **retaining sovereign ownership**; it is *not* PSU equity privatization.
> - **Domar Directionality:** If nominal GDP growth falls *below* the sovereign interest rate ($g < r$), Debt-to-GDP **rises** automatically even if the Primary Deficit is zero.
