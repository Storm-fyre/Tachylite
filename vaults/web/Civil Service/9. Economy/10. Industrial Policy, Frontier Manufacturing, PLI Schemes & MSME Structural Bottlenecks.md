# Industrial Policy, Frontier Manufacturing, PLI Schemes & MSME Structural Bottlenecks

No large developing economy has transitioned to high-income status without passing through a deep manufacturing expansion. Manufacturing uniquely combines **unconditional productivity convergence** (factory output scales rapidly toward global technological frontiers), **tradable export surpluses**, and **mass absorption of semi-skilled labour** migrating off low-productivity farms.

India bypassed this classical Lewis-model sequence, leaping directly from agrarian dominance into skill-intensive services while its manufacturing share stagnated between **14% and 17% of GDP** for three decades—far short of the **25% target** set by the National Manufacturing Policy (2011) and Make in India (2014). Between 2020 and 2026, Indian industrial policy underwent a decisive doctrinal mutation: moving from horizontal deregulation toward active **Production-Linked Incentives (PLI)**, and finally—via **Union Budget 2026–27**—pivoting upstream into **seven strategic frontier ecosystems, component depth, legacy cluster revival, and "Champion SMEs"**.

## India's Manufacturing Stagnation, Make in India & the 14-Sector PLI Architecture

### Anatomy of Stagnation & the 2026 Statistical Nuance

Under MoSPI's revised **FY 2022–23 GDP series (February 2026)**, manufacturing value added compounded at a brisk **10.88% CAGR between FY 2022–23 and FY 2025–26** (PIB, November 2026), yet its aggregate share of GDP still hovers near **16–17%**. Four structural distortions locked India out of East Asia's labour-intensive factory boom:

- **Factor-Market Rigidity & Premature Capital Intensity:** Legacy threshold-based labour laws (requiring state permission to retrench at ≥100 workers until the November 2025 enforcement of the Four Labour Codes) and costly land assembly nudged large Indian corporations toward capital-intensive sectors (refining, autos, pharma, petrochemicals) while keeping labour-intensive apparel, footwear, and toys trapped in tiny informal workshops.
- **Inverted Duty Structures & Input Protectionism:** Rising tariffs on intermediate components (steel, chemicals, man-made fibres, electronics sub-assemblies) combined with mandatory **Quality Control Orders (QCOs)** on basic industrial inputs inflated domestic factory costs—forcing a September 2026 policy course-correction via the *Transition Facilitation Order* to ease MSME input bottlenecks.
- **Scale & Logistics Drag:** High industrial power cross-subsidies, fragmented sub-scale factories, and road-skewed freight logistics raised landed export costs relative to Vietnam, Bangladesh, and China.
- **Make in India (2014 → 2.0):** Initiated across 25 (later expanded to 27: 15 manufacturing and 12 services) sectors, Make in India successfully liberalised FDI caps, launched the **National Single Window System (NSWS)**, built the GIS-enabled **India Industrial Land Bank (IILB)**, and deployed **Phased Manufacturing Programmes (PMP)** and public procurement preferences. However, horizontal ease-of-doing-business alone could not overcome the **~8–12% structural "cost disability"** facing greenfield factories competing against subsidised East Asian clusters—triggering the 2020 turn toward targeted **PLI schemes**.

### The 14-Sector PLI Framework — Mechanism, Mid-Course Outcomes & Structural Critiques

Launched in March 2020 and scaled across **14 key sectors**, the PLI architecture is coordinated centrally by the **Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT)** while executed by respective Administrative Ministries. Aspirants should maintain **dual-standard outlay parity**: initially announced at **₹1.97 lakh crore**, the approved cumulative financial outlay is cited as **₹1.91 lakh crore** across 2026 Parliament replies and NITI Aayog evaluations.

- **Economic Design:** Unlike legacy capital subsidies that rewarded upfront spending regardless of factory viability, PLI pays a **post-facto output incentive (typically 4–6% on incremental net sales over a designated base year for 5 years)** strictly conditional on achieving verified minimum investment, sales growth, and progressive **Domestic Value Addition (DVA)** benchmarks. Crucially, because incentives are linked to *production/sales* rather than *export performance*, the design seeks to stay compliant with **WTO Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (ASCM)** disciplines—though strict DVA localization mandates still invite multilateral scrutiny.
- **The 14 Covered Sectors:** (1) Large-Scale Electronics Manufacturing (**LSEM**), (2) IT Hardware (2.0), (3) Pharmaceuticals, (4) Bulk Drugs (KSMs/APIs), (5) Medical Devices, (6) Automobiles & Auto Components, (7) Advanced Chemistry Cell (**ACC**) Batteries, (8) High-Efficiency Solar PV Modules, (9) Telecom & Networking Products, (10) Food Processing, (11) Textiles (MMF apparel/fabrics & technical textiles), (12) Specialty Steel, (13) White Goods (ACs & LED lights), and (14) Drones & Drone Components.
- **Verified Mid-Course Performance (PIB July/November 2026 & NITI Aayog–IIM Ahmedabad *Impact Assessment of PLI Schemes*, November 2026):**
  - **Headline Traction:** Across 836+ approved applications, cumulative realised investment hit **₹2.40 lakh crore by March 2026** (76% of the ₹3.15 lakh crore full-tenure target; crossing ₹2.60 lakh crore by June 2026), driving **₹22.66 lakh crore in cumulative production/sales** (64% of ₹35.37 lakh crore target; ₹23.8 lakh crore by June 2026) and **₹15.2+ lakh crore in cumulative exports**.
  - **Standout Verticals:** Domestic mobile-phone production surged **28-fold** (from ₹18,000 crore in FY15 to ₹5.45 lakh crore in FY25), slashing mobile imports by **77%** so that **99.2%** of handsets sold in India are domestically made. Pharmaceuticals clocked ₹3.64 lakh crore in sales, indigenising **191 bulk drugs for the first time** (including Penicillin G); Telecom achieved **60% import substitution**; and Medical Devices initiated domestic output of 54 high-end systems (MRI, CT-Scan, LINAC).
- **Four Structural Critiques (NITI Aayog–IIMA Report & Economists):**
  1. **Severe Employment Shortfall:** The November 2026 NITI Aayog–IIMA evaluation recorded **8.49 lakh direct jobs** as of March 2026—just **28% of the 30.19 lakh full-tenure target** (though PIB's broader direct-plus-indirect tally stands at **14.15–14.6 lakh**)—confirming that PLI predominantly incentivised capital-intensive automation over mass job creation.
  2. **Sluggish Incentive Disbursement:** Only **₹35,354 crore (18.51% of the ₹1.91 lakh crore outlay)** had been disbursed by March 2026 despite 58% of the implementation window elapsing, reflecting slow verification cycles and delayed commercial commissioning in capital-heavy verticals.
  3. **Shallow Domestic Value Addition (The "Screwdriver Assembly" Risk):** Critics (including Raghuram Rajan) observe that early LSEM gains were heavily concentrated in final SKD/CKD smartphone assembly (~15–20% DVA), accompanied by a surge in Chinese/East Asian imports of PCBs, display modules, camera sensors, and semiconductors.
  4. **Uneven Sectoral Uptake & MSME Exclusion:** High minimum investment/turnover eligibility bars favoured large conglomerates and MNCs while excluding mid-sized firms. Lagging verticals—especially **ACC Batteries, Specialty Steel, IT Hardware, and Textiles**—forced corrective redesigns (e.g., in **October 2026**, the Ministry of Textiles halved the Part-2 turnover threshold for Textiles PLI Round 4 from ₹200 crore to ₹100 crore to onboard MSMEs).

## Budget 2026–27 Frontier Manufacturing Pivot, Legacy Clusters & Spatial Enclaves

### From Assembly Subsidies to Upstream Ecosystem Sovereignty

Union Budget 2026–27 marks an explicit second-generation evolution in Indian industrial strategy—dubbed *Disciplined Swadeshi* by the Economic Survey 2025–26. Rather than proliferating new final-assembly PLIs, the **"First Kartavya"** channels public capital into **seven strategic frontier sectors** targeting upstream materials, capital goods, R&D IP, and deep component value chains, alongside revitalising 200 brownfield legacy clusters and building Champion SMEs.

### Strategic Mineral Value Chains & Upstream Industrial Security

Modern frontier manufacturing—EV traction motors, grid-scale storage, semiconductor wafers, and precision aerospace alloys—rests on highly concentrated upstream refining chains where China controls 60–90% of global processing capacity. India's response combines the **National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM, approved January 2025)**—a seven-year, **₹16,300 crore** sovereign blueprint (leveraging ₹18,000 crore from PSUs across 30 notified critical minerals)—with the **₹7,280 crore Sintered Rare Earth Permanent Magnet (REPM) Scheme (November 26, 2025)** (offering ₹6,450 crore in sales-linked incentives plus ₹750 crore in capital subsidies to build 6,000 MTPA of integrated oxide-to-metal-to-magnet capacity, attracting 20 competitive bids by August 2026) and Budget 2026–27's **four-state Rare Earth Corridors**.

Industrial policy here operates purely on value-chain economics: raw mineral extraction without domestic beneficiation and magnet sintering simply exports unprocessed concentrates only to re-import finished magnets at tenfold markups. For the underlying mineral geology, offshore/monazite beach-sand belts, and spatial reserve distribution across Odisha, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu, see [[16. Mineral & Energy Cartography and Critical Minerals#Strategic Critical Minerals and Rare Earth Cartography|Critical Minerals & Rare Earth Cartography]].

### Spatial Industrial Architecture — NICDC Corridors, BHAVYA, PM MITRA & SEZ Reform

Greenfield factories struggle most with land assembly, environmental clearances, and last-mile utility delays. India is scaling plug-and-play industrial real estate through four interlocking spatial instruments:

- **NICDP & 12 New Industrial Smart Cities (NICDC):** Administered by the **National Industrial Corridor Development Corporation (NICDC)** across **11 national corridors** anchored by the flagship Japan-partnered Delhi–Mumbai Industrial Corridor (DMIC; operational nodes include Dholera SIR, Shendra–Bidkin, Vikram Udyogpuri, and Greater Noida). In **August 2024**, the Cabinet approved **12 new greenfield industrial smart cities** at an estimated cost of **₹28,602 crore** across 10 states (including Khurpia, Rajpura–Patiala, Dighi, Palakkad, Agra, Prayagraj, Gaya, Zaheerabad, Orvakal, Kopparthy, Jodhpur–Pali, and Tumakuru) aligned with PM Gati Shakti multimodal arteries.
- **BHAVYA (*Bharat Audyogik Vikas Yojana*, 2026) & PM MITRA Parks:** Approved in March 2026 and notified in April 2026, **BHAVYA** commits **₹33,660 crore over six years (FY 2026–32)** to develop **100 plug-and-play industrial parks** via Centre–State–private SPVs (up to ₹1 crore/acre assistance, with up to 25% usable for external connectivity). In textiles, **7 approved PM MITRA Parks** (₹4,445 crore outlay up to FY28 across Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Gujarat, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, and Maharashtra) integrate the entire **"5F" value chain** (*Farm → Fibre → Factory → Fashion → Foreign*) inside unified 1,000+ acre sites, now reinforced by Budget 2026–27's challenge-mode Mega Textile Parks for technical textiles.
- **Special Economic Zones (SEZ Act, 2005) & the Baba Kalyani Reform Arc:** An SEZ is a specifically delineated duty-free enclave **deemed foreign territory for customs and trade operations**, overseen by a Development Commissioner and legally required to achieve **positive Net Foreign Exchange (NFE)** over five years. As of February 2026, India has **368 notified SEZs** (recording >₹11.70 lakh crore in exports during Apr–Dec FY26, heavily IT/ITES-skewed). Yet manufacturing SEZs suffered severe hollowing out after the imposition of **Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT)**, the **2020 Sunset Clause** terminating Section 10AA income-tax holidays, WTO ASCM challenges against export-linked subsidies, and an **inverted Domestic Tariff Area (DTA) penalty**: goods sold by an Indian SEZ factory into India's domestic market attract full *finished-good* customs duty, rendering Indian SEZ output costlier at home than duty-free imports from ASEAN FTA partners.
- **Baba Kalyani Committee (2018), DESH Stalemate & Budget 2026–27 One-Time DTA Relief:** The **Baba Kalyani Committee (2018)** urged reinventing SEZs from isolated export silos into **Employment and Economic Enclaves (3Es)** evaluated on jobs and value addition rather than NFE, and permitting DTA sales by paying customs duty strictly on *inputs foregone* rather than final products. While this blueprint shaped the draft **DESH (Development of Enterprise and Service Hubs) Bill, 2022**, legislative enactment remains stalled over Finance Ministry concerns regarding domestic tariff arbitrage. Responding pragmatically to global tariff turbulence, **Union Budget 2026–27** (implemented via **CBIC Notification No. 11/2026-Customs**, effective **April 1, 2026 to March 31, 2027**) introduced a **special one-time concessional-duty DTA sales window** for manufacturing SEZ units operational on or before March 31, 2025—capped at **30% of peak annual export FOB turnover** in the preceding three years and contingent on a **minimum 20% domestic value addition**, alongside relaxed land minimums for semiconductor and electronics SEZs (approved in June 2025 for hubs like Sanand and Dharwad).

## MSMEs — Revised Thresholds, "Dwarfism" & the Champion SMEs Architecture

### Macroeconomic Footprint & the 2025–26 Composite Classification Revision

India's Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises constitute the decentralized shock-absorber of the economy—accounting for **31.1% of GDP**, **35.4% of manufacturing output**, **48.58% of total exports**, and employing **32.8–38.9 crore people** across **8.7+ crore registered units** (PIB June 2026 & Economic Survey 2025–26), second only to agriculture.

Historically, the **MSMED Act, 2006** classified units solely by plant-and-machinery investment while separating manufacturing from services—creating an arbitrary ceiling that discouraged capital deepening. In July 2020, India abolished the manufacturing–services split and adopted a unified **composite criterion** (both Investment AND Annual Turnover, with **export turnover fully excluded** from the turnover cap). To eliminate "growth-cliff anxiety" and adjust for inflation, **Union Budget 2025–26** (notified via **S.O. 1364(E)**, effective **April 1, 2025**) raised **investment limits by 2.5×** and **turnover limits by 2×**:

- **Micro Enterprise:** Investment in plant & machinery/equipment ≤ **₹2.5 crore** *(earlier ₹1 cr)* **AND** Annual Turnover ≤ **₹10 crore** *(earlier ₹5 cr)*.
- **Small Enterprise:** Investment ≤ **₹25 crore** *(earlier ₹10 cr)* **AND** Annual Turnover ≤ **₹100 crore** *(earlier ₹50 cr)*.
- **Medium Enterprise:** Investment ≤ **₹125 crore** *(earlier ₹50 cr)* **AND** Annual Turnover ≤ **₹500 crore** *(earlier ₹250 cr)*.

*Atomic Classification Mechanics:* Both conditions must hold simultaneously—if an enterprise breaches **either** the investment ceiling or the turnover ceiling, it automatically graduates upward to the next tier. Conversely, downward reclassification occurs only when the enterprise falls below *both* limits. Registration occurs digitally, free of cost, with zero renewal on the PAN/GSTN-integrated **Udyam Portal**; meanwhile, the **Udyam Assist Platform (UAP)**—developed by SIDBI and MoMSME—formalises GST-exempt **Informal Micro Enterprises (IMEs)** (2.92+ crore onboarded) so they qualify for **Priority Sector Lending (PSL)** benefits.

### The Pathology of "Dwarfism" & the "Missing Middle"

Indian manufacturing displays a pathological **bimodal firm-size distribution** ("missing middle"): over 98% of firms are tiny micro-enterprises (mostly sub-10-worker informal units), a sliver are giant corporate groups, and almost no mid-sized factories (50–500 workers) populate the centre. The **Economic Survey 2018–19** famously separated **Infants** (young small firms with high growth velocity) from **Dwarfs** (enterprises older than 10 years still employing <100 workers)—showing that dwarfs represent over half of all manufacturing establishments yet generate under 15% of employment and under 8% of productivity.

Dwarfism is not cultural; it is an **incentive-induced equilibrium** driven by three binding bottlenecks:

1. **Perverse Size-Based Policy Cliffs:** Blanket age-blind tax exemptions, concessional credit, and legacy labour thresholds historically punished firms for crossing small-scale caps, incentivising horizontal splitting into multiple benami micro-units rather than vertical scaling.
2. **Collateral-Choked Credit Gap (₹20–25 Lakh Crore):** The **RBI Expert Committee on MSMEs chaired by U.K. Sinha (June 2019)** estimated an addressable formal credit gap of **₹20–25 lakh crore**. Banks demanded hard immovable collateral because audited MSME balance sheets lag operations by 12–18 months, starving asset-light growth firms of working capital and risk equity.
3. **Working-Capital Asymmetry (Delayed Payments):** Large private corporations and PSUs routinely exploited monopsony bargaining power to stretch MSME supplier credit cycles to 90–180 days—locking an estimated **₹10.7 lakh crore (~6% of GVA)** of small-firm cash flow in unpaid receivables.

### Institutional & Legislative Arsenal (U.K. Sinha, RAMP, Samadhaan, Sec 43B(h) & Budget 2026–27)

India's post-2019 MSME policy has systematically dismantled these three traps through credit guarantees, cash-flow underwriting, statutory payment penalties, and growth-equity architecture:

- **U.K. Sinha Committee Blueprint (2019) & RAMP (2022–FY27):** The Sinha panel catalysed the shift from collateral-based lending to **GSTN/Account-Aggregator cash-flow underwriting**, proposed a **₹10,000 crore Government-anchored Fund of Funds** (realised as the **SRI Fund**), and urged strengthening TReDS and distressed-asset revival. Institutionally, the **Raising and Accelerating MSME Performance (RAMP)** programme—a **World Bank-assisted Central Sector Scheme** with a **₹6,062.45 crore ($808 million)** outlay (₹3,750 crore World Bank loan + ₹2,312.45 crore GoI, FY 2022–27)—links disbursements to **Disbursement Linked Indicators (DLIs)** via State **Strategic Investment Plans (SIPs)**, strengthening Centre–State MSME governance and funding two green sub-schemes: **MSE-GIFT** (interest subvention for green technology) and **MSE-SPICE** (credit-linked capital subsidy for circular-economy projects).
- **Credit Guarantees & Cash-Flow Credit (CGTMSE & SRI Fund):** Jointly established by GoI and SIDBI in 2000, **CGTMSE** provides collateral-free loan guarantees (carrying zero bank risk weight on the guaranteed tranche). Union Budget 2025–26 doubled the CGTMSE guarantee ceiling for Micro & Small Enterprises from **₹5 crore to ₹10 crore** (unlocking ₹1.5 lakh crore additional credit over five years), doubled startup cover to **₹20 crore**, introduced ₹20 crore term-loan cover for exporter MSMEs, and launched **₹5 lakh customised Udyam Micro Credit Cards**. For equity risk capital, the **Self-Reliant India (SRI) Fund** (2021, ₹50,000 crore total Fund-of-Funds structure combining ₹10,000 crore GoI anchor capital with ₹40,000 crore private daughter-fund leverage) received an **additional ₹2,000 crore top-up in Budget 2026–27** specifically for micro-enterprises.
- **Payment Enforcement — MSMED Act, Samadhaan, AI-ODR & Section 43B(h) (Now Section 37, Income-Tax Act 2025):** Under **Section 15 of the MSMED Act, 2006**, buyers must settle dues to registered Micro and Small suppliers within **15 days** (without a written agreement) or a hard cap of **45 days** (with a written agreement); delays attract **Section 16 compound interest at 3× the RBI Bank Rate** (compounded monthly and strictly non-deductible under Section 23). While **MSME Samadhaan** routes unpaid claims to **161 Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Councils (MSEFCs)**—receiving 2.56 lakh applications worth ₹55,244 crore by June 2026—slow ~23% disposal rates prompted the launch of an **AI-enabled Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) Portal (June 2025)**. The sharpest enforcement lever arrived via **Section 43B(h) of the Income-Tax Act, 1961** (inserted by Finance Act 2023; carried forward unchanged as **Section 37 of the Income-Tax Act, 2025** effective April 1, 2026): any amount owed to a registered **Micro or Small** manufacturer or service provider remaining unpaid beyond the 15/45-day window at year-end (March 31) is **disallowed as a taxable business expense** for that year (with *zero* grace period up to the ITR filing date) and becomes deductible only in the year of actual cash payment. *Policy Nuance:* While forcing rapid corporate payment discipline, Section 43B(h) triggered an unintended side-effect—some risk-averse buyers shifted orders from Udyam-registered MSEs toward unregistered vendors or Medium enterprises outside its ambit.
- **Budget 2026–27 "Champion SMEs" Three-Pronged Package:** Designed expressly to graduate promising small firms into globally competitive mid-sized anchors:
  1. **Equity Support:** Launches a dedicated **₹10,000 crore SME Growth Fund** deploying performance-based risk equity into scalable future Champions, paired with the **₹2,000 crore SRI Fund** micro-enterprise top-up.
  2. **Liquidity Support (Four-Point TReDS Architecture):** **TReDS** (Trade Receivables Discounting System)—an RBI-regulated digital marketplace where MSMEs auction buyer-accepted invoices to banks/NBFCs on a *without-recourse* basis—has already unlocked >₹7 lakh crore (expanded in 2024 by halving mandatory buyer onboarding turnover from ₹500 crore to **₹250 crore**, and streamlined by the **RBI TReDS Directions, 2026**). Budget 2026–27 transforms TReDS through four structural linkages: (i) **mandating TReDS as the settlement platform for all CPSE purchases from MSMEs**; (ii) extending **CGTMSE credit guarantees for invoice discounting on TReDS**; (iii) integrating **GeM (Government e-Marketplace) with TReDS** so financiers underwrite verified government purchase orders instantly; and (iv) **securitising TReDS receivables as Asset-Backed Securities (ABS)** to unlock secondary-market institutional liquidity.
  3. **Professional Compliance Support ('Corporate Mitras'):** Empowers **ICAI, ICSI, and ICMAI** to roll out modular short-term certifications building an accredited cadre of affordable para-professionals (**Corporate Mitras**) assisting MSMEs across Tier-II and Tier-III towns with GST, statutory audits, and corporate governance.

## Circular Economy, E-Waste & Urban Mining as Industrial Policy

Traditional manufacturing runs on a **linear "take–make–dispose" throughput model** that amplifies raw-material import bills, hazardous landfill burdens, and carbon intensity. A **Circular Economy** re-engineers industrial value chains around **remanufacturing, repairability, Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), and secondary mineral recovery**—turning post-consumer waste into high-grade domestic industrial feedstock while insulating Indian exporters from carbon border regimes like the EU CBAM.

- **E-Waste & Battery Waste Regulatory Architecture:** India's **E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022** (effective April 1, 2023) overhauled legacy 2016 norms by expanding coverage to **106 categories of electrical and electronic equipment**—notably bringing **solar photovoltaic modules, panels, and cells** inside the regulatory net—and establishing a **digitised, tradable EPR certificate market** administered by CPCB. Mandatory recycling targets scale progressively to **70% during FY 2025–26 and FY 2026–27**, reaching **80% from FY 2027–28**. Crucially, batteries are governed under a separate parallel statute—the **Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022**—imposing distinct collection, recycling, and minimum recovered-material reuse quotas across EV, portable, automotive, and industrial batteries.
- **The Informal Recycling Paradox:** India generates roughly **1.75 million metric tonnes (MMT) of e-waste** and **~60 kilotonnes of spent lithium-ion batteries (LIBs)** annually, yet formal collection rates linger near **~10% for e-waste and <5% for batteries**. Over three-quarters of discarded electronics leak to informal backyard scrapyards that employ crude acid baths and open burning—recovering barely a fraction of gold/copper while permanently losing cobalt, lithium, and rare-earth elements and poisoning urban groundwater.
- **Urban Mining & the 2025–26 Critical Mineral Recycling Pivot:** End-of-life electronics and EVs represent far richer **"urban ore bodies"** than geological mines: printed circuit boards contain **18–25% copper** alongside gold and palladium; spent LIBs hold **5–20% cobalt and 5–7% lithium**; discarded NdFeB magnets contain **25–30% neodymium**; and catalytic converters yield platinum-group metals. Because greenfield primary mines take 10–15 years to commercialise, **urban mining** offers India's fastest domestic hedge against critical mineral import dependency:
  - **₹1,500 Crore Critical Mineral Recycling Incentive Scheme (Approved September 3, 2025; Guidelines October 2, 2025; FY 2025–26 to FY 2030–31):** A flagship pillar of the **National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM)** targeting **270 ktpa of recycling capacity** and **40 ktpa of critical mineral output** from e-waste, spent LIBs, and end-of-life catalytic converters. Incentives combine a **20% Capex subsidy** with incremental-sales **Opex subsidies** (with **one-third of the outlay reserved for small/new recyclers and startups**). *Key Design Discipline:* Subsidies reward **actual chemical/hydrometallurgical extraction of refined critical minerals**—explicitly **excluding units engaged solely in shredding batteries into intermediate "black mass"**.
  - **Black Mass Export Prohibition (DGFT, November 2026):** Complementing Budget 2025–26 customs duty exemptions on imported LIB scrap, the **DGFT prohibited exports of Lithium-Ion Battery Black Mass in November 2026**—halting the leakage of India's shredded battery powder to foreign refineries and locking secondary feedstock inside domestic urban-mining plants.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Revised 2025 MSME Composite Thresholds (Effective April 1, 2025):** Do NOT mark legacy 2020 limits (`₹1/10/50 cr` investment; `₹5/50/250 cr` turnover) or pre-2020 manufacturing-vs-services splits! Current limits are **2.5× investment & 2× turnover**: **Micro** (`≤₹2.5 cr` & `≤₹10 cr`), **Small** (`≤₹25 cr` & `≤₹100 cr`), **Medium** (`≤₹125 cr` & `≤₹500 cr`). **Export turnover is excluded** from turnover calculation; crossing *either* ceiling upgrades the enterprise.
> - **MSME Priority Sector Lending Trap (UPSC Prelims 2023):** **ALL bank loans to MSMEs**—manufacturing and services alike—qualify under RBI's **Priority Sector Lending (PSL)** guidelines (with a specific 7.5% ANBC sub-target for Micro enterprises). Informal micro units registered on the **Udyam Assist Platform (UAP)** also qualify for PSL.
> - **Section 43B(h) / Section 37 (Income-Tax Act 2025) Scope Trap:** The 15-day (no contract) / 45-day (written contract) tax-disallowance hammer applies **ONLY to registered Micro and Small manufacturers/service providers**—it explicitly **excludes Medium enterprises** AND **excludes wholesale/retail traders** (even if Udyam-registered for PSL). Unlike standard Section 43B dues, paying before the ITR filing due date does **NOT** save the deduction for the previous fiscal year.
> - **PLI Governance & Dual-Standard Outlay:** **DPIIT** is the *overall coordinating/monitoring nodal department*, while *individual Administrative Ministries* implement sector-specific PLI schemes. Outlay figures appear as **₹1.97 lakh crore** (original announcement) vs. **₹1.91 lakh crore** (2026 Parliament/NITI Aayog reporting). Both **domestic and foreign companies** operating in India are eligible (tested in UPSC Prelims 2023).
> - **SEZ Legal Fiction & 2026 DTA Relief Conditions:** Under the SEZ Act, 2005, goods moving from an SEZ into India's **Domestic Tariff Area (DTA)** are legally treated as **imports** (attracting customs duty), while DTA-to-SEZ supplies are treated as **exports**. Budget 2026–27's one-time concessional DTA relief (Apr 2026–Mar 2027) applies strictly to **manufacturing SEZ units** operational on/before March 31, 2025, requiring **≥20% value addition** and capped at **30% of peak annual export FOB** over the prior 3 years.
> - **Critical Mineral Recycling Scheme vs. Black Mass Trap:** The **₹1,500 crore Critical Mineral Recycling Incentive Scheme (2025)** under NCMM incentivises **ONLY actual extraction/refining of critical minerals**—units performing solely mechanical shredding into **"black mass"** are **ineligible**, and in **November 2026 DGFT prohibited black-mass exports**.
> - **E-Waste vs. Battery Waste Statutory Split:** Solar PV modules/panels fall under the **E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022** (106 categories), whereas EV, portable, and industrial batteries are governed separately under the **Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022**.
> - **TReDS Recourse & Buyer Threshold:** Invoice discounting on **TReDS** operates strictly on a **without-recourse basis** against the MSME seller (default risk shifts to the corporate/CPSE buyer). Mandatory buyer onboarding turnover threshold was halved from ₹500 crore to **₹250 crore (2024)**, and **Budget 2026–27 mandated TReDS settlement for all CPSE purchases from MSMEs**.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Threshold & Exclusion Inversions:** Testing obsolete 2006 or 2020 MSME limits against the **April 2025 2.5×/2× composite thresholds**, omitting **export turnover exclusion**, or claiming **Medium enterprises and wholesale/retail traders** fall under the Section 43B(h)/Section 37 45-day tax-disallowance clause.
- **Eligibility & Incentive Scope Swaps:** Framing **PLI** as an *input/capital-only grant* restricted to *domestic firms*, or claiming **black-mass shredders** qualify for the **₹1,500 crore Critical Mineral Recycling Incentive Scheme**.
- **SEZ Trade-Direction Inversion:** Confusing SEZ-to-DTA sales (legally **imports**, attracting customs duties unless exempted) with exports, or overlooking the **≥20% value-addition** and **30% peak-export cap** governing Budget 2026–27's one-time DTA relief window.
- **Institutional Attribution Traps:** Attributing **TReDS** regulation to SEBI/SIDBI instead of **RBI**, swapping **E-Waste Rules 2022** (which cover solar PV panels) with **Battery Waste Management Rules 2022**, or treating **RAMP** as a Centrally Sponsored Scheme rather than a **World Bank-assisted Central Sector Scheme**.
