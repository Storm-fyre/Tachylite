# Chapter 6 - Inflation Dynamics, 2026 MoSPI Statistical Revisions & Household Financial Savings

Inflation is not a one-off jump in the price of onions; it is a **sustained, generalized erosion in the purchasing power of money** across an economy. Between February and July 2026, India executed the most comprehensive statistical recalibration in its post-independence history—resetting **CPI (`2024 = 100`)**, **IIP (`2022–23 = 100`)**, **WPI (`2022–23 = 100`)**, launching India's first **Producer Price Indices (PPIs)**, and expanding the **Core Industries Index from eight to nine sectors**. Simultaneously, post-pandemic household balance sheets have undergone a structural shift—away from net financial surpluses toward real estate, gold, and high-velocity retail leverage—reshaping how inflation transmits into interest rates, domestic savings, and financial stability.

## Inflation Mechanics, Taxonomies & Subcontinental Supply Drivers

### Causal Engines - Demand-Pull, Cost-Push & Wage–Price Spirals

Every inflation episode originates in an imbalance between nominal purchasing power chasing output and the economy's physical capacity to supply it, classified across three canonical mechanisms:

- **Demand-Pull Inflation ("Too Much Money Chasing Too Few Goods"):** Triggered when **Aggregate Demand ($C + I + G + NX$)** outstrips full-employment potential output (**positive output gap**). Classic drivers include expansionary monetary policy (low policy rates, excess banking liquidity), pro-cyclical fiscal stimulus (large debt- or money-financed deficits), rising household disposable incomes, and export booms. Because demand-pull inflation stems from excess spending, **central bank rate hikes work effectively** by cooling credit and investment demand.
- **Cost-Push Inflation (Adverse Supply Shock):** Triggered when **Aggregate Supply shifts leftward** due to rising production costs—spiking crude oil, imported fertilizer or edible oil prices, monsoon-damaged harvests, indirect tax hikes, or logistics disruptions—even while domestic demand remains muted. Crucially, hiking policy rates cannot irrigate a drought-hit crop or extract domestic crude; aggressive monetary tightening during a pure supply shock risks crushing growth faster than it tames prices.
- **Wage–Price Spiral (Second-Round Persistence):** The dangerous bridge turning a temporary supply shock into entrenched inflation. When persistent food or fuel inflation unanchors **household inflation expectations**, workers bargain for higher nominal wages (or receive automatic **Dearness Allowance** indexation) to defend real incomes; firms pass rising wage bills into retail prices, validating expectations and triggering another round of wage demands. Central banks tighten rates during supply shocks specifically to **break this second-round spillover**, not to grow tomatoes.

### Headline vs. Core Inflation & the Analytical Vocabulary of Price Movements

Policymakers split inflation into two operational layers to separate short-term noise from durable demand pressure:
- **Headline Inflation:** Captures total price changes across the *entire* consumption basket—including volatile food and fuel items. Because households actually eat food and buy petrol, headline CPI governs real cost-of-living erosion, wage expectations, and India's statutory **Flexible Inflation Targeting (4% ± 2% CPI-Combined)** mandate.
- **Core Inflation (Underlying Inflation):** Strips out the volatile **Food & Beverages** and **Fuel & Light** components to measure persistent, demand-sensitive pricing power across services and manufactured goods. *(India-specific nuance: because gold and silver ornaments sit inside the Personal Care & Effects sub-group, global bullion rallies can artificially lift Indian "core" CPI; RBI therefore frequently tracks **"Core ex-Gold/Silver"** and **"Core ex-Petrol/Diesel"** to read true domestic demand.)*

Precision in Prelims requires razor-sharp boundaries across seven directional and structural price states:

1. **Disinflation:** A **slowdown in the rate of inflation** while prices are still rising—e.g., inflation cooling from `6.0%` to `3.0%` (the general price level moves up, just more slowly). India witnessed sharp disinflation across 2025–26 as food shocks unwound.
2. **Deflation:** A **sustained fall in the general price level** (negative inflation rate, e.g., `-1.5%`). Far from being benign, deflation triggers a destructive **debt-deflation spiral**: households defer consumption waiting for lower prices, corporate revenues shrink while nominal debt stays fixed, real interest rates spike, and bankruptcies multiply.
3. **Reflation:** Deliberate monetary or fiscal stimulus (rate cuts, tax cuts, public spending) deployed to pull an economy out of deflation or deep recession back toward its target inflation and full-employment output trend.
4. **Stagflation:** Simultaneous **stagnant growth + high unemployment + high inflation**—classically induced by severe adverse supply shocks (like the 1973/1979 oil embargoes) that paralyze monetary policy because cutting rates worsens inflation while hiking rates deepens recession.
5. **Skewflation:** Asymmetric, localized price spikes concentrated in a narrow basket of essentials (for instance, tomatoes, onions, and pulses surging 40% while core manufacturing and services remain flat at 3%). India's inflation profile frequently exhibits skewflation rather than broad-based overheating.
6. **Shrinkflation (Package Downsizing):** Disguised inflation where FMCG manufacturers retain an iconic retail price point (e.g., a ₹5 or ₹10 biscuit or soap pack) while quietly **shrinking net weight or volume**, raising the effective per-gram price without altering the sticker price. Statistical agencies counter this via **unit-price / quality adjustments**.
7. **Built-In / Structural & Menu-Cost Friction:** Beyond demand and supply, emerging economies suffer **structural inflation** from infrastructural bottlenecks (segmented mandis, poor cold chains) and incur real resource losses (**menu costs** of repricing and **shoe-leather costs** of minimizing cash holdings).

### The Phillips Curve, NAIRU & Expectations

A.W. Phillips (1958) documented an empirical inverse trade-off between **unemployment** and **inflation**: in the **short run**, pushing unemployment below equilibrium tightens labour markets and accelerates wage-price inflation. However, **Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps** demolished the illusion of a permanent exploitable trade-off via the **Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve**:

$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta (u_t - u^*) + \epsilon_t$$

Here, $\pi_t^e$ represents expected inflation, $u^*$ is the **NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment)**—the structural natural unemployment floor consistent with stable inflation—and $\epsilon_t$ captures supply shocks. If policymakers try to hold unemployment permanently below $u^*$ via easy money, inflation initially rises, workers revise $\pi_t^e$ upward, and the short-run Phillips curve shifts vertically upward until unemployment snaps back to $u^*$ at a permanently higher inflation rate. Consequently, the **long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the NAIRU**. In India, massive informal-sector labour slack and recurring agricultural supply shocks ($\epsilon_t$) render the Phillips curve relatively flat and unstable—meaning output gaps explain far less headline variance than weather and energy shocks.

### Subcontinental Supply Drivers & the Base Effect

Six recurrent structural drivers dominate India's inflation cycle:
- **Global Crude Oil & Energy Pass-Through:** Importing ~85–88% of its crude requirement, India absorbs global energy spikes across diesel freight, aviation fuel, petrochemicals, plastics, and fertilizer subsidies. While administrative retail fuel freezes periodically mute direct CPI energy prints, **WPI Fuel & Power** (which surged past 24%–30% YoY during the 2026 West Asian crude spike) reflects pass-through immediately into industrial margins.
- **Edible Oils (~55%–60% Import Dependence):** Domestic oilseed deficits leave Indian kitchen budgets exposed to Indonesian palm-oil export curbs, Black Sea sunflower disruptions, and customs-duty swings.
- **TOP Crops (Tomato, Onion, Potato):** Perishable horticultural staples plagued by acute seasonality, weak cold-chain logistics, and **cobweb production cycles** (high prices in Year 1 trigger over-planting and price crashes in Year 2, triggering acreage cuts and price spikes in Year 3).
- **Pulses (Structural Protein Inflation):** Low irrigation coverage, rainfed cultivation, and inelastic household protein demand create chronic supply deficits whenever kharif or rabi acreage falters.
- **Monsoon Anomalies, El Niño & Heatwaves:** Over half of India's net sown area remains rainfed; **El Niño** events suppress southwest monsoon rainfall while unseasonal heatwaves shrivel wheat and vegetable yields, shrinking reservoir levels for subsequent rabi crops.
- **The Base Effect vs. Sequential Momentum:** Because official inflation is reported Year-on-Year ($\pi_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-12}}{P_{t-12}} \times 100$), an unusually high denominator ($P_{t-12}$) from a spike a year earlier mechanically depresses today's headline print (**favourable base effect**), while an unusually low prior-year base artificially inflates today's print (**unfavourable base effect**) even when month-on-month prices barely budged. Discerning analysts always separate **base effects** from **month-on-month (MoM) sequential momentum**.

## The 2026 Statistical Overhaul - CPI, WPI/PPI, IIP, Core Industries & ASI

Between February and July 2026, MoSPI and DPIIT synchronized India's macroeconomic yardsticks around modern post-pandemic benchmarks (`2024` for CPI; `2022–23` for GDP, IIP, WPI, PPI, and Core Industries). For UPSC Prelims 2027, holding **Dual-Standard Numeric Parity**—knowing *both* the legacy `2011–12 / 2012` series and the live `2022–23 / 2024` series—is indispensable.

### Consumer Price Index - NSO's February 12, 2026 Revision (`2012 → 2024 Base`)

Compiled monthly by the **National Statistics Office (NSO), MoSPI**, CPI (Rural, Urban, and **Combined**) tracks retail prices paid directly by households and serves as RBI's legal monetary-policy anchor. On **February 12, 2026** (releasing January 2026 provisional data at 2.75% YoY), MoSPI transitioned CPI from `2012 = 100` to **`2024 = 100`**, anchored on expenditure weights from the **Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) 2023–24** using the **Modified Mixed Reference Period (MMRP)** (combining 7-day, 30-day, and 365-day recall windows) and base prices collected across calendar year 2024.

Five structural upgrades redefine retail inflation measurement under **CPI `2024 = 100`**:
1. **Sharp Cut in Food Weight (Engel's Law):** **Food & Non-Alcoholic Beverages** dropped from **`45.86%`** (in CPI 2012) to **`36.75%`** (in CPI 2024)—mirroring rising household incomes spending proportionally less on staples and more on housing, mobility, and services. *(MoSPI methodological nuance: part of this 9.11 percentage-point drop is pure re-classification under COICOP 2018; under the legacy 2012 grouping, food would have fallen from `45.86%` to `40.10%`, whereas under the 2024 grouping, 2012 food stood at `42.62%`.)*
2. **COICOP 2018 Global Alignment:** Replaces the legacy 6-group COICOP 1999 structure (where an opaque `28.32%` "Miscellaneous" bucket hid health, education, and transport) with **12 Divisions**, 43 Groups, 92 Classes, and 162 Sub-classes.
3. **Housing & Rural Rent Integration:** The second-largest division—**Housing, Water, Electricity, Gas & Other Fuels (`17.67%`)**—consolidates shelter and utilities while capturing **rural house rent for the first time** (CPI 2012 tracked urban house rent exclusively at `10.07%`). Transport (`8.80%`), Clothing & Footwear (`6.38%`), and Health (`6.10%`) also gain explicit divisional visibility.
4. **Modernized Basket & Digital Markets:** Expands weighted items from **299 → 358 items** (**308 goods + 50 services**), adding OTT streaming subscriptions, airfares, telecom plans, and exercise equipment while retiring obsolete relics like VCRs and DVD players. Price capture widens to **1,465 rural markets** (up from 1,181), **1,395 urban markets** (up from 1,114), and **12 online e-commerce markets** across major cities (>25 lakh population).
5. **Statistical Formula & Monetary Consequence:** Elementary indices use the **Jevons index** (geometric mean, short-index formulation) and higher-level indices use the **Young / Modified Laspeyres index**. Macroeconomically, cutting food's share from ~46% to ~37% **dampens monsoon-induced headline CPI whiplash**, giving the RBI Monetary Policy Committee cleaner signal-to-noise visibility without abandoning headline targeting.

### Labour Bureau Price Indices - CPI-IW, CPI-AL & CPI-RL

Distinct from NSO's all-India CPI, three targeted retail indices are compiled monthly by the **Labour Bureau** (*Ministry of Labour & Employment*, Chandigarh/Shimla) for statutory wage and allowance indexation:
- **CPI for Industrial Workers (`CPI-IW`, Base `2016 = 100`):** Revised from `2001 = 100` effective September 2020 (Food & Beverages `39.20%`, Housing `17.75%`, Misc `29.81%`; released on the last working day of the following month). Directly governs **Dearness Allowance (DA)** revisions for Central/State government employees, PSUs, and organized industrial minimum wages.
- **CPI for Agricultural Labourers (`CPI-AL`) & Rural Labourers (`CPI-RL`, Base `2019 = 100`):** In a crucial 2025 upgrade, the Labour Bureau replaced the nearly four-decade-old **`1986–87 = 100`** base with **`2019 = 100`** effective **May 2025** onwards (covering 34 States/UTs; released on the 20th of the following month). Because rural and farm labourers devote a much higher share of income to food than the average urban household, CPI-AL/RL swings sharper during cereal/vegetable shocks and informs agricultural minimum-wage revisions. *(Note: **CPI-UNME** for Urban Non-Manual Employees was permanently discontinued in 2010.)*

### Wholesale Price Index (`2022–23 Base`), Historic PPI Launch & CPI–WPI Divergence

Compiled monthly by the **Office of the Economic Adviser (OEA), DPIIT** (*Ministry of Commerce & Industry*), the **Wholesale Price Index (WPI)** tracks bulk first-stage commodity transactions. On **June 15, 2026**, DPIIT executed a dual milestone: revising **WPI from `2011–12` to `2022–23 = 100`** while simultaneously launching India's long-awaited **Producer Price Indices (PPIs)**:

- **Key Upgrades in WPI (`2022–23 = 100`):**
  - **Expanded Basket (`697 → 957` items):** Still covers **goods only (strictly excludes services)**.
  - **Weighting Shift (`Net Traded Value → Gross Value of Output`):** Weights now reflect total domestic production (**GVO**) from a producer's perspective rather than wholesale traded value (`GVO + Imports − Exports`), paired with short-term chain-based elementary indices and **Targeted Mean Imputation** (replacing stale Carry-Forward missing-price plugs).
  - **Energy & Green Transition Reclassification:** **Crude Petroleum & Natural Gas** shifted out of Primary Articles into **Fuel & Power**, while **Solar, Wind, and Nuclear Electricity** entered the Electricity sub-group.
  - **Revised Major Group Weights (`2022–23` vs. `2011–12`):** **Manufactured Products `63.13%`** *(was `64.23%`)*, **Primary Articles `22.76%`** *(was `22.62%`)*, and **Fuel & Power `14.11%`** *(was `13.15%`)*—with the composite **WPI Food Index** sitting at **`24.99%`** *(was `24.38%`)*.
- **India's Historic Producer Price Index (PPI) Suite (June 15, 2026, Base `2022–23`):** Measuring true factory-gate prices received by domestic producers (*excluding indirect taxes like GST and retail/wholesale transport margins*), DPIIT launched three parallel tracks aligned with IMF standards: **(i) Monthly Output PPI (OPPI)**, **(ii) Monthly Trial Input PPI (IPPI)** (experimental for Manufacturing), and **(iii) Quarterly Service Producer Price Index (Service PPI)**—filling India's historic blind spot by tracking producer prices across **seven services** (*Banking, Securities Transaction, Insurance, Pension Fund Management, Railways, Air Passenger, and Telecom*). To allow legacy infrastructure and commercial price-escalation contracts to transition smoothly, **WPI will run alongside PPI for five years and be discontinued thereafter**.
- **Mechanics of CPI–WPI Divergence:** Even after the 2026 base updates, CPI and WPI frequently move in opposite directions for four structural reasons: **(i)** **Services Exclusion** (WPI ignores healthcare, education, rent, and telecom, which drive sticky CPI core inflation), **(ii)** **Weight Asymmetry** (CPI places `36.75%` on food vs. WPI's `24.99%`, while WPI places `77.24%` combined on manufactured goods + fuel/power, tying WPI tightly to global crude, steel, and chemical cycles), **(iii)** **Tax & Margin Wedges** (CPI includes indirect taxes like GST and retail/logistics margins; WPI excludes indirect taxes and retail mark-ups), and **(iv)** **Pass-Through Lags** (input-cost spikes hit WPI immediately, as seen during May–October 2026's West Asian energy shock, before filtering selectively into retail CPI with a 1–3 quarter lag).

### Industrial Volume Architecture - IIP (`2022–23 Base`), Nine Core Industries & ASI

While CPI and WPI measure *prices*, three linked datasets measure real *industrial volume* and factory fundamentals:

- **Index of Industrial Production (IIP — Base `2022–23 = 100`):** Compiled monthly by **NSO, MoSPI** as a short-term **physical volume indicator** released on the **28th of every succeeding month** (~4-week lag, shortened from the legacy 6-week cycle). Originally scheduled for May 28, 2026, the revised `2022–23` series debuted on **June 1, 2026** (using WPI deflators) and was immediately **re-released on June 29, 2026 adopting DPIIT's new Output PPI as the deflator** for all **234 value-based item groups** (`36.02%` of total IIP weight)—markedly improving real-volume precision. Marking its 10th base revision since 1937, the `2022–23` IIP aligns with **UNSD IRIIP 2010 (ISIC Rev. 4 Sections B–E)** and **NIC-2025**:
  - **Expanded from 3 to 4 Broad Sectors (GVA-Derived Weights):** **Manufacturing `76.06%`** *(down slightly from `77.63%`)*, **Mining & Quarrying `11.05%`** *(down from `14.37%`; newly adding **minor minerals and rare earth minerals**)*, **Electricity & Gas Supply `10.87%`** *(up from `7.99%` Electricity alone; newly reporting separate **Renewable vs. Non-Renewable** power indices + gas supply)*, and a brand-new fourth sector—**Water Supply, Sewerage & Waste Management `2.02%`**.
  - **Modernized Product Basket (`839 → 1,042` products across `407 → 463` item groups):** Adds spacecraft/aircraft parts, CCTV cameras, medical stents, vaccines, magnetic-stripe cards, and non-woven textiles while dropping kerosene, CFLs, fluorescent tubes, and sewing machines.
  - **Use-Based Classification (6 Groups):** Tracks demand origin across **Primary Goods**, **Capital Goods** (private/public investment proxy), **Intermediate Goods**, **Infrastructure/Construction Goods**, **Consumer Durables**, and **Consumer Non-Durables** (rural/mass-consumption pulse). *(In parallel, MoSPI also piloted India's first monthly **trial Index of Services Production — ISP**, base `2024–25`, leveraging GST data to monitor formal services.)*
- **Index of Core Industries (ICI — Revised July 20, 2026 from Eight to Nine Sectors!):** Compiled monthly by **OEA, DPIIT** (released around the 20th of the following month as a high-frequency advance barometer ahead of IIP). On **July 20, 2026**, DPIIT reset ICI from `2011–12` to **`2022–23 = 100`**, delivering three seismic Prelims-grade shifts:
  1. **Expansion from 8 to 9 Core Industries:** **Iron Ore (`4.90%`)** was newly inducted as the **ninth core industry** given its foundational role in steelmaking.
  2. **Lower Share in IIP:** Because the `2022–23` IIP expanded into gas, water/waste, and modern manufacturing, the combined weight of core industries in IIP shifted from **`40.27%`** *(legacy 8 sectors in `2011–12` IIP)* to **`32.88%`** *(new 9 sectors in `2022–23` IIP)*.
  3. **Top-Weight Ranking Inversion & Anti-Double-Counting Fixes:** **Electricity (`30.93%`) overtook Refinery Products (`22.57%`)** as the #1 core sector *(inverting the `2011–12` hierarchy where Refinery Products led at `28.04%` ahead of Electricity at `19.85%`)*. The full `2022–23` descending order is: **Electricity (`30.93%`) > Refinery Products (`22.57%`) > Steel (`17.58%`) > Crude Oil (`7.43%`) > Coal (`5.60%`) > Iron Ore (`4.90%`) > Cement (`4.41%`) > Natural Gas (`3.84%`) > Fertilisers (`2.73%`, still lowest)**. Methodologically, **Steel** moved from *net* to *gross* production (matching IIP) and **Coal** retains strictly *Raw Coal* (excluding washed coal and middlings to eliminate double counting).
- **Annual Survey of Industries (ASI):** Conducted annually by **NSO, MoSPI**, ASI is India's definitive statutory structural survey of the **registered (organized) manufacturing factory sector**—covering factories registered under **Sections `2m(i)`** (≥10 workers with power) and **`2m(ii)`** (≥20 workers without power) of the *Factories Act, 1948*, bidi/cigar establishments, non-CEA electricity undertakings, and ≥100-employee units in State Business Registers. *(Unincorporated/informal establishments sit outside ASI and are captured by **ASUSE**.)* Crucially, ASI supplies the **factory sampling frame and manufacturing weighting architecture** for IIP. Per **ASI 2024–25** (released September–November 2026), registered factories expanded to **2.67 lakh** (2.19 lakh operational), manufacturing GVA rose **9.59%** to **₹26.94 lakh crore**, fixed capital jumped **10.54%** to **₹51.12 lakh crore**, and total factory engagement crossed **2.10 crore** (+7.19%, adding >14 lakh jobs)—led in GVA by Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Uttar Pradesh (>54% combined) and in employment by Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat, UP, and Haryana (>56%).

## Macro-Household Impacts & the Changing Balance Sheet of Indian Savings

Inflation is never distributionally neutral; it rewires real returns, tax brackets, exchange rates, and household portfolio choices across the macroeconomy.

### Real Interest Rate Suppression, Fiscal Drag & Currency Pressure

- **Real Interest Rate Suppression & the "Inflation Tax":** Under the **Fisher Equation** ($r \approx i - \pi^e$; exact: $1 + r = \frac{1 + i}{1 + \pi}$), what determines purchasing-power accumulation is the **real interest rate ($r$)**, not the nominal coupon ($i$). Whenever retail inflation exceeds bank fixed-deposit rates (a recurring post-pandemic reality when FD rates hovered at 5.5%–6.5% against 6%–7% CPI), real deposit returns turn **negative**. This operates as an unlegislated **inflation tax**: it silently transfers real wealth from **net creditors** (middle-class depositors, fixed-income pensioners) to **net debtors** (leveraged corporations and the sovereign borrower, whose real debt-to-GDP erodes via higher nominal GDP growth).
- **Fiscal Drag (Bracket Creep):** In progressive tax systems whose nominal income slabs are not automatically indexed to CPI, inflation induces **fiscal drag**. When a worker's nominal salary rises 7% purely to match 7% inflation, their *real* income is flat—yet the extra nominal rupees push them into a higher marginal tax slab (or erode fixed nominal deduction caps). Real tax revenue rises automatically while real household disposable income shrinks, acting as a built-in fiscal stabilizer at the cost of quietly squeezing middle-class savings.
- **External Competitiveness & Currency Depreciation Loop:** Under **Relative Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)**, if India's domestic inflation persistently exceeds trading-partner inflation while the nominal rupee stays rigid, Indian exports grow dearer in real terms—driving **REER (Real Effective Exchange Rate) appreciation** and widening the Current Account Deficit. Eventually, market pressure forces **nominal INR depreciation**, which raises the rupee landed cost of imported crude oil, edible oils, fertilizers, and electronics—feeding **imported cost-push inflation** back into the domestic economy.

### Household Financial Savings - Physical Asset Shift & Unsecured Retail Credit Stress

Macroeconomically, **households are India's primary net lenders**—supplying the domestic financial surplus required to fund both the combined General Government fiscal deficit (~7.5%–8% of GDP) and private corporate capital expenditure without relying dangerously on volatile foreign debt. The crucial balance-sheet identity is:

$$\text{Net Household Financial Savings (NHFS)} = \text{Gross Financial Savings (Assets)} - \text{Gross Financial Liabilities (New Borrowing)}$$

Over the post-pandemic cycle, Indian household balance sheets underwent a dramatic three-part transformation documented across RBI Annual Reports and Financial Stability Reports (2024–2026):

1. **The Multi-Decade Trough & Partial Stabilization:** After spiking temporarily to ~11.6% of GDP during the FY21 lockdowns, **Net Household Financial Savings** fell sharply to a near five-decade low of **~4.9%–5.3% of GDP in FY23** and **~5.2%–5.3% in FY24** (legacy `2011–12` GDP series), before recovering partially to **~6.0%–7.1% of GDP in FY25** (~7.1% on the revised `2022–23` GDP base; hitting 7.6% in Q4 FY25 per RBI FSR Dec 2025).
2. **Gross Asset Resilience vs. Portfolio Shift to Physical Assets:** Contrary to alarmist claims that Indians stopped saving altogether, **gross financial savings stayed broadly steady at ~11%–12.3% of GDP**—even while undergoing internal **"financialization"** away from traditional bank deposits toward **mutual fund SIPs, equities, and insurance/pension funds** (deposits, insurance, and pensions still anchor ~69.2% of household financial wealth stock). Rather, the squeeze on *net* financial savings reflected two simultaneous forces: **(a)** households aggressively pivoted capital into **physical assets**—namely **residential real estate** (post-COVID housing upcycle funded via mortgages) and **gold/silver ornaments** (serving as a classic inflation and rupee-depreciation hedge)—and **(b)** a **structural surge in household financial liabilities**, with annual new borrowing jumping to **~6.5% of GDP in FY24** (moderating to ~4.9% in FY25).
3. **Unsecured Retail Credit Stress & the Gold-Loan Pivot:** Household debt-to-GDP climbed from ~33% pre-pandemic to **~41%–49% of GDP by 2025–26** (RBI FSR Dec 2025 gross financial liabilities at `41.3%` of GDP; broader BIS household-debt series reaching `49.1%` by March 2026). Crucially, a rising share of debt shifted from durable asset formation toward frictionless **unsecured consumption credit**—credit cards, small-ticket digital personal loans, fintech BNPL, and microfinance—exposing lower-income and young urban borrowers to rising debt-service ratios and repayment delinquencies. When the **RBI raised risk weights by +25 percentage points** on unsecured consumer credit and bank lending to NBFCs in **November 2023**, unsecured credit growth moderated sharply—yet household borrowing demand migrated rapidly into **collateralized gold loans**, causing scheduled commercial bank loans against gold jewellery to surge nearly five-fold from ₹0.93 lakh crore (March 2024) to **₹4.61 lakh crore (March 2026)**.

**Macroeconomic Stakes:** Sustained compression of Net Household Financial Savings below ~6%–7% of GDP tightens India's domestic savings–investment balance. When household financial surpluses fall short of the public-sector borrowing requirement, commercial banks face structural **deposit mobilization stress** (elevated Credit-to-Deposit ratios above 80%), sovereign bond yields stay sticky, and private corporate investment faces higher borrowing costs—linking retail inflation and household savings directly to India's long-run growth ceiling.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **CPI Survey Year vs. Base Year Trap:** The new CPI base year is **`2024 = 100`** (first released **February 12, 2026**); its weights come from **HCES `2023–24`** (MMRP). Do not confuse the survey year (`2023–24`) with the base year (`2024`) or the GDP/IIP/WPI base year (`2022–23`).
> - **CPI Food Weight & Rural Rent Upgrade:** Under CPI `2024 = 100`, Food & Beverages dropped from `45.86%` to **`36.75%`** (still the single largest division!), while **rural house rent** and **12 online marketplaces** are now included for the first time.
> - **WPI vs. Service PPI Nuance:** Even after the **June 15, 2026** revision (`2022–23` base, `957` items, GVO weights), **WPI itself still excludes services**. Services producer inflation is now captured separately by DPIIT's new quarterly **Service PPI** across 7 services—and WPI is slated for **discontinuation after 5 years** as users transition to PPI.
> - **IIP 2026 Deflator & 4th Sector Trap:** IIP (`2022–23` base, NSO) expanded from 3 sectors to **4 sectors** by adding **Water Supply, Sewerage & Waste Management (`2.02%`)** (alongside Gas Supply and Rare Earths in Mining). Furthermore, since **June 29, 2026**, MoSPI deflates value-based IIP groups using **DPIIT's Output PPI**, *no longer WPI*!
> - **Core Industries — 8 vs. 9 Sectors & #1 Weight Flip:** Legacy textbooks state *"8 Core Industries = `40.27%` of IIP, Refinery Products #1 (`28.04%`)"*. Effective **July 20, 2026** (`2022–23` base), ICI covers **9 Core Industries** (adding **Iron Ore**, `4.90%`), comprises **`32.88%` of IIP**, and **Electricity (`30.93%`)** has overtaken **Refinery Products (`22.57%`)** as #1! *(Fertilisers remains last in both series.)*
> - **Labour Bureau Base-Year Update:** **CPI-AL and CPI-RL** are no longer on the ancient `1986–87` base—they shifted to **`2019 = 100`** effective **May 2025** (`CPI-IW` remains on `2016 = 100`).
> - **Disinflation vs. Deflation:** Disinflation means inflation is *positive but slowing* (prices still rise); Deflation means inflation is *negative* (general price level falls).
> - **Inflation Winners vs. Losers:** Unanticipated inflation **benefits debtors/borrowers** (who repay fixed nominal loans in cheaper rupees) and **hurts creditors/lenders and fixed-income savers**—and **does not** automatically make **Inflation-Indexed Bonds (IIBs)** tax-exempt (both interest and capital gains on IIBs remain fully taxable!).
> - **Demand-Pull vs. Cost-Push Wage Indexing:** In UPSC's strict analytical taxonomy (Prelims 2021), **inflation-indexing wages** feeds *cost-push* dynamics and **rising interest rates** dampen demand; **expansionary monetary policy, fiscal stimulus, and higher purchasing power** drive *demand-pull* inflation.
> - **ASI vs. ASUSE Coverage:** The **Annual Survey of Industries (ASI)** tracks *registered* factories (Factories Act Sections 2m(i)/2m(ii)) and feeds IIP's manufacturing frame—it does **not** capture the informal/unincorporated sector (tracked by **ASUSE**).
> - **Why Net Household Financial Savings Fell:** The FY23–FY24 drop in NHFS was driven predominantly by **surging household financial liabilities (borrowing)** and a shift toward **physical assets (housing/gold)**—*not* a collapse in gross financial asset accumulation.
