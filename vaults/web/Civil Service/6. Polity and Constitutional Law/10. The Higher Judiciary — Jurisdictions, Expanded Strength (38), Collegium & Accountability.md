# Chapter 10: The Higher Judiciary — Jurisdictions, Expanded Strength (38), Collegium & Accountability

India's higher judiciary embodies an extraordinary constitutional paradox. By doctrinal design, the Supreme Court of India is arguably the world's most potent constitutional court—self-recruiting through the Collegium, armed with Basic Structure review, and wielding plenary equity under Article 142. Yet by operational reality, the institution grapples with over 92,000 pending cases at the apex level, persistent High Court vacancy rates hovering near 26%, recurring executive pocket-vetoes on judicial recommendations, and an all-or-nothing impeachment mechanism exposed anew in 2025–2026.

Understanding Part V, Chapter IV (**Articles 124–147**) and Part VI, Chapter V (**Articles 214–231**) therefore demands dual mastery: surgical bare-text precision for Prelims elimination traps, and structural clarity on the shifting boundary between **judicial independence**, **separation of powers**, and **democratic accountability** for Mains GS2.

---

## 1. Unified Judicial Architecture & the 2026 Capacity Expansion (38 Judges)

Unlike the United States—where federal courts enforce federal statutes and state courts enforce state laws in parallel silos—India operates a **single, integrated judicial hierarchy** modeled on the Government of India Act, 1935. The Supreme Court, inaugurated on January 28, 1950 (succeeding the Federal Court of India and Privy Council), stands at the apex, overseeing **25 High Courts** and the subordinate judiciary beneath them. Every court in this pyramid enforces both Union and State legislation without jurisdictional bifurcation.

### Constitutional Composition, Qualifications & Temporary Benches

Under **Article 214**, there shall be a High Court for each State, though **Article 231** (amended by the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**) empowers Parliament by law to establish a common High Court for two or more States or for States and a Union Territory (e.g., Bombay, Gauhati, Punjab & Haryana, and Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh). Under **Article 241**, Parliament may constitute a High Court for a Union Territory (the Delhi High Court, 1966, remains the sole exclusively UT High Court). Across India's 25 High Courts, the aggregate sanctioned strength stands at **1,122 judges** (847 permanent and 275 additional), with working vacancies hovering around **26% (~292–295 seats)** in late 2026 and Allahabad High Court holding the highest sanctioned strength (160) against Sikkim's lowest (3).

Eligibility criteria create sharp asymmetric filters between the two constitutional tiers:

- **Supreme Court Judge (Article 124(3)):** Must be a citizen of India and either (a) a High Court judge (or of two or more HCs in succession) for at least **5 years**, OR (b) an advocate of a High Court (or successive HCs) for at least **10 years**, OR (c) a **"distinguished jurist"** in the opinion of the President. Notably, no individual has ever been appointed to the Supreme Court under the distinguished jurist category.
- **High Court Judge (Article 217(2)):** Must be a citizen of India and either (a) held a **judicial office** in Indian territory for at least **10 years**, OR (b) been an **advocate** of a High Court (or successive HCs) for at least **10 years**. Crucially, the **"distinguished jurist" route does NOT exist** for High Court appointments.
- **Age & Tenure:** The Constitution prescribes **no minimum age** for either court. Supreme Court judges hold office until **age 65** (Article 124(2)), while High Court judges retire at **age 62** (raised from 60 by the **15th CAA, 1963**; Article 217(1)). Disputes regarding a Supreme Court judge's age are determined by such authority and manner as Parliament prescribes by law (Article 124(2A)), whereas a High Court judge's age is decided by the **President after consultation with the CJI**, whose decision is final (Article 217(3)).

To manage temporary vacancies or docket spikes, the Constitution provides four distinct non-permanent mechanisms whose procedural differences are prime Prelims traps:

- **Acting Chief Justice (Art 126 SC / Art 223 HC):** Appointed by the President when the office of CJI/HC CJ is vacant or the incumbent is unable to perform duties.
- **Ad hoc Judges of the Supreme Court (Art 127):** When a quorum of permanent SC judges is lacking, the **CJI**, with the **previous consent of the President** and after **consultation with the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court**, may request a **sitting High Court judge** duly qualified for SC appointment to sit as an *ad hoc* judge. Retired judges cannot be appointed under Article 127.
- **Attendance of Retired Judges at SC Sittings (Art 128):** The **CJI**, with the **previous consent of the President** and the **consent of the person concerned**, may request a retired SC judge, a retired Federal Court judge, or a retired HC judge (duly qualified for SC appointment) to sit and act as an SC judge.
- **Additional, Acting & Retired HC Judges (Art 224 & Art 224A):** Under **Article 224**, the President may appoint qualified persons as **Additional Judges** (for temporary workload increase, max **2 years**) or **Acting Judges** (during temporary absence of a permanent judge), neither holding office past age 62. Under **Article 224A** (inserted by the **15th CAA, 1963**), a **High Court Chief Justice**, with the **previous consent of the President** and the retired judge's consent, may request a retired HC judge to sit and act temporarily. On **January 30, 2025**, the Supreme Court relaxed its earlier *Lok Prahari* (2021) trigger criteria (such as the 20% vacancy threshold), permitting High Courts facing acute criminal appeal backlogs to appoint up to 10% of sanctioned strength as *ad hoc* retired judges under Article 224A.

### The Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026 (Strength = 38)

**Article 124(1)** originally fixed the Supreme Court at the Chief Justice of India plus **"not more than seven other Judges"** (total 8), empowering **Parliament by ordinary law**—not a constitutional amendment—to prescribe a larger number. Parliament exercised this power via the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956**, ratcheting sanctioned strength upward across seven statutory revisions: **8 (1950) → 11 (1956) → 14 (1960) → 18 (1977/78) → 26 (1986) → 31 (2009) → 34 (2019) → 38 (2026)**.

In May 2026, following a request from 53rd CJI **Surya Kant** (in office since November 24, 2025) highlighting docket congestion and Constitution Bench demands, the Union Cabinet cleared a four-judge expansion. Because Parliament was in recess, President Droupadi Murmu promulgated the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026** under Article 123 on **May 16, 2026**. Parliament subsequently replaced it with the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2026 (Act No. 14 of 2026)**—introduced in the Lok Sabha on July 20, passed on August 3, returned by the Rajya Sabha on August 5, and receiving Presidential assent on **August 11, 2026** (with retrospective effect from **May 16, 2026**). The Act amends Section 2 of the 1956 Act, raising puisne judges from 33 to **37**, taking total sanctioned strength to **38 (37 + CJI)**. Following three HC Chief Justices taking oath on October 6, 2026, the working strength reached **37 judges** (one short of capacity).

Two constitutional and structural controversies frame this expansion:

1. **The Money Bill Route Controversy:** The 2026 Bill was certified by the Lok Sabha Speaker as a **Money Bill under Article 110** on the rationale that salaries, staff costs, and infrastructure for additional judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India under Article 146(3). Critics argued that altering judicial composition under Article 124(1) is a primary institutional change to which financial outlay is merely incidental—meaning it ought to be a Financial Bill Category I/II (Article 117) allowing Rajya Sabha amendments rather than bypassing the Upper House.
2. **Strength vs. Structural Reform (Pendency Arithmetic):** Per the Bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons and NJDG data, Supreme Court pendency stood at **92,101 cases as of January 1, 2026** (climbing to **92,823 by April 30, 2026**), because 2025 saw **75,410 fresh institutions** against **65,615 disposals** despite near-full working strength. Worse, convening major Constitution Benches—such as the **9-judge Sabarimala reference Bench** in May 2026—diverts over a quarter of the Court from regular dockets. Yet adding four judges revives the structural diagnosis of the **229th Law Commission Report (2009)**: because India's Supreme Court functions overwhelmingly as a regular court of appeal via Special Leave Petitions, expanding headcount alone cannot resolve systemic pendency or regional access inequities. The 229th Report proposed splitting the Court into a permanent **Constitution Bench at Delhi** and **four regional Cassation Benches** (Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai/Hyderabad)—a proposal repeatedly rejected by the SC Full Court (February 2010), even though **Article 130** empowers the CJI, with Presidential approval, to appoint other seats outside Delhi. A writ petition seeking a **National Court of Appeal** remains pending before a Constitution Bench since 2016.

## 2. Constitutional Jurisdictions & Extraordinary Powers

The Supreme Court's authority spans five concentric rings—federal adjudication, fundamental rights enforcement, universal appellate supervision, advisory counsel, and plenary equity—while High Courts command geographically bounded yet substantively broader writ and supervisory powers.

### Exclusive Federal Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)

Under **Article 131**, the Supreme Court exercises **original and exclusive** jurisdiction in any federal dispute between: (a) the Government of India and one or more States; (b) the Government of India and any State(s) on one side and one or more States on the other; or (c) two or more States. Two strict jurisdictional gates apply: first, the dispute must involve a question of law or fact on which the **existence or extent of a legal right** depends (excluding purely political controversies); second, **private citizens or private entities cannot be parties** to an Article 131 suit (unlike *State of Karnataka v. UoI*, 1978, which allowed States to challenge Union executive/statutory intrusions affecting their legal rights; and the ongoing 2024 Constitution Bench reference in *State of Kerala v. UoI* testing Union borrowing caps under Article 293).

Crucially, the Constitution carves out **explicit exclusions** from Article 131:
- Disputes arising out of any **pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, engagement, or *sanad*** continuing in operation (Proviso to Art 131);
- **Inter-State river water disputes** barred by parliamentary law under **Article 262** (Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956);
- Matters referred to the **Finance Commission** (Article 280);
- Adjustment of certain expenses and pensions between the Union and States (Article 290); and
- Ordinary commercial disputes between the Centre and States, or recovery of damages by a State against the Centre.

Do not confuse Article 131 with other original powers: disputes over the election of the **President and Vice-President** also vest exclusively and originally in the Supreme Court under **Article 71** (final decision), while **Article 32** writ jurisdiction is original but **non-exclusive** (concurrent with High Courts under Article 226). High Courts separately hold original jurisdiction over revenue matters, admiralty, probate, matrimonial cases, election petitions for MPs/MLAs under the RPA, 1951, and—for the four chartered/statutory Presidency-lineage High Courts (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Delhi)—ordinary original civil jurisdiction above specified pecuniary limits.

### Writ Jurisdiction — Article 32 vs. Article 226

Both the Supreme Court (**Article 32**) and High Courts (**Article 226**) issue the five prerogative writs (*Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto*), yet their structural reach diverges sharply:

- **Substantive Breadth:** Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to issue writs **solely for the enforcement of Part III Fundamental Rights** (though **Article 139** authorizes Parliament to confer non-FR writ powers on the SC, Parliament has never done so). Conversely, Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for Fundamental Rights **and "for any other purpose"**—encompassing ordinary statutory and common-law rights. Thus, High Court writ jurisdiction is **wider** than the Supreme Court's.
- **Nature of Remedy:** Moving the Supreme Court under Article 32 is itself a **Fundamental Right** (Dr. Ambedkar's "heart and soul" of the Constitution); the SC cannot refuse a valid FR petition merely on discretion (though it encourages first approaching HCs under *Kanubhai Brahmbhatt*). Article 226 is a **discretionary constitutional remedy**; a High Court may refuse relief if an efficacious alternative statutory remedy exists.
- **Territorial Reach:** Article 32 runs nationwide against any authority within India. Under Article 226(1)–(2) (amended by the **15th CAA, 1963**), a High Court may issue writs to any person/authority within its territorial jurisdiction **or outside its territory if the cause of action arises, wholly or in part**, within its territory. Both Article 32 and Article 226 are unamendable facets of the **Basic Structure** (*L. Chandra Kumar v. UoI*, 1997).

### Appellate & Supervisory Jurisdictions (Articles 132–134A, 139A, 227–228)

The Supreme Court sits as the final court of appeal across three structured statutory channels plus one discretionary override:
- **Constitutional Appeals (Art 132):** Lies from any HC judgment, decree, or final order (civil, criminal, or other proceeding) if the HC certifies under **Article 134A** that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**.
- **Civil Appeals (Art 133):** Post the **30th CAA, 1972** (which abolished the ₹20,000 pecuniary threshold), civil appeal lies only when the HC certifies that the case involves a **substantial question of law of general importance** requiring Supreme Court determination.
- **Criminal Appeals (Art 134):** Lies as of right where an HC (a) on appeal reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to **death**, OR (b) withdraws a trial from a subordinate court and sentences the accused to **death**; OR on HC certificate of fitness. Parliament enlarged this via the *Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970* to include reversals resulting in **life imprisonment or imprisonment of 10+ years**.
- **Certificate of Appeal (Art 134A):** Inserted by the **44th CAA, 1978**, empowering the HC—either *suo motu* or on an oral application immediately after judgment—to decide whether to grant a certificate under Articles 132, 133, or 134.
- **Transfer & Superintendence:** Under **Article 139A** (inserted by the **42nd CAA, 1976**), the SC may withdraw cases involving identical substantial questions of general importance pending before the SC and one/more HCs (or multiple HCs), or transfer cases between HCs for ends of justice. At the state tier, **Article 227** grants High Courts **both administrative and judicial superintendence** over all courts and tribunals within their territory—**excluding courts/tribunals constituted under Armed Forces laws** (Art 227(4))—while **Article 228** empowers HCs to withdraw subordinate court cases involving substantial constitutional interpretation.

### Plenary Special Leave Jurisdiction (Article 136)

**Article 136(1)** confers a sweeping, residuary, discretionary power upon the Supreme Court to grant **special leave to appeal** from *any* judgment, decree, determination, sentence, or order in *any* cause or matter passed by *any* court or tribunal in India. Only one textual carve-out exists: **Article 136(2)** bars SLPs against any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the **Armed Forces** (note that statutory appeals from the Armed Forces Tribunal exist separately under the AFT Act, 2007).

Because Article 136 covers interlocutory orders, administrative tribunals, and even quasi-judicial bodies exercising sovereign adjudicatory power, it acts as a constitutional safety net against grave miscarriages of justice. Structurally, however, liberal SLP admission—accounting for over **80% of Supreme Court filings**—has converted India's apex constitutional court into an overburdened routine court of error, driving the pendency crisis that necessitated the 2026 strength expansion.

### Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143) & the 16th Presidential Reference (Nov 2025)

**Article 143** empowers the President to seek the Supreme Court's opinion across two constitutionally distinct limbs—a favourite Prelims differentiation trap:

1. **Article 143(1) — Public Importance Questions (Discretionary):** If it appears to the President that a question of law or fact has arisen (or is likely to arise) of such public importance that obtaining the SC's opinion is expedient, the President may refer it, and the Court **"may"**, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report its opinion. Because the verb is permissive, the SC can **decline to answer**—as it did in the **1993 Ayodhya Reference (*Dr. M. Ismail Faruqui*, Special Reference No. 1 of 1993)**, returning the query unanswered as superfluous and lacking constitutional purpose.
2. **Article 143(2) — Pre-Constitution Treaty Disputes (Mandatory):** Notwithstanding the Proviso to Article 131, the President may refer a dispute arising out of pre-Constitution treaties, covenants, or *sanads*, and the Supreme Court **"shall"** report its opinion. Here, tendering advice is **constitutionally mandatory**.

Under **Article 145(3)**, every Article 143 reference must be heard by a minimum **5-Judge Constitution Bench**, and opinions must be delivered in open court (Article 145(4)). Legally, an advisory opinion is **not a binding judgment or decree** (*Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College Society*, 1974 9-Judge Bench), though it carries immense institutional and persuasive weight (*In Re: Special Courts Bill*, 1978).

The constitutional contours of Article 143 were dramatically tested in the **16th Presidential Reference — *In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India* (`2025 INSC 1333`, Special Reference No. 1 of 2025, decided November 20, 2025)**. President Murmu referred 14 questions following the April 8, 2025 two-judge verdict in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (`2025 INSC 481`), which had fixed 1–3 month timelines for Governors/President and invoked Article 142 to grant "deemed assent" to stalled Bills. Answering through an anonymous 111-page "Opinion of the Court", the five-judge Bench led by then-CJI **B.R. Gavai** (with JJ. Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, and A.S. Chandurkar) settled a major Article 143 maintainability dispute: **answering a Presidential Reference is a distinct constitutional consultative function, not an appellate review of an earlier judgment**. Without unsettling the specific inter-partes relief already granted in *Tamil Nadu*, the Bench answered Questions 1–11 to reset institutional law on Articles 200, 201, 142, and 361 (while declining Q12 and Q14 as irrelevant and Q13 as overbroad).

### Court of Record & Contempt Jurisprudence (Articles 129, 215 & 1971 Act)

**Article 129** (Supreme Court) and **Article 215** (High Courts) declare both institutions to be **Courts of Record**, conferring two inherent attributes: (i) their proceedings and judgments carry perpetual evidentiary value and cannot be questioned when produced before any court; and (ii) they possess summary power to punish for **contempt of themselves** (and, per *Delhi Judicial Service Association*, 1991, and Section 10 of the 1971 Act, for contempt of subordinate courts/tribunals).

**Critical Bare-Text Distinction:** The Constitution **does NOT define "contempt of court," "civil contempt," or "criminal contempt"**. Rather, Parliament legislated the **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** pursuant to the **H.N. Sanyal Committee Report (1963)** (under List I Entry 77 for SC and List III Entry 14 for other courts), while **Article 19(2)** expressly lists "contempt of court" as a reasonable restriction on free speech. The 1971 Act bifurcates contempt into:
- **Civil Contempt (Sec 2(b)):** Wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ, or breach of an undertaking given to a court.
- **Criminal Contempt (Sec 2(c)):** Publication (words, signs, visible representations) or act that (i) **scandalises or tends to scandalise**, or lowers the authority of any court; (ii) prejudices or interferes with due judicial proceedings; or (iii) obstructs the administration of justice.

Two statutory guardrails temper criminal contempt. First, under **Section 15**, private citizens initiating criminal contempt require the written consent of the **Attorney General / Solicitor General** (for SC) or **Advocate General** (for HC)—though constitutional courts can always initiate ***suo motu* contempt** without law-officer consent. Second, the **Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act, 2006** amended **Section 13(b)** to introduce **justification by truth** as a valid defence, subject to twin cumulative tests: the court must be satisfied that the defence is both **in public interest** and **bona fide**. Critics—amplified during ***In Re: Prashant Bhushan* (2020)**, where the SC imposed a symbolic ₹1 fine for critical tweets—argue that the vague "scandalising the court" limb chills legitimate democratic critique of judicial administration (a ground abolished in the UK in 2013).

### Stare Decisis (Art 141), Review (Art 137) & Curative Petitions (*Rupa Ashok Hurra*)

Under **Article 141**, the law declared by the Supreme Court is **binding on all courts** within India, reinforced by **Article 144** requiring all civil and judicial authorities to act in aid of the Supreme Court. Only the ***ratio decidendi*** (the legal principle necessary for the decision) binds under Article 141—not *obiter dicta* (passing remarks) or decisions rendered *per incuriam* (in ignorance of binding statute/precedent). Crucially, the phrase "all courts" **does NOT include the Supreme Court itself**: in ***Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar* (1955)**, the SC held it may overrule its own erroneous precedents through larger Benches (smaller or co-ordinate Benches remain bound by larger/earlier co-ordinate Benches).

To balance finality (*interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium*) against error correction, two extraordinary post-judgment mechanisms operate:
- **Review Jurisdiction (Article 137):** Expressly empowers the Supreme Court to review any judgment or order it pronounces, subject to Parliamentary law and Article 145 Rules (Supreme Court Rules, 2013 prescribe a **30-day filing window**, circulation in chambers without oral hearing except death-penalty cases post-*Mohd. Arif*, 2014, and narrow grounds: error apparent on the face of the record or discovery of new material evidence). **Notice the structural contrast:** High Courts lack a mirror constitutional review Article comparable to Article 137; they exercise review jurisdiction via statutory provisions (Section 114 / Order 47 CPC) or inherent plenary power as Courts of Record under Article 215 (*Shivdeo Singh*, 1963).
- **Curative Petition (*Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra*, 2002 5-Judge Bench):** Because Article 137 exhausts formal review, the SC judicially carved out a final, rarest-of-rare remedy anchored in its inherent powers under **Articles 129 and 142**. A curative petition lies after a review petition is dismissed by circulation **only** where the petitioner establishes a gross miscarriage of justice—specifically (i) violation of principles of **natural justice** (*audi alteram partem*), (ii) **non-disclosure of judge's bias** or conflict of interest, or (iii) **abuse of the court's process**. Strict procedural gates deter abuse: mandatory certification by a **Senior Advocate**, certification by an Advocate-on-Record that it is the first curative plea, and initial circulation to the **three senior-most SC judges** plus the judges who delivered the original verdict (if available).

### Complete Justice (Article 142) — From *Shilpa Sailesh* (2023) to the Nov 2025 Reset

**Article 142(1)** vests exclusively in the Supreme Court—and **NOT in High Courts** (*Anil Kumar Jain v. Maya Jain*, 2009)—the plenary power to pass any decree or order necessary for doing **"complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it"**, enforceable throughout India. Because "complete justice" is constitutionally undefined, Article 142 has generated recurring separation-of-powers friction across three doctrinal phases:

1. **Statutory Freedom vs. Substantive Floor:** In *Delhi Judicial Service Association* (1991) and *Union Carbide* (1991), the SC declared that **prohibitions or limitations in ordinary statutes cannot constrain constitutional power under Article 142**. Conversely, in *Prem Chand Garg* (1962) and *Supreme Court Bar Association v. UoI* (1998), Constitution Benches cautioned that Article 142 is **supplementary, not supplantive**: it cannot override Fundamental Rights or build a new statutory edifice in direct defiance of express substantive legislative prohibitions.
2. **Equitable Expansion — *Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan* (May 1, 2023, 5-Judge Bench):** Unanimously held that the SC may invoke Article 142 to directly dissolve a marriage on the non-statutory ground of **"irretrievable breakdown of marriage"** and waive the mandatory 6–18 month cooling-off period under Section 13B(2) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955—even where one spouse objects—while clarifying that litigants cannot bypass Family Courts by filing direct Article 32/226 writs. The Court similarly deployed Article 142 proactively in public-interest crises (e.g., *In Re: Phalodi Accident*, 2025, enforcing safe national highway standards).
3. **The Constitutional Boundary Reset — 16th Presidential Reference (`2025 INSC 1333`, Nov 20, 2025):** Marking the sharpest institutional pullback on Article 142 in decades, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench decisively repudiated the April 2025 two-judge *Tamil Nadu Governor* innovation of using Article 142 to declare **"deemed assent"** for Bills delayed by a Governor. The Bench ruled that **"deemed assent" is alien to the Constitution**: Article 142 cannot substitute an express constitutional authority's legislative assent under Articles 200/201 or judicially fabricate uniform timelines where the constitutional text is deliberately silent. To prevent gubernatorial paralysis without breaching separation of powers, the Court confined judicial relief to a **"limited mandamus"** directing the Governor to act within a reasonable time only upon prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite inaction.

## 3. Judicial Appointments — The Collegium System, NJAC & Representation Deficits

No constitutional provision has undergone a more sweeping interpretive transformation than **Article 124(2)** and **Article 217(1)**. The word **"Collegium" appears nowhere in the Constitution of India**. Textually, Article 124(2) states that every Supreme Court judge shall be appointed by the President after **"consultation"** with such SC and HC judges as the President deems necessary, with a proviso making consultation with the **Chief Justice of India mandatory** for all judges other than the CJI. Similarly, **Article 217(1)** mandates Presidential appointment of High Court judges after consulting the CJI, the State Governor, and (for puisne HC judges) the HC Chief Justice; **Article 222** provides for HC judge transfers by the President after consulting the CJI.

### The Four Judges Cases & the NJAC Invalidation

Triggered by executive supersessions of senior judges during the 1970s (A.N. Ray in 1973; M.H. Beg in 1977) and punitive Emergency-era transfers, the Supreme Court progressively inverted "consultation" from executive discretion into binding judicial self-selection across four landmark rulings:

- **First Judges Case (*S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981 — 7-Judge Bench, 4:3):** Held that **"consultation" does NOT mean "concurrence"**. The President (acting on Union Cabinet advice) enjoyed **executive primacy** and could override the CJI's views for cogent reasons. Transfers under Article 222 could proceed without the judge's consent.
- **Second Judges Case (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association [SCARA] v. UoI*, 1993 — 9-Judge Bench, 7:2):** Overruled *S.P. Gupta*. Reasoning that judicial independence is part of the Basic Structure and the executive is the largest litigant before courts, the majority read **"consultation" as "concurrence"**. However, primacy belonged not to the CJI individually, but to the **institutional opinion of the judiciary**—formed by the CJI after consulting **two senior-most SC judges** (birth of the Collegium). The executive could return a recommendation once for reconsideration, but a **unanimous reiteration bound the President**. It also codified the **seniority norm** for appointing the CJI.
- **Third Judges Case (*In Re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998* — 9-Judge Bench, unanimous):** Responding to President K.R. Narayanan's Article 143 reference, the Court refined and broadened the Collegium architecture:
  - **SC Appointments:** **CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges**. If even **two** Collegium members record adverse opinions, the CJI must not forward the name. Consultation with the senior-most SC judge hailing from the candidate's parent HC is mandatory.
  - **HC Appointments:** **CJI + 2 senior-most SC judges** (distinct from the 5-member SC panel!), acting on initial proposals from the **HC Collegium** (HC CJ + 2 senior-most HC judges) alongside inputs from the State Government and Union Law Ministry.
  - **HC Judge Transfers (Art 222):** **CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges** plus views of the Chief Justices of both the transferring and transferee High Courts.
- **Fourth Judges Case (*SCARA v. Union of India*, October 16, 2015 — 5-Judge Bench, 4:1):** Parliament had unanimously enacted the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** (inserting Articles 124A, 124B, 124C) and the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014** to replace the Collegium with a six-member commission: **CJI (Chair) + 2 senior-most SC judges + Union Law Minister + 2 "eminent persons"** (chosen by a panel of PM, CJI, and LoP/leader of largest opposition party, with at least one from SC/ST/OBC/Minority/Women). Crucially, **any two members could veto** any appointment. A 4:1 majority (JJ. Khehar, Lokur, Goel, Joseph) struck down both the 99th CAA and NJAC Act as violating the **Basic Structure (Judicial Independence)**—holding that judicial primacy in appointments is constitutionally essential, that the Union Law Minister's presence created a conflict of interest given the State's status as top litigant, and that the undefined "eminent persons" double-veto could paralyze unanimous judicial recommendations. **Justice J. Chelameswar's lone dissent** defended the NJAC as a valid check-and-balance against an opaque, unaccountable "imperium in imperio" where judges appoint themselves.

### The 11-Year MoP Impasse, Executive Pocket-Vetoes & Opacity Shifts

While reviving the Collegium in December 2015, the Supreme Court acknowledged its structural flaws and directed the Union Government to finalize a revised **Memorandum of Procedure (MoP)** in consultation with the CJI, focusing on eligibility criteria, transparency, a permanent Secretariat, and complaint redressal. **Eleven years later, the revised MoP remains unfinalized**—stalemated primarily over the Centre's insistence on a unilateral rejection clause citing "national security" or "public interest" and disagreement over secretariat staffing. On **November 18, 2026**, a Supreme Court Bench (JJ. M.M. Sundresh and P.B. Varale) issued formal notice to the Union Government and SC Secretary General on a PIL by NGO ***Lok Prahari*** challenging the 11-year MoP delay and seeking to bring Collegium minutes under the RTI Act.

In the procedural vacuum left by the 1999 MoP—which prescribes **no binding deadlines** for executive processing—an informal **executive pocket-veto** has emerged as a powerful counter-check:
- **Indefinite Sitting:** The Union Law Ministry neither notifies nor formally returns sensitive Collegium recommendations, forcing candidates to withdraw or age out.
- **Selective Segregation ("Pick-and-Choose"):** Clearing preferred names from a consolidated Collegium batch while stalling others quietly distorts **inter-se seniority** on the Bench—affecting future Chief Justiceships and Collegium membership.
- **Reiteration Resistance:** Despite *Second Judges* making reiterated names binding, reiterated High Court recommendations (e.g., Advocate Saurabh Kirpal, John Sathyan) have faced multi-year executive stasis.

Simultaneously, the Collegium's own transparency record regressed in 2025–2026. Departing from earlier practice under which detailed **Collegium "Resolutions"** publicly articulated seniority rankings, merit assessments, IB inputs, and diversity considerations, the Court in 2026 largely reverted to publishing terse **"Statements"** naming recommendees without reasons—keeping the evaluative resolutions confidential.

### Women's Representation & the "Leaky Pipeline"

Because **Articles 124, 217, and 224 contain no caste or gender reservations**, diversity on India's constitutional benches depends entirely on Collegium intent. Historically, representation has remained starkly skewed: since Justice M. Fathima Beevi became the first woman SC judge in 1989 (39 years after independence), only **13 women** have ever been elevated to the Supreme Court as of November 2026.

Recent 2026 appointments brought modest recovery from a stark trough (between May 2025 and June 2026, Justice B.V. Nagarathna was the sole sitting woman judge on the apex bench). Under CJI Surya Kant's Collegium, Senior Advocate **V. Mohana** was elevated directly from the Bar on **June 2, 2026** (only the second woman directly from the Bar after Justice Indu Malhotra), followed by Gujarat High Court Chief Justice **Sunita Agarwal** on **October 6, 2026**. As of November 2026, the Supreme Court sits with **3 women judges out of 37 (~8.1%)**—with **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** serving on the Collegium and slated to become **India's first woman Chief Justice of India in September 2027** (for roughly 36 days). Across the 25 High Courts, women held **111 of 781 working judgeships (~14%)** as of July 2026 (PIB data from February 2026 recorded 170 women HC judges appointed since 2014, including 96 over five years).

This disparity reflects a **structural "leaky pipeline"**: while women now secure 40–50%+ of entry-level subordinate judiciary posts via competitive State Judicial Service examinations, their numbers collapse at the Higher Judiciary tier. Key institutional leaks include (i) the predominance of **direct-Bar elevations** (~65–70% of HC seats) where women face entrenched courtroom biases, unequal brief distribution, and skewed Senior Advocate designations; (ii) the rigid **10-year continuous practice** filter colliding with uncompensated caregiving breaks; (iii) opaque, network-driven Collegium shortlisting ("uncle-judge" and old-boys' club dynamics); and (iv) near-total exclusion from State Bar Council leadership—a bottleneck the Supreme Court sought to address via landmark orders in **December 2025 and August–September 2026** mandating **30% women's representation** (20% elected, 10% co-opted) in every State Bar Council.

## 4. Judicial Independence vs. Institutional Accountability

An independent judiciary is indispensable to constitutionalism, yet unchecked independence without accountability breeds institutional insularity. The Indian Constitution constructs formidable shields around judges while leaving internal disciplinary mechanisms deliberately austere—a tension sharply exposed across 2018–2026.

### Constitutional Insulation & the Post-Retirement Cooling-Off Vacuum

The Constitution insulates Supreme Court and High Court judges through seven interlocking structural guarantees:
1. **Security of Tenure:** Judges do **not** hold office at the President's pleasure; they cannot be removed except by an extraordinary parliamentary process (Articles 124(4), 217(1)(b)).
2. **Charged Expenditure (with a Federal Twist):** Salaries, allowances, and pensions of SC judges and administrative expenses of SC staff are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**—non-votable by Parliament (Articles 112(3)(d)(i), 146(3)). **Watch the High Court split:** HC judges' salaries/allowances and HC staff expenses are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State** (Articles 202(3)(d), 229(3)), whereas **HC judges' pensions are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** (Article 112(3)(d)(iii)) to insulate retired judges from State political leverage.
3. **Non-Diminution of Terms:** Salaries, allowances, leave, and pension rights cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment, **except during a Financial Emergency under Article 360** (Articles 125(2), 221(2)).
4. **Legislative Gag on Judicial Conduct:** **Article 121** (Parliament) and **Article 211** (State Legislatures) strictly prohibit discussing the conduct of any SC or HC judge in the discharge of duties, **except upon a formal removal motion** in Parliament (note: State Legislatures can *never* discuss judicial conduct, since they have zero role in removal!).
5. **Administrative Autonomy:** The CJI (Article 146) and HC Chief Justices (Article 229) recruit court officers and staff free from executive control.
6. **One-Way Jurisdictional Ratchet:** Parliament may **enlarge** Supreme Court jurisdiction (Article 138) but cannot curtail its constitutionally entrenched powers.
7. **Post-Retirement Practice Restrictions:** **Article 124(7)** imposes a blanket ban preventing any retired Supreme Court judge from pleading or acting in **any court or before any authority** in India. By contrast, **Article 220** (relaxed by the **7th CAA, 1956**) bars a retired **permanent** High Court judge from practising in any court or authority **except the Supreme Court and OTHER High Courts** (retiring **Additional** HC judges face no constitutional practice bar at all).

**The Cooling-Off Accountability Gap:** Crucially, neither Article 124(7) nor Article 220 prohibits retired SC or HC judges from accepting **post-retirement executive, statutory, or political offices**—including Governorships, Rajya Sabha nominations, tribunal chairmanships, and commissions of inquiry. As early as 1958, the **14th Law Commission Report** (M.C. Setalvad) warned that the lure of post-retirement patronage by the government—the largest litigant—casts doubt over pre-retirement independence. Yet proposals for a mandatory **two-year cooling-off period** or higher retirement age (65/67) with full-pay pensions remain unlegislated.

### Removal of Judges & the 2025–2026 Justice Yashwant Varma Stress-Test

The Constitution **never uses the word "impeachment" for judges** (reserving it solely for the President under Article 61); Articles 124(4) and 217(1)(b) (read with Article 218) prescribe **"removal"** by Presidential order strictly on two grounds: **"proved misbehaviour or incapacity"**. Neither term is defined in the Constitution. Pursuant to Article 124(5), Parliament regulated the investigation procedure through the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**:

1. **Initiation:** A removal motion signed by at least **100 Lok Sabha MPs** (to the Speaker) or **50 Rajya Sabha MPs** (to the Chairman). Only MPs—not the Union Government—can initiate it.
2. **Admission Discretion:** The Speaker or Chairman may consult material and **either admit OR refuse to admit** the motion (*Sub-Committee on Judicial Accountability*, 1991; note: rejection is not barred by the 1968 Act).
3. **Three-Member Statutory Inquiry Committee (Sec 3(2)):** If admitted, the Presiding Officer constitutes a fact-finding committee comprising: (i) the **CJI or a sitting SC judge**; (ii) the **Chief Justice of a High Court**; and (iii) a **distinguished jurist**. If notices are given in **both Houses on the same day**, the Proviso to Section 3(2) contemplates joint constitution upon admission in both Houses.
4. **Parliamentary Special Majority & Presidential Order:** Only if the committee records a finding of guilt does the House debate the motion (if acquitted, the motion dies automatically). Both Houses must pass the address **in the same session** by a **Special Majority**—a majority of total membership of that House AND not less than two-thirds of members present and voting. Upon presentation, the President issues the removal order.

Because of this towering threshold, **no Supreme Court or High Court judge in independent India has ever been formally removed by Presidential order**. Justice V. Ramaswami survived a 1993 Lok Sabha vote due to Congress abstentions, while Justices **Soumitra Sen** (Calcutta HC, 2011) and **P.D. Dinakaran** (Sikkim HC, 2011) resigned before the final vote.

To fill the disciplinary void beneath impeachment, the Supreme Court adopted a non-statutory **In-House Procedure (1999)** (rooted in *C. Ravichandran Iyer*, 1995 and *Addl. District & Sessions Judge 'X'*, 2014), allowing the CJI to convene a three-judge peer committee to probe misconduct, withdraw judicial work, advise resignation, or recommend parliamentary removal. Both the In-House Procedure and the 1968 Act suffered an unprecedented stress-test in the **2025–2026 Justice Yashwant Varma affair**:

- Following the March 14, 2025 discovery of sacks of charred currency at the official Delhi residence of then-Delhi HC judge Justice Yashwant Varma, CJI **Sanjiv Khanna** repatriated him to Allahabad HC, unprecedentedly published the preliminary Delhi HC CJ report and video evidence online, and constituted a three-judge In-House Committee (JJ. Sheel Nagu, G.S. Sandhawalia, Anu Sivaraman). Upon its indictment and Justice Varma's refusal to resign, CJI Khanna wrote to the President and Prime Minister recommending removal.
- Dismissing Justice Varma's challenge to the In-House Procedure, the SC held that the **CJI is not a "mere post office"** between the committee and the President, affirming the constitutional legitimacy of internal self-regulation.
- After same-day removal notices were filed in both Houses on July 21, 2025—the exact day Rajya Sabha Chairman Jagdeep Dhankhar abruptly resigned—Lok Sabha Speaker Om Birla admitted the motion and unilaterally constituted a three-member 1968 Act Inquiry Committee on August 12, 2025 (Justice Aravind Kumar, Madras HC CJ M.M. Shrivastava, Senior Advocate B.V. Acharya). In **`2026 INSC 65` (January 22, 2026)**, the Supreme Court rejected Justice Varma's challenge under the Proviso to Section 3(2), holding that one House's non-admission or temporary Presiding Officer vacancy cannot paralyze the other House's independent statutory initiation.
- Yet in **April 2026**, Justice Varma **resigned before the Inquiry Committee's conclusion and parliamentary vote**—becoming the **third High Court judge** to forestall removal via resignation, thereby terminating proceedings while retaining post-retirement entitlements. This episode starkly exposed India's **"all-or-nothing" accountability gap**: lacking a statutory **Judicial Standards and Accountability framework** with graded minor sanctions (public admonition, suspension of administrative duties, pension forfeiture, or independent oversight), judicial misconduct escapes enforceable consequence whenever a judge exits pre-vote.

### Master of the Roster (*Shanti Bhushan*, 2018) & CJI Office under RTI (*Subhash Chandra Agarwal*, 2019)

Two internal governance doctrines define day-to-day judicial transparency and power distribution:

- **Master of the Roster (*Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India*, 2018):** Following the unprecedented January 12, 2018 press conference by four senior-most SC judges questioning selective bench allocation of politically sensitive cases under CJI Dipak Misra, a writ petition urged reading "Chief Justice" in **Article 145** and the **Supreme Court Rules, 2013** to mean the five-judge **Collegium** rather than the CJI alone. Rejecting the plea, a two-judge Bench (JJ. A.K. Sikri and Ashok Bhushan) reaffirmed that the **CJI alone is the Master of the Roster** with exclusive administrative prerogative to constitute Benches and allocate cases (mirroring HC Chief Justices under *State of Rajasthan v. Prakash Chand*, 1998). While clarifying that the CJI is judicially only **"first among equals"**—unable to influence or overrule another judge's judicial order—critics emphasize that unchecked single-person roster discretion invites perceptions of "bench-hunting" unless bound by transparent, subject-wise, algorithmic allocation rules.
- **CJI Office under RTI (*CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal*, November 2019 — 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Upholding the 2010 Delhi High Court Full Bench verdict, the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that the **Supreme Court—and the Office of the CJI—constitutes a "public authority" under Section 2(h) of the RTI Act, 2005**. Rejecting the argument that transparency erodes judicial independence, the Bench held that independence and accountability are mutually reinforcing. However, it balanced the right to know against **privacy (Section 8(1)(j))** and **fiduciary exemptions (Section 8(1)(e))** through a **case-by-case proportionality and larger public interest test**. Thus, while general compliance with asset declarations (per the 1997 Full Court Resolution) and final Collegium outcomes can be disclosed, third-party personal data, Intelligence Bureau inputs, and confidential Collegium deliberations enjoy conditional protection—a boundary now actively reopened by the **November 18, 2026 *Lok Prahari* notice**.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **High Court Pensions vs. Salaries Split:** HC judges' salaries and allowances are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State** (Art 202(3)(d)), **NOT** the CFI—but their **pensions** are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India** (Art 112(3)(d)(iii)). Conversely, SC salaries, allowances, and pensions all come exclusively from the CFI.
> - **Ad Hoc vs. Retired Judges Trap:** **Article 127** (*ad hoc* SC judges) applies **ONLY to sitting High Court judges** qualified for SC elevation—never retired judges. Retired SC/HC judges sitting temporarily in the SC come under **Article 128** (CJI + President's prior consent + judge's consent). Retired HC judges sitting in HCs come under **Article 224A** (HC CJ + President's prior consent + judge's consent; inserted by 15th CAA, 1963).
> - **No "Distinguished Jurist" in High Courts:** The distinguished jurist qualification exists **exclusively under Article 124(3)** for the Supreme Court; **Article 217(2)** restricts HC eligibility strictly to 10-year judicial officers or 10-year HC advocates.
> - **Collegium & Impeachment — Zero Textual Mention:** Neither the word **"Collegium"** nor **"impeachment"** (for judges) nor **"civil/criminal contempt"** nor a definition of **"proved misbehaviour or incapacity"** appears in the Constitution. Also remember the Collegium size split: **CJI + 4** for SC appointments and HC transfers, but **CJI + 2** for HC appointments.
> - **Article 143(1) vs. 143(2) Mandatory Trap:** Under **Art 143(1)** (public importance), the SC *may* decline to advise (as in 1993 Ayodhya); under **Art 143(2)** (pre-Constitution treaties excluded from Art 131), tendering opinion is **mandatory** ("shall"). Both require a minimum **5-Judge Bench** (Art 145(3)).
> - **Armed Forces Carve-Out & Review Asymmetry:** Both **Article 136(2)** (SC SLP) and **Article 227(4)** (HC superintendence) exclude courts/tribunals constituted under **Armed Forces laws**. Similarly, **Article 137** expressly grants review power only to the SC (HCs rely on CPC/inherent Art 215 powers), and **Article 142** ("complete justice") vests **exclusively in the SC**, never in High Courts.
> - **Removal Motion Discretion & State Legislatures:** The Speaker/Chairman **CAN refuse to admit** a judicial removal motion under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968; **State Legislatures** can *never* discuss HC judge conduct (Art 211), unlike Parliament during a formal removal motion (Art 121).
> - **Post-Retirement Practice Trap:** **Art 124(7)** imposes a *total* practice bar on retired SC judges, whereas **Art 220** lets retired **permanent** HC judges practice in the **SC and OTHER High Courts** (and imposes zero bar on retired Additional HC judges).

> [!info] Mains Arsenal
> - **Core Constitutional Dialectic — Judicial Primacy vs. Democratic Legitimacy:** Frame judicial appointments not as a binary "Collegium vs. Executive" turf war, but as a design trade-off between **insulation from political capture** (India's Collegium avoids US-style partisan Senate confirmation polarization) and **democratic transparency/accountability** (Collegium suffers from opacity, self-selection, and executive pocket-vetoes). Cite **UK's Judicial Appointments Commission (JAC)** (independent lay-majority statutory selection body with open applications) alongside a **finalized MoP + Permanent Judicial Secretariat** as the institutional sweet spot.
> - **Constitutional Morality as the Accountability Fulcrum (2025 GS2 Core):** Anchor *NCT of Delhi v. UoI* (2018) and Dr. Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly warning: **constitutional morality** checks both executive overreach (protecting judicial independence) and judicial insularity (mandating transparent rosters, reasoned Collegium resolutions, mandatory asset publication, and recusal norms).
> - **Fixing the "All-or-Nothing" Accountability Gap (2026 Live Anchor):** Use the **April 2026 Justice Yashwant Varma resignation** (3rd HC judge to pre-empt removal after Soumitra Sen & P.D. Dinakaran) and `2026 INSC 65` to argue for statutory codification of the **In-House Procedure** with graded minor sanctions (censure, divestment of judicial work, pension withholding on indictment) and a **2-year post-retirement cooling-off period** (14th Law Commission, 1958).
> - **Calibrating Article 142 & Structural Pendency (2025–2026 Live Anchor):** Pair *Shilpa Sailesh* (2023) (constructive equity in private justice) against the **Nov 20, 2025 16th Presidential Reference (`2025 INSC 1333`)** (self-restraint barring "deemed assent" to protect separation of powers). Pair the **2026 SC expansion to 38 judges** (amid 92,823 pending cases and 9-judge Constitution Benches) with the **229th Law Commission Report's** structural cure: separating an apex **Constitutional Court** from **Regional Cassation Benches** to cure SLP bloat.
