# Federal Architecture I — Legislative, Administrative, Inter-State Water & Trade Relations (Parts XI & XIII)

India's constitutional federation was engineered not by sovereign states bargaining away power upward, as in Philadelphia in 1787, but by a unitary colonial state devolving authority outward while guarding against Partition-era centrifugal collapse. That origin story explains the structural asymmetry baked into Parts XI and XIII: coordinate autonomy in ordinary governance paired with decisive central overrides during crises, economic integration, or inter-state deadlock.

Mastering this architecture demands separating **bare-text distribution rules** (Articles 245–263 and 301–307) from **judicial recalibration**—above all, the Supreme Court's doctrinal pivot from its 1963 unitary skepticism toward 2024–2026 federalist restoration.

---

## Nature of Indian Federalism - Doctrinal Taxonomy and Live Modes

### Constitutional Taxonomy and Competing Academic Lenses

The Constitution deliberately omits the word *"federation"*, styling India in **Article 1(1)** as a **"Union of States"**. Explaining this choice in the Constituent Assembly, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar underlined two structural consequences: the Indian federation is not the product of an indestructible compact among sovereign units, and no constituent State enjoys a right to secede.

Constitutional theorists have struggled to fit this hybrid geometry into classical binary boxes, producing a spectrum of diagnostic labels:

- **K.C. Wheare (*"Quasi-Federal"*):** Classified India as a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features, citing central overrides over State Lists, Governors' reservation powers, and Article 3 boundary alteration.
- **Ivor Jennings (*"Federation with a Strong Centralizing Tendency"*):** Emphasized that India retains genuine dual polity structures while channeling macroeconomic planning and security control toward New Delhi.
- **Granville Austin (*"Cooperative Federalism"*):** Argued that India pioneered cooperative federalism by design—producing a strong general government without reducing States to mere administrative agents of the Centre.
- **W.H. Morris-Jones (*"Bargaining Federalism"*):** Observed that federal balance operates through continuous political negotiation between New Delhi and State capitals, oscillating sharply between single-party dominance and coalition eras.
- **Paul Appleby (*"Extremely Federal"*):** Reversed Wheare's thesis from an administrative lens, pointing out that the Union lacks an independent field bureaucracy across India and depends overwhelmingly on State machinery to execute national legislation.

### Supreme Court Trajectory - From Unitary Tilt to Basic Structure

The Supreme Court's own reading of Indian federalism has undergone a dramatic sixty-year inversion—moving from denying State sovereignty to entrenching federalism as an unamendable constitutional pillar.

In ***State of West Bengal v. Union of India* (1963, 6-Judge Bench, 5:1)**, West Bengal challenged Parliament's power under the Coal Bearing Areas (Acquisition and Development) Act, 1957 to acquire State-owned coal lands. Writing for the five-judge majority, **Chief Justice B.P. Sinha** held that India is not a true federation in the American sense because States lack separate constitutions, dual citizenship, territorial indestructibility (Article 3), and constituent supremacy over Article 368.

Yet it was **Justice K. Subba Rao's lone dissent** in 1963 that laid the intellectual foundation for modern Indian constitutionalism. Subba Rao insisted that both Union and States derive **coordinate, plenary authority** directly from the same Constitution, and neither may encroach upon the other's allotted sphere absent express constitutional sanction.

During the Emergency aftermath, ***State of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (1977)** echoed the 1963 centralist tone while upholding mass dissolution of nine State Assemblies under Article 356. That centralist orthodoxy was decisively overturned by the 9-Judge Constitution Bench in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994)**.

Drawing directly on Justice Subba Rao's 1963 dissent, *Bommai* elevated **federalism to the Basic Structure** of the Constitution. The Court held that federalism is not a matter of administrative convenience; within the spheres demarcated by the Seventh Schedule, **States are supreme and are not mere appendages or satellites of the Centre**. Subsequent Constitution Benches—from *Kuldip Nayar* (2006) to *State of UP v. Lalta Prasad Vaish* (2024)—have operationalized this coordinate-sovereignty ethos.

### Cooperative, Competitive, and Combative Federalism in 2026

Operational federalism in India rarely inhabits a single pure mode; it simultaneously exhibits three competing dynamics shaped by party-system geometry, fiscal incentives, and institutional friction:

- **Cooperative Federalism:** Vertical and horizontal collaboration to solve shared governance problems that cross State borders. Institutional anchors include the **GST Council (Art 279A)**, **NITI Aayog's Governing Council**, **Zonal Councils**, and joint disaster-response architectures.
- **Competitive Federalism:** Horizontal rivalry among States to attract private capital, improve human-capital outcomes, and climb centrally published performance rankings—such as DPIIT's Business Reforms Action Plan, NITI Aayog's SDG India Index, Export Preparedness Index, and the Aspirational Districts/Blocks Programme. While competition spurs administrative innovation, unchecked rivalry risks a **"race to the bottom"** via unsustainable industrial tax waivers, diluted labour standards, and widening divergence between coastal/southern industrial hubs and landlocked northern States.
- **Combative (Confrontational) Federalism:** Structural collision between a centralizing Union executive and politically divergent State governments. Live 2024–2026 flashpoints include Governor delays on State Bills, withdrawal of **general consent to the CBI** under Section 6 of the DSPE Act, 1946 by over eight States, disputes over central deputation of IAS officers, BSF territorial jurisdiction expansion, unilateral Central CSS branding conditionalities, and State-level autonomy pushes such as Tamil Nadu's **Kurian Joseph High-Level Committee on Union–State Relations (2025–26)** (which submitted Part I in February 2026 before being folded into the reconstituted State Law Commission in late 2026).

## Legislative Relations - Territorial Reach, Seventh Schedule, and Doctrinal Mechanics (Articles 245–255)

Part XI, Chapter I (**Articles 245–255**) governs the legislative division of labour across two orthogonal axes: **territorial reach** (where a law operates) and **subject-matter competence** (what a legislature may regulate).

### Territorial Competence, Extra-Territoriality, and the Nexus Limit (Article 245)

Under **Article 245(1)**, Parliament may enact laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India, while a State Legislature may enact laws only for the whole or any part of that State. Crucially, Parliament's territorial reach faces explicit constitutional exceptions: the President may make regulations modifying or repealing Parliamentary Acts for certain Union Territories (**Article 240**), while Governors hold parallel regulatory filters in **Fifth Schedule** areas and Governors/President in **Sixth Schedule** areas.

**Article 245(2)** shields Parliamentary legislation from invalidation solely on the ground that it has **extra-territorial operation**—enabling Indian statutes (such as the Income Tax Act, BNS extra-territorial clauses, or the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018) to bind Indian citizens, ships, aircraft, and cross-border transactions abroad.

However, extra-territorial competence is **not unbounded**. In ***GVK Industries Ltd. v. Income Tax Officer* (2011, 5-Judge Constitution Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that Article 245(1) read with Article 245(2) does **not** empower Parliament to legislate for foreign territories or extra-territorial events that bear **no real, non-illusory nexus** with India's territory, interests, welfare, or security. Enacting laws completely divorced from India would violate international comity and amount to an abdication of Parliament's constitutional mandate to legislate *"for"* India.

State Legislatures possess **no extra-territorial legislative power** under Article 245(2). Nevertheless, a State law may validly reach persons, property, or transactions located outside its borders if it satisfies the **Doctrine of Territorial Nexus**, crystallized in ***Wallace Brothers & Co. Ltd. v. CIT* (1948)**, ***State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala* (1957)** (upholding Bombay's tax on cross-border prize competitions printed in Bangalore but drawing entries and revenue from Bombay), and ***Tata Iron & Steel Co. v. State of Bihar* (1958)**. The doctrine imposes a strict **two-limb test**:
1. The connection (nexus) between the State and the extra-territorial subject-matter must be **real and not illusory**.
2. The liability sought to be imposed must be **pertinent to that connection**.

### Subject-Matter Allocation, Seventh Schedule Parity, and Residuary Powers (Articles 246, 246A, 248)

**Article 246** establishes a three-tier legislative hierarchy anchored in the **Seventh Schedule**, inherited from the Government of India Act, 1935. Its drafting deploys deliberate *non-obstante* ("Notwithstanding anything...") and *"subject to"* clauses to hardwire **Union federal supremacy** in cases of irreconcilable overlap:

- **Article 246(1) — List I (Union List):** Exclusive Parliamentary jurisdiction; operates *"notwithstanding"* clauses (2) and (3), giving List I supreme precedence.
- **Article 246(2) — List III (Concurrent List):** Shared jurisdiction of Parliament and State Legislatures; operates *"notwithstanding"* clause (3) (State List), but *"subject to"* clause (1) (Union List).
- **Article 246(3) — List II (State List):** Exclusive State jurisdiction in normal times; expressly *"subject to"* clauses (1) and (2).
- **Article 246(4) — Union Territories:** Grants Parliament plenary power to legislate on *any* subject—including State List entries—for any territory of India not included in a State.
- **Article 246A — Simultaneous GST Power (101st CAA, 2016):** Operates *"notwithstanding anything contained in articles 246 and 254"*, creating a sui generis **simultaneous taxing channel** for both Parliament and State Legislatures on intra-State GST while reserving inter-State GST exclusively to Parliament under Art 246A(2).

**Dual-Standard Numeric Parity (Seventh Schedule Entry Counts):** Aspirants must track both the original 1950 baseline, legacy working textbook tallies, and strict effective active counts following deletions under the 7th, 42nd, and 101st Amendments:
- **List I (Union List):** Originally **97** entries; strictly **98** active entries post-101st CAA (Entries 92 and 92C omitted; frequently cited in legacy textbooks as **100** numbered/working subjects). Covers Defence, Foreign Affairs, Atomic Energy, Railways, Banking, Insurance, Inter-State Trade & Commerce (Entry 42), Inter-State Migration and Inter-State Quarantine (Entry 81), and Corporation Tax (Entry 85).
- **List II (State List):** Originally **66** entries; strictly **59** active entries post-101st CAA (following 42nd CAA transfers and 101st CAA deletion of Entries 52 & 55; legacy textbooks often cite **61**). Covers Public Order (Entry 1), Police (Entry 2), Public Health (Entry 6), Intoxicating Liquors (Entry 8), Agriculture (Entry 14), Water subject to List I Entry 56 (Entry 17), Land (Entry 18), and Taxes on Agricultural Income (Entry 46).
- **List III (Concurrent List):** Originally **47** entries; currently **52** active entries. The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** executed the single largest legislative centralization in Indian history by transferring **five subjects** from the State List to the Concurrent List: (1) **Education**, (2) **Forests**, (3) **Weights and Measures**, (4) **Protection of Wild Animals and Birds**, and (5) **Administration of Justice; constitution and organisation of all courts except the Supreme Court and High Courts**.

**Residuary Powers (Article 248 & List I Entry 97):** Deviating from the US and Australian models—where unenumerated powers remain with the constituent States—India followed the **Canadian model** (and departed from the 1935 Act, which vested residue in the Governor-General). **Article 248** confers exclusive power on Parliament to make any law, **including imposing any tax**, with respect to any matter not enumerated in List II or List III. In ***Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon* (1972)**, a 7-Judge Bench established the **"negative test"**: once a court confirms a subject does not fall within List II (or List III), Parliament's competence is automatically complete under Art 248/Entry 97 without hunting for an affirmative List I entry.

### Interpretive Doctrines and the Mechanics of Repugnancy (Article 254)

Because legislative entries are drafted in broad thematic language, overlaps are structurally inevitable. Courts resolve boundary conflicts through a disciplined sequence of interpretive rules—starting with **liberal/plenary construction** (*United Provinces v. Atiqa Begum*, 1941: entries denote wide fields of legislation carrying **ancillary and incidental powers**) and **harmonious construction** (reading apparently clashing entries to give full effect to both before declaring supremacy).

When conflict persists, three core doctrines govern validity:

1. **Doctrine of Pith and Substance:** Derived from Privy Council jurisprudence (*Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce*, 1947) and adopted in ***State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (1951)**. Courts examine the statute as a whole—its object, scope, and effect—to identify its true character (*"pith and substance"*). If the law falls substantially within the enacting legislature's allotted list, **incidental encroachment** onto another list does not invalidate it.
2. **Doctrine of Colourable Legislation:** Anchored in the maxim *"what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly"*. In ***K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa* (1953)**, the Supreme Court clarified a vital Prelims nuance: colourability tests **legislative competence, NOT legislative motive**. If a legislature possesses competence, its *mala fide* intent cannot strike down the statute; conversely, a noble motive cannot save a law disguised to usurp another list.
3. **Doctrine of Repugnancy (Article 254):** Applies **exclusively to collisions within the Concurrent List (List III)** (*M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India*, 1979; *Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar*, 1983). Conflicts between a List I law and a List II law are *not* Article 254 repugnancy cases—they are resolved via **Article 246 supremacy** and Pith and Substance.

In ***Deep Chand v. State of UP* (1959)** and ***M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India* (1979)**, the Supreme Court crystallized three tests for finding repugnancy:
- **Direct irreconcilable conflict:** Obeying one law necessitates disobeying the other.
- **Exhaustive code (Occupied Field):** Parliament intended to lay down a complete, exhaustive statutory code on that List III subject, leaving zero room for State supplementation.
- **Identical subject-matter:** Both statutes occupy the same field (even if traced to *different* entries within List III, as clarified in *Rajiv Sarin* and *Innoventive Industries Ltd.*, 2017).

Once repugnancy is established, **Article 254** operates in three distinct moves:
- **General Rule — Art 254(1):** The Parliamentary law prevails (whether enacted *before or after* the State law), and the State law is void strictly *to the extent of the repugnancy*.
- **Presidential Assent Override — Art 254(2):** A repugnant State law on a List III subject **prevails within that State** if it was reserved for the consideration of the President and received Presidential assent.
- **Parliamentary Counter-Override — Proviso to Art 254(2):** Parliament is **not barred** from subsequently enacting a fresh law on the same matter—including one adding to, amending, varying, or **directly or impliedly repealing** the President-assented State law (*Zaverbhai Amaidas v. State of Bombay*, 1954).

### Five Extraordinary Grounds of Union Legislation on the State List

In five constitutionally specified circumstances, the normal boundary of List II yields to Parliamentary entry, accompanied by **Article 251** (which grants temporary overriding primacy to Parliamentary laws made under Articles 249 and 250 without stripping State Legislatures of concurrent legislative competence). Beyond these five direct avenues, the Centre also exercises indirect legislative veto control over State statutes through **mandatory/discretionary reservation of Bills for Presidential assent** under Articles 200–201 (obligatory if a Bill derogates from High Court powers), prior Presidential sanction under Articles 304(b) and 31A/31C, and reservation of State Money Bills during a Financial Emergency under **Article 360(4)(a)(ii)**. Note also **Article 255**: failure to obtain prior Presidential recommendation or sanction is a procedural defect cured automatically if the Act subsequently receives Presidential assent.

### The Landmark October 2024 Reset - *State of UP v. Lalta Prasad Vaish*

For thirty-five years, a centralizing judicial interpretation stripped States of regulatory and fee-levying power over industrial alcohol. On **October 23, 2024**, a **9-Judge Constitution Bench** in ***State of Uttar Pradesh v. M/s Lalta Prasad Vaish* (`2024 INSC 812`)**, voting **8:1** (Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud writing for eight judges; **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** delivering the lone dissent), decisively overturned that regime.

The constitutional clash pitted **List II Entry 8** (*"Intoxicating liquors, that is to say, the production, manufacture, possession, transport, purchase and sale of intoxicating liquors"*) against **List I Entry 52** (*"Industries, the control of which by the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest"*) read with **Item 26 of the First Schedule and Section 18G of the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 (IDRA)**.

In ***Synthetics and Chemicals Ltd. v. State of UP* (1990 [decided Oct 1989], 7-Judge Bench)**, the Court had narrowed *"intoxicating liquors"* in Entry 8 exclusively to **potable beverage alcohol** fit for human consumption. By treating non-potable **industrial alcohol** (rectified spirit, extra-neutral alcohol, denatured spirit) as an IDRA-controlled industry under Entry 52, *Synthetics* left States unable to regulate or police the primary chemical feedstock routinely diverted into fatal spurious-liquor tragedies.

Overruling *Synthetics and Chemicals*, the 8-judge majority in *Lalta Prasad Vaish* established four foundational federal principles:

- **Mischief-Based Expansion of Entry 8:** *"Intoxicating liquor"* cannot be artificially restricted to potable beverages. Grounded in public-health mischief, Entry 8 covers **all variants of alcohol prone to noxious misuse** or illicit conversion into potable alcohol—expressly encompassing industrial alcohol, rectified spirit, and denatured spirit.
- **Harmonizing Specific State Entries Against General Union Entries:** List I Entry 52 is a *general* entry governing industries, whereas List II Entry 8 is a *specific* industry-cum-regulatory entry. Parliament cannot deploy a general declaration under Entry 52 (Item 26 of IDRA) to swallow an exclusive State field; Item 26 of IDRA must therefore be read down to **exclude the industry of intoxicating liquor** as broadly defined.
- **Restoring List III Entry 33:** The majority rebuked *Synthetics* for effectively effacing **Concurrent List Entry 33(a)** (trade, commerce, production, supply, and distribution of products of Entry 52 industries), reaffirming that mere parliamentary control over an industry under Entry 52 does not automatically extinguish State competence under List III Entry 33 unless Parliament occupies the field via an explicit Section 18G notified order.
- **Justice Nagarathna's Lone Dissent:** Maintaining that *"intoxicating liquor"* carries a settled commercial meaning confined to potable beverages, Justice Nagarathna argued that industrial alcohol is a vital chemical raw material for pharmaceuticals and manufacturing that rightly belongs under uniform Union oversight via Entry 52 and the IDRA.

Paired with the July–August 2024 9-Judge verdict in *MADA v. SAIL* (affirming State taxing power over mineral rights), *Lalta Prasad Vaish* marks a historic **federalist recalibration**—signaling that the Supreme Court will no longer allow elastic Union industrial entries to cannibalize plenary State fields.

## Administrative Relations - Executive Control, Security Friction, and Cooperative Machinery (Articles 256–263)

Part XI, Chapter II (**Articles 256–263**) translates legislative division into day-to-day executive governance. Because India operates a **dual-polity but integrated-administration** model—lacking parallel federal trial courts or federal field police—the Constitution weaves together binding Union compliance directives, mutual delegation tools, shared All-India Services, and multilateral consultative councils.

### Executive Power Alignment and the Concurrent List Rule (Articles 73 & 162)

Executive power generally tracks legislative competence: Union executive authority covers List I (**Article 73(1)(a)**), and State executive authority covers List II (**Article 162**). However, the **Concurrent List** follows a deliberate federal decentralizing rule: under the **Provisos to Articles 73(1) and 162**, the **executive power over List III subjects vests ordinarily in the States**, *unless* the Constitution or a specific Parliamentary statute expressly confers execution upon the Union or its officers.

### Union Directions and the Article 365 Coercive Rod (Articles 256, 257, 365)

To prevent State executives from nullifying Parliamentary statutes through administrative non-cooperation, the Constitution equips New Delhi with enforceable administrative directives:

- **Article 256 (General Obligation):** Every State's executive power must be exercised to ensure **compliance with laws made by Parliament** and existing laws applicable in that State; the Union may issue such directions as appear necessary for that purpose.
- **Article 257 (Non-Impediment & Strategic Assets):** State executive power must not impede or prejudice the exercise of Union executive power. Specifically, the Union may direct States regarding the **construction and maintenance of means of communication** declared of national or military importance (**Art 257(2)**) and measures for the **protection of railways** within the State (**Art 257(3)**). Extra costs incurred by a State complying with Art 257(2)–(3) are reimbursed by the Union—with disputes settled by an **arbitrator appointed by the Chief Justice of India (Art 257(4))**.
- **Specialized Directives Elsewhere:** Welfare schemes for Scheduled Tribes (**Art 339(2)**), mother-tongue primary instruction for linguistic minorities (**Art 350A**), and development of Hindi (**Art 351**).
- **Article 365 (The Nuclear Sanction):** If any State fails to comply with or give effect to *any* direction issued in the exercise of Union executive power under the Constitution, **it shall be lawful for the President to hold that a situation has arisen in which the State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution**—directly unlocking **Article 356 (President's Rule)**. While Dr. Ambedkar defended Art 365 as a logical necessity, the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** cautioned that it must remain a last-resort deterrent preceded by formal warning and consultation.

### Asymmetric Mutual Delegation (Articles 258 & 258A)

To avoid rigid administrative silos, the Constitution permits intergovernmental delegation—though with a striking **unilateral asymmetry** favouring Parliament:

- **Article 258(1) (Executive Entrustment by Union):** The President may, **with the consent of the State Government**, entrust Union executive functions conditionally or unconditionally to that State or its officers.
- **Article 258(2) (Legislative Imposition by Parliament):** A law enacted by Parliament (on a Union subject) may confer powers and impose duties upon a State or its officers **WITHOUT the State's consent**. Under **Art 258(3)**, the Union must compensate the State for extra administrative costs, determined if disputed by a **CJI-appointed arbitrator**.
- **Article 258A (Reverse Entrustment by State — inserted by 7th CAA, 1956):** The Governor of a State may, **with the consent of the Government of India**, entrust State executive functions to the Union or its officers. Critically, a **State Legislature has NO parallel power** to unilaterally impose duties on Union officers without Central consent.

### CAPF Deployments, Internal Security, and Investigative Federalism

Law and order (**Public Order — List II Entry 1; Police — List II Entry 2**) reside exclusively with the States. Yet **Article 355** imposes a constitutional obligation on the Union to protect every State against **external aggression and internal disturbance** and to ensure constitutional governance.

During the Emergency, the **42nd Amendment Act (1976)** inserted both **Article 257A** (empowering unilateral Union armed-force deployment in States) and **List I Entry 2A** (*"Deployment of any armed force of the Union or any other force subject to the control of the Union... in any State in aid of the civil power"*). Crucially, while the **44th Amendment Act (1978) repealed Article 257A**, it **left List I Entry 2A intact**.

Consequently, reading **Article 355** together with **List I Entry 2A**, the Union possesses constitutional authority to deploy Central Armed Police Forces (CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP, SSB) or the Army in aid of civil power **even without formal State consent**. While both the **Sarkaria Commission** (recommending prior consultation as federal etiquette) and the **Punchhi Commission (2010)** (proposing a statutory framework for time-bound localized *suo motu* deployment during grave communal flare-ups) urged guardrails, State consent is a convention, not a constitutional veto.

This unilateral security power contrasts sharply with **central criminal investigation**:
- **CBI General Consent Friction:** Derived from the **Delhi Special Police Establishment (DSPE) Act, 1946**, the CBI requires State Government consent under **Section 6** to exercise jurisdiction inside a State. Starting with West Bengal in November 2018, over eight opposition-ruled States revoked *"general consent"*, forcing case-by-case permissions or Constitutional Court mandates—turning Section 6 into a persistent theatre of combative federalism.
- **BSF Territorial Jurisdiction:** The Union's 2021 notification harmonizing BSF search-and-seizure jurisdiction to **50 km** inside border States (expanding it from 15 km in Punjab, West Bengal, and Assam while narrowing Gujarat's 80 km zone) triggered **Article 131** original suits by Punjab and West Bengal alleging encroachment on List II Entry 2 policing.

### All-India Services - The Dual-Control "Steel Frame" (Article 312)

Defended in the Constituent Assembly by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** as the indispensable *"steel frame"* safeguarding administrative uniformity and national integration, **All-India Services (Article 312)** are recruited centrally by the UPSC and allocated to State cadres, yet serve both the Union and States across their careers. Beyond the constitutionally inherited **IAS** and **IPS**, Parliament created the **Indian Forest Service (IFoS)** in 1966; creating any new All-India Service (such as a proposed All-India Judicial Service under Art 312(3)) requires a **Rajya Sabha resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting**.

AIS officers operate under **dual control**: immediate posting, transfer, and operational supervision belong to the State Government, whereas ultimate disciplinary penalties (dismissal, removal, or compulsory retirement) vest exclusively in the **Central Government** (Article 311 safeguards).

**Live Administrative Friction (Rule 6(1) & 2026 Cadre Policy):** Under **Rule 6(1) of the IAS (Cadre) Rules, 1954**, central deputation requires the mutual concurrence of the State and Central Governments—yet its **Proviso** stipulates that in the event of disagreement, **the matter shall be decided by the Central Government and the State shall give effect to that decision**. Acute shortfalls in States nominating officers against their Central Deputation Reserve (CDR) prompted the Union's controversial 2021–22 draft amendment proposing unilateral central pull-out deadlines, drawing stiff opposition from States citing acute local cadre vacancies. Separately, on **January 23, 2026**, DoPT notified a reformed **Cadre Allocation Policy (effective from CSE-2026)** abolishing the five-zone structure in favour of **four alphabetical groups** spanning 25 cadres on a rotational merit cycle to ensure cross-regional distribution of top rankers.

### Full Faith and Credit Clause (Article 261)

**Article 261(1)** mandates that **full faith and credit** shall be given throughout the territory of India to public acts, records, and judicial proceedings of the Union and of every State. Under **Article 261(3)**, **final judgments or orders delivered by civil courts** in any part of India are capable of execution anywhere within India according to law. A sharp Prelims boundary applies: Article 261(3) covers **civil decrees only**; one State is not constitutionally obliged under Art 261(3) to enforce the *penal/criminal* laws or criminal judgments of another State absent specific parliamentary procedural statutes (such as BNSS extradition/warrant provisions).

### Cooperative Institutional Machinery - ISC, Zonal Councils, NEC, and NITI Aayog

India's non-judicial intergovernmental dialogue rests on three structurally distinct tiers—**constitutional enabling** (ISC), **statutory regional** (Zonal Councils & NEC), and **executive policy** (NITI Aayog).

#### 1. Inter-State Council (Article 263) & the September 2026 Amendment Order

**Article 263** is a **constitutional enabling provision**: it empowers the President (*"If at any time it appears to the President that the public interests would be served..."*) to establish a Council by Presidential Order—making it distinct from self-constituted constitutional bodies like the **Finance Commission (Art 280)** or **GST Council (Art 279A)**. Its constitutional remit spans three duties:
- **(a)** Inquiring into and advising upon disputes which may have arisen between States;
- **(b)** Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all States, or the Union and States, have a common interest;
- **(c)** Making recommendations for the better coordination of policy and action on such subjects.

While the President earlier invoked Art 263 for sectoral bodies (Central Council of Health, 1952; Central Council of Local Self-Government, 1954), a **permanent Inter-State Council** was established only on **May 28, 1990** via Presidential Order following the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)** recommendation. Tellingly, the 1990 Order initially conferred only clauses **(b) and (c)** (omitting clause (a) dispute-inquiry), preserving the Council as an **advisory, consensus-driven forum** complementing the Supreme Court's binding legal jurisdiction under **Article 131** (unlike Art 131, the ISC can entertain both legal and non-legal political disputes, though its advice is non-binding).

**Composition & 2024–2026 Developments:** Chaired by the **Prime Minister**, the ISC comprises Chief Ministers of all States, CMs of UTs with Legislatures, Administrators of UTs without Legislatures, six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the PM (plus permanent Union Minister invitees), with a **Standing Committee** chaired by the **Union Home Minister**. Reconstituted in May 2022 and November 2024, the President issued the **Inter-State Council (Amendment) Order, 2026** (notified by MHA on **September 9, 2026**; Gazetted **September 18, 2026**) under Article 263.

The **2026 Amendment Order** substituted the Proviso to Clause 2 of the 1990 Order to explicitly enumerate **four contingencies** in which a Governor or Lieutenant Governor shall be invited to ISC meetings:
1. A State under a Proclamation under **Article 356** (Governor);
2. NCT of Delhi under an Order under **Article 239AB** (LG);
3. UT of Puducherry under an Order under **Section 51 of the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963** (LG);
4. UT of Jammu and Kashmir under an Order under **Section 73 of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019** (LG).

**Structural Deficit:** Despite the 1990 Order contemplating meetings **at least thrice a year** and the September 2026 procedural update, the full Inter-State Council **has not convened since its 11th meeting in July 2016**—a decade-long institutional dormancy that leaves India's premier constitutional Centre–State forum underutilized precisely when federal disputes have intensified.

#### 2. Statutory Zonal Councils and the North Eastern Council (NEC)

Established as **statutory advisory bodies** under **Part III (Section 15) of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956** (conceived by Jawaharlal Nehru to counter linguistic-reorganization parochialism), India has **five Zonal Councils**:
- **Northern:** Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Rajasthan, J&K, Ladakh, NCT of Delhi, Chandigarh.
- **Central:** Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand.
- **Eastern:** Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal.
- **Western:** Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu.
- **Southern:** Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Puducherry *(with Andaman & Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep as special invitees)*.

The **Union Home Minister** serves as the **common ex-officio Chairman** of all five Zonal Councils, while Chief Ministers of constituent States serve as **Vice-Chairman by annual rotation**. Since 2011, the **Inter-State Council Secretariat** services both the ISC and the five Zonal Councils—and unlike the dormant ISC, Zonal Councils and their Standing Committees have met actively, resolving hundreds of granular inter-state issues (police coordination, border infrastructure, DBT/banking penetration, and fast-track POCSO courts).

Distinct from the 1956 Act, the **North Eastern Council (NEC)** is a **statutory regional planning and advisory body** constituted under the **North Eastern Council Act, 1971** (amended in **2002** to induct **Sikkim** as the eighth constituent State alongside the Seven Sisters, operating under the Ministry of DoNER). Crucially—and directly tested in **UPSC Prelims 2024**—under **Section 3 of the amended NEC Act**, its statutory *members* are the **Governors** of the eight States, the **Chief Ministers** of the eight States, and **three members nominated by the President**. Following the **June 2018 Union Cabinet repositioning**, the **Union Home Minister** serves as *ex-officio Chairman* and the **Minister of DoNER** as *Vice-Chairman*, enabling the NEC to address security, narcotics, and boundary issues alongside regional development (as demonstrated at the **73rd NEC Plenary in Shillong on June 4, 2026**).

#### 3. NITI Aayog (Executive Resolution, 2015)

Created by a **Union Cabinet resolution on January 1, 2015** (neither constitutional nor statutory), **NITI Aayog** replaced the centralized Planning Commission to pivot from top-down Plan-fund allocation toward cooperative policy design and competitive benchmarking. Its **Governing Council**—chaired by the Prime Minister and uniting all State/UT Chief Ministers and UT LGs/Administrators—convened its **11th Governing Council Meeting on June 11, 2026** around the theme *"Inclusive Human Development for Viksit Bharat@2047"*, marking the **first time Chief Ministers of all 28 States participated** simultaneously. Backed by annual **National Conferences of Chief Secretaries** (5th edition held December 2025), NITI Aayog fosters bureaucratic convergence—yet critics note that its lack of statutory standing and independent fiscal-grant authority limits its capacity to mediate hard Centre–State constitutional deadlocks.

## Inter-State River Water Disputes - Constitutional Ouster vs. Judicial Intervention (Article 262)

Because most major Indian peninsular and Himalayan rivers traverse multiple State boundaries, water allocation represents India's most emotionally combustible inter-state fault line.

### The Constitutional Geometry (Article 262, List II Entry 17, List I Entry 56)

The Seventh Schedule splits water governance across two asymmetric entries:
- **List II Entry 17 (State List):** *"Water, that is to say, water supplies, irrigation and canals, drainage and embankments, water storage and water power"* — expressly made **"subject to the provisions of Entry 56 of List I"**.
- **List I Entry 56 (Union List):** *"Regulation and development of inter-State rivers and river valleys to the extent to which such regulation and development under the control of the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest."* Parliament enacted the **River Boards Act, 1956** under Entry 56 to create advisory basin boards, yet **not a single River Board has ever been constituted** under it—leaving basin-level integrated planning structurally hollowed out.

To prevent inter-state water feuds from degenerating into adversarial zero-sum civil litigation, **Article 262** crafts a **unique constitutional exception**:
- **Article 262(1):** Parliament may *by law* provide for the adjudication of any dispute or complaint regarding the **use, distribution, or control** of the waters of any inter-state river or river valley.
- **Article 262(2):** *Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution*, Parliament may by law provide that **neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction** in respect of any such dispute or complaint.

Notice the atomic constitutional distinction: **Article 262(2) is the sole provision empowering Parliament to completely oust the Supreme Court's exclusive federal Original Jurisdiction under Article 131** (which itself carries an explicit Proviso deferring to Parliament's Art 262 exclusion).

### The ISRWD Act, 1956 and the Article 136 Judicial Bypass

Exercising Article 262, Parliament enacted the **Inter-State River Water Disputes (ISRWD) Act, 1956** (significantly amended in **2002** on Sarkaria Commission lines). Upon a State's complaint, if negotiations fail, the Central Government must constitute a **Water Disputes Tribunal** within **one year**. Following the 2002 Amendment, the Tribunal must deliver its decision within **three years** (extendable by two years, plus further extensions under Section 5(3) for references), and once published in the Official Gazette, **Section 6(2)** clothes the award with **"the same force as an order or decree of the Supreme Court"**. **Section 6A** empowers the Centre to frame statutory schemes and establish implementation authorities (such as the Cauvery Water Management Authority), while **Section 11** operationalizes Art 262(2) by barring all courts—including the Supreme Court—from entertaining any water dispute.

In practice, however, the statutory ouster under Section 11 has proved porous on **two decisive judicial routes**:

1. **Appellate Bypass via Article 136 (SLP):** In ***State of Karnataka v. State of Tamil Nadu* (December 9, 2016 maintainability ruling, culminating in the February 16, 2018 final Cauvery judgment)**, the Union Attorney General argued that Art 262(2), Section 11, and Section 6(2)'s "SC decree" deeming fiction barred appeals against the 2007 Cauvery Tribunal award. Rejecting the Union's objection, a 3-Judge Bench held that **Article 262(2) and Section 11 bar only *original adjudication* of a water dispute**; a statutory legal fiction under Section 6(2) cannot extinguish the Supreme Court's plenary constitutional appellate power under **Article 136** to scrutinize a Tribunal's final award for legal or constitutional error.
2. **Non-Water-Dispute Boundary under Section 2(c) (Article 131 Suits):** Where a State challenges *implementation failures*, *statutory interpretation*, or *Union inaction* rather than water allocation per se—such as Punjab's refusal to construct the **Sutlej–Yamuna Link (SYL) Canal** or Tamil Nadu's **Original Suit (O.S. No. 1 of 2018)** compelling the Centre to constitute a tribunal on the Pennaiyar river—the Supreme Court entertains suits directly under **Article 131**, holding them outside Section 2(c)'s narrow definition of a "water dispute".

### Live River Basin Flashpoints and the 2024–2026 Reform Impasse

India's ad-hoc tribunal architecture has turned temporary adjudication into multi-decade institutional purgatory—vividly underscored by 2025–2026 developments:

- **Cauvery Basin (Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Puducherry):** In its landmark **February 2018 verdict**, the Supreme Court declared inter-state river water a **national asset** over which no State enjoys proprietary ownership (invoking the **Equitable Apportionment Doctrine** over rigid colonial Harmon/prior-appropriation claims). Accounting for groundwater availability in the Cauvery delta, the Court awarded Karnataka an extra 14.75 TMC (final 50%-dependability shares out of 740 TMC: **TN 404.25 TMC, Karnataka 284.75 TMC, Kerala 30 TMC, Puducherry 7 TMC**, plus 14 TMC environmental/sea outflow), mandated **pro-rata distress sharing** measured at **Biligundlu**, and directed creation of the **Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA)** and **Cauvery Water Regulation Committee (CWRC)** under Section 6A. Demonstrating newfound institutional deference to expert basin bodies, the Supreme Court in **October 2026** rejected Tamil Nadu's challenge to CWC's permission for Karnataka's Mekedatu DPR preparation, and on **November 20, 2026** dismissed Tamil Nadu's applications seeking ~11.72 TMC in make-up releases from Karnataka, refusing to second-guess CWMA's technical assessments.
- **Krishna (KWDT-II) & Ravi–Beas Tribunals (Perpetual Extensions):** Constituted in **April 2004**, KWDT-II's 2010/2013 award remains un-gazetted due to a 2011 SC stay and complex Additional Terms of Reference reallocating water between **Telangana and Andhra Pradesh** post-2014 bifurcation. Similarly, the **Ravi and Beas Waters Tribunal**, constituted in **April 1986** (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan), remains unresolved four decades later. On **June 15, 2026**, the Centre issued Gazette notifications granting **yet another one-year extension** to both tribunals.
- **Mahanadi Water Disputes Tribunal (Odisha–Chhattisgarh):** Constituted in **March 2018** over upstream barrages in Chhattisgarh, the tribunal received its **third extension in April 2026 until January 2027** following chairperson vacancy delays, even as the Jal Shakti Ministry and CWC broker parallel Chief Minister-level mediation.
- **Pennaiyar River Dispute (Tamil Nadu–Karnataka):** On **February 2, 2026**, after repeated ministerial negotiation rounds collapsed over Karnataka's upstream check dams on the Pennaiyar and Markandeya tributary, the Supreme Court disposed of Tamil Nadu's Article 131 suit by ordering the Centre to formally notify the **Pennaiyar Water Disputes Tribunal** within one month.
- **Mahadayi (Goa, Karnataka, Maharashtra):** Post-2018 award friction continues over Karnataka's Kalasa–Banduri drinking-water diversion and ecological clearance litigation.

**Structural Bottlenecks & Lapsed Statutory Reform:** Five pathologies cripple the 1956 regime: (i) **inordinate executive delay** in constituting tribunals and gazetting awards; (ii) **zero statutory data transparency** (States contest hydrological baselines due to the absence of an independent national data bank); (iii) **open-ended Section 5(3) extensions** turning ad-hoc tribunals into permanent sinecures; (iv) **adversarial winner-take-all framing** ignoring climate-induced basin variability and ecological flows; and (v) **endless Art 136 litigation**.

To cure these flaws, Parliament introduced the **Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Bill, 2019**, proposing a mandatory **Disputes Resolution Committee (DRC)** for pre-adjudication negotiation (1 year + 6 months), replacing ad-hoc bodies with a **single permanent Tribunal** with multiple benches, strict **2+1 year adjudication timelines**, **automatic Gazette notification** upon decision, and a centralized data collection agency. Although passed by the Lok Sabha on July 31, 2019, the Bill stalled in the Rajya Sabha and **lapsed upon the dissolution of the 17th Lok Sabha in June 2024**—leaving India's water-federalism overhaul pending legislative re-introduction as of November 2026.

## Inter-State Trade, Commerce and Intercourse - Economic Federalism (Part XIII, Articles 301–307)

Political union collapses rapidly if constituent States erect protectionist tariff walls, discriminatory entry taxes, or export embargoes against one another. Modeled loosely on **Section 92 of the Australian Constitution**, **Part XIII (Articles 301–307)** was drafted to transform fragmented British provinces and 560+ princely states into a **single integrated national common market**.

### Article 301 vs. Article 19(1)(g) - The Structural Architecture

**Article 301** declares: *"Subject to the other provisions of this Part, trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free."* Two surgical nuances define its reach:
- **Both Inter-State and Intra-State Scope:** Unlike the US Commerce Clause (which reaches interstate commerce), the phrase *"throughout the territory of India"* protects **both inter-state AND intra-state** trade and movement (*Atiabari Tea Co.*, 1961).
- **Article 301 vs. Article 19(1)(g):** Article 19(1)(g) is an individual **Fundamental Right** available exclusively to **citizens** (enforceable via Article 32) protecting the freedom to practice any trade or occupation. Article 301 is a **structural constitutional right** (not a Fundamental Right) protecting the *free flow and movement of trade itself*; it can be invoked by **non-citizens and corporate entities**, and is enforced via **Article 226** or civil suits (not directly under Art 32 unless coupled with a Part III violation).

### Graded Constitutional Restrictions (Articles 302–305 & 307)

Freedom under Article 301 is expressly *"subject to"* carefully calibrated exceptions that again reflect India's asymmetric federal bias:

- **Article 302 (Parliamentary Restrictions):** Parliament may by law impose restrictions on trade, commerce, or intercourse between States or within any part of India as may be required in the **"public interest"**. Notice the deliberate textual omission: unlike Article 304(b) for States, Article 302 does **not** qualify Parliament's restrictions with the word **"reasonable"** (giving Parliament wider judicial deference).
- **Article 303(1) & (2) (Non-Discrimination & the Scarcity Override):** Clause (1) forbids *both* Parliament and State Legislatures from enacting laws giving **preference to one State over another** or **discriminating between States**. Clause (2) carves out a crucial asymmetry: **Parliament ALONE** (never a State Legislature!) may override Clause (1) and enact preferential/discriminatory measures to deal with a **situation arising from scarcity of goods** in any part of India.
- **Article 304(a) & (b) (State Legislative Exceptions):** Notwithstanding Articles 301 or 303, a State Legislature may by law:
  - **Clause (a) — Non-Discriminatory Taxation:** Impose on goods imported from other States or UTs **any tax to which similar goods manufactured or produced in that State are subject**, so as **not to discriminate** between imported and local goods.
  - **Clause (b) — Reasonable Restrictions:** Impose such **"reasonable restrictions"** on freedom of trade within or with that State as may be required in the **"public interest"**—strictly subject to the **Proviso requiring previous sanction of the President** before introducing the Bill or amendment in the State Legislature (a requirement curable by subsequent Presidential assent under Article 255).
- **Article 305 (Existing Laws & State Monopolies):** Immunizes pre-Constitution *"existing laws"* and (following the **4th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1955**) completely shields laws creating **State monopolies** in trade or business from Article 301/303 challenge.
- **Article 307 (Statutory Authority):** Empowers Parliament by law to appoint an appropriate authority (envisioned akin to the US Interstate Commerce Commission) to execute Articles 301–304. Parliament has **never exercised Article 307** to date.

### Jurisprudential Revolution - From *Atiabari* to the 9-Judge *Jindal Stainless* Verdict (2016)

For fifty-five years, Indian fiscal federalism was tangled in litigation over whether State entry taxes and motor-vehicle taxes violated Article 301:

1. ***Atiabari Tea Co. Ltd. v. State of Assam* (1961, 5-Judge Bench):** Striking down an Assam tax on tea carried by road and inland waterways, the Court held that **taxation falls within Article 301** whenever a tax *directly and immediately* impedes the movement or transport of goods, requiring the State to jump through Article 304(b)'s Presidential-sanction hoop.
2. ***Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) Ltd. v. State of Rajasthan* (1962, 7-Judge Bench):** Recognizing that *Atiabari* crippled routine State revenue collection, a larger bench invented the **"compensatory tax" doctrine**. Regulatory measures or taxes levied as a *quid pro quo* to fund trading infrastructure (roads, bridges, lighting) were deemed to *facilitate* rather than restrict trade—placing them outside Article 301. (Later narrowed into a rigid proportionality/quantification test in *Jindal Stainless (2)*, 2006, unleashing massive entry-tax litigation across States.)
3. ***Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana* (November 11, 2016, 9-Judge Constitution Bench, 7:2):** Chief Justice T.S. Thakur's 7-judge majority (**Justices D.Y. Chandrachud and Ashok Bhushan dissenting**) dismantled five decades of judicial improvisation by **expressly overruling *Atiabari*, *Automobile Transport*, and *Jindal (2006)*** on taxation and compensatory levies. Its five binding propositions now govern Part XIII:
   - **Taxes *Simpliciter* Are Outside Article 301:** The word *"free"* in Article 301 does **NOT** mean *"free from taxation"*. Taxation is a sovereign attribute of every federal unit under Article 265 and the Seventh Schedule; a **non-discriminatory tax** never infringes Article 301.
   - **Compensatory Tax Theory Abolished:** The *"compensatory tax"* doctrine lacked any textual or juristic foundation in the Constitution and was discarded completely.
   - **Discrimination Alone Is Prohibited (Art 304(a)):** Only **discriminatory taxes** that handicap out-of-state goods relative to locally produced equivalents violate Part XIII.
   - **Art 304(a) and 304(b) Are Disjunctive:** Clauses (a) and (b) operate independently—a discriminatory tax violating **Article 304(a)** **cannot be salvaged** even by securing prior Presidential sanction under **Article 304(b)**.
   - **Entry Tax & Regional Incentives Validated:** A State entry tax on goods entering a local area is constitutionally permissible even if similar goods are not manufactured inside the taxing State, and targeted tax exemptions to develop **economically backward areas** do not breach Article 304(a) unless designed as hostile protectionism.

While the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016)** subsequently subsumed State entry taxes, octroi, and CST into the unified **GST** regime (detailed in Chapter 14), *Jindal Stainless* remains active binding law governing excluded commodities (petroleum, alcoholic liquor for human consumption), motor-vehicle taxation, and State fiscal autonomy under Part XIII.

The Union Home Minister acts as *ex-officio Chairman* (and DoNER Minister as Vice-Chairman) via the **2018 Union Cabinet repositioning**.
> - **Article 249 Majority & Survival Split:** Requires **Rajya Sabha** (never Lok Sabha) resolution by **$\ge 2/3$ of members present and voting** (NOT $2/3$ of total membership!). Resolution lasts **1 year** (renewable); law survives **6 months** after resolution lapses. Post-101st CAA, both **Art 249 and Art 250** cover **GST under Art 246A** too!

Conversely, laws enacted by Parliament during President's Rule (**Art 357(2)**) **do NOT lapse** after President's Rule ends—they remain active until altered or repealed by the restored State Legislature.
> - **Article 258(2) Consentless Delegation:** The President needs **State consent** to entrust Union executive functions (**Art 258(1)**), and a Governor needs **Union consent** under **Art 258A**—but **Parliament by law** can unilaterally impose duties on State officers **without State consent (Art 258(2))**!
> - **Entry 2A vs. Repealed Art 257A:** The **44th CAA (1978)** repealed the 42nd CAA's **Article 257A**, *but retained* **List I Entry 2A**—preserving the Union's legal power (with Art 355) to deploy CAPFs/Army in aid of civil power without mandatory State consent.
> - **Seventh Schedule Favorite Prelims Entries (UPSC Prelims 2024):** **Inter-State Trade & Commerce (Entry 42)**, **Inter-State Migration & Inter-State Quarantine (Entry 81)**, and **Corporation Tax (Entry 85)** are all **Union List**; **Taxes on Agricultural Income (Entry 46)** and **Industrial Alcohol ("intoxicating liquors", Entry 8 post-*Lalta Prasad Vaish* 2024)** are **State List**.
> - **Article 262(2) Ouster vs. Article 136 SLP:** Section 11 of the ISRWD Act, 1956 bars **original** suits under Article 131 over water disputes, **NOT** appeals by Special Leave under **Article 136** against a Tribunal's final award (*State of Karnataka v. State of TN*, 2016/2018).
> - **Part XIII Asymmetries:** Only **Parliament** (never a State) may discriminate between States to tackle **scarcity of goods (Art 303(2))**. Post-***Jindal Stainless* (2016)**, non-discriminatory taxes do **not** breach Art 301, the **compensatory tax test is dead**, and a discriminatory tax breaching **Art 304(a)** CANNOT be saved by Presidential sanction under **Art 304(b)**.
> - **Article 368(2) Proviso Entrenchment (UPSC Prelims 2025):** Amending **any List in the Seventh Schedule** or the **extent of executive power of the Union/States (Art 73/162)** mandates **Special Majority + ratification by $\ge 50\%$ of State Legislatures**.

- **Institutional Origin Swaps:** UPSC systematically mixes constitutional enabling bodies (**ISC — Art 263**), statutory regional bodies (**Zonal Councils — SRA 1956; NEC — NEC Act 1971**), and executive bodies (**NITI Aayog, NSC**) inside "How many of the above?" triplets.
