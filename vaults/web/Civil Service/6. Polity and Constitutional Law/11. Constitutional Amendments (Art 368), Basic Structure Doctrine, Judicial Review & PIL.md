# Chapter 11 - Constitutional Amendments (Art 368), Basic Structure Doctrine, Judicial Review & PIL

Every enduring written constitution must resolve an architectural tension: if it is too rigid, social change fractures the republic; if it is too flexible, transient legislative majorities dismantle constitutionalism itself. India's Constituent Assembly engineered a calibrated synthesis—borrowing the amending spirit from South Africa while avoiding both Britain's unfettered parliamentary supremacy and the United States' near-impenetrable supermajority locks.

Over seven decades, that text collided with political ambition, giving birth to India's most consequential contribution to global constitutional jurisprudence: the **Basic Structure Doctrine**. Paired with **Judicial Review** and **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**, this framework governs where Parliament's constituent authority ends, where judicial guardianship begins, and when proactive courts slip from constitutional defence into institutional overreach.

---

## 1. Constitutional Amendment Mechanics (Part XX, Article 368)

**Part XX** contains a single article—**Article 368** (*Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor*)—which empowers Parliament, acting in exercise of its **"constituent power"** (Article 368(1), inserted by the **24th CAA, 1971**), to amend any provision of the Constitution by way of **addition, variation, or repeal**. Constitutional scholar K.C. Wheare commended this design for striking a rare equilibrium between rigidity and flexibility: routine federal-administrative adjustments require ordinary legislation, core rights require parliamentary supermajorities, and federal compact clauses demand state co-sovereignty.

### The Three Tiers of Constitutional Amendment

Strictly speaking, **Article 368** provides only **two** formal amendment procedures—Special Majority alone, and Special Majority plus State ratification. Yet the Constitution itself embeds a **third, informal tier** outside Article 368 via express deeming clauses (**Articles 4(2), 169(3), 239A(2), 239AA(7)(b), 312(4), Paragraph 7(2) of the Fifth Schedule, and Paragraph 21(2) of the Sixth Schedule**), which stipulate that specified alterations made by ordinary parliamentary law *"shall not be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of Article 368."*

1. **Tier 1 — Simple Majority of Parliament (Outside Article 368):** Passed like ordinary legislation (>50% of members present and voting). Bare-text precision separates two distinct mechanisms inside this tier:
   - **Express Non-Article 368 Deeming Clauses** (*"No such law... shall be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368"* — directly altering or supplementing constitutional text/Schedules): admission/establishment or internal reorganization of States (**Articles 2, 3, 4(2)**), creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils (**Article 169(3)**, following a State Assembly special-majority resolution), Union Territories' legislatures (**Articles 239A(2) and 239AA(7)(b)**), creation of an All-India Service (**Article 312(4)**), and administration of **Fifth Schedule (Para 7(2))** and **Sixth Schedule (Para 21(2))** areas.
   - **Default / Transitional Legislative Clauses** (*"until Parliament by law otherwise provides"* or *"unless Parliament by law prescribes a larger number"* — where the bare constitutional text itself is **never amended**): **Second Schedule** emoluments, parliamentary quorum (**Article 100(3)**), MPs' salaries and privileges, official English usage (**Article 343(3)**), **Supreme Court puisne judge strength (Article 124(1)**—whose bare text still reads *"not more than seven other Judges"*, while the expanded 37+1 strength operates via the 1956/August 2026 statutory Act), citizenship acquisition/termination (**Article 11**), and delimitation of constituencies.
2. **Tier 2 — Special Majority of Parliament (Article 368(2)):** Requires **both** (i) a **majority of the total membership** of each House (>50% of total sanctioned strength, irrespective of vacancies or absentees—i.e., ≥272 in a 543-member Lok Sabha), **AND** (ii) a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting**. Applies to Part III (Fundamental Rights), Part IV (DPSPs), and every constitutional provision not assigned to Tier 1 or Tier 3.
   - **Live 2026 Procedural Demonstration:** On **April 17, 2026**, the **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026**—introduced a day earlier to unlock 2011-Census delimitation, expand Lok Sabha's constitutional ceiling from 550 to 850 seats, and fast-track the 106th CAA women's quota—was **defeated on the Lok Sabha floor**. With 528 MPs voting, it cleared the absolute-majority hurdle (298 Ayes vs. 230 Noes) yet fell 54 votes short of the mandatory two-thirds threshold (**352 votes**), forcing withdrawal of its companion Delimitation and UT Laws Bills. This defeat illustrated how Article 368(2)'s supermajority transforms regional federal consensus into a functional counter-majoritarian lock.
3. **Tier 3 — Special Majority + Ratification by Not Less Than Half of State Legislatures (Proviso to Article 368(2)):** Amendments altering federal structural clauses require, in addition to Article 368(2) passage in both Houses, ratification by **resolutions passed by Simple Majority** in the legislatures of at least **50% of the States** (currently ≥14 of 28 States; Union Territory assemblies do **not** count as States) **before** presentation to the President for assent. No time limit governs State ratification, nor can State Legislatures propose amendments to the Bill. Under bare-text Clauses **(a) to (e)** of the Proviso, mandatory State ratification covers:
   - **(a)** **Articles 54 & 55** (Presidential election & manner), **Article 73** (extent of Union executive power), **Article 162** (extent of State executive power), **Article 241** (High Courts for UTs), and **Article 279A (GST Council—added by the 101st CAA, 2016)**;
   - **(b)** **Chapter IV of Part V** (Supreme Court), **Chapter V of Part VI** (High Courts), and **Chapter I of Part XI** (Union–State Legislative Relations);
   - **(c)** Any of the three Lists in the **Seventh Schedule**;
   - **(d)** Representation of States in Parliament (**Fourth Schedule**); and
   - **(e)** The provisions of **Article 368 itself**.

### Judicial Enforcement of State Ratification — *Rajendra N. Shah* (2021)

Parliament cannot evade the Proviso to Article 368(2) through textual indirection. In ***Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah* (July 20, 2021 — 3-Judge Bench, 2:1)**, the Supreme Court examined the **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011**, which had inserted **Part IX-B** (Articles 243ZH–243ZT) prescribing uniform constitutional rules for co-operative societies without seeking 50% State ratification. Because *"co-operative societies"* sits exclusively in **List II, Entry 32 (State List)**, curtailing State legislative latitude effectively altered the Seventh Schedule's distribution of powers under Proviso clause (c).

The Court unanimously held that bypassing State ratification rendered Part IX-B unconstitutional regarding **State Co-operative Societies**. Applying the **Doctrine of Severability**, the 2:1 majority (JJ. R.F. Nariman and B.R. Gavai; J. K.M. Joseph dissenting on severability) struck down Part IX-B for intra-State cooperatives while preserving its validity exclusively for **Multi-State Co-operative Societies** (List I, Entry 44) and Union Territories.

### Mandatory Procedural Locks Under Article 368

Four rigid procedural rules distinguish an Article 368 Constitution Amendment Bill from Ordinary and Money Bills:

- **Initiation Locus & Zero Presidential Gatekeeping:** A Constitution Amendment Bill may be introduced **only in either House of Parliament**—never in a State Legislature (contrast with Article 169). It may be piloted by a **Minister OR a Private Member**, and requires **NO prior recommendation or permission of the President**.
- **Separate Passage & Complete Bar on Joint Sittings:** Both Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha hold **co-equal constituent power**. Each House must independently pass the Bill by Special Majority; **Article 108 Joint Sitting is completely unavailable** to break a deadlock, preventing Lok Sabha's larger membership from overriding the Council of States.
- **Mandatory Presidential Assent (24th CAA, 1971):** Before 1971, Article 368(2) merely stated the Bill shall be presented to the President *"for his assent."* The **24th CAA, 1971** substituted the mandatory command ***"who shall give his assent to the Bill"***—stripping the President of absolute, suspensive, and pocket vetoes over Constitution Amendment Bills.
- **Constituent Power vs. Ordinary "Law" & Dead-Letter Clauses:** The 24th CAA also inserted **Article 13(4)** and **Article 368(3)** clarifying that Article 13 does not apply to Article 368 amendments. Later, the **42nd CAA, 1976** inserted **Article 368(4)** (barring courts from questioning any constitutional amendment on any ground) and **Article 368(5)** (declaring *"no limitation whatever"* on Parliament's constituent power); both clauses were **struck down as unconstitutional in *Minerva Mills* (1980)** and remain void despite appearing in bare-text editions.

**Live Amendment Pipeline (as of November 21, 2026):** The **106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023** (*Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*) remains the latest enacted amendment. Two major Bills—the **Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024** (*One Nation, One Election*, inserting Article 82A) and the **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** (mandating automatic removal of a PM, CM, or Minister detained for 30 consecutive days on charges carrying 5+ years imprisonment, amending Articles 75, 164, and 239AA)—remain under **Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC)** scrutiny, while the **131st Amendment Bill, 2026** failed in Lok Sabha on April 17, 2026.

## 2. Genealogy & Consolidation of the Basic Structure Doctrine

The **Basic Structure Doctrine** rests on a foundational distinction in constitutional theory: **original constituent power** belongs solely to *"We, the People"* acting through the Constituent Assembly, whereas Parliament's power under Article 368 is a **derivative constituent power** created *by* the Constitution. To "amend" means to improve or modify an existing edifice, never to demolish its load-bearing pillars and substitute an alien identity. Influenced in part by German jurist **Dietrich Conrad's** 1965 lectures in India highlighting implied constitutional limitations (paralleling Article 79(3)'s Eternity Clause in Germany's Basic Law), the Supreme Court forged this doctrine across a 75-year dialectic with Parliament.

Notably, the Constitution **never mentions or defines the phrase "Basic Structure"** anywhere in its text. Nor has the Supreme Court ever frozen an exhaustive closed catalogue of basic features; instead, it identifies core structural pillars inductively, case by case.

### Doctrinal Evolution: From Absolute Plenary Power to Structural Supremacy

1. ***Sankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India* (1951 — 5-Judge Bench):** Challenging the **1st CAA, 1951** (which inserted Articles 31A, 31B, and the Ninth Schedule to shield zamindari abolition), petitioners argued that a constitutional amendment abridging Fundamental Rights was void "law" under **Article 13(2)**. The Court unanimously rejected the plea, sharply bifurcating ordinary legislative power (subject to Art 13) from constituent power under Article 368—handing Parliament unfettered authority to amend Fundamental Rights.
2. ***Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (1965 — 5-Judge Bench, 3:2):** Challenging the **17th CAA, 1964**, the majority reaffirmed *Sankari Prasad*. Yet two landmark dissents fractured the consensus: Justice **M. Hidayatullah** questioned making Fundamental Rights a "plaything of a special majority," while Justice **J.R. Mudholkar** first coined the germ of **"basic features,"** asking whether changing the Constitution's basic identity fell within the verb "amend."
3. ***I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967 — 11-Judge Bench, 6:5):** In a seismic reversal led by CJI K. Subba Rao, the Court overruled *Sankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*. Holding that Article 368 merely outlined *procedure* rather than conferring substantive constituent power, the majority ruled that a constitutional amendment **IS "law" within Article 13(2)**—meaning **Parliament could NOT abridge or take away any Fundamental Right** (suggesting only a fresh Constituent Assembly could do so). To avoid chaos, the Court imported the US **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling**, leaving the 1st, 4th, and 17th CAAs intact while barring future Part III abridgments.
4. **Parliamentary Counter-Strike (24th & 25th CAAs, 1971):** After *R.C. Cooper* (Bank Nationalization, 1970) and *Madhavrao Scindia* (Privy Purses, 1970), Parliament enacted the **24th CAA, 1971** (inserting Art 13(4) and Art 368(1)/(3) to assert absolute constituent power and mandatory Presidential assent) and the **25th CAA, 1971** (inserting **Article 31C**, shielding laws implementing Articles 39(b)/(c) from Articles 14, 19, and 31—plus a second clause barring judicial review of any legislative declaration to that effect).
5. ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (April 24, 1973 — 13-Judge Bench, 7:6):** Before the largest Bench ever convened in Indian judicial history, the Court brokered a grand constitutional compromise via **Justice H.R. Khanna's decisive swing opinion**:
   - It **overruled *Golaknath***, upholding the **24th CAA** and confirming Parliament can amend *any* part of the Constitution—including Fundamental Rights and the Preamble.
   - It upheld the first limb of **Article 31C** (25th CAA), **but struck down its second limb** barring judicial review of legislative declarations.
   - Above all, by a **7:6 majority**, it established that Article 368's amending power is subject to an **implied substantive limitation**: Parliament cannot alter, damage, or destroy the **"Basic Structure" (or essential features)** of the Constitution.
6. ***Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975 — 5-Judge Bench):** The first operational invocation of Basic Structure to invalidate a constitutional amendment. During the Emergency, Parliament passed the **39th CAA, 1975**, inserting **Article 329A(4)** to retrospectively validate Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's disputed 1971 Rae Bareli election and oust judicial scrutiny. While upholding her election on ordinary statutory amendment grounds, the Bench struck down Article 329A(4) for violating **Democracy, Free and Fair Elections, Rule of Law, Separation of Powers, and Judicial Review**.
7. ***Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (1980 — 5-Judge Bench, 4:1):** Parliament had struck back via the **42nd CAA, 1976**, amending Article 31C to immunize laws furthering *all* DPSPs (Section 4) and adding **Articles 368(4) and 368(5)** to extinguish judicial review over amendments (Section 55). Striking down both sections, CJI Y.V. Chandrachud authored the doctrinal linchpin: ***"Parliament, under Article 368, has a limited amending power, and it cannot use that limited power to enlarge itself into an absolute power; a limited amending power is itself a basic feature of the Constitution."*** The Bench also locked in **Judicial Review** and the **Harmony and Balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs** ("two wheels of a chariot") as Basic Structure.
8. ***Waman Rao v. Union of India* (1981 — 5-Judge Bench):** Solved the retroactivity puzzle by establishing **April 24, 1973** (the exact date of *Kesavananda Bharati*) as the bright constitutional **watershed**. All constitutional amendments and Ninth Schedule enactments inserted *before* April 24, 1973 remained valid and immune; all amendments and Ninth Schedule additions enacted *on or after* April 24, 1973 became strictly testable against Basic Structure.
9. ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994 — 9-Judge Bench) & *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997 — 7-Judge Bench):** *Bommai* entrenched **Federalism, Secularism, Democracy, Unity and Integrity, Social Justice, and Judicial Review of Article 356 Proclamations** as unamendable features. Three years later, *L. Chandra Kumar* struck down Clauses 2(d) of Article 323A and 3(d) of Article 323B (42nd CAA) that had excluded High Court writ oversight over tribunals, declaring **Judicial Review by the Supreme Court (Art 32) and High Courts (Art 226/227)** an inviolable pillar of Basic Structure.
10. ***M. Nagaraj v. Union of India* (2006 — 5-Judge Bench):** While upholding the 77th, 81st, 82nd, and 85th CAAs enabling SC/ST promotion quotas, the Court systematized Basic Structure methodology into two analytical filters: the **"Width Test"** (measuring whether the amended power's outer boundaries erase constitutional limits like the 50% ceiling, creamy layer, or administrative efficiency) and the **"Identity Test"** (checking whether the Constitution's overarching equality identity survives intact).
11. ***I.R. Coelho (Dead) by LRs v. State of Tamil Nadu* (January 11, 2007 — 9-Judge Unanimous Bench):** Enacted in 1951 to shield 13 agrarian laws, the **Ninth Schedule (Article 31B)** had ballooned to **284 statutes** by the **78th CAA, 1995**—including non-land laws like Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation statute (76th CAA) and laws previously struck down by courts (e.g., *Gudalur Janmam Estates Act*). Reaffirming *Waman Rao*'s April 24, 1973 cut-off, CJI Y.K. Sabharwal's unanimous nine-judge Bench dismantled blanket Ninth Schedule immunity via a **two-step "Rights Test / Essence of Rights Test"** and **"Direct Impact and Effect Test"**:
    - First, examine whether the post-1973 Ninth Schedule statute abridges Part III rights—above all the **Golden Triangle (Articles 14, 19, and 21)**.
    - Second, assess whether the *direct impact and effect* of that abridgment damages or destroys the **essence of the right** forming part of Basic Structure (later clarified in *Glanrock Estate*, 2010: a mere technical breach of an ordinary statutory facet of equality does not fail; it must strike at the *overarching principle* of Basic Structure). If it crosses that threshold, the Ninth Schedule shield fails and the law is void.
12. **Contemporary Consolidation & 2024–2026 Frontiers:** The doctrine remains actively enforced—striking down the **99th CAA/NJAC** in ***SCARA v. UoI* (2015)** for impairing **Judicial Independence**, while upholding the **103rd CAA (10% EWS quota)** 3:2 in ***Janhit Abhiyan* (2022)** on the ground that economic affirmative action does not destroy equality's basic identity. In **November 2024**, the SC in ***Dr. Balram Singh v. UoI*** upheld the 42nd CAA's insertion of *"Socialist"* and *"Secular"* in the Preamble as harmonious with Basic Structure, and a 9-Judge Bench in ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra*** reaffirmed unamended **Article 31C** while rejecting an absolute socialist reading of Article 39(b). On **November 19, 2025**, ***Madras Bar Association v. UoI* (`2025 INSC 1330`, `(2026) 2 SCC 1`)** invalidated key provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 for breaching **Separation of Powers and Judicial Independence**—confirming that while Basic Structure directly polices constitutional amendments, its structural norms (when anchored to constitutional articles) equally strike down subversive ordinary statutes, compelling Parliament to repeal the 2021 Act and enact the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 (`Act No. 17 of 2026`, effective August 25, 2026)** establishing the statutory 5-member **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**. Simultaneously, the **Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025** (currently before a JPC) faces acute Basic Structure scrutiny over whether automatic removal of a PM/CM after 30 days' pre-conviction detention violates **Parliamentary Democracy, Federalism, Separation of Powers, and Rule of Law**.

## 3. Judicial Review, Activism vs. Overreach & Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

### Judicial Review — Constitutional Anchors & Grounds

**Watch this classic Prelims trap (UPSC 2020):** the exact term **"judicial review" appears NOWHERE in the Constitution of India**. Yet the authority of the Supreme Court and High Courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments, constitutional amendments, and executive actions—and invalidate those transgressing constitutional boundaries—flows directly from a dense web of express articles: **Article 13** (declaring laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights void), **Articles 32 and 226** (writ remedies), **Articles 131–136** (federal, appellate, and SLP jurisdictions), **Articles 245, 246, and 254** (federal legislative competence and repugnancy), and **Article 372** (pre-constitutional laws).

Across Indian jurisprudence—synthesizing *State of A.P. v. McDowell* (1996) with *Shayara Bano* (2017)—courts strike down plenary legislation strictly on three constitutional axes (never merely because a judge considers a policy unwise):
1. **Lack of Legislative Competence:** Transgressing Seventh Schedule boundaries (e.g., Parliament encroaching on List II without an Article 249/250/252/253/356 trigger, or a State intruding on List I).
2. **Infringement of Part III or Express Constitutional Limits:** Violating Fundamental Rights—including the modern **Manifest Arbitrariness** test under Article 14 (invoked to invalidate instant triple talaq in *Shayara Bano*, 2017; Section 377 in *Navtej Singh Johar*, 2018; and the **Electoral Bonds Scheme** in ***ADR v. Union of India*, February 2024**)—or breaching mandatory constitutional provisions like Article 301 or Proviso to Article 368(2) (*Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021).
3. **Destruction of Basic Structure:** Specifically policing Constitutional Amendments enacted under Article 368 (and reinforcing structural separation-of-powers scrutiny over statutes impairing independent institutions).

Comparatively, India occupies a calibrated middle ground. Unlike the **United Kingdom**—where Parliament is sovereign and courts cannot strike down primary statutes—Indian courts routinely invalidate Acts of Parliament. Initially narrower than **US Judicial Review** (because Article 21 originally used *"procedure established by law"* rather than American *"due process of law"*), India's review power expanded dramatically after ***Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978)** fused Articles 14, 19, and 21 into **substantive due process** ("just, fair, and reasonable" law).

### Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Overreach ("Judicial Legislation")

**Judicial Activism** denotes proactive, rights-expanding constitutional interpretation designed to cure legislative inertia or executive paralysis and realize transformative constitutionalism. Landmark illustrations include inventing Basic Structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973), filling statutory vacuums through binding interim guidelines (**Vishaka Guidelines, 1997** on workplace sexual harassment until Parliament enacted the POSH Act, 2013; **Prakash Singh, 2006** police reforms; **D.K. Basu, 1997** arrest safeguards), constitutionalizing environmental protection (*M.C. Mehta*—CNG transition, Taj Trapezium, Absolute Liability), recognizing **passive euthanasia and living wills** (*Common Cause*, 2018/2023), barring **"Bulldozer Justice"** (November 2024), and elevating **menstrual health and hygiene** into Article 21/21A (*Dr. Jaya Thakur*, January 30, 2026).

**Judicial Overreach**—often criticized as **"judicial legislation"** or **"judicial adventurism"**—occurs when courts cross the **Separation of Powers** line (Article 50) to dictate fiscal allocations, design intricate executive policies, or substitute express constitutional functions without democratic accountability or technical expertise. Recurring examples cited in GS2 scholarship include:
- The **2016 National Anthem order** (*Shyam Narayan Chouksey*) mandating playing the anthem in cinema halls (later modified to optional in 2018);
- The **2017 Highway Liquor Ban** barring liquor outlets within 500 metres of national and state highways, triggering massive state excise shocks and hospitality job losses;
- Prolonged micro-management of private sporting bodies (the BCCI–Lodha reforms); and
- The **April 8, 2025** two-judge *Tamil Nadu Governor* ruling declaring **"deemed assent"** under Article 142 and prescribing fixed timelines for Governors and the President—an overreach subsequently checked via principled **Judicial Restraint** by the five-judge Constitution Bench in the **November 20, 2025 16th Presidential Reference (`2025 INSC 1333`)**.

As the Supreme Court itself cautioned in ***Asif Hameed v. State of J&K* (1989)** and ***Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club* (2008)**, *"the Constitution does not permit the court to direct or advise the executive in matters of policy"*; sustained legitimacy depends on **judicial restraint**, confining activism strictly to rights-protection and express legislative vacuums.

### Public Interest Litigation (PIL) — Mechanics, Evolution & Anti-Abuse Guardrails

Conceptually adapted from American public interest law, **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**—termed **Social Action Litigation (SAL)** by Professor Upendra Baxi—was indigenized in post-Emergency India by **Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer** (*Mumbai Kamgar Sabha*, 1976; *Fertilizer Corporation Kamgar Union*, 1981) and **P.N. Bhagwati** (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979; *S.P. Gupta*, 1981). Like Basic Structure and Judicial Review, **PIL is NOT defined in the Constitution or any statute**; it is a judge-made procedural revolution rooted in Articles 32 and 226.

PIL transformed India's apex court into arguably the world's most accessible—and powerful—constitutional forum through five doctrinal innovations:
- **Relaxation of *Locus Standi*:** Abandoning the adversarial rule that only an aggrieved victim may sue, ***S.P. Gupta v. UoI* (1981)** empowered any *bona fide* public-spirited citizen or organization to espouse the cause of impoverished, detained, or voiceless classes.
- **Epistolary Jurisdiction:** Treating simple letters, postcards, telegrams, or newspaper articles addressed to judges as formal writ petitions (e.g., *Sunil Batra* prisoner torture; *Hussainara Khatoon* releasing ~40,000 undertrials in Bihar; *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* bonded labour).
- **Inquisitorial Fact-Finding:** Appointing socio-legal commissions, expert committees, and *amici curiae* to gather ground evidence rather than relying solely on litigant pleadings.
- **Continuing Mandamus:** Instead of disposing of a case with a single unenforceable order, courts retain jurisdiction over years or decades to issue rolling interim directions and audit executive compliance—perfected in ***Vineet Narain v. Union of India* (1997)** (insulating CBI/ED directors and conferring statutory status on CVC in the Jain Hawala scandal) and ***T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. UoI* (1996–present)** (redefining "forest" broadly and creating the Central Empowered Committee for ongoing forest governance).
- **Compensatory Constitutional Torts:** Awarding monetary compensation directly under Article 32/226 for illegal detention or rights violations (*Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*, 1983).

As chronicled by the Supreme Court, PIL evolved across **three distinct phases**: **Phase I (1970s–1980s)** focused on marginalized groups (undertrials, bonded labour, custodial violence); **Phase II (1980s–1990s)** shifted to ecology, forests, and sustainable development (*M.C. Mehta*, *Vellore Citizens*); and **Phase III (1990s–present)** targeted institutional probity, administrative accountability, and electoral transparency (*Vineet Narain*, *2G Spectrum*, *ADR* Electoral Bonds).

However, relaxed standing inevitably bred abuse—degenerating into **"Publicity, Political, Private, or *Paisa* Interest Litigation,"** crowding out criminal and civil dockets and stalling infrastructure projects. To restore discipline, the Supreme Court in ***State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010)** laid down **eight binding guidelines** for the SC and all High Courts:
1. Encourage genuine and *bona fide* PILs while effectively curbing petitions driven by extraneous considerations;
2. Direct every High Court to frame formal PIL rules regulating admission and scrutiny;
3. **Verify the credentials and antecedents** of the petitioner *prima facie* before entertaining any PIL;
4. Verify the *prima facie* correctness of the petition's factual contents;
5. Confirm that **substantial public interest**—rather than personal, commercial, or political vendetta—is involved;
6. Prioritize petitions involving larger public interest, gravity, and urgency over routine cases;
7. Ascertain that the petition carries no personal gain, private motive, or oblique agenda; and
8. **Impose heavy exemplary costs** on busybodies, meddlesome interlopers, and blackmailers filing frivolous petitions.

Complementing *Chaufal*, the Supreme Court's administrative **PIL Guidelines** (1988, updated 2003) restrict letter-petitions to 10 core public-interest categories (bonded labour, neglected children, police harassment, custodial deaths, atrocities on women/SCs/STs, environmental pollution, riot victims) while **expressly excluding** landlord–tenant disputes, service/pension/gratuity matters, routine medical/educational admissions, and early-hearing requests.

## 4. Macro-Constitutional Synthesis — Landmark CAAs (1st to 106th)

Tracking India's **106 enacted Constitutional Amendment Acts** (1951–2023) reveals the political biography of the Republic: agrarian land redistribution in the 1950s, centralizing executive battles in the 1970s, anti-defection and grassroots democratization in the 1980s–1990s, fiscal-federal and affirmative-action recalibration in the 2010s–2020s, and live representational/governance debates reflected in the unpassed **129th (ONOE)**, **130th (Minister Detention)**, and defeated **131st (Delimitation)** Bills of 2024–2026.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Textual Absence Triple Trap (UPSC 2020):** None of the three terms—**"Basic Structure," "Judicial Review,"** or **"Public Interest Litigation (PIL)"**—is defined or even mentioned explicitly in the Constitution of India. All three are judicial doctrines derived from constitutional text and structure.
> - **Article 368 Procedural Traps (UPSC 2022):** A Constitution Amendment Bill requires **NO prior recommendation of the President** (contrast with Art 3 or Money Bills), can be introduced by a **Private Member** in **either House**, has **NO Joint Sitting (Art 108)** upon deadlock, and commands **mandatory Presidential assent** (zero veto power since the 24th CAA, 1971).
> - **Proviso to Article 368(2) — Exact Entrenched Clauses:** Do not miss **Article 279A (GST Council)** inside Clause (a) (added by the 101st CAA, 2016) or **Article 241 (UT High Courts)**. Remember that **Part III (Fundamental Rights)**, **Part IV (DPSPs)**, and **Article 3 (State boundaries)** do **NOT** require 50% State ratification—whereas altering the **Seventh Schedule** (even indirectly, per *Rajendra N. Shah*, 2021) or **Article 368 itself** DOES! Also note: State ratification uses **Simple Majority**, has **no constitutional time limit**, and counts **only States (never UT assemblies)**.
> - **Tier 1 Simple Majority Bare-Text Split ("Express Deeming" vs. "Default Legislative"):** Creating/renaming States (Art 3), creating/abolishing a State Legislative Council (Art 169), or increasing SC judge strength (Art 124(1)) are **NOT amendments under Article 368**—yet separate two distinct textual devices: (i) **Express Non-368 Deeming Clauses** (**Art 4(2), 169(3), 239A(2), 239AA(7)(b), 312(4), 5th Sch Para 7(2), 6th Sch Para 21(2)**), where ordinary law modifies/supplements constitutional text or Schedules; vs. (ii) **Default / Transitional Legislative Clauses** (**Art 11, 100(3), 124(1), 343(3)**), where the bare constitutional text itself is **never amended** (e.g., Art 124(1) still reads *"not more than seven other Judges"*, while the 37+1 strength operates purely via the 1956/2026 statutory Act).
> - **April 24, 1973 Cut-Off (*Waman Rao* & *I.R. Coelho*):** Basic Structure scrutiny over **Ninth Schedule (Article 31B)** enactments is **strictly prospective** from **April 24, 1973** (*Kesavananda Bharati* verdict date)—NOT retrospective to 1951! Pre-1973 Ninth Schedule laws remain shielded; post-1973 laws face the *Coelho* Essence-of-Rights / Direct Impact test (and subsequent amendments to even pre-1973 laws are reviewable).
> - **Article 368(4) & (5) Trap:** Although inserted by the 42nd CAA (1976) to bar judicial review of amendments, both clauses were **invalidated by the Supreme Court in *Minerva Mills* (1980)** and are void law—never treat them as operative constitutional rules.
> - **86th CAA vs. 97th CAA vs. 106th CAA Distinctions:** Only the **86th CAA (2002)** simultaneously amended FRs (21A), DPSPs (45), and Fundamental Duties (51A(k)). Under the **106th CAA (2023)**, the 1/3rd women's quota applies to Lok Sabha, State Assemblies, and NCT Delhi Assembly—**NOT** to the Rajya Sabha or State Legislative Councils!

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature

- **Special Majority & Ratification Misclassification:** UPSC routinely plants Fundamental Rights, DPSPs, or Art 3 state reorganization into the "requires 50% State ratification" bucket—or swaps Special Majority into Tier 1 items like Art 169 Legislative Councils and Art 124(1) SC strength.
- **Retrospective vs. Prospective Ninth Schedule Trap:** Examiners swap *Waman Rao* (1981) / *I.R. Coelho* (2007) as either granting blanket immunity to all Ninth Schedule laws OR applying Basic Structure retrospectively to pre-1973 land-reform Acts—both false (the bright line is **April 24, 1973**).
- **Case-to-Doctrine Inversion:** Conflating *Golaknath* (1967 — held FRs wholly unamendable) with *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973 — held FRs *are* amendable subject to Basic Structure), or attributing the striking down of Article 368(4)–(5) to *Kesavananda* instead of *Minerva Mills* (1980).
- **Constitutional Text Hallucination:** Attributing judge-made doctrines ("Basic Structure," "Judicial Review," "PIL," "Continuing Mandamus," "Curative Petition") to express definitions inside the Constitution or statutory Acts.
