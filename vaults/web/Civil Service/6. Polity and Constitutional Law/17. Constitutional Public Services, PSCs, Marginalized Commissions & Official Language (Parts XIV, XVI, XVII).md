# Chapter 17 - Constitutional Public Services, PSCs, Marginalized Commissions & Official Language (Parts XIV, XVI, XVII)

Parts XIV, XVI, and XVII of the Constitution house the **administrative sinews, social-justice monitors, and linguistic shock-absorbers** of the Indian Republic. While Chapters on Parliament, the Executive, and the Judiciary govern how political authority is won and checked, these three Parts solve an equally fragile design problem: how to run a neutral, continent-sized bureaucracy across federal tiers, police substantive equality for historically excluded groups, and hold together a multilingual federation without triggering secessionist rupture.

In both Prelims and GS2 Mains, examiners treat these Parts as a high-precision minefield. Prelims routinely weaponizes bare-article asymmetries—such as why a Governor appoints a State Public Service Commission member but only the President can remove them, why Pali and Prakrit break the old Classical–Eighth Schedule overlap, or why the NCSC still guards Anglo-Indians after 2020. Mains GS2 probes the underlying structural tension: whether constitutional status without coercive enforcement power turns commissions into toothless paper tigers, and whether All-India Services and language directives bend cooperative federalism toward centralization.

## Public Services Under the Union and the States (Part XIV, Chapter I - Articles 308–314)

The Constituent Assembly faced a sharp paradox in 1949. Nationalist leaders deeply distrusted the colonial Indian Civil Service (ICS), yet Partition's administrative collapse convinced Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel that a fragile new republic could not survive without an insulated, permanent civil service. Part XIV (Articles 308–323) codifies Patel's bargain: civil servants remain subordinate to elected political masters, but their recruitment, tenure, and discipline are shielded from partisan spoils.

### Scope and Legislative Competence - Articles 308 and 309

**Article 308** defines the expression "State" for Part XIV. Prior to August 2019, it expressly excluded Jammu and Kashmir; following the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 and Constitution Orders 272–273, the Part XIV framework operates uniformly across the Union and its territories.

**Article 309** vests plenary authority in the **appropriate Legislature**—Parliament for Union services and State Legislatures for State services—to regulate recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts. Because enacting detailed statutes for every cadre is slow, the **Proviso to Article 309** empowers the **President** (for the Union) and the **Governor** (for a State) to frame executive rules governing recruitment and service conditions until the legislature enacts a law.

Two constitutional principles govern Article 309 rules:
- **Statutory Force:** Rules framed under the Proviso are not mere administrative instructions; they carry the full force of law until superseded by an Act of the appropriate Legislature (*B.S. Vadera v. Union of India*, 1968).
- **Constitutional Subordination:** Article 309 opens with the words *"Subject to the provisions of this Constitution."* Neither a statute nor an executive rule under Article 309 can override Fundamental Rights (Articles 14 and 16) or dilute the tenure safeguards entrenched in Article 311.

### The Doctrine of Pleasure - Article 310

**Article 310(1)** transplants the English common-law maxim *durante bene placito* ("during pleasure"): every member of a Defence Service, a Civil Service of the Union, an All-India Service, or holder of a defence/civil post under the Union holds office during the **pleasure of the President**. Every member of a Civil Service of a State or holder of a civil post under a State holds office during the **pleasure of the Governor**.

Unlike the United Kingdom, where Crown pleasure was historically absolute unless clipped by Parliament, **Indian pleasure is constitutionally bounded**. The opening phrase *"Except as expressly provided by this Constitution"* carves out two decisive shields:
1. **Excluded Constitutional Functionaries:** Judges of the Supreme Court (Art 124) and High Courts (Art 217), the Comptroller and Auditor General (Art 148), the Chief Election Commissioner (Art 324(5)), and Chairmen/Members of Public Service Commissions (Art 317) do **not** hold office at executive pleasure. Their tenures are governed by bespoke impeachment or Supreme Court inquiry mechanisms.
2. **Article 311 Procedural Fence:** For civilian bureaucrats, executive pleasure cannot be exercised arbitrarily; it is strictly fenced by Article 311. Crucially, however, **Defence personnel**—including civilians occupying posts connected with defence—fall **outside Article 311** and remain governed solely by Article 310 read with the Army, Navy, and Air Force Acts.

**Article 310(2)** introduces a narrow contractual exception. Where the President or Governor appoints a person possessing special qualifications on contract to a civil post (not being an All-India Service, Defence Service, or regular Civil Service), the contract may stipulate payment of compensation if the post is abolished or the officer is required to vacate it early for reasons unconnected with misconduct.

### Civil-Servant Safeguards and Summary Exceptions - Article 311

Article 311 acts as the constitutional firewall converting permanent bureaucracy from partisan servants into neutral trustees of the state. Its coverage is precise: it protects members of a **civil service of the Union, an All-India Service, a civil service of a State, or holders of a civil post under the Union or a State**. Employees of public sector undertakings (PSUs), statutory corporations, municipalities, and universities are **not** holders of "civil posts" under Article 311, even when those entities qualify as "State" under Article 12.

Article 311 embeds two distinct substantive guarantees:

1. **Subordinate-Authority Bar - Article 311(1):** No covered civil servant shall be **dismissed or removed** by an authority **subordinate** to the authority by which they were appointed. The benchmark is the *actual* appointing authority at the time of appointment, not merely an officer of lower rank in general hierarchy. Notice the bare-text nuance: **Article 311(1) covers only dismissal and removal—NOT reduction in rank**, whereas Article 311(2) covers all three.
2. **Inquiry and Hearing Mandate - Article 311(2):** No covered civil servant shall be **dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank** except after an **inquiry** in which they have been informed of the charges against them and given a **reasonable opportunity of being heard** in respect of those charges.

Judicial interpretation has sharply demarcated which adverse actions trigger Article 311(2). In ***Parshotam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India* (1958)**, the Supreme Court held that Article 311(2) applies only when termination or reversion carries **penal consequences** (loss of accrued benefits or stigma), distinguishing punishment from termination *simpliciter* of a probationer or temporary employee. Similarly, compulsory retirement in public interest (under Fundamental Rule 56(j)) is not a "punishment" unless founded on unproven stigmatic allegations. Conversely, in ***Debashish Mohapatra v. District and Sessions Judge, Jagatsinghpur* (`2026 INSC 743`)**, the Supreme Court reaffirmed that once an employee is **confirmed in service**, authorities cannot terminate them by a *simpliciter* administrative order citing alleged initial appointment irregularities without conducting a full Article 311(2) inquiry.

The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** significantly truncated the hearing right under Article 311(2). Originally, civil servants enjoyed a *two-stage* opportunity: first during the inquiry on the charges, and second via a show-cause notice on the *proposed penalty*. The 42nd Amendment abolished the **second show-cause notice on penalty**. Yet in ***Managing Director, ECIL v. B. Karunakar* (1993)**, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench restored procedural fairness by holding that whenever the Inquiry Officer differs from the Disciplinary Authority, supplying a copy of the **inquiry report** to the delinquent officer before deciding guilt remains an integral part of the "reasonable opportunity" under Article 311(2).

#### The Second Proviso to Article 311(2) and *Tulsiram Patel* (1985)

The **Second Proviso to Article 311(2)** carves out **three exhaustive exceptions** where a civil servant can be dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank **without holding any inquiry**:
- **Clause (a) - Criminal Conviction:** Where the penalty is imposed on the ground of **conduct which has led to conviction on a criminal charge**. The focus is on the underlying *conduct*, not automatic mechanical dismissal for a minor traffic offence.
- **Clause (b) - Impracticability of Inquiry:** Where the authority empowered to impose the penalty is satisfied, **for reasons to be recorded in writing**, that it is **not reasonably practicable** to hold such an inquiry.
- **Clause (c) - Security of the State:** Where the **President or the Governor**, as the case may be, is satisfied that in the **interest of the security of the State** it is not expedient to hold such an inquiry. Note that Clause (c) requires **personal satisfaction** of the President/Governor (acting on ministerial aid and advice, not delegated to subordinate disciplinary officers) and does **not** require recording reasons in writing for communication.

In the landmark 5-Judge Constitution Bench verdict ***Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel* (1985)**, the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the Second Proviso and clarified four binding rules that still govern 2026 service jurisprudence:
1. **Express Exclusion of *Audi Alteram Partem*:** Because the Second Proviso is itself a constitutional command rooted in public interest and state security, principles of natural justice cannot be imported through the back door of Article 14 to nullify it.
2. **Narrow Reading of "Not Reasonably Practicable":** Under Clause (b), mere administrative inconvenience, gravity of allegations, or fear of witness delay does not justify bypassing inquiry. Objective, contemporaneous material must exist showing that holding an inquiry is genuinely impossible (e.g., pervasive intimidation, violent insurgency, or terror networks destroying witness safety).
3. **Written Reasons Are Mandatory:** Recording specific, non-boilerplate reasons in writing under Clause (b) is a **constitutional obligation**. Throughout 2025–2026, constitutional courts have repeatedly quashed summary police and civil-service dismissals where states mechanically parroted the text of Article 311(2)(b) without producing contemporaneous material.
4. **Scope of Finality under Article 311(3):** Although Article 311(3) declares the authority's decision on impracticability "final," *Tulsiram Patel* held that finality binds only departmental authorities—it **never bars judicial review** under Articles 226, 32, or 136 if satisfaction is *mala fide*, perverse, or founded on wholly extraneous grounds.

> *(Reform Context: The Second Administrative Reforms Commission [4th Report, *Ethics in Governance*] recommended repealing Article 311 and replacing it with a statutory framework under Article 309 to curb misuse as a shield for corrupt officials—a proposal never adopted due to fears of executive politicization. Broader civil-service operational reforms, lateral entry debates, and Mission Karmayogi capacity-building sit within the Governance hub.)*

### All-India Services - Article 312 and Federal Tension

**Article 312** represents one of Indian federalism's boldest unitary innovations. In classical dual federations like the United States, federal and state bureaucracies operate in watertight compartments. India deliberately created **All-India Services (AIS)** common to both the Union and the States to preserve uniform administrative standards, national integration, and cross-regional executive experience.

**Bare-Article Creation Mechanics:**
- **Exclusive Rajya Sabha Trigger - Article 312(1):** Parliament can create one or more new All-India Services **only if the Council of States (Rajya Sabha)** first declares by a resolution supported by **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** that it is **necessary or expedient in the national interest** to do so. Lok Sabha has **zero initiating power** here. Once the Rajya Sabha resolution passes, Parliament enacts an enabling law by **ordinary simple majority**.
- **Existing Services - Article 312(2):** Expressly deemed the **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)** and the **Indian Police Service (IPS)** to be services created by Parliament under Article 312. Exercising its statutory power under the **All-India Services Act, 1951** (amended in 1963 following a Rajya Sabha resolution), Parliament constituted the third active AIS—the **Indian Forest Service (IFoS)**—in **1966**. (The 1963 amendment also authorized an Indian Medical and Health Service and Indian Service of Engineers, neither of which was operationalized due to State opposition.)
- **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS) - Articles 312(1), (3), (4):** Inserted by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, enabling Parliament to create an **AIJS** following the same Rajya Sabha two-thirds resolution. **Article 312(3)** mandates that the AIJS shall not include any post **inferior to that of a District Judge** (defined in Article 236). **Article 312(4)** clarifies that any parliamentary law creating the AIJS—even if it consequentially modifies Part VI Chapter VI—shall **not be deemed an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368**. AIJS remains uncreated as of November 2026 due to federal resistance from States and High Courts over judicial independence and local language competence.

**Dual Control Architecture and Cooperative Friction:**
All-India Service officers are recruited centrally by UPSC, trained at central academies (LBSNAA, SVPNPA, IGNFA), and allotted to **State cadres** (with a deliberate insider–outsider ratio to insulate officers from local parochialism). While serving in a State, **immediate operational control** (postings, transfers, minor penalties, suspension) rests with the **State Government**, whereas **ultimate disciplinary control** (dismissal, removal, compulsory retirement) rests exclusively with the **Central Government (President)** under the AIS (Discipline and Appeal) Rules, 1969.

This dual loyalty has become a recurring federal flashpoint. State governments—historically Tamil Nadu (Rajamannar Committee, 1971) and West Bengal—have argued that AIS erodes State autonomy, particularly during episodes like the Centre's 2021–2022 attempt to amend **Rule 6(1) of the IAS (Cadre) Rules, 1954** to empower unilateral central deputation of officers without State concurrence. Conversely, the Sarkaria Commission (1988) and Punchhi Commission (2010) endorsed retaining AIS as vital connective tissue, recommending statutory Civil Services Boards (*T.S.R. Subramanian*, 2013) and fixed tenures to protect officers from arbitrary state transfers while preserving consensual deputation norms.

**Transitional & Repealed Clauses - Articles 312A, 313, and 314:**
Original **Article 314** had constitutionally entrenched the lavish pay, pension, and disciplinary privileges of pre-1947 British-era ICS officers. The **28th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972** **repealed Article 314** and inserted **Article 312A**, empowering Parliament to vary or revoke those pre-Independence service conditions. **Article 313** serves as a transitional continuity clause preserving pre-1950 service laws until modified.

## Public Service Commissions (Part XIV, Chapter II - Articles 315–323)

Public Service Commissions are the constitutional gatekeepers of India's merit bureaucracy. First established on **October 1, 1926** as the Public Service Commission following the **Lee Commission (1924)** recommendation under the Government of India Act, 1919, the institution became the Federal Public Service Commission under the Government of India Act, 1935, and assumed constitutional stature as the **Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)** on January 26, 1950—completing its **centenary on October 1, 2026**.

### The Institutional Triad - Article 315

**Article 315** constructs a three-tier recruitment architecture with a subtle constitutional-versus-statutory distinction that Prelims routinely exploits:
- **UPSC and SPSCs - Direct Constitutional Bodies:** Article 315(1) directly establishes a **Union Public Service Commission** for the Union and a **State Public Service Commission (SPSC)** for each State.
- **Joint State Public Service Commission (JSPSC) - Statutory Body with Constitutional Enabling Clause:** Under **Article 315(2)**, if two or more States agree via **resolutions passed by each House of their respective State Legislatures**, **Parliament may by law** provide for the appointment of a **JSPSC**. Because a JSPSC springs into existence through an **Act of Parliament** rather than directly from constitutional text, standard constitutional doctrine classifies a JSPSC as a **statutory body** (e.g., the short-lived Punjab–Haryana JSPSC in 1966).
- **UPSC Serving State Needs - Article 315(4):** Even without creating a JSPSC, the UPSC may, if requested by the **Governor of a State** and **approved by the President**, agree to serve all or any of the recruitment needs of that State.

### Composition, Qualifications, Tenure, and Re-appointment - Article 316

The Constitution deliberately leaves the **numerical strength** of UPSC, SPSCs, and JSPSCs unfixed, empowering the **President** (for UPSC and JSPSC) and the **Governor** (for SPSC) to determine member strength and service conditions under **Article 318**. By convention, UPSC consists of a Chairperson and 9 to 11 Members.

**Appointments and the Sole Constitutional Qualification:**
- **Appointing Authority - Article 316(1):** The **President** appoints the Chairman and Members of both the **UPSC and a JSPSC**; the **Governor** appoints the Chairman and Members of an **SPSC**.
- **Ten-Year Service Proviso:** The Constitution prescribes **no minimum academic degree, age floor, or legal background** for PSC members. Its sole textual qualification sits in the **Proviso to Article 316(1)**: *as nearly as may be one-half* of the members of every PSC shall be persons who, at the date of appointment, have held office for **at least ten years** either under the Government of India or under a State Government.
- **Acting Chairman - Article 316(1A):** Inserted by the **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963**, authorizing the President (UPSC/JSPSC) or Governor (SPSC) to appoint one of the existing Commission members as Acting Chairman during vacancy or temporary absence.

**Tenure and Non-Reappointment:**
- **UPSC:** **6 years** or until attaining **65 years of age**, whichever is earlier.
- **SPSC and JSPSC:** **6 years** or until attaining **62 years of age**, whichever is earlier *(raised from 60 to 62 by the **41st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**)*.
- **Resignation:** Submitted in writing to the **President** (UPSC and JSPSC) or to the **Governor** (SPSC).
- **Ineligibility for Same Office - Article 316(3):** A person holding office as a PSC member is strictly **ineligible for re-appointment to that same office** upon expiration of term.

### Removal, Suspension, and Live 2025–2026 Jurisprudence - Article 317

**Article 317** engineers one of the sharpest federal asymmetries in the Constitution to insulate State recruitment bodies from State political vendettas: **even though a State Governor appoints SPSC members, ONLY the President of India can remove them**. Removal proceeds along two distinct constitutional tracks:

#### Track 1 - Removal for "Misbehaviour" via Mandatory Supreme Court Inquiry - Article 317(1)
The Chairman or any other member of **UPSC, SPSC, or JSPSC** can be removed on the ground of **misbehaviour** *only* by an order of the **President**, after the **Supreme Court**, on a **reference made to it by the President**, holds an inquiry under Article 145 procedure and reports that the person ought to be removed. Unlike Article 143 advisory opinions, the Supreme Court's inquiry report under Article 317(1) is **constitutionally binding** on the President.

**Article 317(4)** supplies a **deeming conflict-of-interest definition** of misbehaviour: a member is deemed guilty if they are concerned or interested in any contract or agreement made on behalf of the Union or a State, or participate in its profits otherwise than as a member of an incorporated company in common with other shareholders. Because Article 317(4) is deeming rather than exhaustive, Supreme Court precedent reads "misbehaviour" more broadly to protect institutional integrity.

In the landmark judgment ***In re: Mepung Tadar Bage, Member, Arunachal Pradesh Public Service Commission* (`2025 INSC 1047`, August 28, 2025)**, the Supreme Court substantially sharpened Article 317 jurisprudence following an APPSC question-paper leak:
- **Wider Import than "Misconduct":** The Court affirmed that "misbehaviour" under Article 317 carries a wider ambit than technical "misconduct" and encompasses conduct that undermines public confidence in the office.
- **Strict *In Personam* Liability:** Crucially, the Court rejected importing cabinet-style **"collective responsibility"** into PSC removals. Removal proceedings under Article 317(1) are strictly *in personam*—requiring cogent evidence of **specific individual acts** by the charged member. Finding no personal culpability against Bage, the Court dismissed all six referred charges and revoked her suspension.

#### Track 2 - Suspension Pending Supreme Court Reference - Article 317(2)
Under **Article 317(2)**, the **President** (for UPSC or JSPSC) and the **Governor** (for SPSC) may **suspend** from office the Chairman or any member **in respect of whom a reference has been made to the Supreme Court under Article 317(1)**, until the President passes final orders on receipt of the Court's report.

Live 2026 litigation illustrated the exact procedural fence around this clause. In July 2026, the Governor of Karnataka suspended Karnataka Public Service Commission (KPSC) Chairperson Shivashankarappa S. Sahukar amid recruitment-irregularity allegations while recommending a Presidential reference. In ***Shiva Shankarappa S. Sahukar v. The Special Secretary* (August 18, 2026)**, the Karnataka High Court **quashed the suspension order**, holding that the Governor does not possess unilateral personal discretion under Article 317(2) and must act strictly on the **aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers** under Articles 163 and 166—further underscoring that Article 317(2) suspension presupposes a Presidential reference under Article 317(1).

#### Track 3 - Summary Presidential Removal Without Supreme Court Inquiry - Article 317(3)
Notwithstanding Article 317(1), the **President alone** (never the Governor) may summarily remove the Chairman or any member of UPSC, SPSC, or JSPSC **without referring the matter to the Supreme Court** on three objective/incapacity grounds:
1. Is **adjudged an insolvent**;
2. Engages during their term of office in any **paid employment outside the duties of their office**; or
3. Is, in the **opinion of the President**, unfit to continue in office by reason of **infirmity of mind or body**.

### Financial Insulation and Post-Retirement Bars - Articles 318, 319, and 322

To prevent executive coercion through pay cuts or post-retirement sinecures, the Constitution deploys three structural locks:
- **No Disadvantageous Variation - Proviso to Article 318:** The conditions of service of a PSC member cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment.
- **Consolidated Fund Charge - Article 322:** The entire expenses of UPSC (including salaries, allowances, and pensions of members and staff) are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India**, and those of an SPSC are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of the State**—insulating their budgets from annual legislative vote cuts.
- **Strict Post-Retirement Employment Matrix - Article 319:** To eliminate the temptation to curry favour with ruling ministers for post-retirement postings, Article 319 imposes cascading, office-specific bars upon ceasing to hold office:
  - **UPSC Chairman:** Totally **ineligible for any further employment** either under the Government of India or under any State Government.
  - **UPSC Member (other than Chairman):** Eligible **only** for appointment as **Chairman of UPSC** or **Chairman of an SPSC**; barred from all other employment under GoI or any State.
  - **SPSC Chairman:** Eligible **only** for appointment as **Chairman or Member of UPSC**, or **Chairman of *another* SPSC**; barred from all other employment under GoI or any State (and cannot be re-appointed to the same SPSC).
  - **SPSC Member (other than Chairman):** Eligible **only** for appointment as **Chairman or Member of UPSC**, or **Chairman of *that same* SPSC or *any other* SPSC**; barred from all other employment under GoI or any State.

**Crucial Constitutional Precedent on Article 319:** In ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that the **office of Governor of a State is an independent constitutional office, NOT "employment under the Government of India"**—meaning a retired PSC Chairman or Member is legally eligible to be appointed Governor (and similarly eligible for constitutional/statutory tribunals not constituting executive "employment").

### Advisory Remit, Statutory Exclusions, and Legislative Accountability - Articles 320, 321, and 323

**Article 320** defines PSC functions across recruitment and personnel advice:
- **Mandatory Examinations - Article 320(1)–(2):** Conducting examinations for appointments to Union and State services; UPSC must also assist two or more States on request in framing and operating joint recruitment schemes for specialized services.
- **Consultative Jurisdiction - Article 320(3):** The Union/State executive shall consult the Commission on: (a) methods of recruitment to civil services/posts; (b) principles governing appointments, promotions, transfers from one service to another, and candidate suitability; (c) all **disciplinary matters** affecting civil servants (including memorials/petitions); (d) claims for reimbursement of legal-defence costs incurred in official acts; and (e) claims for award of pension for injuries sustained on duty.
- **Extension of Jurisdiction - Article 321:** Parliament (for UPSC) or a State Legislature (for SPSC) may by Act extend PSC functions to services of any **local authority, body corporate constituted by law, or public institution**.

**Three High-Yield Boundaries on PSC Power:**
1. **Explicit Reservation Exclusion - Article 320(4):** Nothing in Article 320(3) requires consulting a PSC regarding the manner in which reservations under **Article 16(4)** are made or how **Article 335** (SC/ST service claims consistent with administrative efficiency) is given effect. The constitutional division of labour is exact: elected governments set affirmative-action policy; PSCs execute merit screening within that policy envelope.
2. **Executive Exemption Regulations - Proviso to Article 320(3) & Article 320(5):** The President or Governor may frame regulations specifying matters/posts where PSC consultation is unnecessary (e.g., the *UPSC (Exemption from Consultation) Regulations, 1958*, covering posts like Ambassadors, tribunal chairpersons, and temporary appointments under one year). Under **Article 320(5)**, every such regulation must be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature for **not less than 14 days** and is subject to legislative repeal or modification during that session.
3. **Advisory, Non-Binding Status (*Manbodhan Lal Srivastava*, 1957):** In ***State of UP v. Manbodhan Lal Srivastava* (1957, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that the consultation requirement in Article 320(3)(c) is **directory, not mandatory**: non-consultation neither voids a disciplinary order nor confers a justiciable cause of action on the employee. Instead, accountability operates politically through **Article 323**: UPSC submits an Annual Report to the President (SPSC to the Governor; **JSPSC to the Governor of *each* concerned State**), which must be laid before the Legislature accompanied by an **explanatory memorandum** justifying every single case where the Commission's advice was not accepted.

**Institutional Reality Check:** While UPSC has preserved high credibility—even deploying biometric face authentication in CSE 2026 to reinforce exam sanctity—**State Public Service Commissions** suffer chronic institutional decay: opaque political patronage in member appointments, recurring paper leaks (APPSC, RPSC, KPSC, UKPSC), multi-year litigation freezes, and proliferation of executive-controlled Staff Selection Boards that siphon Group B/C posts away from constitutional PSC oversight. Note also UPSC's institutional overlap with the **Central Vigilance Commission (CVC)** on vigilance-related disciplinary cases; whenever their advice diverges, UPSC's status as a constitutional body gives it institutional primacy, though ultimate decision-making remains with the government.

## Constitutional Commissions for Marginalized Classes (Part XVI - Articles 338, 338A, 338B & 342A)

Part XVI (*Special Provisions Relating to Certain Classes*) pairs substantive social-justice guarantees with dedicated constitutional watchdogs. Over seven decades, India's monitoring machinery evolved from a single solitary officer into three co-equal, specialized constitutional commissions—culminating in a sharp 2021 federalism clash between the Supreme Court and Parliament over backward-class identification.

### Institutional Evolution - 1950 to 2021

1. **Original Single Officer (1950):** Article 338 originally mandated only a single **Special Officer for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** (designated the Commissioner for SCs and STs) to investigate safeguards and report to the President.
2. **Executive Multi-Member Experiment (1978 & 1987):** Dissatisfied with the lone officer's bandwidth, the Union Government created a non-statutory multi-member Commission in 1978 alongside the Commissioner, renaming it the National Commission for SCs and STs in 1987.
3. **65th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1990 (effective March 12, 1992):** Abolished the single Special Officer and replaced Article 338 with a high-level, multi-member constitutional **National Commission for SCs and STs** endowed with civil-court investigative powers.
4. **89th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003 (effective February 19, 2004):** Bifurcated the combined body into two separate constitutional commissions—the **National Commission for Scheduled Castes (NCSC)** under **Article 338** and the **National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST)** under **Article 338A**—recognizing that tribal deprivations (forest alienation, Fifth/Sixth Schedule autonomy, development displacement) require distinct policy expertise from caste-based untouchability and urban-rural exclusion.
5. **102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018 (effective August 15, 2018):** Elevated the **National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC)**—previously a statutory body set up under the NCBC Act, 1993 pursuant to the *Indra Sawhney* (1992) directive—into a full constitutional body under **Article 338B**. Simultaneously, it inserted **Article 342A** (presidential notification of SEBCs) and **Article 366(26C)**, repealed the 1993 Act, and **deleted "other backward classes" from Article 338(10)**.

**The Anglo-Indian Residual Anomaly - Article 338(10):** While the 102nd Amendment stripped Backward Classes from NCSC's remit, it deliberately **left the Anglo-Indian community inside Article 338(10)**. Even after the **104th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2019** discontinued reserved Anglo-Indian nominations in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies from January 25, 2020, **NCSC—and NOT NCST, NCBC, or NCM—remains constitutionally obligated under Article 338(10)** to monitor constitutional and legal safeguards for the Anglo-Indian community.

### Structure, Mandate, Civil-Court Powers, and Enforcement Limits

Articles **338 (NCSC)**, **338A (NCST)**, and **338B (NCBC)** follow an almost identical structural template:

- **Composition & Appointment (Clauses 3–4):** Each Commission consists of a **Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson, and three other Members** (five total), appointed by the **President by warrant under his hand and seal**. Crucially, the Constitution **does NOT specify their tenure, qualifications, or removal grounds**—leaving conditions of service and tenure to be determined by the **President by rule** (presently fixed at a **3-year term**, renewable for a maximum of two terms; the Chairperson holds Union Cabinet Minister rank, Vice-Chairperson holds MoS rank).
- **Five-Fold Core Mandate (Clause 5):**
  1. Investigate and monitor all matters relating to constitutional and legal safeguards for the community;
  2. Inquire into **specific complaints** regarding deprivation of rights and safeguards (Note: as a statutory body from 1993 to 2018, NCBC could only examine list-inclusion/exclusion requests; constitutionalization under Art 338B(5) granted NCBC full grievance-redressal and development-advisory powers for the first time);
  3. Participate and advise on the **planning process of socio-economic development** and evaluate progress under the Union and States;
  4. Present **annual reports** (and special reports whenever deemed fit) to the **President** on the working of safeguards;
  5. Recommend effective measures for welfare, protection, and socio-economic advancement. *(For NCST, the President notified additional functions in 2005 covering ownership rights over Minor Forest Produce, mineral/water resource rights, relief and rehabilitation of displaced tribal groups, curbing shifting cultivation, and full implementation of PESA, 1996.)*
- **Legislative Tabling (Clauses 6–7):** The President lays every report before **each House of Parliament** along with a memorandum explaining action taken or reasons for non-acceptance. Any portion concerning a State Government is forwarded by the President to the **State Governor**, who lays it before the **State Legislature** with a corresponding explanatory memorandum.
- **Civil-Court Investigative Powers (Clause 8):** While investigating any safeguard or inquiring into any complaint, each Commission exercises all powers of a **civil court trying a suit**—specifically: summoning and enforcing attendance from anywhere in India and examining on oath; requiring discovery and production of documents; receiving evidence on affidavits; requisitioning public records from any court or office; and issuing commissions for examining witnesses and documents.
- **Mandatory Policy Consultation (Clause 9):** The Union and every State Government *shall* consult the respective Commission on **all major policy matters** affecting SCs, STs, or SEBCs—subject to the **105th Amendment Proviso to Article 338B(9)** discussed below.

#### The Structural Enforcement Deficit (*Indian Overseas Bank*, 1996 & UPSC Mains 2018/2026)

A decisive constitutional distinction separates **investigative power** from **enforcement power**. Despite possessing civil-court powers under Clause 8, NCSC, NCST, and NCBC are **recommendatory, non-adjudicatory bodies**—not courts or tribunals.

In ***All India Indian Overseas Bank SC and ST Employees' Welfare Association v. Union of India* (1996)**, the Supreme Court held that Article 338(8) confers only the **procedural powers of a civil court for investigation and inquiry**; it does **not** empower the Commission to issue binding directions, temporary injunctions, stay orders, or compel employers to alter promotion lists. For the same structural reason (directly examined in **UPSC Mains 2018 GS2**), **NCSC cannot enforce SC reservations in religious minority educational institutions**: first, because **Article 15(5)** expressly exempts minority institutions covered by **Article 30(1)** from mandatory reservations (*Pramati Educational & Cultural Trust*, 2014); and second, because NCSC lacks adjudicatory enforcement authority even where statutory reservations apply.

In practice, as highlighted by **UPSC Mains 2026 GS2**, four institutional bottlenecks blunt their substantive-equality impact:
- **Non-Binding Recommendations:** Ministries routinely ignore recommendations with cursory boilerplate explanations in Action Taken Memoranda.
- **Tabling Delays:** Annual reports frequently sit un-tabled before Parliament and State Legislatures for years, stripping public accountability of real-time urgency.
- **Executive Dependence & Vacancies:** Lack of constitutionally fixed tenure/removal protection, partisan appointments, chronic Chairperson/Member vacancies, and thin regional investigation staff undermine independence.
- **Remedial Fragmentation:** Actual penal enforcement relies externally on criminal courts and police executing the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, where conviction rates remain low.

### The *Maratha Quota* Verdict (2021) and the 105th Constitutional Amendment Reset

The **102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018** triggered an unintended federal earthquake. Alongside creating NCBC (Article 338B), it inserted **Article 342A**—mirroring Articles 341 (SCs) and 342 (STs)—empowering the President, after consulting the Governor, to notify Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs) in relation to a State or UT, with Parliament holding sole power to include or exclude entries thereafter.

When Maharashtra enacted the SEBC Act, 2018 granting reservation to the Maratha community (initially 16%, trimmed by the Bombay High Court to 12% in education and 13% in jobs), a 5-Judge Constitution Bench delivered ***Dr. Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, Maharashtra* (May 5, 2021)** with two monumental holdings:
1. **Unanimous 50% Ceiling Enforcement:** Struck down the Maratha quota for breaching the *Indra Sawhney* (1992) 50% ceiling without establishing "extraordinary circumstances," and unanimously refused to refer *Indra Sawhney* to a larger bench.
2. **3:2 Centralization of Backward-Class Lists:** Reading Article 342A literally, the **3-Judge majority** (Justices S. Ravindra Bhat, L. Nageswara Rao, and Hemant Gupta; Justices Ashok Bhushan and S. Abdul Nazeer dissenting) held that post-102nd Amendment, **States had lost their constitutional power to identify SEBCs**—leaving **only the President/Parliament** competent to prepare a single national SEBC list.

Overnight, over 670 State-specific OBC communities outside the Central List faced legal invalidity. Moving with rare bipartisan speed, Parliament enacted the **105th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2021** (effective **August 15, 2021**) to execute a surgical federal restoration across three Articles:
- **Article 342A(1)–(2) Narrowed to Central List:** Expressly clarified that the Presidentially notified SEBC list is the **"Central List"** prepared and maintained **for the purposes of the Central Government** only.
- **Article 342A(3) Restoring State/UT Autonomy:** Newly inserted clause explicitly empowering **every State and Union Territory**, by law, to prepare and maintain **for its own purposes** a list of SEBCs, entries in which **may be different from the Central List**.
- **Proviso to Article 338B(9):** Expressly exempted States and UTs from the obligation to consult the NCBC when exercising their independent listing power under **Article 342A(3)**.
- **Article 366(26C) Amended:** Redefined "socially and educationally backward classes" to encompass lists notified under *both* the Central List and State/UT lists under Article 342A.

**Crucial Constitutional Asymmetry Post-105th CAA:**
- For **SEBCs/OBCs (Article 342A):** **Dual lists exist**—the Centre maintains the Central List (President/Parliament), while States/UTs independently maintain State/UT Lists. *(Note: 105th CAA only restored *who identifies* SEBCs; it did **not** touch the *Indra Sawhney* 50% cap.)*
- For **SCs (Article 341) and STs (Article 342):** **Single Presidential List only**—States have **zero competence** to add, delete, or alter SC/ST lists (though post-*State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* [Aug 2024, 7-Judge Bench], States may *sub-classify* among communities already inside the Presidential SC/ST list without altering the list itself!).

## Official Language Jurisprudence (Part XVII - Articles 343–351 & Eighth Schedule)

No question fractured the Constituent Assembly more viscerally than language. Hindi-belt delegates demanded immediate nationwide adoption of Hindi as the sole national language; southern and eastern delegates warned that sudden linguistic majoritarianism would disenfranchise non-Hindi citizens from Union administration. The **Munshi–Ayyangar Formula** brokered a calibrated constitutional truce: **the Indian Constitution designates NO "National Language"**—only **Official Language(s)** governed by Part XVII's four carefully balanced chapters.

### Chapter I - Language of the Union and the 1963–1967 Compromise (Articles 343–344)

**Article 343(1)** declares that the official language of the Union shall be **Hindi in Devanagari script**, while the form of numerals used for Union official purposes shall be the **international form of Indian numerals** (not Devanagari numerals, unless Parliament subsequently authorizes Devanagari numerals under Art 343(3)).

**Article 343(2)** enacted a **15-year transitional runway** (until January 26, 1965) during which English continued for all official Union purposes. Crucially, **Article 343(3)** empowered Parliament to provide by law for the continued use of **English** even *after* the 15-year window expired.

**The Official Languages Act, 1963 and Its 1967 Federal Veto:**
As the 1965 deadline approached, Parliament enacted the **Official Languages Act, 1963** (Section 3 effective January 26, 1965), allowing English to continue *"in addition to Hindi"*. However, the permissive phrasing ("may continue") sparked massive anti-Hindi agitations across Tamil Nadu and southern India in 1965. Delivering on Nehru's assurance, Parliament enacted the **Official Languages (Amendment) Act, 1967**, cementing **indefinite bilingualism**:
- English became **mandatory** for Union–non-Hindi State communication (and accompanied by English translations when Hindi States communicate with non-Hindi States).
- Crucially, Section 3(5) embedded a **federal statutory veto**: the bilingual regime cannot be terminated until **resolutions for discontinuance of English are passed by the Legislatures of ALL States that have not adopted Hindi as their official language**, followed by a resolution passed by **both Houses of Parliament**.

**Commission and Parliamentary Committee - Article 344:**
- **Official Language Commission - Article 344(1):** President required to constitute a Commission at the expiration of **5 years** (1955) and **10 years** (1960) from constitutional commencement, comprising a Chairman and members representing each **Eighth Schedule** language. Only the **1955 Commission (chaired by B.G. Kher)** was ever constituted; no second Commission was formed in 1960.
- **Parliamentary Committee - Article 344(4) & Section 4 of OLA, 1963:** A **30-member Committee** (**20 Lok Sabha + 10 Rajya Sabha**, elected by proportional representation with single transferable vote) examines the Commission's recommendations and reports to the President. The first Committee (1957) was chaired by Govind Ballabh Pant. Subsequently, a standing **Committee of Parliament on Official Language** was constituted in **1976 under Section 4 of the Official Languages Act, 1963**, chaired by the **Union Home Minister**, which reviews progressive Hindi adoption across central ministries and submits periodic volumes directly to the President.

### Chapter II - Regional Languages (Articles 345–347)

Part XVII grants States sweeping linguistic autonomy to prevent central homogenization:
- **State Official Languages - Article 345:** A State Legislature may by law adopt **any one or more of the languages in use in the State** OR **Hindi** as its official language(s). **High-yield rule:** A State's official language **need NOT be listed in the Eighth Schedule**—hence English serves as the official language of Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Arunachal Pradesh, Mizo in Mizoram, and Kokborok alongside Bengali/English in Tripura. Until a State legislates otherwise, English continues.
- **Inter-State & Union–State Link Language - Article 346:** The official language authorized for Union purposes (presently Hindi/English under OLA 1963) serves as the link language between the Union and a State, and between two States. However, the **Proviso to Article 346** allows any two or more States to mutually agree to use **Hindi** for communication between themselves.
- **Presidential Directive for Substantial Minorities - Article 347:** On a demand being made, if the **President** is satisfied that a **substantial proportion of a State's population** desires the use of any language spoken by them to be recognized by that State, the President may direct that such language be officially recognized throughout the State or in any specified part thereof.

### Chapter III - Language of the Judiciary and Authoritative Texts (Articles 348–349)

To preserve an integrated, nationwide precedent system across India's single judicial hierarchy, **Article 348** imposes a strict English constitutional default unless Parliament legislates otherwise:

- **Mandatory English Default - Article 348(1):** Until Parliament by law otherwise provides:
  - **(a)** All proceedings in the **Supreme Court** and in **every High Court**, and
  - **(b)** The **authoritative texts** of all Bills, Acts, Ordinances, Orders, Rules, Regulations, and Bye-laws at both **Union and State** levels—**shall be in the English language**.
  - Because Parliament has enacted **no law altering Supreme Court language**, the **Supreme Court conducts all proceedings and delivers all binding judgments solely in English**. (Note: Subordinate courts are outside Article 348; their language is decided by respective State Governments under CPC/BNSS procedural laws.)
- **High Court Exception - Article 348(2) vs. Section 7 of Official Languages Act, 1963:** This two-step constitutional–statutory split is one of the sharpest bare-text distinctions in Indian polity:
  - **Proceedings Only - Article 348(2):** The **Governor of a State**, with the **previous consent of the President**, may authorize the use of Hindi or any other official language of the State in **proceedings** in the High Court having its principal seat in that State. Crucially, the **Proviso to Article 348(2)** expressly excludes **any judgment, decree, or order** of that High Court.
  - **Judgments/Decrees/Orders - Section 7 of OLA, 1963:** Separately operationalized via statute, enabling the Governor, with **previous Presidential consent**, to authorize optional use of Hindi or the State official language *in addition to English* for High Court **judgments, decrees, or orders**, **mandatorily accompanied by an English translation issued under the High Court's authority**.
- **Authoritative State Legislative Translations - Article 348(3):** Where a State Legislature prescribes a non-English language for State Bills, Acts, Ordinances, or subordinate orders, a translation in English published under the **Governor's authority in the Official Gazette** is deemed the **authoritative text** in English.
- **Expired Special Procedure - Article 349:** Required previous Presidential sanction (after considering Article 344 Commission/Committee views) before introducing any parliamentary Bill affecting Article 348(1) during the first 15 years (1950–1965).

**Live Federal Friction on High Court Languages (2026 Status):**
As confirmed by the Ministry of Law and Justice in Rajya Sabha on **March 19, 2026**, only **four High Courts** are authorized to use Hindi—**Rajasthan (1950), Uttar Pradesh (1969), Madhya Pradesh (1971), and Bihar (1972)**. Repeated State proposals to authorize **Tamil** (Madras HC), **Gujarati** (Gujarat HC), **Bengali** (Calcutta HC), **Kannada** (Karnataka HC), and **Hindi** (Chhattisgarh HC) have been withheld based on a **1965 Union Cabinet decision** requiring prior **Chief Justice of India (CJI) concurrence**. The Supreme Court Full Court has consistently declined, citing the **All-India transfer policy of High Court judges** (where out-of-state judges could not preside over vernacular hearings). Petitions challenging this 1965 Cabinet barrier on grounds of access to justice (Articles 14 and 21) remain active in 2026. Meanwhile, the Supreme Court has pragmatically deployed AI-assisted translation via **SUVAS** (*Supreme Court Vidhik Anuvaad Software*) and multilingual **e-SCR** judgment repositories to bridge litigant comprehension without fragmenting constitutional precedent.

### Chapter IV - Special Directives and Linguistic Minority Safeguards (Articles 350–351)

Following the linguistic reorganization of Indian states in 1956, millions of speakers became linguistic minorities overnight inside newly drawn borders. Acting on the **States Reorganisation Commission (SRC, 1953–55)** recommendations, the **7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956** grafted **Articles 350A and 350B** onto Part XVII:

- **Right of Grievance Representation - Article 350:** Every person is entitled to submit a representation for the redress of any grievance to any officer or authority of the Union or a State in **any of the languages used in the Union or in the State**, as the case may be.
- **Primary Education in Mother-Tongue - Article 350A (7th CAA, 1956):** Directs every State and every local authority within the State to endeavour to provide adequate facilities for **instruction in the mother-tongue at the primary stage of education** to children belonging to linguistic minority groups; empowers the **President** to issue directions to any State to secure such facilities.
  - *Constitutional Nuance:* Article 350A creates an obligation on the *State to provide facilities*, not a coercive mandate on parents. In ***State of Karnataka v. Associated Management of English Medium Primary & Secondary Schools* (2014, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that a State **cannot compel** mother-tongue instruction in private unaided schools against parental choice, which is protected under Article 19(1)(a). Article 350A aligns structurally with the **National Education Policy (NEP) 2020** recommendation for mother-tongue/regional-language instruction at least up to Grade 5.
- **Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities - Article 350B (7th CAA, 1956):** Establishes a single-member constitutional authority—officially designated the **Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities in India (CLM)** (created July 1957)—appointed by the **President**.
  - *Mandate & Reporting:* Investigates all matters relating to constitutional safeguards for linguistic minorities and reports to the President at directed intervals; the President causes all reports to be laid before **each House of Parliament** and sent to the **Governments of the States concerned**.
  - *Administrative Architecture & Dual-Standard Location Hygiene:* The Constitution neither defines "linguistic minority" (determined at the **State level** per *T.M.A. Pai Foundation*, 2002) nor prescribes CLM's tenure, qualifications, or removal. Administratively, CLM functions under the **Ministry of Minority Affairs** (transferred from MHA). *Dual-Standard Note:* While legacy Polity textbooks routinely cite **Allahabad (Prayagraj)** as CLM's headquarters (where it operated after 1957), official Union records reflect that the **headquarters shifted back to New Delhi with effect from June 1, 2015**, supported by three **regional offices at Belagavi (Belgaum), Chennai, and Kolkata** headed by Assistant Commissioners.
- **Directive for Development of Hindi - Article 351:** Directs the Union to promote the spread of Hindi so it serves as a medium of expression for all elements of India's **composite culture**, securing its enrichment by assimilating forms, style, and expressions from **Hindustani** and other **Eighth Schedule** languages, and drawing vocabulary **primarily on Sanskrit** and secondarily on other languages.

### The Eighth Schedule (22 Languages) vs. Eleven Classical Languages of India

Aspirants frequently conflate the **Eighth Schedule** (a constitutional register linked to **Articles 344(1) and 351**) with **Classical Language status** (a purely executive cultural recognition). Keeping their legal origins, amendments, and 2024–2026 divergence crystal-clear is non-negotiable for Prelims.

#### Evolution of the Eighth Schedule (14 → 22 Languages)
- **Original 14 Languages (1950):** Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Malayalam, Marathi, Oriya, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, and Urdu.
- **21st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1967 (+1 → 15):** Added **Sindhi**.
- **71st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 (+3 → 18):** Added **Konkani, Manipuri (Meitei), and Nepali** *(directly tested in UPSC Prelims 2024)*.
- **92nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003 (enforced Jan 2004; +4 → 22):** Added **Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, and Santhali**.
- **96th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011 (Rename only):** Changed spelling from "Oriya" to **Odia** (total remained 22).

**Critical Eighth Schedule Negatives:**
- **English is NOT in the Eighth Schedule** (nor are Bhojpuri, Rajasthani, Tulu, Kokborok, Garo, Khasi, Mizo, **Pali, or Prakrit**).
- **Zero Fixed Inclusion Criteria:** Unlike Classical Languages, there are **no fixed constitutional or statutory criteria** for adding a language to the Eighth Schedule. Both the **Ashok Pahwa Committee (1996)** and the **Sitakant Mohapatra Committee (2003)** proposed objective benchmarks, but none have been formally adopted; demands from 38+ languages remain pending.
- **Practical Consequences of Inclusion:** Representation on the Article 344 Official Language Commission, source-pool status for enriching Hindi (Art 351), simultaneous translation eligibility in Parliament, option as a medium/paper in UPSC CSE, and default Sahitya Akademi literary recognition.

#### Eleven Classical Languages of India (2004–2026 Overhaul)

**Classical Language** (*Shastriya Bhasha*) is **NOT a constitutional category**. It is an **executive/administrative designation** conferred by the **Union Cabinet** on the recommendation of the **Ministry of Culture**, advised by the **Linguistic Experts Committee (LEC)** constituted under the **Sahitya Akademi**.

**Chronological Roster (Total = 11 as of November 2026):**
- **First Wave (2004–2014 — 6 Languages):** **Tamil (2004)** [first recognized, Oct 12, 2004], **Sanskrit (2005)**, **Kannada (2008)**, **Telugu (2008)**, **Malayalam (2013)**, and **Odia (2014)**.
- **Second Wave (October 3, 2024 Union Cabinet Approval — +5 Languages):** **Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, and Bengali**. *(Linguistic breakdown: 4 Dravidian [Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam], 4 living Indo-Aryan [Odia, Marathi, Assamese, Bengali], and 3 historic/liturgical Indo-Aryan [Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrit]).*

**The Crucial 2024 Criteria Revision (2004 → 2005 → 2024):**
- In **2004**, antiquity was set at 1,000+ years alongside ancient literary heritage and an "original, unborrowed literary tradition".
- In **November 2005**, antiquity was raised to **1,500–2,000 years** and a fourth criterion on distinctness/discontinuity from modern offshoots was added.
- In **2024**, the LEC **deleted** the restrictive third requirement (*"literary tradition must be original and not borrowed from another speech community"*)—which had long blocked Indo-Aryan vernaculars sharing Sanskritic roots—and **replaced** it with **"Knowledge texts, especially prose texts in addition to poetry, epigraphical and inscriptional evidence"**. The four live criteria today are:
  1. High antiquity of early texts/recorded history over a period of **1,500–2,000 years**;
  2. A body of ancient literature/texts considered a heritage by generations of speakers;
  3. **Knowledge texts, especially prose texts in addition to poetry, epigraphical and inscriptional evidence**; and
  4. The Classical Language and literature could be **distinct from its current form or discontinuous with later forms of its offshoots**.

**Institutional Entitlements & October 3, 2026 Expansion:**
Designated Classical Languages receive two major annual international awards for eminent scholars, UGC-funded Professional Chairs in Central Universities, dedicated research/digitization funding, and a **Centre of Excellence (CoE)**. Marking the two-year anniversary of the 2024 expansion, on **October 3, 2026**, the **Ministry of Education** formally approved **five new Centres of Excellence** for the newly designated tongues:
1. **Classical Assamese:** Tezpur University, Assam
2. **Classical Bengali:** Visva-Bharati University, West Bengal
3. **Classical Marathi:** Deccan College Post-Graduate and Research Institute (Deemed University), Pune, Maharashtra
4. **Classical Pali:** Central University of Odisha, Odisha
5. **Classical Prakrit:** Central University of Gujarat, Gujarat

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Post-Oct 2024 Classical–Eighth Schedule Inversion:** Legacy test banks state *"All Classical Languages of India are listed in the Eighth Schedule."* **That statement is now FALSE.** Since October 3, 2024, India has **11 Classical Languages**, of which only **9** sit in the Eighth Schedule—**Pali and Prakrit are Classical Languages NOT included in the Eighth Schedule**.
> - **SPSC Appointment–Suspension–Removal Triangle (Art 316 & 317):** The **Governor** appoints SPSC members and may **suspend** them *only after* a Presidential reference to the Supreme Court (acting on ministerial aid & advice, Karnataka HC Aug 2026), but **ONLY the President** can remove them—never the Governor. Similarly, for a **JSPSC**, the **President** appoints and removes members, yet its annual report goes to the **Governors of concerned States**, not Parliament!
> - **Article 311(1) vs. 311(2) Penalty Scope:** **Reduction in rank** triggers the inquiry safeguard under **Article 311(2)**, NOT the subordinate-authority bar under **Article 311(1)** (which covers only *dismissal* and *removal*). Also, Defence personnel and PSU employees are **excluded** from Article 311.
> - **Article 312 Exclusive Rajya Sabha Trigger & AIJS Non-368 Rule:** Creating a new All-India Service requires a **Rajya Sabha** resolution by **2/3rd of members present and voting** (NOT 2/3rd of total strength, NOT an equal Lok Sabha power). A parliamentary law establishing an **All-India Judicial Service** (posts not inferior to District Judge) is expressly **outside Article 368** (Art 312(4)).
> - **Article 319 Post-Retirement Precision:** A retired **UPSC Chairman** is barred from *all* GoI/State employment, **except** constitutional offices like **Governor** (*Hargovind Pant*, 1979). A retired **SPSC Member** *can* become Chairman of the **same** SPSC, whereas a retired **SPSC Chairman** can only head *another* SPSC or join UPSC.
> - **Article 320(4) Consultation Bar:** UPSC/SPSCs **need NOT be consulted** on reservation policy under **Article 16(4)** or **Article 335**. Nor is their disciplinary advice under Article 320(3)(c) legally binding (*Manbodhan Lal Srivastava*, 1957).
> - **Anglo-Indians Still Under NCSC (Art 338(10)):** The **102nd CAA (2018)** removed OBCs from Article 338(10), and the **104th CAA (2019)** ended Anglo-Indian legislative nominations—yet **Article 338(10) still vests Anglo-Indian safeguard monitoring with the NCSC**.
> - **105th CAA SEBC Asymmetry vs. SC/ST Lists:** Post-105th CAA (2021), States/UTs maintain **independent SEBC/OBC lists** under **Article 342A(3)** without consulting NCBC (Proviso to Art 338B(9)). By contrast, States have **zero power** to modify Presidential **SC (Art 341)** or **ST (Art 342)** lists.
> - **High Court Language Two-Key Lock:** **Article 348(2)** allows a Governor (with prior Presidential consent) to authorize Hindi/State language in High Court **proceedings only** (proviso bars judgments/orders); extending it to **judgments, decrees, or orders** requires **Section 7 of the Official Languages Act, 1963** plus an authorized English translation. Meanwhile, a State's official language under **Article 345** need **not** be in the Eighth Schedule.
> - **Zero Constitutional Fixation Trap:** The Constitution does **NOT** fix the member strength of UPSC/SPSC, the tenure/qualifications/removal grounds of **NCSC/NCST/NCBC/CLM**, or any definition of **"linguistic minority"**.

### Prelims Trap Signature

1. **Constitutional vs. Statutory Status Conflation:** Examiners frequently slip **JSPSC** (statutory body under Art 315(2) Act of Parliament) or **Classical Language status** (executive Cabinet recognition under Ministry of Culture) alongside constitutional mandates to induce false-positive eliminations.
2. **Authority Swap on Federal Offices:** Swapping **Governor vs. President** across the lifecycle of an **SPSC** (Governor appoints & suspends post-reference; President removes), a **JSPSC** (President appoints/removes; Governors receive reports), or **High Court language** (Governor authorizes *only with previous Presidential consent*).
3. **Outdated Pre-2021 / Pre-2024 Statements:** Deploying legacy textbook truths that were overturned by recent law—such as *"States cannot prepare their own OBC lists post-102nd CAA"* (false after **105th CAA, 2021**), *"All Classical Languages are in the Eighth Schedule"* (false after **Oct 2024** inclusion of Pali/Prakrit), or *"Original unborrowed tradition is a criterion for Classical Languages"* (replaced in **2024** by prose/epigraphical knowledge texts).
4. **Powers of a Civil Court = Enforcement Power Fallacy:** Framing NCSC, NCST, or NCBC as empowered to issue **binding injunctions, stay administrative orders, or enforce reservations**—conflating procedural investigative powers (Clause 8) with binding judicial authority (*Indian Overseas Bank*, 1996).
