# Federal Architecture II — Fiscal Federalism, Mineral/GST Verdicts, 16th Finance Commission & Borrowing

Every federation lives or dies by how it reconciles two structural mismatches. In India, buoyant, mobile revenue bases (income tax, corporation tax, customs) are centralized in New Delhi to preserve economic unity, while sticky, high-visibility expenditure duties (health, policing, schooling, irrigation) rest with the States—which together budget to spend **62% more than the Union** in FY 2026–27.

That built-in **Vertical Fiscal Imbalance**, compounded by sharp inter-state income disparities (**Horizontal Fiscal Imbalance**), makes **Part XII (Articles 264–293)** the circulatory system of Indian constitutionalism. Between 2024 and 2026, four inflection points have redrawn this fiscal settlement: the **16th Finance Commission's performance-weighted formula**, the **February 2026 sunset of the GST Compensation Cess**, Parliament's **August 2026 statutory countermove against *MADA v. SAIL***, and the **pending 5-Judge Constitution Bench battle over State borrowing under Article 293**.

## Constitutional Tax Architecture - Part XII Revenue Buckets and Inter-Governmental Immunity

### Authority of Law and the Four-Way Levy Taxonomy (Article 265)

**Article 265** lays down the bedrock fiscal rule of constitutional democracy: *"No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law."* Two surgical implications follow: the word **"law"** means a valid statute enacted by a competent legislature—never an executive order, rule, or budget speech—and statutory authority must independently cover **both levy (imposition) and collection**.

Constitutional jurisprudence distinguishes four distinct fiscal exactions whose classification dictates whether revenue is shared with States:

- **Tax:** A compulsory exaction of money by a public authority for general public purposes, enforceable by law, with **no *quid pro quo*** (no direct service returned to the payer). Taxes under the Union List enter the Divisible Pool unless expressly carved out.
- **Fee:** A charge levied in exchange for a specific regulatory service or special benefit conferred on the payer (***Shirur Mutt*, 1954**). While modern courts accept broad correlation rather than arithmetic exactness, fees remain legally distinct from taxes and fall outside revenue-sharing pools.
- **Cess:** A **tax earmarked for a specific statutory purpose** (e.g., Health and Education Cess, Road and Infrastructure Cess, Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess). Proceeds are first credited to the **Consolidated Fund of India (Art 266(1))** and must be appropriated by Parliament into dedicated reserve funds; crucially, **cesses levied for specific purposes are excluded from the Divisible Pool under Article 270(1)**.
- **Surcharge (Article 271):** An **additional levy ("tax on tax")** imposed by Parliament **exclusively for general Union purposes** (unlike a cess, it requires no earmarked end-use). Proceeds go 100% to the Consolidated Fund of India and are permanently excluded from the Divisible Pool—though the **101st Amendment Act (2016)** expressly **barred Parliament from levying any Article 271 surcharge on GST**.

### Five Revenue-Sharing Buckets and Ancillary Fiscal Guardrails (Articles 268–289)

Rather than allowing overlapping double taxation, the Seventh Schedule enforces strict **tax separation** between List I and List II (the sole deliberate exception being simultaneous GST under **Article 246A**). Part XII then routes Union-collected revenues through five constitutional channels, alongside three critical procedural and federal safeguards:

- **Article 274 (Prior Presidential Recommendation):** No Bill imposing or varying any tax in which States are interested, altering the meaning of *"agricultural income"*, modifying distribution principles, or imposing an Article 271 surcharge can be introduced in Parliament without the **prior recommendation of the President**.
- **Article 276 (Professional Tax Cap):** Empowers States and local bodies to levy taxes on professions, trades, callings, and employments (without encroaching on Union income tax), subject to an upper ceiling of **₹2,500 per person per annum** (raised from ₹250 by the **60th CAA, 1988**).
- **Article 279 (Finality of CAG Certification):** Defines *"net proceeds"* of any tax as gross proceeds minus the **cost of collection**, and declares that the **certificate of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** ascertaining net proceeds is **final**.
- **Articles 285 & 289 (Inter-Governmental Tax Immunity):** Under **Article 285**, property of the Union is **absolutely exempt** from all State and local taxes unless Parliament expressly provides otherwise. Under **Article 289(1)**, property and income of a State are exempt from Union taxation—**except** commercial trade or business operations carried on by a State, which Parliament may tax unless declared incidental to ordinary governmental functions (**Art 289(2)–(3)**). In ***In Re: Sea Customs Act* (1963, 9-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court narrowed Article 289 immunity strictly to **direct taxes on State property and income**, holding that the Union may validly levy **indirect taxes** (customs, central excise) on goods imported or manufactured by State governments.

## Mineral Tax Federalism - *MADA v. SAIL* and the 2026 Statutory Countermove

Nowhere has the tug-of-war between resource-rich States and macroeconomic uniformity played out more dramatically than over mineral taxation. The Seventh Schedule splits mining authority asymmetrically: **List I Entry 54** lets Parliament regulate mines and mineral development in the public interest (exercised via the **Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 [MMDR Act]**), subordinating **List II Entry 23** (State mineral regulation).

Crucially, however, taxing entries reside in the State List with **divergent textual riders**:
- **List II Entry 50:** *"Taxes on mineral rights **subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development**."*
- **List II Entry 49:** *"Taxes on lands and buildings"* — containing **NO "subject to Parliament" subordination clause whatsoever**.

### Overruling *India Cement* - The July 25 & August 14, 2024 9-Judge Verdicts

For thirty-five years, States were stripped of mineral-taxing autonomy by a single doctrinal misstep. In ***India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1990 [decided Oct 1989], 7-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that **mining royalty is itself a tax**, rendering State cesses measured on royalty ultra vires. When a 5-Judge Bench in ***State of West Bengal v. Kesoram Industries Ltd.* (2004)** dismissed that line as a *"typographical error"*, the resulting conflict stayed pending for a quarter-century before reaching a **9-Judge Constitution Bench**.

On **July 25, 2024**, voting **8:1** (Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud authoring the majority opinion; **Justice B.V. Nagarathna** entering a lone dissent), the Bench in ***Mineral Area Development Authority (MADA) v. Steel Authority of India Ltd.* (`2024 INSC 554`)** delivered a landmark fiscal-federalism reset:

1. **Royalty Is Contractual, Not a Tax:** Royalty payable under Section 9 of the MMDR Act is a **contractual consideration** paid by a mining lessee to the lessor (proprietor of subsoil rights) for extracting minerals—not a compulsory sovereign tax exaction. ***India Cement* (1990) was expressly overruled**.
2. **Plenary State Taxing Competence under Entry 50:** Because Parliament's regulatory competence under List I Entry 54 does not encompass a taxing power, **States hold exclusive competence under List II Entry 50** to tax mineral rights. While Parliament *can* enact statutory limitations (even a prohibition) on such taxes under Entry 50's rider, the existing MMDR Act, 1957 contained **no such tax limitation**.
3. **Independent Competence over Mineral-Bearing Land (Entry 49):** States independently possess plenary authority under **List II Entry 49** to tax **mineral-bearing lands**, and may legitimately adopt **mineral yield, mineral value, or royalty** as the *measure* of that land tax without converting it into a prohibited levy. *(A 7-Judge Constitution Bench in **Arjun Flour Mills**, November 4, 2026, reaffirmed this sharp doctrinal line between the **subject** of a tax and its **measure** while upholding *S. Kodar*.)*
4. **Justice Nagarathna's Macroeconomic Dissent:** Warning that unchecked state-level levies would trigger cascading raw-material inflation across coal, steel, and power grids and provoke a destructive *"race to the bottom"*, Justice Nagarathna maintained that the MMDR Act occupied the entire fiscal field.

In its **August 14, 2024 Consequential Order**, the 9-Judge Bench rejected the Union's and PSU miners' plea for **prospective-only** operation. Balancing resource-rich States' fiscal reliance (Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan) against industrial shock, the Court permitted States to recover retrospective tax arrears strictly from **April 1, 2005 onwards**, staggered across **12 annual instalments beginning April 1, 2026**, while granting a **blanket waiver of all pre-July 25, 2024 interest and penalties**.

### The 2025–2026 Institutional Pushback - Curative Petition and Section 9D (MMDR Amendment Act, 2026)

After the Supreme Court dismissed the Union's **Review Petition in October 2024**, the Central Government escalated along two parallel tracks:
- **Judicial Track:** In **November 2025**, the Union filed a **Curative Petition** challenging *MADA* for alleged grave macroeconomic error—which remains pending adjudication as of November 2026.
- **Legislative Countermove — MMDR (Amendment) Act, 2026 (`Act No. 20 of 2026`):** Enacted rapidly in August 2026 (**Presidential Assent: August 17, 2026; effective August 22, 2026**), Parliament amended Section 2 of the MMDR Act to extend Union declaration to *"mineral bearing lands"* and inserted **Section 9D**:
  - **Section 9D(1):** Prohibits State Governments from imposing any tax, cess, or other levy on **(a) mineral rights** OR **(b) mineral-bearing lands** (whether based on mineral quantity, value, royalty, or otherwise) except under conditions or restrictions prescribed by the **Central Government**.
  - **Section 9D(2):** Operates *"notwithstanding... any judgment, decree or order of any court"* to invalidate all qualifying State levies not deposited with or collected by the State prior to commencement—extinguishing uncollected post-2005 retrospective arrears unlocked by *MADA*.

**Live GS2 Constitutional Fault Line:** While Parliament invoked the express limiting rider inside **List II Entry 50** to justify capping taxes on *mineral rights*, **List II Entry 49** (*taxes on lands and buildings*) carries **no constitutional subordination to Parliament**. Attempting to curb Entry 49 land taxes via an ordinary Entry 54 statute—and extinguishing court-validated State arrears via a sweeping *non-obstante* clause—sets up a high-stakes Basic Structure and Seventh Schedule test.

## GST Federalism - Pooled Sovereignty, *Mohit Minerals*, and the 2026 Compensation Sunset

### The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act (2016) and Pooled Sovereignty

Before 2017, India's indirect tax architecture resembled a fragmented tariff archipelago: the Union taxed manufacturing (Excise) and services, while States taxed intra-State sales (VAT), entry of goods (Octroi/Entry Tax), and entertainment, cascading across borders via origin-based Central Sales Tax (CST). The **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** replaced this compartmentalized fiscal sovereignty with **"pooled sovereignty"**—a unified, destination-based Value Added Tax system (**Goods and Services Tax**, launched July 1, 2017).

Four constitutional mechanisms anchor GST federalism:
- **Article 246A (Simultaneous Legislative Power):** Operates *"notwithstanding anything contained in articles 246 and 254"*, conferring **concurrent-yet-simultaneous** taxing power on Parliament and every State Legislature over intra-State supplies (**Art 246A(1)**), while vesting exclusive competence over inter-State supplies and imports in Parliament (**Art 246A(2)**).
- **Constitutional Carve-Outs (States' Last Tax Handles):** **Alcoholic liquor for human consumption** is permanently excluded from GST by constitutional definition (**Article 366(12A)**). Five **petroleum products**—*petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, motor spirit (petrol), natural gas, and aviation turbine fuel (ATF)*—are constitutionally deferred under **Article 279A(5)** until the GST Council recommends bringing them under GST. Together, alcohol and petroleum remain the primary autonomous revenue levers keeping State treasuries liquid.
- **No Union Surcharge on GST:** Article 271 explicitly prohibits Parliament from slapping unshared Union surcharges on GST, insulating the GST base from the cess-surcharge leak afflicting direct taxes.

### GST Council Architecture and Asymmetric Voting Math (Article 279A)

Constituted by the President within 60 days of the 101st CAA, the **GST Council (Article 279A)** is a permanent, self-created constitutional forum embodying cooperative fiscal governance. It is chaired by the **Union Finance Minister**, with the **Union Minister of State for Finance (Revenue)** and the **Finance/Taxation Minister (or nominated Minister) of every State** (plus legislative UTs—Delhi, Puducherry, J&K) as members.

Its decision rules embed a delicate **asymmetric dual veto**:
- **Quorum:** **50%** of total members of the Council.
- **Weighted Voting:** The **Central Government** holds **one-third ($33.33\%$)** of total votes cast; **all State Governments combined** hold **two-thirds ($66.67\%$)** (divided equally among States regardless of economic size or population).
- **Decision Threshold:** Every decision requires a majority of **not less than three-fourths ($75\%$)** of the weighted votes of members present and voting.

Check the arithmetic: because the Union alone commands **$33.33\%$** ($> 25\%$), **no resolution can pass without the Centre's approval**, even if all 28 States and 3 legislative UTs vote in unison. Conversely, States require a cross-party coalition of at least **37.5%+ of participating States** ($> 25\%$ total weight) to block a Union-backed proposal. Under **Article 279A(11)**, the Council is also mandated to establish a **dispute-adjudication mechanism** between the Union and States or among States—a constitutional obligation that remains un-operationalized nearly a decade later.

### *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022) - Persuasive Advice and "Uncooperative Federalism"

Did pooling tax sovereignty under Article 279A turn Parliament and State Legislatures into rubber stamps of the GST Council? On **May 19, 2022**, a 3-Judge Bench led by Justice D.Y. Chandrachud in ***Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.*** answered with a resounding **no**.

Striking down reverse-charge IGST levied on ocean freight in CIF import contracts, the Court established three landmark constitutional rules:
1. **Recommendations Are Persuasive, Not Binding on Legislatures:** Article 246A confers equal, simultaneous plenary power on Parliament and State Legislatures without making them subordinate to Article 279A. GST Council **"recommendations"** carry high persuasive and collaborative weight, **NOT legally binding force** over primary legislation.
2. **Primary Legislation vs. Delegated Rules:** While Parliament and State Legislatures remain sovereign when enacting GST Acts, where they *voluntarily* stipulate inside the CGST/SGST Acts that the executive shall notify tax rates or rules *"on the recommendations of the Council"*, delegated secondary rules remain statutorily bound by Council decisions.
3. **Validating "Uncooperative Federalism":** Rejecting the view that federal harmony demands unanimity, the Court celebrated **"uncooperative federalism"**—asserting that spirited dissent, bargaining, and refusal by States inside fiscal forums are legitimate democratic mechanisms protecting sub-national autonomy.

### GST 2.0 and the February 2026 Discontinuation of the Compensation Cess

To persuade manufacturing-heavy States to surrender origin-based VAT/CST, Parliament enacted the **GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017** (under Section 18 of the 101st CAA), guaranteeing States **14% annual compounded revenue growth** over a 2015–16 base for five years (**July 2017 – June 2022**), financed via a **GST Compensation Cess** on luxury and sin goods. When COVID-19 collapsed collections, the Centre borrowed ~₹2.69 lakh crore via back-to-back loans for States and extended the cess solely for debt servicing till March 31, 2026.

At the historic **56th GST Council Meeting (September 3, 2025)**, the Council launched **GST 2.0** (effective September 22, 2025)—collapsing the 12% and 28% tiers into a simplified **5% and 18% core structure plus a 40% demerit slab** on sin/luxury goods (and replacing coal's ₹400/tonne cess with 18% GST). Having retired the ₹2.69 lakh crore COVID loans ahead of schedule, the Centre **formally discontinued the GST Compensation Cess effective February 1, 2026**.

While simplifying compliance, this sunset triggered fresh fiscal friction across 2026: with GST rate rationalization trimming near-term revenues and the compensation shield permanently gone, producing and welfare-heavy States alike renewed demands inside the Council for a **post-2026 revenue-risk guarantee**—highlighting a widening institutional chasm where the Finance Commission defers GST shortfalls to the GST Council, and the Council points back to the Finance Commission. *(Macro GST slab mechanics, input tax credit chains, and inflation impacts are treated in Economy.)*

## 16th Finance Commission Formula and Horizontal Devolution

### Constitutional Mandate, Quasi-Judicial Profile, and Action Taken (Articles 280 & 281)

Envisioned in the Constituent Assembly as the **"balancing wheel of fiscal federalism"**, the **Finance Commission (Article 280)** is a **quasi-judicial constitutional body** constituted by the President **every five years** (or earlier). Under **Article 280(2)** and the **Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951**, it comprises a **Chairman** (experienced in public affairs) and **four members** drawn from High Court judges/eligible jurists, public-finance accounting experts, financial administrators, and specialized economists.

Its four constitutional duties under **Article 280(3)** are recommending: **(a)** vertical and horizontal sharing of net Union tax proceeds; **(b)** principles governing **Article 275** grants-in-aid; **(c)** measures to augment State Consolidated Funds to supplement **Panchayats (Art 243I)** and **Municipalities (Art 243Y)** based on State Finance Commission reports; and **(d)** any other matter referred by the President in the interest of sound finance. Though **advisory** (non-binding legally), its core devolution formulas are accepted by unbroken constitutional convention. Under **Article 281**, the President must lay the Commission's report alongside an **Explanatory Memorandum of Action Taken (ATR)** before both Houses of Parliament.

Constituted on **December 31, 2023**, the **16th Finance Commission**—chaired by **Dr. Arvind Panagariya** (with members **T. Rabi Sankar** [replacing Ajay Narayan Jha], **Annie George Mathew**, **Dr. Manoj Panda** [replacing Dr. Niranjan Rajadhyaksha], and part-time member **Dr. Soumya Kanti Ghosh**; Secretary: **Ritwik Ranjanam Pandey**)—submitted its report to the President on **November 17, 2025**. Tabled alongside the Union Budget on **February 1, 2026**, its accepted recommendations govern the five-year award period **FY 2026–27 to FY 2030–31** (launching FY 2026–27 BE transfers of **₹16.56 lakh crore**: ₹15.26 lakh crore in tax devolution plus ₹1.4 lakh crore in FC grants).

### Vertical Devolution (41%) and the "Grand Bargain" on Cess-Surcharge Erosion

The 16th FC **retained States' vertical share in the Divisible Pool at 41%**—unchanged from the 15th FC (which had adjusted the 14th FC's historic jump from 32% to 42% downward by 1% solely to accommodate the newly carved Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh). Rejecting demands from over 18 States to raise vertical devolution to **50%**, the Commission imparted *"semi-permanence"* to the 41% anchor.

Crucially, however, headline devolution masks severe **base erosion via cesses and surcharges**. Excluded from Article 270, Union cesses and surcharges (plus collection costs) hit a decade peak of **13.5% of Gross Tax Revenue (GTR)** in 2021–22 and stood at **₹4.23 lakh crore in 2025–26**—dragging States' *effective* share of Central GTR down from **~35% in 2015–16 to ~30% in 2023–24**.

To break this deadlock without a constitutional amendment, the 16th FC proposed two institutional remedies:
1. **A "Grand Bargain":** The Union should merge most cesses and surcharges into basic shareable taxes to expand the divisible pool, in exchange for States accepting a calibrated nominal devolution share yielding higher predictable, untied flows.
2. **Article 279 Net-Proceeds Transparency:** Mandatory annual public disclosure of **CAG-certified net tax proceeds** under Article 279 to eliminate opacity over actual divisible-pool calculations.

### The Overhauled Horizontal Formula - Rebalancing Equity and Economic Efficiency

While holding the vertical pie steady, the 16th Finance Commission executed India's boldest horizontal redesign in decades—shifting decisively from pure **"entitlement-based equalization"** toward **"performance- and output-weighted federalism"**:

- **Contribution to GDP — NEW (10% weight; replaces Tax & Fiscal Efforts 2.5%):** For the first time, economic output directly enters tax devolution. Rather than using raw GSDP (which would let mega-economies like Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, and Uttar Pradesh monopolize the slice), the 16th FC applied a **concave square-root transformation**: each State's weight equals the **square root of its average nominal GSDP** (2018–19 to 2023–24, excluding pandemic year 2020–21) divided by the sum of square roots of all States' GSDPs. This rewards productive industrial hubs without crushing smaller efficient States.
- **Income Distance (42.5%, trimmed from 45%):** Remains the dominant equity anchor, but its benchmark shifted from the 15th FC's **highest single large State** (Haryana) to the **average per capita GSDP of the top three large States** (2018–19 to 2023–24, excluding 2020–21). Lower-income States continue receiving larger shares, though the 2.5 percentage-point cut moderates pure redistribution.
- **Population — 2011 Census (17.5%, raised from 15%):** Retains 2011 Census baselines (abandoning 1971 Census weights permanently after 14th FC's transition) while increasing its share by 2.5 percentage points to capture scale-driven public expenditure needs.
- **Demographic Performance (10%, trimmed from 12.5%):** Completely **redefined**—replacing the 15th FC's inverse Total Fertility Rate (TFR) metric with **actual population growth between 1971 and 2011**. States achieving slower forty-year demographic expansion (primarily Southern States) score higher, even as the Commission flagged the coming *"aging before becoming rich"* fiscal risk and trimmed the weight by 2.5 percentage points.
- **Area (10%, trimmed from 15%):** Reduced by 5 percentage points to create headroom for the GDP contribution criterion, moderating the windfall to geographically sprawling States like Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh.
- **Forest (10%, unchanged weight, broadened definition):** Whereas the 15th FC counted only *very dense* and *moderately dense* forest canopy, the 16th FC expanded the metric to incorporate **open forests** AND **forest cover gains between 2015 and 2023**—rewarding active afforestation rather than static legacy canopy alone.

### North–South Inter-State Realignment, Abolition of RDGs, and the Art 275 vs. Art 282 Tension

The 16th FC formula produced immediate, measurable horizontal realignments across India's 28 States:
- **Major Gainers (Efficiency & Southern/Western Tilt):** **Karnataka** gained the most (**+48 bps** to **4.13%**), followed by **Kerala (+46 bps** to **2.38%**), **Gujarat (+28 bps)**, **Haryana (+27 bps** to **1.36%**), and **Punjab (+19 bps)**—alongside gains for **Andhra Pradesh (4.22%)**, **Telangana (2.17%)**, **Maharashtra**, and a marginal uptick for **Tamil Nadu (+1.8 bps**, rising from **4.079% to 4.097%**).
- **Major Losers (Populous/Large-Area & Himalayan Contraction):** **Madhya Pradesh (-50 bps** to **7.35%**), **Arunachal Pradesh (-41 bps** to **1.35%**), **Uttar Pradesh (-32 bps** to **17.62%**, still receiving the single largest share), **West Bengal (-31 bps** to **7.22%**), **Bihar (-11 bps** to **9.95%**), **Odisha (-11 bps)**, **Rajasthan (5.93%)**, and **Sikkim (0.34%**, lowest overall).

Yet political friction cut in **both directions**. Southern States—citing high GST contributions, demographic aging, and acute urban-infrastructure bills—argued that stagnant 41% devolution, shrunk untied flows from cesses, and the **complete abolition of Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants (RDGs)** (which totaled ₹2.94 lakh crore under the 15th FC) hit fiscally stressed States like Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Punjab, and Himachal Pradesh abruptly. Conversely, poorer Northern and Northeastern States warned that replacing equity weights with GDP contribution risks entrenching structural divergence between coastal growth corridors and lagging heartland economies.

Simultaneously, the 16th FC concentrated its **₹9.47 lakh crore** in statutory **Article 275** grants into just two channels:
1. **Third-Tier Local Bodies (₹7.91 lakh crore — ₹4.35 lakh cr RLBs + ₹3.56 lakh cr ULBs):** Split **80% Basic** (50% untied + 50% tied to sanitation/SWM and water) and **20% Performance** (linked to State resource transfers and local Own Source Revenue growth), supplemented by **₹56,100 crore in Special Infrastructure Grants** for wastewater systems in cities of 10–40 lakh population and **₹10,000 crore in Urbanisation Premium Grants** for merging peri-urban villages—all strictly conditioned on three entry gates (constitutional local elections, public audited accounts, and timely State Finance Commissions).
2. **Disaster Risk Management (₹2,04,401 crore total SDRF/SDMF corpus; Union share ₹1,55,916 crore):** Retaining **90:10** cost-sharing for Northeastern/Himalayan States and **75:25** for general States.

Beyond the Finance Commission, however, lies the chronic **Article 275 vs. Article 282** distortion. Even after the 2015 Sub-Group of Chief Ministers rationalized Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) into *Core of the Core*, *Core (60:40 / 90:10)*, and *Optional* tiers, heavy Union reliance on conditional **Article 282** CSS transfers forces States to divert untied revenues toward matching central priorities. Rounding out its tough fiscal-discipline stance, the 16th FC flagged the explosion of **unconditional cash transfers**—which jumped from 3% in 2018–19 to **20.2% of State subsidy spending across 21 States in 2025–26** (with large-group cash schemes reaching 47.4% of transfers)—urged closure of **308 inactive SPSEs**, and tied special capital-investment assistance for legacy DISCOM debt to **DISCOM privatization**.

## Public Borrowing and Sub-National Debt Federalism - Article 293 and *State of Kerala v. UoI*

With GST harmonizing indirect taxes and the Finance Commission locking tax shares for five-year blocks, **public borrowing has emerged as the sharpest live frontier of Indian fiscal federalism**.

### The Asymmetric Constitutional Frame (Articles 292 & 293)

The Constitution deliberately separates sovereign borrowing powers across Articles 292 and 293:

- **Article 292 (Union Borrowing):** The Union executive may borrow **both within AND outside India** upon the security of the Consolidated Fund of India, within limits fixed by Parliament by law. Tellingly, Parliament has **never enacted a statutory debt ceiling directly under Article 292**, relying instead on policy-anchored glide paths under the **FRBM Act, 2003**.
- **Article 293(1) (Strict Domestic Tether for States):** A State executive may borrow **ONLY within the territory of India** upon the security of the Consolidated Fund of the State, within limits fixed by its State Legislature. States are **constitutionally barred from borrowing directly from foreign governments or international bond markets**; external multilateral loans (World Bank, ADB, JICA) must route through the Government of India on a back-to-back basis.
- **Articles 293(2), (3) & (4) (Permanent Central Gatekeeping):** The Union may lend to States or guarantee State loans (**Art 293(2)**). Crucially, under **Article 293(3)**, a State **may NOT raise "any loan" without the consent of the Government of India** if *any part* of a prior Union loan—or a loan guaranteed by the Union—remains outstanding. Under **Article 293(4)**, the Union may grant consent *"subject to such conditions, if any, as it may think fit to impose"*.

Because every single Indian State owes historical and ongoing loans to New Delhi (including 50-year interest-free Special Assistance capex loans), **Article 293(3) operates in practice as a permanent Union veto over State open-market borrowing** (State Development Loans auctioned via the RBI).

### Off-Budget SPSE Liabilities and Net Borrowing Ceilings (NBC)

Following the 12th Finance Commission's debt-relief compact, all States enacted **Fiscal Responsibility Legislation (FRLs)** anchoring fiscal deficits near **3% of GSDP**. Facing rising committed spending (interest, salaries, and pensions absorbed **52% of State revenue receipts** in 2026–27 estimates) and revenue shocks, several States increasingly routed capital expenditure and social-security payouts **off-budget** through State Public Sector Enterprises (SPSEs) and Special Purpose Vehicles—most visibly Kerala's **Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board (KIIFB)** and **Kerala Social Security Pension Ltd. (KSSPL)**—whose debt was guaranteed and serviced out of State budgets.

Citing the 15th Finance Commission and the 2018 amendment to Section 4 of the FRBM Act, the Union Ministry of Finance from 2022–23 onward tightened its Article 293(3) consent letters by **deducting off-budget SPSE borrowings and net Public Account liabilities directly from States' annual Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)** (capped at 3% of GSDP, plus conditional power-sector tranches). The **16th Finance Commission (February 2026)** strongly backed this fiscal orthodoxy, recommending that States **strictly discontinue all off-budget borrowings** and uniformly fold them into official fiscal deficit and public debt definitions.

### *State of Kerala v. Union of India* (`2024 INSC 253`) - The Pending 5-Judge Constitution Bench Showdown

In December 2023, Kerala filed **Original Suit No. 1 of 2024 under Article 131**, challenging the Union's March and August 2023 NBC letters and Section 4 of the amended FRBM Act, 2018 as an unconstitutional infringement on fiscal federalism and State legislative autonomy.

On **April 1, 2024**, a 2-Judge Bench (**Justices Surya Kant and K.V. Viswanathan**) delivered an order carrying two major constitutional markers:
1. **Refusal of Interim Borrowing Relief (Triple-Test Failure):** Rejecting Kerala's plea for an immediate ₹10,722 crore borrowing injunction, the Bench held that unchecked sub-national over-borrowing creates systemic macroeconomic spillovers affecting sovereign credit ratings and national interest rates—tipping the *"balance of convenience"* in favour of the Union. Yet the Court sharply **disapproved of the Union's attempt to condition interim liquidity on Kerala withdrawing its Article 131 suit**, affirming that invoking Article 131 is an inviolable constitutional right.
2. **Reference to a 5-Judge Constitution Bench (Art 145(3)):** Observing that **Article 293 had never received authoritative judicial interpretation** since 1950, the Bench referred **seven substantial constitutional questions** to a 5-Judge Constitution Bench—**still pending adjudication as of November 2026**:
   - Does **Article 293** vest an enforceable constitutional right in a State to borrow from the Union or open markets, and how far may the Union regulate it under Art 293(3)–(4)?
   - Does the phrase **"any loan"** in Article 293(3) encompass **open-market loans, SPSE/parastatal off-budget borrowings, and Public Account liabilities**?
   - What is the permissible scope of judicial review over Union fiscal-policy caps under Article 14 (manifest arbitrariness) and federalism, and do unilateral Union ceilings conflict with the **RBI's statutory role as public debt manager**?

> - **Alcohol vs. Petroleum GST Exclusion Nuance:** **Alcoholic liquor for human consumption** is **permanently excluded** by constitutional text (**Art 366(12A)**, needing an Art 368 amendment to include); the **5 petroleum products** are merely **deferred** under **Art 279A(5)** and can be brought into GST via a GST Council recommendation without amending the Constitution!
> - **MADA v. SAIL (2024) + MMDR Amendment Act, 2026 Dual Trap:** *MADA* (9-Judge, 8:1) overruled *India Cement* to hold **mining royalty is NOT a tax** (it is contractual consideration) and affirmed State taxing power under **List II Entries 49 & 50**. However, Parliament's **MMDR (Amendment) Act, 2026 (Section 9D, effective Aug 22, 2026)** subsequently barred fresh State taxes/cesses on mineral rights AND mineral-bearing lands without Central conditions and extinguished uncollected retrospective arrears.

> - **Article 279 CAG Finality & Article 274 Prior Recommendation:** CAG certification of *"net proceeds"* under **Art 279** is **final**; Bills altering agricultural income definition, State-interest taxes, or Art 271 surcharges require **prior Presidential recommendation (Art 274)**.
> - **Article 292 vs. Article 293 Borrowing Trap:** The Union can borrow domestically AND abroad (**Art 292**); States can borrow **strictly within India (Art 293(1))** and require **Central consent for ANY loan** so long as any Union/Union-guaranteed loan remains unpaid (**Art 293(3)**).
> - **Finance Commission Status Trap:** The FC is a **constitutional, quasi-judicial, NON-permanent** body whose recommendations are **advisory** (not legally binding), constituted under **Art 280** (NOT a statutory body—though member qualifications are set by the 1951 Act).
