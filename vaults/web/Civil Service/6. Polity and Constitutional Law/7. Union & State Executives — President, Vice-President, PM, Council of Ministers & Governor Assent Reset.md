# Chapter 7 - Union & State Executives — President, Vice-President, PM, Council of Ministers & Governor Assent Reset

India’s executive architecture rests on a deliberate constitutional asymmetry. The Constituent Assembly fused the **Westminster model of responsible parliamentary government**—prioritizing continuous democratic accountability over rigid executive stability—onto a **quasi-federal republic** with an indirectly elected Head of State at the Union and a centrally appointed constitutional head in every State.

This structural choice generates two enduring institutional tensions that dominate both Prelims mechanics and Mains GS2 jurisprudence. At the Union level, the friction lies between **constitutional formalism** (executive power vested in the President) and **political centralism** (the ascent of Cabinet and Prime Ministerial dominance over Parliament). At the State level, the friction is far sharper: the Governor simultaneously acts as the **constitutional head of a responsible State government** and the **federal sentinel of the Union**, a dual mandate that culminated in the historic **April–November 2025 Article 200/201 Bill Assent Showdown**.

## The President of India — Architecture, Election & Removal (Articles 52–62, 71)

Part V, Chapter I (**Articles 52–78**) defines the Union Executive as comprising the President, the Vice-President, the Prime Minister, the Council of Ministers, and the Attorney General of India. Under **Article 53(1)**, the executive power of the Union vests in the President and is exercised either directly or through subordinate officers in accordance with the Constitution, while **Article 53(2)** vests the Supreme Command of the Defence Forces in the President, subject to regulation by parliamentary law.

Every executive action of the Government of India is formally expressed to be taken in the President’s name (**Article 77(1)**), and authenticated under rules framed by the President (**Article 77(2)–(3)**). Under **Article 78**, the Prime Minister bears an explicit constitutional duty to communicate all Cabinet decisions relating to Union administration and legislative proposals to the President, furnish requested information, and—if the President requires—submit for the Council of Ministers' collective consideration any matter decided individually by a minister.

### Electoral College, Vote-Value Arithmetic & Disputes (Articles 54–55, 71)

The President is indirectly elected by an **Electoral College** (**Article 54**) designed to ensure federal parity between the Union and the States without creating a directly elected rival to the Prime Minister. Membership is strictly confined to **elected representatives**:

- **Included:** Elected members of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha), elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of all States, and elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the Union Territories of **NCT Delhi and Puducherry** (added by the **70th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992**, effective 1995).
- **Excluded:** Nominated members of both Houses of Parliament (12 in Rajya Sabha), nominated members of State Assemblies, **all members (elected and nominated) of State Legislative Councils**, and elected members of the Legislative Assembly of the **Union Territory of Jammu & Kashmir** (because J&K’s UT assembly has not been inserted into the Explanation to Article 54 via a constitutional amendment).

**Article 55** engineers two mathematical principles—**uniformity** in the scale of representation among different States, and **parity** between all States taken together and the Union Parliament:

$$\text{Value of 1 MLA's Vote} = \frac{\text{Total Population of the State (1971 Census)}}{\text{Total Elected Members of the State Legislative Assembly} \times 1000}$$

If the remainder after dividing by 1,000 is **500 or more**, the vote value of each MLA increases by 1. The total value of all MLAs' votes across India is then divided equally among all elected MPs:

$$\text{Value of 1 MP's Vote} = \frac{\text{Total Value of Votes of All MLAs of All States and Eligible UTs}}{\text{Total Number of Elected Members of Both Houses of Parliament}}$$

Two structural nuances are chronic Prelims traps. First, "population" in Article 55 remains frozen at the **1971 Census**—originally fixed by the 42nd CAA (1976) and extended by the **84th CAA (2001)** until the relevant figures of the **first Census taken after 2026** are published. Second, voting occurs via **Proportional Representation by Single Transferable Vote (PR-STV)** through a **secret ballot**; political parties **cannot issue whips**, and cross-voting does not attract Tenth Schedule disqualification. Victory requires securing an **electoral quota** ($\lfloor \frac{\text{Total Valid Votes}}{2} \rfloor + 1$), not a mere plurality.

Under **Article 58**, a presidential candidate must be a citizen of India (by birth **or** naturalization, unlike the US natural-born citizen requirement), at least **35 years old**, qualified for election as a **Lok Sabha** member, and hold no **Office of Profit** under the Union, State, or local authorities. Crucially, sitting Presidents, Vice-Presidents, Governors, and Union or State Ministers are constitutionally deemed *not* to hold an office of profit for this purpose. Under **Article 60**, the President takes an oath—administered by the Chief Justice of India—to **"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law."**

Election disputes are exclusively and finally adjudicated by the **Supreme Court** (**Article 71(1)**). If the Supreme Court voids a President’s election, acts done by them prior to the declaration remain valid (**Article 71(2)**). Crucially, under **Article 71(4)** (inserted by the **11th CAA, 1961**, upheld in *In Re: Presidential Poll*, 1974), a Presidential or Vice-Presidential election **cannot be challenged on the ground of any vacancy in the Electoral College**—meaning a dissolved State Assembly does not halt a Presidential poll. Under **Article 62(2)**, a casual vacancy caused by death, resignation (addressed to the Vice-President, who must immediately communicate it to the Lok Sabha Speaker under Art 56(2)), or removal must be filled **within six months**, and the newly elected President serves a **full five-year term**.

### Impeachment Mechanics (Article 61)

Article 61 creates the **strictest removal threshold** anywhere in the Indian Constitution. The sole ground is **"violation of the Constitution"**—a phrase deliberately left undefined in the constitutional text.

- **Initiation:** Charges may be preferred by **either House of Parliament** via a resolution moved after at least **14 days' written notice** signed by not less than **one-fourth of the total membership** of that House.
- **First Passage:** The resolution must be passed by a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the total membership** of the initiating House (not merely two-thirds present and voting).
- **Quasi-Judicial Investigation:** The other House investigates the charge (or causes it to be investigated), during which the President has the constitutional right to appear and be represented.
- **Removal:** If the investigating House passes a resolution by **not less than two-thirds of its total membership** sustaining the charge, the President stands removed from that date.

Observe the **electoral–impeachment asymmetry**: **nominated MPs** of Parliament do *not* vote in the Presidential election, yet **participate in impeachment**; conversely, **elected MLAs** of States and eligible UTs vote in the election, yet have **zero role in impeachment**. No Indian President has ever been impeached.

## Westminster Aid & Advice, Vetoes, Ordinances & Clemency

### Aid and Advice — From Convention to Binding Text (Articles 74–75)

The original text of **Article 74(1)** stated simply that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to "aid and advise the President in the exercise of his functions." Early disputes—notably President Rajendra Prasad’s assertion of independent authority over the Hindu Code Bill—forced the Supreme Court to settle the Westminster nature of the Indian executive:

- ***Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955):** Held that the Indian Constitution mirrors the British parliamentary cabinet system; the President is a formal constitutional head, while real executive power resides in the Council of Ministers accountable to the legislature.
- ***U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi* (1971):** Held that **Article 74(1) is mandatory**, not optional. Even after the Lok Sabha is dissolved, a caretaker Council of Ministers must remain in office to aid and advise the President; the President cannot exercise executive power without ministerial advice.
- ***Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Cemented the foundational rule of Indian executive jurisprudence. Wherever the Constitution requires the **"satisfaction"** of the President or the Governor (including under Articles 123, 213, 352, 356, 311(2)(c)), it means the **institutional satisfaction of the Council of Ministers**, not personal satisfaction—except in the narrow spheres where the Constitution expressly requires the *Governor* to act in discretion.

Parliament codified this doctrine through two paired amendments: the **42nd CAA (1976)** explicitly bound the President to act in accordance with ministerial advice, and the **44th CAA (1978)** inserted the **Proviso to Article 74(1)** empowering the President to send advice back for **reconsideration once** (as President K.R. Narayanan did successfully against President’s Rule recommendations for Uttar Pradesh in 1997 and Bihar in 1998), after which reconsidered advice becomes unconditionally binding.

Under **Article 74(2)**, courts are barred from inquiring into *whether any, and if so what*, advice was tendered by Ministers to the President. However, a 9-Judge Bench in ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994)** clarified that Article 74(2) protects only the *advice itself*, **not the underlying material** on which that advice was formed—enabling judicial review of executive proclamations for *mala fides* or wholly irrelevant grounds.

Unlike the Governor, the President possesses **zero express constitutional discretion**. Presidential discretion is strictly **situational**, arising only when parliamentary arithmetic breaks down: appointing a Prime Minister in a hung Lok Sabha or upon the sudden death of an incumbent with no clear successor; dismissing a Council of Ministers that refuses to resign after losing a Lok Sabha floor vote; and deciding whether to dissolve the Lok Sabha when advised to do so by a Cabinet that has already lost its majority.

### Presidential Veto Architecture (Articles 111, 201 & 368)

Every parliamentary Bill requires Presidential assent under **Article 111** to become law. Indian constitutional practice recognizes three veto modalities (unlike the US President's *qualified veto*, which Congress can override only by a 2/3rd supermajority):

1. **Absolute Veto (Withholding Assent):** The Bill dies permanently. Because the President acts on ministerial advice, this is exercised primarily on **Private Members' Bills** passed by Parliament or on Government Bills when the Cabinet resigns before assent and the incoming Cabinet advises withholding (e.g., President Rajendra Prasad on the PEPSU Appropriation Bill, 1954; President R. Venkataraman on the Salary, Allowances and Pension of MPs Amendment Bill, 1991).
2. **Suspensive Veto (Return for Reconsideration):** Under the Proviso to Article 111, the President may return a non-Money Bill once for reconsideration. If both Houses pass the Bill again—with or without amendments—by an **ordinary simple majority**, the President **must** give assent. **Money Bills cannot be returned** under Article 111 (they may only be assented to or withheld, though convention precludes withholding since Money Bills are introduced with prior Presidential recommendation).
3. **Pocket Veto (Indefinite Inaction):** Because Article 111 prescribes **no time limit** for Presidential action (contrasting with the US President's 10-day clock), the President can keep a Bill pending indefinitely—most famously exercised by President Giani Zail Singh on the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986.

Two sharp constitutional boundaries complete the veto matrix. First, for **Constitutional Amendment Bills under Article 368**, the **24th CAA (1971)** stripped the President of all veto options—the President **"shall give his assent."** Second, for **State Bills reserved by a Governor under Article 200**, the President acts under **Article 201**: the President may assent, withhold assent, or direct the Governor to return a non-Money Bill to the State Legislature, which must reconsider it within **six months**. Crucially, even if the State Legislature re-passes the Bill and re-submits it, the **President is NOT bound to give assent**—granting the Union executive an effective absolute or pocket veto over reserved State legislation, whose non-justiciability and freedom from court-imposed timelines was reaffirmed by the Constitution Bench in **November 2025 (`2025 INSC 1333`)**.

### Ordinance-Making Power — Constitutional Safeguards & Anti-Fraud Jurisprudence (Articles 123 & 213)

Modelled on Section 42 of the Government of India Act, 1935, the ordinance power allows the executive to legislate during a parliamentary recess—a feature absent from both the British and American constitutions. Under **Article 123** (President) and **Article 213** (Governor), an ordinance is **not a parallel source of legislation**, but an emergency stopgap bound by strict pre-conditions:

- **Legislative Recess:** Either **both Houses** or **one House** of Parliament must not be in session (because lawmaking requires passage by both Houses; an ordinance issued while both Houses are in session is void). In States, the Legislative Assembly (or either House in a bicameral State) must not be in session.
- **Immediate Action Satisfaction:** The President/Governor (acting on ministerial aid and advice) must be satisfied that circumstances exist rendering **immediate action** necessary.
- **Co-Extensive Competence & Scope:** An ordinance carries the same force and limitations as an Act of the respective legislature. It **can amend or repeal any statutory Act** (Central or State), alter tax laws, and take **retrospective (backdated) effect**. However, it **cannot amend the Constitution**, cannot traverse outside the legislature’s Seventh Schedule competence, and **cannot abridge Fundamental Rights** because **Article 13(3)(a)** explicitly defines "law" to include ordinances.
- **Lifespan & Mandatory Laying:** Every ordinance must be laid before both Houses upon reassembly. It ceases to operate **six weeks from the date of reassembly** (if Houses reassemble on different dates, six weeks are counted from the *later* date), unless approved earlier by an Act, disapproved earlier by resolutions of both Houses, or withdrawn at any time by the President/Governor. Combining the six-month maximum gap between sessions (**Article 85/174**) with the six-week post-reassembly window yields a theoretical maximum lifespan of **six months and six weeks**.
- **Three Mandatory Presidential-Instruction Triggers for Governors (Proviso to Art 213(1)):** Unlike the President, a Governor **cannot promulgate an ordinance without prior instructions from the President** if: (i) a Bill containing the same provisions would have required previous Presidential sanction for introduction (e.g., **Article 304(b)** trade restrictions); (ii) the Governor would have deemed it necessary to reserve such a Bill for Presidential consideration; or (iii) a State Act with those provisions would be invalid without Presidential assent (e.g., repugnancy override under **Article 254(2)**).

The Supreme Court has progressively erected a doctrinal wall against **"Ordinance Raj"**:

- ***R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (1970)** & ***A.K. Roy v. Union of India* (1982):** Clarified that Presidential satisfaction is open to challenge if actuated by *mala fides* or wholly extraneous motives. Although the **38th CAA (1975)** attempted to make ordinance satisfaction final and non-justiciable, the **44th CAA (1978)** deleted that immunity clause.
- ***D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987, 5-Judge Bench):** Confronting Bihar's routine re-promulgation of 256 ordinances between 1967 and 1981 (some kept alive for 14 years without legislative debate), the Court held that mechanical **re-promulgation of ordinances without placing them before the legislature is a "fraud on the Constitution"** and a colourable usurpation of legislative power.
- ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2017, 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Delivered the definitive modern reset, overruling earlier benches (*Bhupendra Kumar Bose*, 1962; *T. Venkata Reddy*, 1985) that had treated rights created under lapsed ordinances as permanently enduring. The 7-Judge Bench established four binding rules: (1) placing an ordinance before the legislature under Articles 123(2)/213(2) is a **mandatory constitutional obligation**, failure of which constitutes constitutional fraud; (2) re-promulgation subverts parliamentary democracy; (3) executive satisfaction under Articles 123/213 is **subject to judicial review** for oblique motive or fraud; and (4) **no irreversible or enduring rights automatically survive** when an ordinance lapses or fails legislative approval—prior actions survive only under the narrow test of **constitutional necessity and public interest** where undoing them is practically impossible or grossly unjust.

### Pardoning & Clemency Jurisprudence (Articles 72 & 161)

Executive clemency serves as a constitutional safety valve against judicial infallibility and undue rigour. Articles 72 (President) and 161 (Governor) deploy five distinct instruments:

- **Pardon:** Completely absolves the convict of both the **sentence and the conviction**, erasing all punishments, penalties, and statutory disqualifications.
- **Commutation:** Substitutes one form of punishment for a **lighter character** (e.g., death sentence commuted to life imprisonment; rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment).
- **Remission:** Reduces the **period/duration** of the sentence without altering its nature (e.g., five years' rigorous imprisonment reduced to two years).
- **Respite:** Awards a **lesser sentence** in place of the original sentence due to a special personal circumstance (e.g., physical disability or pregnancy of a woman offender).
- **Reprieve:** Grants a **temporary stay** on the execution of a sentence (especially death), affording the convict time to seek pardon or commutation from the President.

**Jurisdictional Asymmetry:** The President’s power under **Article 72** spans three domains: (a) punishments or sentences by a **Court-Martial**; (b) offences against any law relating to a matter to which Union executive power extends; and (c) **all cases where the sentence is a sentence of death** (even if the conviction arose under a State law). The Governor’s power under **Article 161** covers only offences against laws within State executive competence. A Governor has **zero jurisdiction over Court-Martial sentences** and **cannot grant an outright pardon for a death sentence**. However, as clarified in *Maru Ram* (1980) and ***State of Haryana v. Raj Kumar* (2021)**, a Governor **can suspend, remit, or commute a death sentence** (or a life sentence) in a State-law offence, and constitutional clemency under Articles 72/161 overrides the statutory 14-year minimum incarceration cap imposed by **Section 433A CrPC (now Section 475 BNSS, 2023)**.

Five landmark verdicts govern the boundaries of constitutional clemency and statutory remission:

1. ***Maru Ram v. Union of India* (1980, 5-Judge Bench) & *Kehar Singh v. Union of India* (1989, 5-Judge Bench):** Established that clemency under Articles 72 and 161 is exercised **strictly on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers** (not personal discretion). Under *Kehar Singh*, the President may independently scrutinize the evidence and take a view different from the courts without acting as an appellate tribunal, and the petitioner has **no constitutional right to an oral hearing**.
2. ***Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh* (2006):** Codified **limited judicial review** of clemency orders on five specific grounds: (i) passed without application of mind; (ii) *mala fide*; (iii) based on extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations (such as political affiliation, religion, or caste); (iv) relevant material kept out of consideration; or (v) suffers from arbitrariness.
3. ***Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014):** Held that **inordinate, unexplained, and unreasonable executive delay** in deciding mercy petitions violates **Article 21** (cruel psychological torture on death row) and constitutes a supervening circumstance entitling the prisoner to **commutation to life imprisonment**. The Court refused to prescribe a rigid time limit for the President, but also recognized mental illness and unlawful solitary confinement as commutation grounds and mandated a minimum **14-day gap** between communicating mercy rejection and execution.
4. ***A.G. Perarivalan v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2022):** Held that in offences falling within State executive competence (e.g., murder under IPC Section 302), the **Governor is bound by the State Cabinet’s recommendation under Article 161** and has no authority to sit on a mercy plea for years or refer an Article 161 advice to the President.
5. ***Bilkis Yakub Rasool v. Union of India* (January 8, 2024, `2024 INSC 24`):** Drawing a razor-sharp line between **constitutional clemency (Art 72/161)** and **statutory remission (Sections 432–435 CrPC, now Sections 473–477 BNSS, 2023)**, the Court quashed the Gujarat government’s August 2022 premature release of eleven convicts in the 2002 gang-rape and massacre case. It held that under Section 432(7) CrPC (BNSS Section 473(7)), when a trial is transferred across states, the **"appropriate Government"** empowered to consider remission is strictly the State where **trial and conviction occurred (Maharashtra)**, not the State where the crime took place (Gujarat). Because Gujarat lacked jurisdiction and acted pursuant to an earlier order obtained through suppression of material facts (*fraud on court*), while bypassing negative opinions from the CBI and the Mumbai Special Judge, the remission orders were declared *non est*—affirming that executive leniency must bow to rule of law and victims’ rights.

**Comparative Note (India vs. USA — 2025 GS2 Focus):** Under Article II, Section 2 of the US Constitution, the American President exercises clemency in **personal, unfettered discretion** without Cabinet advice, limited strictly to **federal offences** (zero authority over State crimes) and excluding impeachments. Crucially, American jurisprudence (*Ex parte Garland*, 1866) permits **"pre-emptive pardons"**—clemency granted *before* formal indictment or conviction (e.g., Gerald Ford’s 1974 pardon of Richard Nixon; Joe Biden’s 2024–25 pre-emptive pardons). India’s Article 72 text (*"convicted of any offence"*) strictly restricts clemency to **post-conviction** cases, binds the President to Cabinet advice, extends even to State-law death sentences, and subjects clemency to *Epuru Sudhakar* judicial review.

## The Vice-President of India & the 2025 Mid-Term Vacancy Precedent (Articles 63–71)

Modelled broadly on the American Vice-Presidency, the Indian Vice-President (**Article 63**) occupies the second-highest office in the warrant of precedence and serves as the **ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** (**Articles 64 and 89**). Yet the constitutional succession design diverges sharply from the United States: when a US President dies or resigns, the Vice-President succeeds to the Presidency for the *entire unexpired term*; in India, under **Article 65**, the Vice-President merely acts as **Acting President** until a fresh President is elected within **six months**, during which period the Vice-President ceases to perform the duties of Rajya Sabha Chairman (**Article 64 Proviso**).

- **Electoral College (Article 66(1)):** Comprises **all members—both elected AND nominated—of both Houses of Parliament** (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha). Unlike the Presidential poll, **State and UT MLAs are completely excluded** (Dr. Ambedkar defended this because the Vice-President’s primary routine function is presiding over the Council of States, with Presidential succession being only temporary). Voting uses PR-STV by secret ballot.
- **Qualifications & Oath (Articles 66(3), 69):** Citizen of India, minimum age **35**, qualified for election as a member of the **Rajya Sabha** (contrast President: Lok Sabha), and holding no office of profit. Oath is administered by the President (or a nominee).
- **Removal Procedure (Article 67(b)):** The Constitution prescribes **no formal impeachment** and **enumerates no specific grounds** for removing the Vice-President. A removal resolution can be introduced **only in the Rajya Sabha** (after 14 days' notice), must be passed by an **Effective Majority** (*"a majority of all the then members of the Council"*, i.e., more than 50% of total membership excluding actual vacant seats), and must be **agreed to by the Lok Sabha** by a **Simple Majority** (>50% of members present and voting).

### Constitutional Mechanics Illuminated by the July–September 2025 Transition

On **July 21, 2025**, Vice-President **Jagdeep Dhankhar resigned mid-term** under **Article 67(a)** (by writing addressed to the President), triggering India’s first early Vice-Presidential election since 1987. On **September 9, 2025**, **C. P. Radhakrishnan** defeated B. Sudershan Reddy (452 to 300 votes) and assumed office on **September 12, 2025** as India’s 15th Vice-President. This rare mid-term transition brought four high-yield Prelims mechanics into sharp focus:

1. **No Six-Month Ceiling (Article 68(2)):** Whereas a Presidential casual vacancy *must* be filled within six months (**Article 62(2)**), a Vice-Presidential casual vacancy is only required to be filled **"as soon as possible"**—no rigid constitutional deadline applies.
2. **No "Acting Vice-President":** The Constitution contains **no provision for an Acting Vice-President**. During a mid-term vacancy, the office remains empty; only the legislative duties of presiding over the Rajya Sabha devolve upon the **Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** under **Article 91(1)**.
3. **Holding-Over Clause Limitation (Article 67(c)):** A Vice-President continues to hold office beyond five years until a successor takes charge *only* upon **expiration of term**, not after tendering a mid-term resignation.
4. **Full Five-Year Term:** A Vice-President elected to fill a mid-term casual vacancy serves a **full five-year term** from assuming office, not the unexpired remainder of the predecessor’s tenure.

## Prime Minister, Council of Ministers & Cabinet Architecture (Articles 74–78, 163–167)

In Bagehot’s famous formulation adapted to India, the Prime Minister and Cabinet form the **"hyphen that joins, the buckle that fastens"** the executive and legislative branches. Under **Article 75(1)** (State counterpart: **Article 164(1)**), the President appoints the Prime Minister—conventionally the leader commanding a majority in the Lok Sabha—and appoints other Ministers strictly on the Prime Minister’s advice.

A Prime Minister or Chief Minister may belong to **either House** of the legislature (unlike modern British convention confining the PM to the House of Commons). Even a **non-member** may be appointed PM, CM, or Minister for up to **six consecutive months** (**Article 75(5) / Article 164(4)**), failing which they cease to hold office. Two Supreme Court boundaries prevent abuse of this window: in ***S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab* (2001)**, the Court barred repeated six-month re-appointments of an unelected person within the same legislative term as a subversion of representative democracy; in ***B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2001)**, a 5-Judge Bench held that a person legally disqualified from contesting elections (e.g., upon conviction under Section 8 of the RPA, 1951) **cannot** be sworn in as Chief Minister under Article 164(4).

### Collective vs. Individual Responsibility & the 91st Amendment Reset

Parliamentary governance operates on two interlocking tracks of accountability—plus one conspicuous absence:

- **Collective Responsibility (Article 75(3) / Article 164(2)):** The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible **specifically to the Lok Sabha** (in States, to the Legislative Assembly)—**not** to Parliament or the State Legislature as a whole. Ministers "swim and sink together": passage of a No-Confidence Motion in the Lok Sabha compels the entire Council (including Rajya Sabha ministers) to resign, and any minister who cannot publicly defend a Cabinet decision must quit.
- **Individual Responsibility (Article 75(2) / Article 164(1)):** Ministers hold office during the **"pleasure of the President/Governor."** In constitutional practice, this vests the Prime Minister or Chief Minister with disciplinary authority to drop a dissenting or non-performing minister without jeopardizing the government’s survival.
- **Absence of Legal Responsibility:** Unlike Britain—where every public act of the Crown requires ministerial countersignature attaching personal legal liability to the minister—India has no system of ministerial legal responsibility, and **Article 74(2)** insulates ministerial advice from judicial inquiry.

To curb jumbo ministries engineered to buy coalition loyalty and stop horse-trading, Parliament enacted the **91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003**:

- **The 15% Ministerial Ceiling (Articles 75(1A) & 164(1A)):** Total Ministers, **including the PM or CM**, cannot exceed **15% of the total strength of the Lok Sabha** (or State Legislative Assembly). For smaller States, the Proviso to Article 164(1A) establishes a **minimum floor of 12 Ministers** (including the CM). *(Note the statutory/constitutional exception: for NCT Delhi under **Article 239AA(4)**, the cap is tighter—**10% of the 70-member Assembly**, i.e., a maximum of **7 Ministers** including the CM.)*
- **Mandatory Tribal Welfare Minister (Proviso to Article 164(1)):** Immediately preceding Article 164(1A), the **Proviso to Article 164(1)** mandates a Minister in charge of **Tribal Welfare** (who may in addition be in charge of the welfare of Scheduled Castes and backward classes, or any other work) in four specific States: **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, and Odisha**—with **Bihar** having been deleted and Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand inserted by the **94th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2006**.
- **Anti-Defection Ministerial Bar (Articles 75(1B), 164(1B) & 361B):** Any legislator disqualified on grounds of defection under the Tenth Schedule is barred from appointment as a Minister—or any remunerative political post—until their term expires or they win re-election, whichever comes earlier.

### Council of Ministers, the "Cabinet" & Cabinet Committees

The Constitution **does not classify ministers** into hierarchical ranks; the three-tier division into **Cabinet Ministers**, **Ministers of State** (Independent Charge or attached), and **Deputy Ministers** derives from parliamentary convention and the Salaries and Allowances of Ministers Act, 1952. Remarkably, the word **"Cabinet"** was absent from the original Constitution and appears **only once** in the entire text—in **Article 352(3)**, inserted by the **44th CAA (1978)** to ensure a National Emergency requires the written recommendation of the "Cabinet" (defined there as the PM and Ministers of Cabinet rank), preventing unilateral Prime Ministerial proclamations like June 1975.

To manage complex inter-ministerial coordination without overburdening the full Cabinet, India relies on **Cabinet Committees**. These are **extra-constitutional bodies**—never mentioned in the Constitution—established by the Prime Minister under the **Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961**, framed pursuant to **Article 77(3)**. They may be permanent (**Standing**) or temporary (**Ad hoc**), draw primarily from Cabinet Ministers (though non-Cabinet Ministers are not barred from membership or attendance), and can take binding government decisions subject to review by the full Cabinet. Crucially, **whenever the Prime Minister is a member of a Cabinet Committee, the PM invariably chairs it**.

Following the 18th Lok Sabha elections, the Union Government reconstituted **eight Standing Cabinet Committees** on **July 3, 2024**:

- **Six Chaired by the Prime Minister:** (1) **Appointments Committee of the Cabinet (ACC)**—the bureaucratic nerve centre comprising solely the PM and Home Minister; (2) **Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS)**—PM, Defence, Home, Finance, and External Affairs; (3) **Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA)**; (4) **Cabinet Committee on Political Affairs (CCPA)**—historically termed the *"Super-Cabinet"* for domestic and foreign policy coordination; (5) **Cabinet Committee on Investment and Growth (CCIG)**; and (6) **Cabinet Committee on Skill, Employment and Livelihood**.
- **Two Without the Prime Minister:** (7) **Cabinet Committee on Accommodation (CCA)**, chaired by the **Home Minister**; and (8) **Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs**, which schedules parliamentary sessions and legislative business, chaired by the **Defence Minister**.

**Structural Shift — Cabinet Dominance & the Indian PM vs. US President (2024 & 2026 GS2 Core):** In functional practice, India’s Westminster fusion has shifted power successively from Parliament → Cabinet → **Prime Minister’s Office (PMO)** and inner committees (ACC/CCS). Reinforced by the **Tenth Schedule whip**, executive control over parliamentary calendars, frequent ordinances, Money Bill routing, and declining bill referrals to Parliamentary Committees, an Indian Prime Minister commanding a single-party majority wields **greater unencumbered legislative and executive authority than the President of the United States**. The US President holds a fixed four-year term and independent electoral mandate, yet faces a constitutionally independent Congress, Senate confirmation of appointments and treaties, Congressional "power of the purse," and divided-government gridlock. The Indian PM’s authority is thus **conditionally supreme**—unrivalled during single-party majorities, yet instantly vulnerable during coalition eras or upon losing Lok Sabha confidence.

## The Governor — Dual Mandate, Pleasure Doctrine & Discretion (Articles 153–167)

Part VI, Chapter II (**Articles 153–167**) replicates the parliamentary model at the State level, yet embeds a profound federal asymmetry. Under **Article 153**, there shall be a Governor for each State—modified by the **7th CAA (1956)** to permit appointing the **same person as Governor for two or more States**. Under **Article 155**, the Governor is **appointed by the President** by warrant under their hand and seal. The Constituent Assembly deliberately rejected an *elected* Governor—preferring the **Canadian model** over the American state-governor model—fearing that a directly elected Governor would create a rival centre of political legitimacy against the Chief Minister, breed separatist tendencies, and waste public expenditure on a ceremonial office.

**Qualifications & Constitutional Status:** Under **Article 157**, the sole formal qualifications are Indian citizenship and a minimum age of **35 years** (the two non-binding conventions—that the appointee be an *outsider* to the State and appointed after *consulting the Chief Minister*—have been routinely breached across political regimes). Crucially, in ***Hargovind Pant v. Dr. Raghukul Tilak* (1979, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court held that the Governor holds an **independent constitutional office**, **NOT an employment under the Government of India** and is neither subordinate nor subservient to the Union executive (holding therefore that an ex-RPSC member was not barred by Article 319(d) from becoming Governor).

### The Pleasure Doctrine & Tenure Fragility (Article 156)

Under **Article 156**, a Governor holds a nominal five-year term, **subject to the "pleasure of the President"** (**Article 156(1)**), with **no constitutional grounds specified for removal** and no impeachment shield. Because Presidential pleasure operates on Union Cabinet advice, incoming central governments historically purged Governors appointed by predecessor regimes, eroding gubernatorial neutrality.

In ***B.P. Singhal v. Union of India* (2010, 5-Judge Constitution Bench)**, the Supreme Court sharply curtailed arbitrary removals without extinguishing the pleasure doctrine:

- **No Unfettered Pleasure:** While the President need not issue a show-cause notice, hold an inquiry, or record reasons for removing a Governor, the power under Article 156(1) cannot be exercised **arbitrarily, capriciously, or *mala fide***.
- **Ideological Non-Alignment Is Not a Ground:** A change of government at the Centre, or the fact that a Governor is *"out of sync"* with the ideology and policies of the Union ruling party, is **not a valid ground** for removal. Removal is justified only for compelling reasons such as physical/mental incapacity, corruption, or behaviour unbecoming of constitutional office.
- **Limited Judicial Review:** Although courts will not ordinarily compel disclosure of reasons, if an aggrieved party establishes a *prima facie* case of arbitrariness or *mala fides*, the court can demand the Union produce the underlying material.

### Article 163 Discretion — Express, Situational & Judicial Guardrails

Here lies the single most consequential textual divergence between the President and the Governor. Unlike Article 74, **Article 163(1)** explicitly qualifies ministerial aid and advice with the carve-out: ***"except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion"***. **Article 163(2)** adds that if any question arises whether a matter falls within gubernatorial discretion, the **Governor’s decision shall be final** and unassailable on the ground that they ought or ought not to have acted in discretion.

Gubernatorial discretion bifurcates into two categories:

1. **Express Constitutional Discretion:** Reserving State Bills for Presidential consideration (**Article 200**, mandatory under the 2nd Proviso when a Bill derogates from High Court powers); reporting breakdown of constitutional machinery (**Article 356**); exercising functions as Administrator of an adjoining Union Territory (**Article 239(2)**); determining mineral-royalty shares payable to Autonomous District Councils in **Sixth Schedule** States (Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram); discharging special responsibilities under **Articles 371–371J** (e.g., **Article 371A** Nagaland law and order, **Article 371C** Manipur Hill Areas, **Article 371F** Sikkim peace and equitable advancement, **Article 371H** Arunachal Pradesh law and order); and seeking administrative/legislative information from the CM (**Article 167**).
2. **Situational Discretion:** Selecting a Chief Minister in a hung Assembly; dismissing a ministry that has lost majority confidence and refuses to resign; and dissolving the Assembly when advised by a defeated or minority Council of Ministers.

Because Raj Bhavans repeatedly weaponized Article 163(2) during state political crises, the Supreme Court drew strict federal guardrails:

- ***Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker* (2016, 5-Judge Bench):** Held that **Article 163(2) does NOT confer a general, overarching, or unbridled discretionary power** on the Governor. Discretion exists *only* where expressly conferred or necessarily implied by constitutional provisions. Specifically, a Governor **cannot unilaterally summon, prorogue, or advance an Assembly session under Article 174** against the aid and advice of a Cabinet enjoying legislative majority, nor meddle in internal House proceedings like Speaker-removal motions.
- ***Shivraj Singh Chouhan v. Speaker, MP Legislative Assembly* (2020):** Nuanced *Nabam Rebia* for confidence crises. Where a Governor possesses **objective, tangible material** creating a *prima facie* view that an incumbent government has lost the Assembly's confidence (e.g., mass resignation of MLAs), the Governor **is constitutionally empowered to direct a Floor Test** to test majority on the House floor—not in Raj Bhavan parades.
- ***Subhash Desai v. Governor of Maharashtra* (May 2023, 5-Judge Bench):** Tightened the floor-test trigger further. A Governor **cannot call a floor test merely to settle an intra-party faction split** or rely on internal party rebellions lacking objective indication that MLAs intend to withdraw support from the government itself. Discretion under Article 163 cannot be deployed to *"destabilise or displace"* an elected government.

### Sarkaria (1988) & Punchhi (2010) Blueprints for Reform

Two authoritative Centre–State commissions mapped structural fixes to depoliticize Raj Bhavan—most of which remain unimplemented by Parliament:

- **Sarkaria Commission (1988):** Appointees should be eminent public figures, **outsiders** to the State, detached from recent active politics (especially from the ruling party at the Centre when the State is governed by an opposition party); **Article 155** should be amended to mandate effective consultation with the Chief Minister; Governors must follow a strict **order of preference in hung Assemblies** (pre-poll alliance → single largest party → post-poll coalition with participating partners → post-poll coalition with outside support) followed by a floor test within 30 days; and conventions should govern Bill assent timelines (including a 6-month limit for Presidential disposal under Article 201).
- **Punchhi Commission (2010):** Recommended abolishing the **"doctrine of pleasure"** in favour of a **fixed five-year tenure** with removal only via an **impeachment-like resolution of the State Legislature**; stripping Governors of statutory roles as **ex-officio Chancellors of State Universities** (the precise statutory flashpoint behind the 2020–2025 Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and West Bengal university-bill wars!); mandating a **six-month outer time limit** for Governors to decide on Bills under Article 200; and institutionalizing *"localised emergencies"* under Articles 355–356.

## The Article 200–201 Bill Assent Showdown & the November 2025 Constitutional Reset

No executive conflict in recent Indian constitutional history has reshaped federal jurisprudence as starkly as the three-act confrontation over **gubernatorial inaction on State Bills** under **Articles 200 and 201**.

### Bare-Text Anatomy of Articles 200 and 201

Under the substantive clause of **Article 200**, when a Bill passed by a State Legislature is presented to the Governor, the Governor *"shall declare"* either that they **assent** to the Bill, **withhold assent**, or **reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President**. Two provisos immediately follow:

- **First Proviso:** The Governor *"may, **as soon as possible** after the presentation to him of the Bill for assent, return the Bill if it is **not a Money Bill** together with a message"* requesting reconsideration; if the House passes the Bill again—with or without amendment—the Governor ***"shall not withhold assent therefrom"***.
- **Second Proviso:** The Governor **shall not assent to, but shall mandatorily reserve** for the President any Bill that, in the Governor’s opinion, would so derogate from the powers of the **High Court** as to endanger its constitutional position.

During the Constituent Assembly Debates (July 30, 1949), Dr. B.R. Ambedkar consciously deleted the phrase *"in his discretion"* from Draft Article 175 (now Article 200) and replaced an earlier proposed **six-week deadline** with the open-ended phrase ***"as soon as possible"***. Between 2020 and 2025, Raj Bhavans in several Opposition-ruled States (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab, Telangana, West Bengal) exploited that textual silence by sitting on duly passed Bills for years without granting assent, returning them, or reserving them—manufacturing a *de facto* **pocket veto**.

### Act I (Nov 2023) & Act II (April 2025) — The Judicial Pushback & Timelines

- ***State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor* (November 10, 2023, 3-Judge Bench):** Initiated the doctrinal correction. Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud ruled that the Governor is an unelected symbolic head who **cannot thwart democratic lawmaking through silent inaction**. Reading *"as soon as possible"* as carrying substantive constitutional urgency, the Bench held that if a Governor decides to **withhold assent**, that decision **must be coupled with returning the Bill to the Assembly** under the First Proviso—there is no standalone power to kill a Bill silently.
- ***State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (April 8, 2025, 2-Judge Bench — JJ. J.B. Pardiwala & R. Mahadevan, `2025 INSC 481`):** Confronting Tamil Nadu Governor R.N. Ravi—who sat on 12 Bills (chiefly transferring Vice-Chancellor appointment powers from the Governor-Chancellor to the State Government) for up to three years, summarily declared assent "withheld" on 10 Bills in November 2023, and then **reserved all 10 Bills for the President** *after* the Assembly re-passed them on November 18, 2023—the 2-Judge Bench delivered an aggressively interventionist verdict:
  1. Declared that Article 200 permits **neither an absolute veto nor a pocket veto**, and once a Bill is returned and re-passed under the First Proviso, the Governor **cannot reserve it for the President** (unless materially altered from the original).
  2. Held that the Governor ordinarily acts on **ministerial aid and advice** under Article 200, restricting independent discretion largely to the Second Proviso and mandatory constitutional reservations.
  3. **Prescribed Binding Judicial Timelines** (adapting Sarkaria/Punchhi recommendations and Home Ministry memoranda): **1 month** for withholding/reserving on ministerial advice; **3 months** for withholding/reserving contrary to advice; **1 month** for assenting to re-passed Bills; and **3 months** for the President to decide on reserved Bills under Article 201 (with reasons for any delay).
  4. Declared actions under Articles 200 and 201 **justiciable** and, in an unprecedented use of **Article 142 ("complete justice")**, declared the **10 re-passed Tamil Nadu Bills "deemed to have received assent"** effective November 18, 2023.

### Act III — The 16th Presidential Reference (`2025 INSC 1333`, November 20, 2025)

Viewing judicially legislated deadlines and "deemed assent" as an existential breach of separation of powers, President Droupadi Murmu invoked **Article 143(1)** on **May 13, 2025**—India’s **16th Presidential Reference**—referring **14 constitutional questions** to the Supreme Court. On **November 20, 2025**, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** comprising **Chief Justice B.R. Gavai, Justices Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P.S. Narasimha, and A.S. Chandurkar** delivered a unanimous, 111-page *Opinion of the Court* in ***In Re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India* (`Special Reference No. 1 of 2025`, `2025 INSC 1333`)**.

**Maintainability & Precedential Reach:** Rejecting States' objections that the Reference functioned as a disguised appeal circumventing review/curative procedures, the Bench invoked *In Re: Natural Resources Allocation* (2012) to classify it as a **"functional reference"** aimed at institutional clarity. Crucially, the Bench distinguished between reopening an inter-party *decree* (leaving Tamil Nadu's 10 notified Acts untouched as final relief) and correcting an erroneous *"view of law"* for the future—answering Questions 1–11 while finding Question 13 too broad and declining Questions 12 (Art 145(3) bench strength) and 14 (Art 131 jurisdiction) as unnecessary.

**The Seven Pillars of Active 2026 Law (Para 165, `2025 INSC 1333`):**

1. **Strictly Three Options — No Absolute Veto *Simpliciter* (Para 165.1):** Under Article 200, a Governor has **exactly three constitutional options**: (i) grant assent; (ii) reserve the Bill for the President; or (iii) **withhold assent AND return the Bill with comments** to the Legislature. The First Proviso is bound to and **qualifies "withholds assent"** rather than creating a fourth option—confirming that **a Governor cannot withhold assent *simpliciter* to kill a Bill outright or bury it indefinitely**. For **Money Bills**, the third option (*withhold and return*) is unavailable; the Governor can only assent or reserve.
2. **Independent Gubernatorial Discretion Restored (Para 165.2):** Squarely disapproving the April 2025 Bench’s holding as *"erroneous"* (para 88), the Constitution Bench held that the Governor enjoys **constitutional discretion** in choosing among the three Article 200 options and is **NOT bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers** in discharging this function—re-adopting Paragraph 4.5 of the Punchhi Commission Report (earlier approved in *Nabam Rebia*). *(Note: After reconsideration and re-passage under the First Proviso, the text mandates the Governor "shall not withhold assent therefrom," though the Bench noted the constitutional scheme preserves dialogic space rather than turning the Governor into a rubber stamp.)*
3. **Zero Judicially Fixed Timelines (Paras 165.5 & 165.7):** Because the Constituent Assembly deliberately refrained from embedding rigid numerical clocks in Articles 200 and 201, **courts cannot judicially prescribe timelines** for either the Governor or the President—expressly disapproving all four April 2025 deadlines (1/3/1/3 months) as impermissible judicial lawmaking.
4. **No "Deemed Assent" via Article 142 (Paras 165.10–165.11):** Article 142 cannot substitute or usurp the constitutional assent of the Governor or the President. Judicially creating "deemed assent" amounts to a ***"virtual takeover"*** of executive functions by the judiciary; no State legislation can come into force without actual constitutional assent.
5. **Non-Justiciability on Merits & No Pre-Enactment Adjudication (Paras 165.3, 165.6 & 165.9):** The substantive choice exercised by a Governor under Article 200 or the President under Article 201 is **non-justiciable on merits**. Nor may courts adjudicate the constitutional validity of a **Bill’s contents before it becomes law**.
6. **The Safety Valve — "Limited Mandamus" for Prolonged Inaction (Para 165.3):** Refusing to leave elected legislatures defenseless against gubernatorial stonewalling, the Bench held that in glaring circumstances of **"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite" inaction** frustrating the dialogic process, constitutional courts may issue a **limited writ of mandamus** directing the Governor to act under Article 200 within a **reasonable time**, *without making any observations on the merits or dictating which option to choose*.
7. **Article 361 Reconciled & Optional Article 143 Reference (Paras 165.4 & 165.8):** Following *Rameshwar Prasad* (2006), **Article 361** preserves the Governor’s *personal immunity* from court process, but **does not bar a limited mandamus directed at the constitutional office** in cases of prolonged inaction. Finally, the President is **not legally required** to invoke Article 143 for an advisory opinion every time a Governor reserves a Bill; Presidential subjective satisfaction suffices.

**Constitutional Critique — "Dialogic Federalism" vs. Residual Friction:** The November 2025 reset performed a delicate institutional course-correction. By rejecting judicial deadlines and "deemed assent," it defended **Separation of Powers** against judicial overreach. Simultaneously, by fusing "withholding" to "returning" and preserving **limited mandamus**, it permanently foreclosed any unilateral gubernatorial pocket veto. Yet two fault lines remain live for GS2 analysis: first, because *"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite"* lacks a quantitative threshold, Opposition-ruled States still face protracted case-by-case litigation; second, an **Article 143 advisory opinion** is formally non-binding compared to an **Article 141 judgment**—creating a subtle precedential tension until Parliament either enacts a constitutional amendment codifying Sarkaria/Punchhi timelines or a future adjudicatory bench consolidates `2025 INSC 1333`.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Electoral College vs. Impeachment Inversion:** Nominated MPs are **excluded** from electing the President (**Art 54**), yet **participate** in Presidential impeachment (**Art 61**). Elected State/UT MLAs **vote** in the Presidential election, yet are **excluded** from both Presidential impeachment and the Vice-Presidential election (**Art 66**). Elected MLAs of **Jammu & Kashmir UT** are *not* part of the Presidential Electoral College absent a constitutional amendment to Art 54.
> - **Vice-Presidential Vacancy & Removal Traps:** There is **no Acting Vice-President** in India (Deputy Chairman of RS only presides over the House under Art 91(1)); casual VP vacancies have **no 6-month limit** (**Art 68(2)**: *"as soon as possible"*) unlike the President (**Art 62(2)**: 6 months). VP removal requires **Effective Majority in RS + Simple Majority in LS** (NOT Special Majority, and NO formal impeachment or constitutional grounds) (**Art 67(b)**).
> - **Ministerial & Cabinet Traps:** The Constitution **never classifies** Ministers into Cabinet/MoS/Deputy ranks. The word **"Cabinet"** appears **only once** in the Constitution (**Art 352(3)**, 44th CAA). Cabinet Committees are **extra-constitutional** (under Art 77(3) Business Rules), and the PM does **not** chair all of them (as of 2024–2026: **CCA** is chaired by Home Minister; **Parliamentary Affairs** by Defence Minister). The **15% cap** under the 91st CAA **includes the PM/CM** (and NCT Delhi is capped at **10%** under Art 239AA). Under the **Proviso to Article 164(1)**, a Minister in charge of **Tribal Welfare** is constitutionally mandatory in **Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, and Odisha** (**Bihar** was excluded by the **94th CAA, 2006**).
> - **Ordinance Scope Traps (Tested Prelims 2025):** An ordinance **CAN** amend/repeal any Central/State statutory Act, alter tax statutes, and take **retrospective (backdated) effect**. It **CANNOT** amend the Constitution or abridge Fundamental Rights (**Art 13(3)(a)**). An ordinance lapses **6 weeks after reassembly** (not 6 months!), yielding a maximum life of 6 months + 6 weeks. Under *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017), **rights created under a lapsed ordinance do NOT automatically endure**.
> - **Pardoning & Immunity Traps (Tested Prelims 2025):** Neither the President (**Art 72**) nor the Governor (**Art 161**) exercises clemency in personal discretion—both are bound by Cabinet advice (*Maru Ram*; *Perarivalan*), and both orders are subject to **limited judicial review** (*Epuru Sudhakar*). Under **Art 361(2)**, **no criminal proceedings** can be instituted *or continued* against a sitting President or Governor—whereas **civil proceedings** for personal acts *are* permitted after **2 months' written notice** (**Art 361(4)**).
> - **Post-Nov 2025 Article 200/201 Traps:** The President **cannot *suo motu* reserve** a State Bill without the Governor forwarding it (tested Prelims 2025). Under **`2025 INSC 1333` (Nov 20, 2025)**: (a) the Governor exercises **independent discretion** (NOT bound by State Cabinet advice) when choosing among the **three Art 200 options**; (b) the Governor **cannot return a Money Bill**; (c) **NO fixed judicial timelines** (1/3-month April 2025 deadlines disapproved) and **NO Art 142 "deemed assent"** exist; (d) a **limited mandamus** lies solely for prolonged, unexplained, indefinite inaction; and (e) **Art 201** gives the State Legislature **6 months** to reconsider a President-returned Bill, yet imposes **no time limit on the President or Governor**.

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature

- **Asymmetry Swaps Between Articles 74 & 163 / 111 & 200:** UPSC routinely attributes *express constitutional discretion* to the President (False—only situational) or strips the Governor of discretion under Article 200 (False post-`2025 INSC 1333`), or claims the President must assent to a reserved State Bill if the State Legislature re-passes it under Article 201 (False—mandatory re-passage assent applies only to Article 111 Union Bills and Article 200 First Proviso).
- **Ordinance Mechanics & Life-Cycle Traps:** Watch for inversions claiming an ordinance *"cannot operate retrospectively"* (False), *"expires 6 months after reassembly"* (False—6 weeks), *"can amend the Constitution"* (False), or *"creates permanent rights even after lapsing"* (False—overruled in *Krishna Kumar Singh*, 2017).
- **Electoral College & Removal-Majority Swaps:** Classic traps include inserting nominated MPs into the Presidential Electoral College (False), inserting State MLAs into the Vice-Presidential Electoral College or Presidential impeachment (False), claiming a dissolved State Assembly invalidates a Presidential election (False—Art 71(4)), or asserting that Vice-Presidential removal requires a 2/3rd Special Majority (False—Effective Majority in RS + Simple Majority in LS).
- **Clemency & Immunity Boundary Inversions:** Expect statements claiming the Governor can pardon a death sentence or court-martial sentence (False), that Article 361 bars *civil* proceedings completely during tenure (False—allowed with 2 months' written notice), or that Article 72 clemency is completely immune from judicial review (False—*Epuru Sudhakar*).
