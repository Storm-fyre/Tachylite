# Chapter 12: Subordinate Judiciary, ADR, BNS–BNSS Trial Reforms & the Tribunalization Showdown (Part XIV-A)

If the Supreme Court and High Courts are the intellectual head of India's constitutional order, the subordinate judiciary, Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) forums, and specialized tribunals form its operational spine. Roughly **87% of India's >5.1 crore pending cases** reside in District and Taluka courts, where ordinary citizens first encounter the rule of law—while commercial, regulatory, and service disputes have migrated massively into tribunals and arbitral forums.

Between 2023 and 2026, this entire base layer underwent dramatic doctrinal resets: a 5-Judge Constitution Bench overruled *Dheeraj Mor* on District Judge recruitment (**October 2025**), the Supreme Court published **Draft AI Regulations for Courts (June 2026)**, three Constitution Benches overhauled Indian arbitration law (**2023–2024**), and—most explosively—the Court struck down core provisions of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** on **November 19, 2025 (`2025 INSC 1330`)**, confronting Parliament's repeated legislative overrides head-on.

---

## 1. The Subordinate Judiciary, AIJS & Digital-Procedural Modernization

### Constitutional Architecture (Part VI, Chapter VI — Articles 233–237)

Anchored in the Directive Principle of separating the judiciary from the executive (**Article 50**), **Articles 233–237** insulate District and Taluka courts from State executive capture by vesting decisive supervisory authority in the High Court:

- **Appointment of District Judges (Article 233):** Appointments, postings, and promotions of District Judges in any State are made by the **Governor in consultation with the High Court** exercising jurisdiction (Art 233(1)). Under **Article 233(2)**, a person *not already in the service of the Union or of the State* is eligible for direct appointment only if they have been an advocate or pleader for at least **seven years** and are recommended by the High Court. *(Article 233A, inserted by the 20th CAA, 1966, retrospectively validated certain pre-1966 appointments.)*
- **Recruitment of Other Judicial Officers (Article 234):** Appointments to the State judicial service *other than* District Judges (e.g., Civil Judges / Judicial Magistrates) are made by the **Governor** in accordance with rules framed after consulting **BOTH the State Public Service Commission (SPSC) and the High Court**.
- **Plenary Control of the High Court (Article 235):** Control over District Courts and subordinate courts—including posting, promotion, grant of leave, transfer, and disciplinary inquiries—vests exclusively in the **High Court**. In ***State of West Bengal v. Nripendra Nath Bagchi* (1966)**, a 5-Judge Bench confirmed that "control" includes disciplinary jurisdiction; while formal dismissal or removal orders are issued by the Governor (as appointing authority under Art 311), the High Court's disciplinary recommendation is **binding** on the State Government.
- **Definitions & Extension (Articles 236–237):** **Article 236(a)** defines *"district judge"* expansively to include a judge of a city civil court, additional/joint/assistant district judge, chief judge of a small cause court, chief presidency magistrate, and sessions/additional/assistant sessions judge. **Article 237** empowers the Governor by notification to extend Chapter VI safeguards to specified classes of Executive Magistrates.

### District Judge Direct Recruitment — *Dheeraj Mor* (2020) Overruled by *Rejanish K.V.* (Oct 2025)

For four decades, a rigid interpretive bar divided the subordinate judiciary from direct District Judge recruitment:
- **Overruled Legacy Law (*Satya Narain Singh*, 1985 → *Dheeraj Mor v. High Court of Delhi*, 2020 — 3-Judge Bench):** Construed Article 233(2) restrictively to hold that the **25% direct recruitment Bar quota** was reserved solely for *presently practising* advocates holding 7 years' continuous Bar practice right up to the date of appointment. Serving junior judicial officers (Civil Judges/Munsiffs) were disqualified from competing—even if they had completed 7+ years at the Bar before entering service.
- **Active 2026 Law — *Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa* (`2025 INSC 1208`, October 9, 2025 — 5-Judge Constitution Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai):** **Expressly overruled *Dheeraj Mor* (2020) and *Satya Narain Singh* (1985)**. Reading Article 233(1) as the comprehensive source of appointment and Article 233(2) solely as an eligibility condition for non-service candidates, the Bench held that **serving judicial officers ARE eligible for direct recruitment as District/Additional District Judges** if they possess **7 years' combined experience** (Bar + judicial service). Eligibility is tested on the **date of application** (not appointment), and to ensure parity between streams, the Bench mandated a uniform **minimum age of 35 years** for both advocates and in-service candidates. By a **May 22, 2026** clarificatory order, the SC held that *Rejanish K.V.* operates **prospectively from October 9, 2025**, protecting already-completed selections.

### The All-India Judicial Service (AIJS — Article 312) Federal Debate

Inserted by the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976**, **Article 312(1) & (3)** empowers Parliament to create an **All-India Judicial Service (AIJS)** common to the Union and States if the **Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting** declaring it expedient in the national interest. Two vital bare-text locks apply: (i) the AIJS **shall not include any post inferior to that of a District Judge** (Art 312(3)), and (ii) a parliamentary law creating AIJS is **NOT deemed a constitutional amendment under Article 368** (Art 312(4)).

Despite endorsements from the **14th (1958) and 116th (1986) Law Commission Reports** and the Supreme Court in ***All India Judges Association* (1993)**, consensus among States and High Courts remains deadlocked along clear federal fault lines:
- **Case For AIJS:** Centralizing recruitment on the UPSC model could eliminate India's chronic **~25–30% subordinate court vacancy rate** (>5,000 unfilled posts across 28 fragmented state calendars), standardize judicial competence, improve social diversity, and strengthen the feeder cadre elevated to High Courts.
- **Case Against AIJS (Federal & Access Critique):** Most High Courts and States object that central recruitment erodes **High Court institutional control (Articles 233–235)** and compromises judicial independence. Equally decisive is **Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer's linguistic-access warning**: because District and Sessions judges record oral rural testimony and interpret state-specific land/revenue codes in regional languages, parachuting non-native officers across cadres undermines grassroots access to justice, favours urban English-medium elites, and blocks promotional ladders for state civil judges.

### Pendency (>5 Crore NJDG Backlog), Gram Nyayalayas, e-Courts Phase III & 2026 AI Governance

National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG) trackers place India's aggregate backlog at **over 5.1–5.3 crore cases**, with District and Taluka courts absorbing **~87% (~4.5+ crore cases)**—compounded by a low judge-population ratio (~21 judges per million vs. the 120th Law Commission's 50/million benchmark), infrastructural bottlenecks, and governments acting as litigants in nearly half of all cases. Four structural interventions target this base layer:

- **Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 (enforced October 2, 2009):** Establishes mobile rural courts at the **intermediate Panchayat level**, presided over by a **Nyayadhikari** (Judicial Magistrate First Class rank, appointed by the State Government in consultation with the High Court). Crucially, Gram Nyayalayas exercise **BOTH civil and criminal jurisdiction** (First and Second Schedules), follow **summary procedure** guided by **principles of natural justice** (not strict Evidence Act/BSA rules), promote conciliation using local social workers, and allow appeals within 30 days to the Sessions/District Court (disposal target: 6 months, with no further appeal). Because Section 3(1) makes establishment **optional ("may")** for States, adoption lags badly: per PIB/Department of Justice data, only **488 Gram Nyayalayas have been notified** across 15 States/UTs (~313–318 functional in 10 States; scheme extended till March 31, 2026), falling far short of the 5,000+ originally projected.
- **e-Courts Mission Mode Project Phase III (2023–2027, ₹7,210 crore):** Aims for paperless digital courts through digitization of ~310 crore legacy pages, cloud storage, 4,400 e-Seva Kendras, **Virtual Courts** (automated traffic/challan disposal), full **Interoperable Criminal Justice System (ICJS)** integration (linking police, forensics, prisons, and courts), and vernacular translation of e-SCR judgments into **18+ Indian languages**.
- **SUPACE, SUVAS & the 2026 SC AI Guidelines for the Judiciary:** India's courts actively deploy assistive AI tools—**SUPACE** (*Supreme Court Portal for Assistance in Court's Efficiency* for research/summarization), **SUVAS** (*Supreme Court Vidhik Anuvaad Software* translating across 19 languages), **TERES** (real-time transcription), and **LegRAA** (GenAI legal research). Addressing algorithmic bias and fabricated LLM citations, the Supreme Court's Centre for Research and Planning released a foundational **White Paper on Artificial Intelligence and the Judiciary (November 2025)**, followed on **June 3, 2026** by the SC AI Committee's preliminary **Draft Regulations for Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Courts, 2026** (applicable nationwide to SC, HCs, subordinate courts, tribunals, and statutory adjudicatory bodies). Resting on **human primacy** (*AI may assist, never adjudicate*), the Draft Regulations **strictly prohibit** AI-only verdicts or sentencing, algorithmic bail/recidivism risk-scoring, predictive profiling of witnesses/litigants, judicial surveillance, and undisclosed AI evidence—mandating **AI Disclosure Certificates** by counsel and establishing an **Apex AI Body at the SC** plus High Court AI Committees. Underscoring this zero-tolerance stance, the SC in ***Pooja Ramesh Singh v. Jammu & Kashmir Bank Ltd.* (July 2, 2026)** set aside NCLT/NCLAT orders relying on AI-hallucinated non-existent precedents.

### Trial-Stage Procedural Overhaul Under BNS–BNSS–BSA (Effective July 1, 2024)

Within the courtroom and trial architecture, the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023** and **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023** directly target chronic trial delays. **Zero FIR** and **e-FIR** registration (BNSS Section 173) initiate digitized judicial cognizance, while statutory clocks discipline trial velocity: framing of charges within **60 days** of first hearing (Sec 251/262), judgment delivery within **30 days** of argument completion (extendable to **45 days** with recorded reasons, Sec 258), a ceiling of **two adjournments** per party (Sec 346), **trial *in absentia*** of proclaimed offenders after 90 days (Sec 356), full **electronic trials and remote witness depositions** (Sec 530), mandatory **bail for first-time undertrials** completing **one-third** of the maximum sentence (Sec 479, tightening the earlier 50% rule), and statutory elevation of electronic records to **primary evidence** (BSA Section 63).

## 2. India's Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) Ecosystem

To unclog adversarial dockets, **Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908** (inserted via the 1999 Amendment, effective 2002; systematized in ***Afcons Infrastructure*, 2010**) authorizes civil courts to refer amenable disputes to five ADR tracks: **Arbitration, Conciliation, Judicial Settlement, Lok Adalat, and Mediation**.

### NALSA & Statutory Lok Adalats (Article 39A & Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987)

Operationalizing **Article 39A** (DPSP: equal justice and free legal aid, inserted by the **42nd CAA, 1976**), Parliament enacted the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (enforced 1995), establishing the **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)**—with the **CJI as Patron-in-Chief** and the **second senior-most SC Judge as Executive Chairman**—cascading down to State (SLSA), District (DLSA), and Taluka committees. Under **Section 12**, free legal aid is guaranteed to SCs/STs, women and children, victims of trafficking/*begar*, persons with disabilities, industrial workmen, victims of mass disaster/ethnic/caste violence, persons in custody, and individuals below state-notified income ceilings (₹1–3 lakh in States; ₹5 lakh before SC)—notice that **OBCs and senior citizens do NOT receive automatic universal eligibility** absent income/specific state schemes. Supported by **DISHA (2021–26)** (Tele-Law and Nyaya Bandhu) and the **Legal Aid Defense Counsel System (LADCS)** across 668 districts, Lok Adalats resolved **23.58 crore cases** between 2022–23 and 2024–25 (PIB, November 2025).

Two structurally distinct statutory forums operate under the 1987 Act—a classic Prelims trap:
- **Regular Lok Adalats (Sections 19–22):** Purely **conciliatory and voluntary** ("People's Court"); possesses **zero adjudicatory power** to impose a verdict. Handles pending or pre-litigation civil disputes and **compoundable criminal offences** (never non-compoundable crimes or divorce). **Zero court fee** is charged (any court fee paid in a referred pending suit is **fully refunded** upon settlement). Its award carries the force of a **civil court decree—final, binding, and strictly non-appealable** (subject only to narrow Art 226/227 writ scrutiny for fraud). If settlement fails, the case returns to court with zero penalty.
- **Permanent Lok Adalats (Chapter VI-A, inserted by 2002 Amendment):** Dedicated pre-litigation forums for **Public Utility Services** (transport, postal/telegraph, power/water, sanitation, hospital, insurance, banking, real estate) with a pecuniary ceiling of **₹1 crore** (raised from ₹10 lakh in 2015), chaired by a retired/sitting District Judge (or higher) plus two domain members. **Crucial Distinction:** Unlike regular Lok Adalats, a Permanent Lok Adalat holds **BOTH conciliatory AND adjudicatory jurisdiction**—if parties fail to settle, it may decide the dispute on merits by majority vote, issuing a final, binding, non-appealable award.

### Arbitration Act, 1996 & the 2023–2024 Constitution Bench Trilogy

Built on the **UNCITRAL Model Law**, the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** (amended in 2015, 2019, and 2021 to enforce 12-month arbitral timelines, narrow "public policy" set-aside grounds under Sec 34, and envisage an Arbitration Council of India) was dramatically modernized by three landmark Supreme Court Constitution Bench rulings:

1. **Unstamped Agreements — *In Re: Interplay Between Arbitration Agreements & the Indian Stamp Act* (December 13, 2023 — 7-Judge Unanimous Bench):** **Overruled *N.N. Global II* (2023, 5-Judge)!** Held that an unstamped or insufficiently stamped arbitration agreement is **inadmissible in evidence (a curable fiscal defect under the Stamp Act), NOT void *ab initio***. At the Section 8/11 referral stage, courts must confine scrutiny to the *prima facie* existence of the arbitration agreement and leave stamping/impounding strictly to the arbitral tribunal under the **Kompetenz-Kompetenz** (Sec 16) and **Separability** doctrines.
2. **Non-Signatories — *Cox and Kings Ltd. v. SAP India Pvt. Ltd.* (December 6, 2023 — 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** Entrenched the **"Group of Companies" Doctrine** in Indian arbitration jurisprudence, ruling that a non-signatory affiliate can be bound under Sections 2(1)(h) and 7 where composite-transaction conduct and mutual intention demonstrate its active role in contract negotiation, performance, or termination—distinct from mechanical "alter ego" veil-piercing.
3. **Barring Unilateral PSU Arbitrators — *Central Organisation for Railway Electrification (CORE II) v. ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV)* (November 8, 2024 — 5-Judge Constitution Bench):** **Overruled *CORE I* (2020) and *Voestalpine*!** Struck down asymmetric public-sector clauses forcing private contractors to select arbitrators exclusively from a curated panel prepared by a government PSU. The Bench ruled that unilateral appointment panels breach **equality of parties (Section 18)** and the **rule against bias (*nemo judex in causa sua*)** embedded in **Article 14**—applying **prospectively** to three-member tribunals constituted after November 8, 2024 (harmonizing *Perkins Eastman*, 2019 and *TRF*, 2017).

Complementing arbitration, the **Commercial Courts Act, 2015** (amended 2018) created specialized Commercial Courts for commercial disputes valued at **₹3 lakh and above** (lowered from ₹1 crore in 2018), with **Section 12A** making **Pre-Institution Mediation and Settlement (PIMS)** strictly **mandatory** before filing any commercial suit not seeking urgent interim relief (*Patil Automation*, 2022).

### The Mediation Act, 2023 (Assent: September 14, 2023)

India's first standalone mediation statute institutionalizes mediation alongside—and absorbing—traditional conciliation (deleting Part III of the 1996 Arbitration Act):
- **Voluntary Pre-Litigation Model:** Following Parliamentary Standing Committee critique, Parliament dropped compulsory pre-litigation mediation, making general civil/commercial pre-litigation mediation **voluntary** (while preserving mandatory Sec 12A Commercial Courts mediation and court-referred mediation).
- **Strict Timeline & Decree Enforceability:** Proceedings must conclude within **120 days**, extendable once by **60 days** (maximum **180 days**). A signed **Mediated Settlement Agreement (MSA)** is **final, binding, and enforceable directly like a court decree**, challengeable within **90 days** strictly on four narrow grounds: **fraud, corruption, impersonation, or non-mediable subject matter**.
- **Institutional Pillars & First Schedule Exclusions:** Creates the **7-member Mediation Council of India (MCI)** to regulate mediators and Mediation Service Providers; formally recognizes **Online Mediation** (Chapter VII) and **Community Mediation** (Section 43—three-member panels resolving neighbourhood peace disputes, whose settlements are **NOT enforceable as court decrees**!). The **First Schedule** bars mediation over serious criminal prosecutions (except court-referred compoundable offences), disputes involving minors/persons of unsound mind, taxation/revenue levies, SEBI/CCI/TRAI regulatory enforcement, and third-party rights.
- **Cross-Border Blind Spot:** Although India signed the **UN Singapore Convention on Mediation (2019)**, the 2023 Act applies only to domestic and India-seated international mediations—providing **no enforcement mechanism for foreign-seated MSAs** pending ratification.

## 3. The Tribunalization Regime (Part XIV-A) & the November 2025 Showdown

### Constitutional Architecture — Article 323A vs. Article 323B (42nd CAA, 1976)

Acting on the **Swaran Singh Committee** recommendations, the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** inserted **Part XIV-A** containing two articles—**Article 323A** (*Administrative Tribunals*) and **Article 323B** (*Tribunals for Other Matters*)—to offload technical, repetitive litigation from regular courts into specialized quasi-judicial bodies bound by **principles of natural justice** rather than rigid CPC or Evidence rules.

Precision on the bare-text differences between Articles 323A and 323B is mandatory:
- **Subject Matter:** **Article 323A** is restricted exclusively to **public service and recruitment disputes** of persons appointed to Union, State, local-body, or PSU services (enacted via the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**, creating the **Central Administrative Tribunal [CAT]** and **State Administrative Tribunals [SATs]**). Crucially, CAT **excludes** members of the Armed Forces (handled by the Armed Forces Tribunal), Supreme Court officers/staff, and Parliamentary secretarial staff. By contrast, **Article 323B** covers enumerated non-service domains—taxation, foreign exchange/import-export, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, urban property ceilings, elections to Parliament and State Legislatures, essential foodstuffs, and rent/tenancy. *(Note: Parliament can also establish statutory tribunals like **NGT [2010], NCLT/NCLAT [2013], AFT [2007], and ITAT [1941]** directly under Seventh Schedule legislative entries without relying solely on Art 323B—affirmed in **Union of India v. R. Gandhi, 2010**!)*
- **Competent Legislature:** **Article 323A** tribunals can be created **ONLY by Parliament**; **Article 323B** tribunals may be created by **both Parliament and State Legislatures** within their respective Seventh Schedule competence.
- **Hierarchical Design:** **Article 323A** permits **no hierarchy of tribunals** (one tribunal for the Centre and one for each State/joint states); **Article 323B** expressly authorizes a **multi-tier hierarchy of tribunals** (e.g., original NCLT → appellate NCLAT).

### The 30-Year Separation-of-Powers Battle: *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997) to *MBA* (Nov 19, 2025)

Because tribunals frequently adjudicate disputes where the **Union Government or parent Ministry is the chief litigant**, executive control over tribunal appointments, short tenures, re-appointment discretion, and budgets sparked a thirty-year constitutional showdown between Parliament and the Supreme Court:

1. ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997 — 7-Judge Constitution Bench):** Overruling *S.P. Sampath Kumar* (1987), the Court struck down **Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d)** (along with Section 28 of the 1985 Act) insofar as they stripped High Courts of writ review over tribunals. Declaring **Articles 32 and 226/227 Judicial Review** an inviolable part of **Basic Structure**, the Bench held that tribunals are **supplemental—never substitutes—for High Courts**. Tribunals serve as courts of **first instance** (competent even to test statutory *vires*, except their own parent statute), and their orders are reviewable by a **Division Bench of the jurisdictional High Court** before approaching the Supreme Court.
2. ***Union of India v. R. Gandhi* (2010 — 5-Judge Bench):** While upholding NCLT/NCLAT creation under Seventh Schedule competence, the Court laid down strict independence norms: where tribunals inherit High Court powers, **judicial members must hold judicial-rank parity**, **technical members cannot outnumber judicial members**, selection committees must have judicial primacy, and administrative oversight must shift away from litigant parent ministries to the **Ministry of Law and Justice**.
3. ***Madras Bar Association I–V* (2014–2021) & *Rojer Mathew* (2019):**
   - ***MBA I* (2014, 5-Judge):** Invalidated the **National Tax Tribunal (NTT) Act, 2005** for usurping core High Court jurisdiction over substantial questions of law.
   - ***Rojer Mathew* (2019, 5-Judge):** Struck down Tribunal Rules framed under the **Finance Act, 2017** (which merged 8 tribunals via the Money Bill route—referring the Money Bill validity question to a 7-Judge Bench), mandated a Judicial Impact Assessment, and recommended an independent **National Tribunals Commission**.
   - ***MBA IV* (2020, 3-Judge):** Restored a **5-year tenure** (retirement age **70** for Chairpersons, **67** for Members), ordered eligibility for **advocates with 10+ years' experience**, invalidated the 2020 Rules, and directed creation of an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**.
   - ***MBA V* (July 2021, 3-Judge, 2:1):** Struck down provisions of the **Tribunals Reforms Ordinance, 2021** imposing a **minimum age floor of 50 years**, truncating tenure to **4 years**, and forcing Search-cum-Selection Committees (SCSCs) to recommend a **panel of two names** so the Centre could cherry-pick appointees.
4. **Parliamentary Defiance — The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 (August 2021):** Weeks after *MBA V*, Parliament abolished **9 appellate tribunals** (including the Intellectual Property Appellate Board [IPAB], Film Certification Appellate Tribunal [FCAT], and Airport Appellate Tribunal), returning their dockets to High Courts and commercial courts—**while brazenly re-enacting almost verbatim** the exact provisions invalidated in *MBA V* (**Section 3(1)** age-50 minimum, **Section 3(7)** two-name SCSC panel, **Section 5** four-year tenure, and **Section 7(1)** executive control over allowances) without removing the constitutional basis of the Court's judgment.
5. **The Landmark Showdown — *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (`2025 INSC 1330`, November 19, 2025 — CJI B.R. Gavai & J. K. Vinod Chandran):**
   - **Core Ratio:** Reaffirming *State of Tamil Nadu v. State of Kerala* (2014), the Court held that while Parliament may cure a defect identified in a judgment, it **cannot enact a naked legislative override** recycling invalidated clauses in identical form. The Bench **struck down Sections 3(1), 3(7), 5, and 7(1)** (and allied provisions) of the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** as unconstitutional violations of **Separation of Powers, Judicial Independence, and Judicial Supremacy**.
   - **Binding 2026 Regime:** The judgment (i) **quashed the 50-year minimum age cap**, reopening tribunal benches to qualified advocates with 10+ years' standing; (ii) **restored the 5-year tenure** (subject to retirement caps of **70 for Chairpersons** and **67 for Members**); (iii) **abolished the two-name SCSC panel**, restoring binding **single-name recommendations** by a judiciary-led SCSC; (iv) revived the ***MBA IV* (2020) and *MBA V* (2021)** directions as operative interim law; and (v) issued a **peremptory 4-month mandamus** (deadline: March 2026) directing the Union Government to establish an autonomous **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)** to strip litigant parent ministries of control over tribunal recruitment, budgets, infrastructure, and discipline.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Article 233 vs. Article 234 Appointment Trap:** **District Judges (Art 233)** are appointed by the **Governor in consultation with the High Court ONLY** (NO SPSC!). Judicial officers *below* District Judge (**Art 234**) require consultation with **BOTH the SPSC AND the High Court**.
> - **District Judge Direct Quota — Active 2026 Law (*Rejanish K.V.*, Oct 2025):** Do **NOT** mark *Dheeraj Mor* (2020) as active law! Following ***Rejanish K.V. v. K. Deepa* (`2025 INSC 1208`)**, **in-service judicial officers with 7 years' combined (Bar + Bench) experience ARE eligible** for direct District Judge recruitment (minimum age 35; eligibility tested on date of application; prospective from Oct 9, 2025).
> - **All-India Judicial Service (Art 312) Triple Trap:** Requires a **Rajya Sabha resolution by 2/3rd present & voting** (NOT prior Presidential permission), **cannot include posts below District Judge** (Art 312(3)), and the parliamentary Act creating AIJS is **NOT a Constitutional Amendment under Art 368** (Art 312(4)).
> - **Regular vs. Permanent Lok Adalat Trap:** A **Regular Lok Adalat** has **NO adjudicatory power** (compromise only; zero appeal; court fee refunded); a **Permanent Lok Adalat** (Public Utility Services up to **₹1 crore**) has **BOTH conciliatory AND adjudicatory power** to decide on merits if compromise fails (also final, non-appealable).
> - **NALSA Eligibility Trap (UPSC 2020):** Free legal aid under Sec 12 of the LSA Act, 1987 covers SCs/STs, women, children, disabled persons, custody inmates, industrial workmen, and income-eligible citizens—it does **NOT** automatically cover all **OBCs** or all **senior citizens** irrespective of income.
> - **Mediation Act, 2023 Precision Traps:** Pre-litigation mediation is **voluntary** (NOT mandatory, except Commercial Courts Sec 12A); timeline is **120 + 60 = 180 days**; **Community Mediation** settlements are **NOT enforceable as court decrees**; and the Act does **NOT** enforce foreign-seated MSAs under the Singapore Convention.
> - **Article 323A vs. 323B Four-Way Trap:** **Art 323A** = Parliament ONLY, public services ONLY, NO hierarchy, excludes Defence/SC/Parliament staff. **Art 323B** = Parliament AND State Legislatures, multiple non-service domains, hierarchy permitted. Neither can oust High Court **Art 226/227** writ review (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997).
> - **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 — Active 2026 Status:** Following ***Madras Bar Association* (`2025 INSC 1330`, Nov 19, 2025)**, the **4-year tenure**, **age-50 floor**, and **two-name SCSC panel** are **STRUCK DOWN**; active law mandates **5-year tenure (70/67 retirement cap)**, 10-yr advocate eligibility, single-name binding SCSC selection, and creation of an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**.

> - **Tribunalization vs. Executive Capture (2025–2026 GS2 Bullseye):** Pair the **2025 GS2 PYQ** on 2021 Tribunal rationalization with the **Nov 19, 2025 *MBA* verdict (`2025 INSC 1330`)**. While abolishing redundant tribunals (IPAB, FCAT) reduced institutional fragmentation, re-enacting invalidated executive controls exposed **"executive tribunalization"**—where litigant parent ministries control judges' tenures and salaries. Anchor the solution on an independent **National Tribunals Commission (NTC)**, judicial-majority SCSCs, and pre-enactment Judicial Impact Assessments (*Rojer Mathew*, 2019).
