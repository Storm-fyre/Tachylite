# Fundamental Rights II — Democratic Freedoms, Criminal Due Process & Expanding Article 21 (Articles 19–22)

Articles 19 to 22 form the liberty core of the Indian Constitution, calibrating the eternal tension between **democratic autonomy** and **coercive State power**. Together, they transition from the active civic freedoms of the citizen (Article 19), through non-derogable criminal trial safeguards (Article 20), to the expansive reservoir of human dignity and substantive due process (Articles 21 and 21A), before confronting the Constitution's starkest paradox—constitutionalized peacetime preventive detention alongside strict arrest protections (Article 22).

A structural threshold divides this cluster at the outset. **Article 19 applies exclusively to citizens of India**—excluding foreign nationals and juristic persons such as companies (*State Trading Corporation v. CTO*, 1963; though shareholders may sue where corporate restrictions directly impair their personal rights: *Bennett Coleman*, 1972). By contrast, **Articles 20, 21, 21A, and 22 protect "all persons"** within Indian territory, with Articles 20 and 21 enjoying unique constitutional permanence: post the **44th Amendment Act (1978)**, neither can be suspended even during a Proclamation of National Emergency under Article 359.

## 1. Article 19 - Six Democratic Freedoms & Exhaustive Restrictions

Originally enumerating seven freedoms, Article 19(1) now protects **six democratic liberties** after the **44th Amendment Act, 1978** omitted Article 19(1)(f) (Right to Property). Unlike the US First Amendment—which states freedoms in absolute terms and leaves courts to invent implied limits—the Indian Constitution adopts **codified, exhaustive restriction clauses in Articles 19(2) to 19(6)**.

Any curtailment of an Article 19 freedom must satisfy **three cumulative constitutional gates**:
1. **Authority of "Law":** Restrictions can only be imposed by valid enacted legislation (or delegated rules under it), never by mere executive circulars or administrative instructions (*Bijoe Emanuel*, 1986).
2. **Exhaustive Grounds:** The State cannot invent new grounds of restriction beyond those textually enumerated in the corresponding clause (Arts 19(2)–(6)).
3. **Reasonableness & Proportionality:** Both the substantive restriction and its procedural enforcement must be reasonable—free from arbitrariness, overbreadth, or excessive intrusion (*Chintaman Rao v. State of MP*, 1951; *Modern Dental College*, 2016).

### Article 19(1)(a) - Freedom of Speech and Expression & Article 19(2)

Article 19(2) lists **eight exhaustive grounds** for reasonable restrictions: **sovereignty and integrity of India** *(added by the 16th CAA, 1963)*, **security of the State**, **friendly relations with foreign States** *(1st CAA, 1951)*, **public order** *(1st CAA, 1951)*, **decency or morality**, **contempt of court**, **defamation**, and **incitement to an offence** *(1st CAA, 1951)*. Notably, **"general public interest" is NOT a valid ground** under Article 19(2).

- **Freedom of the Press:** Although not named separately, press freedom is embedded in Article 19(1)(a) as the lifeblood of democratic circulation. In ***Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (1950)** and ***Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi* (1950)**, the Supreme Court struck down entry bans and pre-censorship imposed to preserve "public safety," triggering the **1st Amendment (1951)** to insert *"public order"* and *"reasonable"* into Article 19(2). Subsequent landmarks—***Sakal Papers* (1962)** (invalidating price-page caps), ***Bennett Coleman v. UoI* (1972)** (striking down 10-page newsprint ceilings), and ***Indian Express Newspapers* (1984)** (invalidating targeted newsprint taxes)—established that economic or volume controls directly impairing circulation violate Article 19(1)(a). By contrast, ***K.A. Abbas v. UoI* (1970)** upheld statutory **pre-censorship of cinema** under the Cinematograph Act because motion pictures exert an instantaneous audio-visual impact unlike print.
- **Voter's Right to Know & Democratic Transparency:** Democratic choice is hollow without informed voters. In ***Union of India v. ADR* (2002)** and ***PUCL v. UoI* (2003)**, the Court held that voters hold a Fundamental Right under Article 19(1)(a) to know candidates' criminal antecedents, assets, liabilities, and education. Building directly on this lineage, a 5-Judge Bench in ***ADR v. Union of India* (Feb 2024)** struck down the anonymous **Electoral Bonds Scheme** as violating voters' Article 19(1)(a) right to information regarding political funding.
- **Right to Silence:** Freedom of expression encompasses the freedom *not* to speak. In ***Bijoe Emanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986)**, the Supreme Court reversed the expulsion of three Jehovah's Witness schoolchildren who stood respectfully during the National Anthem without singing due to religious conscience, holding that compelled speech without statutory backing under Article 19(2) violates both Articles 19(1)(a) and 25(1).

#### Proportionality Test & Internet Shutdowns - *Anuradha Bhasin* to Telecom Act 2023

Modern speech restrictions are judged through the **Four-Pronged Proportionality Test**, imported from German/South African jurisprudence in ***Modern Dental College v. State of MP* (2016)** and cemented in ***Puttaswamy* (2017)**: (1) **Legitimate Goal** linked to an enumerated constitutional ground; (2) **Suitability / Rational Nexus** between measure and goal; (3) **Necessity (Least Restrictive Means)**—no less intrusive alternative achieves the same objective; and (4) **Balancing (*Proportionality Stricto Sensu*)**—the public benefit must outweigh the rights deprivation.

In ***Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (Jan 10, 2020)**, reviewing the prolonged telecom blackout in Jammu & Kashmir, the Supreme Court held that **freedom of speech (Art 19(1)(a)) and freedom of trade/profession (Art 19(1)(g)) over the medium of the internet** enjoy full constitutional protection. *(Crucial precision: the SC stopped short of declaring standalone internet access a Fundamental Right—unlike the Kerala High Court in Faheema Shirin [2019] under Article 21.)* Applying proportionality, the Court ruled that **indefinite internet shutdowns are impermissible**, suspension orders must be **published** to enable judicial review, orders must undergo periodic scrutiny, and Section 144 CrPC (now Sec 163 BNSS) cannot be deployed repeatedly to suppress legitimate democratic dissent.

Parliament subsequently repealed the colonial Telegraph Act, 1885 and codified shutdowns under **Section 20(2)(b) of the Telecommunications Act, 2023** and the **Telecommunications (Temporary Suspension of Services) Rules, 2024**. Suspension orders may be issued only on the occurrence of a **public emergency** or in the interest of **public safety** linked to Article 19(2) grounds, solely by the Union or State Home Secretary (a Joint Secretary may act in unavoidable urgency subject to 24-hour confirmation). While the 2024 Rules codify **mandatory publication**, a **15-day maximum cap per order**, and scrutiny by a Review Committee within **5 days**, critics highlight a continuing constitutional deficit: the Review Committee remains purely executive-composed without an express mandate to record proportionality findings.

#### Online Speech, Intermediaries & Synthetic Media

In ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015)**, the Supreme Court struck down **Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000** in its entirety for vagueness, overbreadth, and creating a **"chilling effect"** on online speech. Drawing an enduring three-tier doctrinal line, Justice R.F. Nariman separated **Discussion** and **Advocacy** (both fully protected, however unpopular) from **Incitement**—holding that speech may be curtailed under Article 19(2) *only* when it crosses into direct incitement to public disorder or offences. The Court simultaneously read down **Section 79(3)(b)** so intermediaries lose safe harbour only upon ignoring a **court order or valid government notification** (not private complaints), while upholding **Section 69A** website-blocking due to its narrow Art 19(2) grounds and procedural review safeguards.

The post-2023 explosion of generative AI, voice cloning, and synthetically generated information (SGI) has opened a fresh constitutional front between **Article 19(1)(a)** (political satire, parody, and digital speech) and **Article 21** (reputational dignity, electoral integrity, and personality rights). While executive attempts to authorize unilateral government fact-checking units faced judicial invalidation for chilling political dissent (Bombay HC striking down the 2023 FCU amendment in *Kunal Kamra v. UoI*, Sept 2024), constitutional courts have simultaneously recognized the need for proportionate statutory guardrails against deceptive synthetic media; for the full regulatory and technical architecture governing takedowns, watermarking, and SGI compliance, see [[9. Information Warfare, IT Rules 2026 & Online Radicalization#IT Rules 2021 to 2026 Synthetic Media Overhaul|IT Rules 2026 (Deepfakes/SGI)]].

#### Sedition - *Kedar Nath Singh* (1962) to BNS Section 152

The Constituent Assembly explicitly **deleted "sedition"** from Article 19(2) on K.M. Munshi's motion. Yet colonial **Section 124A IPC** survived because a 5-Judge Constitution Bench in ***Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* (1962)** applied a saving reading-down construction: mere criticism or strong disapproval of government policy is protected speech; Section 124A attracts criminal liability *only* when speech carries an **incitement to violence or tendency to create public disorder**.

Amid rampant police misuse ignoring *Kedar Nath*'s threshold, the Supreme Court in ***S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India* (May 11, 2022)** placed all Section 124A registrations and trials in **abeyance** and referred the challenge to a larger Constitution Bench in September 2023 *(clarifying on May 21, 2026 that legacy IPC 124A trials/appeals may proceed only if the accused affirmatively records no objection)*.

On July 1, 2024, the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023** formally dropped the word "sedition" and replaced Section 124A with **Section 152** (*"Acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India"*). Textually, Section 152 shifts the protected target from "disaffection toward the Government" to **national sovereignty and integrity**, introduces an express *mens rea* (**"purposely or knowingly"**), and covers electronic communication and financial means. However, constitutional critics point to vague, undefined triggers (**"subversive activities," "encouraging feelings of separatist activities"**) and stiffer minimum/alternative imprisonment (life imprisonment, or up to **7 years** plus fine, replacing IPC 124A's 3-year tier). In **August 2025**, the Supreme Court issued notice on Maj. Gen. S.G. Vombatkere's constitutional challenge to **BNS Section 152** and tagged it with the pending Section 124A Constitution Bench batch (awaiting final adjudication as of November 2026).

### Other Article 19 Freedoms - Assembly, Association, Movement, Residence & Trade

- **Article 19(1)(b) — Peaceful Assembly Without Arms (Restrictions: Art 19(3) — Sovereignty & Integrity, Public Order):** Protects public meetings, demonstrations, and processions on public land, but does not confer a right to hold meetings on private property or obstruct public arteries indefinitely. In ***Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police* (*Shaheen Bagh*, 2020)**, the Supreme Court held that while democratic dissent is constitutionally cherished, **indefinite occupation of public roads** upsets the balance against other citizens' Article 19(1)(d) mobility, requiring protests to be confined to designated spaces.
- **Article 19(1)(c) — Associations, Unions & Co-operative Societies (Restrictions: Art 19(4) — Sovereignty & Integrity, Public Order, Morality):** The **97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011** elevated forming **co-operative societies** into an express Fundamental Right. The right to form an association includes the right to *continue* it with chosen members without State imposition of outsiders (*Damayanti Naranga*, 1971) and the negative right *not* to join an association. Crucially, **there is NO Fundamental Right to Strike**: in *Kameshwar Prasad* (1962) and ***T.K. Rangarajan v. Government of Tamil Nadu* (2003)**, the Court held that government employees possess neither a fundamental, statutory, nor moral right to strike (industrial workers enjoy only a regulated *statutory* right under industrial law). Likewise, coercive ***bandhs*** paralyzing civic life are unconstitutional (*Communist Party of India (M) v. Bharat Kumar*, 1998), unlike voluntary peaceful *hartals*.
- **Article 19(1)(d) & (e) — Movement & Residence Across India (Restrictions: Art 19(5) — General Public Interest, Protection of Scheduled Tribes):** Cementing national unity and single citizenship, these twin clauses protect **internal movement and settlement** throughout Indian territory *(distinguish the right to travel **abroad**, which resides under **Article 21**: Satwant Singh Sawhney, 1967; Maneka Gandhi, 1978)*. Under Article 19(5), restrictions protecting **Scheduled Tribes** validate the **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** regime in Northeast India and Fifth/Sixth Schedule restrictions on non-tribal land alienation; public-interest grounds similarly validate externment of habitual offenders and epidemiological quarantines (*State of UP v. Kaushaliya*, 1964).
- **Article 19(1)(g) — Profession, Occupation, Trade or Business (Restrictions: Art 19(6) — General Public Interest, Technical/Professional Qualifications, State Monopolies [1st CAA]):** Guarantees economic autonomy, including the right to close down a loss-making business subject to reasonable labour safeguards (*Excel Wear*, 1978). However, the **Doctrine of *Res Extra Commercium*** (*"things outside commerce"*) carves out inherently noxious or deleterious activities—such as **gambling/prize competitions** (*State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla*, 1957) and **trade in intoxicating liquor** (*Khoday Distilleries v. State of Karnataka*, 1995)—holding that citizens possess *no* Fundamental Right under Article 19(1)(g) to trade in them, empowering the State to impose total prohibition without triggering reasonableness hurdles.

## 2. Article 20 - Non-Derogable Criminal Trial Safeguards

Article 20 erects three ironclad shields against arbitrary penal power. Available to **citizens and foreigners alike** (including corporations where applicable), these guarantees **cannot be suspended even during a National Emergency** (Art 359, 44th CAA).

### Article 20(1) - Protection Against Ex-Post Facto Criminal Laws

Article 20(1) contains **two distinct prohibitions**: (i) no person shall be **convicted** of any offence except for violation of a **"law in force"** at the time of commission of the act; and (ii) no person shall be subjected to a **penalty greater** than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force when the offence was committed (*Kedar Nath v. State of West Bengal*, 1953).

**Atomic Boundaries:**
- Applies **strictly to criminal offences and penal sanctions**—it does **NOT** bar retrospective civil liabilities, retrospective taxation, or preventive detention orders.
- Bars only retrospective **conviction or enhanced sentence**, **NOT** retrospective changes in **trial procedure or evidence rules** (*Rao Shiv Bahadur Singh v. State of Vindhya Pradesh*, 1953)—unless the procedural change creates a new disability or penalty.
- Permits **retrospective beneficial/lenient laws** (*ex post facto* mitigation): under the rule of beneficial construction, an accused is entitled to the advantage of a subsequent statute reducing punishment (*T. Barai v. Henry Ah Hoe*, 1983).

### Article 20(2) - Protection Against Double Jeopardy

Article 20(2) states: *"No person shall be prosecuted **and** punished for the same offence more than once."* Derived from the maxim *nemo debet bis vexari*, India's constitutional guarantee is **deliberately narrower** than the US Fifth Amendment and British common law:
- **Conjunctive "AND" (*Autrefois Convict* Only):** Both prior **prosecution** AND prior **punishment** must have occurred. If an accused was previously *acquitted*, Article 20(2) is not constitutionally triggered—protection against retrial after acquittal (*autrefois acquit*) exists only as a **statutory right** under Section 300 CrPC / **Section 337 BNSS, 2023**.
- **Judicial Forum Requirement (*Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay*, 1953):** "Prosecution" requires proceedings before a **court of law or judicial tribunal**. Departmental disciplinary inquiries against civil servants (*Venkataraman*, 1954) or administrative confiscation of smuggled gold by Sea Customs authorities (*Maqbool Hussain*; *Thomas Dana*, 1959) do not bar subsequent criminal prosecution.
- **Same Offence:** Does not bar prosecution for distinct offences arising from the same transaction if their statutory ingredients differ.

### Article 20(3) - Privilege Against Self-Incrimination & Modern Forensics

Article 20(3) commands: *"No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself."* Three cumulative conditions trigger this privilege: (1) the person must stand **formally accused** of an offence (via FIR, criminal complaint, or formal accusation—not mere witness summons or preliminary customs/ED inquiry prior to accusation: *M.P. Sharma*, 1954; *Raja Narayanlal Bansilal*, 1961); (2) there must be **compulsion** (physical duress, psychological coercion, or legal threat; voluntary confessions under judicial safeguards remain admissible); and (3) the compulsion must force the accused to act as a **witness against themselves**.

- **Physical Evidence vs. Testimonial Compulsion — *State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961, 11-Judge Bench):** Resolving uncertainty left by *M.P. Sharma* (1954), an 11-Judge Bench held that "to be a witness" means imparting **personal knowledge** of relevant facts through oral or written testimony. Compelling an accused to provide **fingerprints, palm/foot impressions, specimen handwriting/signatures, blood/DNA samples**, or **voice samples** (*Ritesh Sinha v. State of UP*, 2019) does **NOT** violate Article 20(3), because intrinsic physical characteristics cannot be altered by coercion and are not testimonial communications of the accused's mind.
- **Neuro-Scientific Interrogation — *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010, 3-Judge Bench):** Addressing modern interrogation technologies, the Supreme Court held that **involuntary administration of Narco-analysis, Polygraph (lie-detector), and Brain Electrical Activation Profile (BEAP / brain mapping)** violates **Article 20(3)** (forcing involuntary testimonial responses directly from the subject's subconscious mind) AND **Article 21** (infringing mental privacy, bodily integrity, and substantive due process). Even where an accused *voluntarily* consents (strictly governed by NHRC safeguards before a Judicial Magistrate), test results themselves are **inadmissible as substantive evidence**—only independent derivative facts discovered pursuant to them may be admitted under Section 27 IEA / **Section 23(2) BSA, 2023**.
- **Criminal Procedure (Identification) Act, 2022:** Replacing the century-old *Identification of Prisoners Act, 1920*, the 2022 Act dramatically widens police authority to collect **"measurements"**—finger/palm/foot impressions, photographs, iris and retina scans, physical/biological samples, and **"behavioural attributes" including signatures and handwriting**—from convicts, arrestees, and preventive detainees (with Section 5 empowering a Magistrate to compel measurements from *any person* to aid an investigation), and authorizes the **National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB)** to store digital records for **75 years**. While the Supreme Court in February 2024 declined a direct Article 32 petition directing challengers to High Courts, active constitutional challenges pending before the **Delhi High Court (including fresh February 2026 petitions by Jamia students whose biometrics were collected during peaceful protests)** target two acute constitutional fault lines: (i) whether vague "behavioural attributes" trespass across *Selvi*'s Article 20(3) boundary, and (ii) whether 75-year bulk biometric retention of non-convicts fails *Puttaswamy*'s Article 21 proportionality test.

## 3. Article 21 & Article 21A - From Positivist Shell to Substantive Due Process

Reading just twenty-one words—*"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law"*—Article 21 has undergone the most profound jurisprudential transformation in comparative constitutional history.

### The Doctrinal Revolution - *Gopalan* (1950) → *R.C. Cooper* (1970) → *Maneka Gandhi* (1978)

1. ***A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950, 6-Judge Bench):** Testing the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, the majority adopted strict textual positivism. Noting that the Constituent Assembly (guided by B.N. Rau's consultation with US Justice Felix Frankfurter) intentionally borrowed **"procedure established by law"** from Article 31 of the Japanese Constitution rather than the US Fifth/Fourteenth Amendment **"due process of law,"** the Court held that *any* validly enacted statutory procedure—however harsh or unjust—satisfied Article 21. Simultaneously, it erected the **Silo (Mutual Exclusivity) Theory**, declaring Articles 19, 21, and 22 watertight compartments so a preventive detention law under Art 21/22 could never be tested for reasonableness under Article 19. Only **Justice Fazl Ali's prophetic dissent** insisted that "law" includes natural justice and that Part III rights overlap.
2. ***R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (Bank Nationalisation, 1970, 11-Judge Bench):** Overturning *Gopalan*'s silo doctrine, the 11-Judge Bench ruled that Fundamental Rights are **not mutually exclusive**; courts must inspect the **direct and inevitable effect** of State action across every affected Article in Part III.
3. ***Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978, 7-Judge Bench):** Challenging the arbitrary impounding of a passport without a hearing, the Supreme Court buried *Gopalan*'s positivism forever and welded **Articles 14, 19, and 21 into the "Golden Triangle."** A law depriving life or personal liberty must simultaneously clear Article 21, satisfy Article 19's reasonableness (where freedom is impacted), and avoid Article 14's arbitrariness. Consequently, **"procedure established by law" must be "right, just, and fair"—not arbitrary, fanciful, or oppressive**—effectively importing **Substantive Due Process** into India's constitutional canon. Equally transformative, the Court read **"life"** not as mere physical survival, but as **life with human dignity** (*Francis Coralie Mullin*, 1981)—encompassing livelihood (*Olga Tellis*, 1985), shelter (*Chameli Singh*, 1996), health (*Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity*, 1996), and clean environment (*Subhash Kumar*, 1991).

### Expanding Frontiers of Article 21 (2014–2026)

#### Privacy, Data Governance & Personality Rights
In ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India* (*Puttaswamy I*, Aug 24, 2017, 9-Judge Bench, unanimous)**, the Supreme Court overruled *M.P. Sharma* (1954) and *Kharak Singh* (1962) to declare the **Right to Privacy** an intrinsic Fundamental Right under **Article 21** and the entire freedom architecture of Part III, encompassing **spatial, bodily, decisional, and informational privacy** *(and formally burying the notorious Emergency-era ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla [1976] verdict)*. Any State invasion of privacy must survive the four-pronged **proportionality test** (legality, legitimate State aim, rational nexus/necessity, and procedural safeguards). Applying this test in ***Puttaswamy II* (Sept 2018, 5-Judge, 4:1 — Aadhaar Judgment)**, the Court upheld Section 7 (targeted welfare subsidies) and PAN-Aadhaar linking as proportionate, while striking down **Section 57** (private corporate Aadhaar authentication), **Section 33(2)** (national-security disclosure authorized by a Joint Secretary), and mandatory linking of bank accounts and mobile SIMs.

*Puttaswamy I*'s recognition of informational self-determination created the constitutional imperative for a statutory personal-data regime governing consent, purpose limitation, and fiduciary accountability across both State and private data ecosystems. Evaluating whether sweeping State exemptions and executive control over the Data Protection Board satisfy *Puttaswamy*'s necessity and procedural-safeguard prongs remains central to digital constitutionalism; for the full statutory and regulatory anatomy, see [[5. Digital Governance, DPI & DPDP Architecture#DPDP Act 2023 and 2025 Rules|DPDP Act 2023 & 2025 Rules]].

At the frontier of generative AI, Indian High Courts (notably Delhi and Bombay HCs across 2023–2026) have increasingly protected **Personality Rights**—control over one's face, voice, persona, and likeness against unauthorized deepfakes and AI-cloned endorsements—by anchoring dignitary harm inside **Article 21 privacy and autonomy** alongside commercial publicity law. Because injunctive relief against synthetic impersonation collides directly with creative satire and journalistic commentary under Article 19(1)(a), judicial enforcement increasingly intersects with India's synthetic-media compliance regime outlined in [[9. Information Warfare, IT Rules 2026 & Online Radicalization#IT Rules 2021 to 2026 Synthetic Media Overhaul|IT Rules 2026 (Deepfakes/SGI)]].

#### Gender, Sexual & Bodily Autonomy - *NALSA* to *Jane Kaushik* & the 2026 Statutory Flashpoint
- ***NALSA v. Union of India* (2014):** Recognized transgender persons as the **"third gender"** and anchored the **right to self-perceived gender identity** within Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(a), and 21.
- ***Navtej Singh Johar v. UoI* (2018, 5-Judge):** Overruled *Suresh Kumar Koushal* (2013) and read down Section 377 IPC to decriminalize consensual adult same-sex relations, elevating **constitutional morality over majoritarian social morality**.
- ***Joseph Shine v. UoI* (2018, 5-Judge):** Invalidated Section 497 IPC (adultery) for treating married women as husbands' property, violating women's sexual autonomy and dignity under Articles 14, 15, and 21.
- ***Shafin Jahan* (Hadiya, 2018) & *Shakti Vahini* (2018):** Affirmed the **right of consenting adults to marry a person of their choice** (free from parental or khap coercion) under Article 21.
- ***X v. Principal Secretary, Health & Family Welfare Dept., NCT Delhi* (Sept 2022, 3-Judge):** Extended safe abortion access (20–24 weeks) under the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP) Act to **unmarried/single women**, grounding reproductive choice in Article 21 bodily autonomy and recognizing **marital rape** strictly for MTP statutory termination access.
- ***Supriyo @ Supriya Chakraborty v. Union of India* (Oct 17, 2023, 5-Judge Bench):** Unanimously held there is **no unqualified Fundamental Right to Marry** under Article 21; by a **3:2 majority**, declined to read same-sex marriage into the Special Marriage Act, 1954 or confer queer civil unions/joint adoption judicially (leaving institutional marriage recognition to Parliament), while **unanimously** affirming queer persons' Article 21 right to choose partners and cohabit free from police or familial coercion.
- ***Jane Kaushik v. Union of India* (Oct 17, 2025, `2025 INSC 1248`, Justices J.B. Pardiwala & R. Mahadevan) & 2026 Developments:** Addressing a transgender teacher terminated by private schools in UP and Gujarat, the Court recognized both **active horizontal discrimination** by private establishments and **omissive State discrimination** from bureaucratic non-enforcement of the 2019 Act; awarded compensation; and constituted an advisory committee headed by **Justice (retd.) Asha Menon** to draft a binding model Equal Opportunity Policy. Continuing monitoring into **2026** (`2026 INSC 129`, Feb 2026; and November 2026 orders), the Court directed the Government of NCT of Delhi to recall gender-specific teaching vacancy notices so transgender candidates compete fairly on merit.
- **Live Constitutional Clash — Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Amendment Act, 2026 (Act No. 3 of 2026):** Passed by Parliament in March 2026 and receiving Presidential assent on **March 30, 2026** *(not yet notified into force as of late 2026; challenged before the Supreme Court)*, the Amendment Act **deletes Section 4(2)** (the statutory right to self-perceived gender identity), narrows the definition of "transgender person" to enumerated socio-cultural and intersex categories (removing self-identified trans-men, trans-women, and genderqueer persons), mandates verification by a **CMO/DCMO-headed medical board** prior to District Magistrate certification, and requires medical reporting of gender-affirming procedures. Directly tested in **UPSC Mains 2026 GS2 (Q1)**, this enactment creates an acute constitutional collision between administrative gatekeeping of welfare benefits and *NALSA*/*Puttaswamy*'s Article 21 guarantee of self-determination and decisional privacy.

#### Right to Die with Dignity - *Common Cause* (2018/2023) to *Harish Rana* (2026)
Following *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996, 5-Judge)—which overruled *P. Rathinam* (1994) to hold that Article 21 does *not* include a right to commit suicide *(though Section 115 of the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 later effectively decriminalized attempted suicide, and BNS 2023 dropped standalone Section 309 IPC)*—the Supreme Court recognized **passive euthanasia** in *Aruna Shanbaug* (2011).

In ***Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018, 5-Judge Bench)**, the Court elevated the **Right to Die with Dignity** into Article 21, legalizing **passive euthanasia** (withholding or withdrawing futile life support; **active euthanasia remains strictly illegal**) and granting legal recognition to **Advance Medical Directives (Living Wills)**. Because the 2018 protocol required burdensome Judicial Magistrate countersignatures, a **5-Judge Constitution Bench modified the guidelines on January 24, 2023**—permitting execution before a **notary or gazetted officer** with two witnesses and imposing a 48-hour decision clock on Primary and Secondary Medical Boards.

On **March 11, 2026 (`2026 INSC 222`, Justices J.B. Pardiwala & K.V. Viswanathan)**, the Supreme Court operationalized passive euthanasia for the first time in an individual case in ***Harish Rana v. Union of India***. Permitting withdrawal of life support for a 32-year-old man in an irreversible Permanent Vegetative State (PVS) for thirteen years without a living will, the Court held that **Clinically Assisted Nutrition and Hydration (CANH)** via a surgically inserted PEG feeding tube constitutes **medical treatment** lawfully withdrawable under the **"best interests" test** upon concurring certification by Primary and Secondary Medical Boards, accompanied by dignified palliative care.

#### Right Against Climate Change - *M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024)
Building on decades of clean-environment rulings (*M.C. Mehta*, *Virender Gaur*), a 3-Judge Bench headed by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud in ***M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (March 21, 2024, `2024 INSC 280`)** explicitly recognized for the first time a distinct Fundamental Right to be **free from the adverse effects of climate change**, anchored jointly in **Articles 14 and 21**. Arising from the clash between protecting the critically endangered **Great Indian Bustard** from overhead transmission wires and India's solar-grid transition in Rajasthan and Gujarat, the Court reasoned that climate harms—extreme heat, food insecurity, sea-level rise, and displacement—inflict disproportionate injuries on poor and vulnerable communities (violating Art 14 equality) while directly eroding health and life (Art 21). *(In a follow-up December 2025 order in the same litigation, the Court further held that statutory Corporate Social Responsibility [CSR] encompasses Corporate Environmental Responsibility.)*

#### Right to Menstrual Health & Hygiene - *Dr. Jaya Thakur* (Jan 30, 2026)
In ***Dr. Jaya Thakur v. Government of India* (Jan 30, 2026, `2026 INSC 97`, Justices J.B. Pardiwala & R. Mahadevan)**, the Supreme Court elevated **dignified Menstrual Health and Hygiene Management (MHM)** from executive welfare policy into an enforceable Fundamental Right resting on a constitutional triad:
- **Article 21:** Absence of safe, hygienic menstrual management strips adolescent girls of bodily dignity, privacy, and health.
- **Article 14 (Substantive Equality):** Treating biological menstruation neutrally without institutional support turns a natural physiological reality into a structural penalty (*"the fault is not hers"*).
- **Article 21A (Right to Education):** Menstrual poverty and absent sanitation drive massive absenteeism and school dropouts, nullifying free and compulsory elementary education.

Issuing a **Continuing Mandamus**, the Bench directed the Union, States, and UTs to guarantee free biodegradable sanitary napkins, separate functional gender-segregated school toilets, safe menstrual-waste disposal, and school-wide sensitization (explicitly including boys to dismantle stigma) across government, aided, and residential schools.

#### Speedy Trial, Bail Jurisprudence & BNSS Section 479
With undertrials constituting roughly **75% of India's prison population**, Article 21's criminal-justice wing centers on pre-trial liberty:
- ***Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar* (1979):** Declared the **Right to Speedy Trial** and **Right to Free Legal Aid** (harmonizing Art 21 with Art 39A) integral Fundamental Rights.
- ***D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997):** Issued 11 mandatory pan-India arrest and custody safeguards—arrest memo attested by witness/family, right to inform relatives, medical examination every 48 hours, visible police identification tags, and district control-room display—to arrest custodial violence.
- ***Satender Kumar Antil v. CBI* (2022):** Reaffirmed **"bail is the rule, jail is the exception,"** mandated strict enforcement of notice-before-arrest rules (Sec 41/41A CrPC; now **Sec 35 BNSS**) for offences punishable up to 7 years (breach entitling automatic bail), and urged Parliament to enact a standalone Bail Act.
- **BNSS Section 479 & *In Re: Inhuman Conditions in 1382 Prisons* (Aug 23, 2024):** Replacing Section 436A CrPC, **Section 479 BNSS** retains mandatory undertrial release upon completing **one-half** of the maximum prescribed sentence, introduces an accelerated **one-third threshold for first-time offenders** (never previously convicted) on personal bond, and casts an affirmative statutory duty on **Jail Superintendents** to move courts. On August 23, 2024, the Supreme Court ruled Section 479 applies **retrospectively** to all pending cases registered prior to July 1, 2024. *Critical constitutional caveats:* unlike Section 436A CrPC (which excluded only death-penalty offences), **Section 479(1) BNSS excludes offences punishable with BOTH death AND life imprisonment**, and **Section 479(2)** bars relief altogether if investigation, inquiry, or trial is pending in **more than one offence or multiple cases**.

#### Pan-India Judgment Against "Bulldozer Justice" (Nov 13, 2024)
Confronting punitive executive demolitions of homes belonging to criminal accused, a Division Bench (Justices B.R. Gavai & K.V. Viswanathan) in ***In Re: Directions in the Matter of Demolition of Structures* (Nov 13, 2024, `2024 INSC 866`)** declared "bulldozer justice" anathema to constitutional governance. Grounding its ruling in **Rule of Law**, **Separation of Powers** (the Executive cannot usurp judicial functions to act as judge, jury, and executioner), **Presumption of Innocence**, the **prohibition on collective punishment** of innocent family members, the **Doctrine of Public Trust**, and the **Right to Shelter under Article 21**, the Court invoked **Article 142** to issue binding pan-India guidelines:
- **15-day prior show-cause notice** served via registered post AND affixed conspicuously on the structure, specifying alleged municipal violations and granting a **personal hearing**.
- **Reasoned speaking demolition order** explaining why lesser remedies (compounding/partial demolition) are infeasible, uploaded to a public digital portal, followed by a mandatory **15-day post-order cooling-off window** to seek appellate/judicial stay.
- **Mandatory videography** of demolition proceedings and inspection report; violation exposes erring officials to **contempt of court, prosecution, and personal monetary restitution** for restoring the demolished property *(reinforced in April 2025 when the SC ordered ₹10 lakh each in compensation for unlawful 2021 Prayagraj demolitions)*.
- **Carve-outs:** Guidelines do *not* apply to unauthorized encroachments on **public roads, footpaths, railway lines, or waterbodies**, nor to demolitions executed pursuant to a **court order**.

### Article 21A - Right to Education (86th CAA, 2002)

Tracing its judicial lineage from *Mohini Jain* (1992) and ***Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh* (1993)** (which confined the implied fundamental right to education to age 14), Parliament inserted **Article 21A** via the **86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002**: *"The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of **six to fourteen years** in such manner as the State may, by law, determine."* Simultaneously, the 86th CAA modified **Article 45** (redirecting DPSP focus to early childhood care and education below age 6) and inserted the **11th Fundamental Duty under Article 51A(k)** (parental/guardian duty for ages 6–14).

Operationalized through the **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009** (which mandates a 25% reservation for disadvantaged/EWS children in private unaided non-minority schools, upheld in *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan*, 2012, and *Pramati*, 2014), Article 21A applies to **all children—citizens and non-citizens alike**. As flagged in Chapter 3, *Pramati*'s blanket exclusion of minority schools from the RTE Act was formally questioned and referred to a larger Constitution Bench in ***Anjuman Ishaat-e-Taleem Trust* (Sept 2025)**.

## 4. Article 22 - Punitive Arrest Protections vs. Preventive Detention

Article 22 divides starkly into two contrasting halves: **Clauses (1)–(2)** erect procedural safeguards for **ordinary punitive arrests** (arrests for offences already allegedly committed), whereas **Clauses (4)–(7)** construct a constitutional code regulating **preventive detention** (executive incarceration without trial to pre-empt anticipated prejudicial conduct).

### Punitive Arrest Safeguards (Art 22(1)–(3)) & the Written Grounds Revolution (2023–2025)

Under **Article 22(1)**, every person arrested under ordinary criminal law has two immediate rights: (a) to be informed, *"as soon as may be,"* of the **grounds of arrest**, and (b) to **consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of their choice**. Under **Article 22(2)**, the arrestee must be produced before the nearest **Magistrate within 24 hours** (excluding necessary journey time), and cannot be detained beyond 24 hours without judicial authority. *(Note: Art 22(1)–(2) do not cover arrests under a court's civil warrant, revenue recovery, or deportation orders—State of Punjab v. Ajaib Singh, 1953.)*

**Article 22(3) — Two Express Exclusions:** Clauses (1) and (2) do **NOT** apply to: (a) any person who is for the time being an **enemy alien**, or (b) any person arrested or detained under any **law providing for preventive detention**. *(Critical nuance tested in **UPSC Prelims 2023**: while Article 22(3)(b) withholds the **constitutional entitlement** to counsel under Art 22(1) from preventive detainees, the Constitution does not positively forbid legal representation—and the Supreme Court in A.K. Roy v. UoI [1982] held that if the State uses a lawyer before the Advisory Board, the detenu must be allowed one too, and may always engage a "friend".)*

**The 2023–2025 Constitutional Quartet on Written Grounds of Arrest:**
Historically, police routinely bypassed Article 22(1) by claiming grounds were "orally read out." Between 2023 and 2025, the Supreme Court transformed Article 22(1) into a potent liberty shield across both special and general penal statutes:
1. ***Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India* (Oct 2023):** Interpreting Section 19 of the PMLA alongside Article 22(1), the Court held that merely reading out grounds is insufficient—investigating agencies must **furnish written grounds of arrest** to the arrestee as a matter of course and without exception.
2. ***Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (May 15, 2024, `2024 INSC 414`):** Extended the mandatory written-grounds rule to arrests under the **UAPA, 1967** and drew a vital conceptual distinction between **"reasons for arrest"** (generic statutory parameters like preventing evidence tampering or ensuring trial appearance) and **"grounds of arrest"** (personal, fact-specific material particular to the accused necessary to oppose remand and seek bail).
3. ***Vihaan Kumar v. State of Haryana* (Feb 7, 2025):** Extended mandatory, meaningful Art 22(1) communication (paired with **Section 47 BNSS / Section 50 CrPC**) to **ordinary IPC/BNS criminal offences**—ruling that non-compliance violates **both Article 22(1) and Article 21**, automatically **vitiates the arrest and subsequent remand order**, and mandates immediate release even if a chargesheet has since been filed.
4. ***Mihir Rajesh Shah v. State of Maharashtra* (Nov 2025):** Synthesizing the trilogy uniformly across BNS and special statutes, the Court confirmed that written grounds **in a language understood by the arrestee** are mandatory in *all* offences, while accommodating genuine on-the-spot investigative exigencies (requiring written service reasonably before remand production so defence counsel can contest custody meaningfully).

### Preventive Detention (Art 22(4)–(7)), Statutory Regimes & *Ameena Begum* (2023)

India is rare among liberal democracies in embedding peacetime preventive detention directly inside its Bill of Rights—a pragmatic compromise struck by B.R. Ambedkar amid Partition violence and insurgencies. **Legislative power** is federally divided under the Seventh Schedule:
- **Parliament Exclusively (List I, Entry 9):** Preventive detention for reasons connected with **defence, foreign affairs, or the security of India**.
- **Concurrent Power — Parliament & State Legislatures (List III, Entry 3):** Preventive detention for reasons connected with the **security of a State, maintenance of public order, or maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community**.

**Constitutional Safeguards (Art 22(4)–(7)):**
- **Three-Month Advisory Board Cap (Art 22(4)):** No preventive detention law can authorize detention beyond **three months** unless an **Advisory Board**—composed of sitting/retired High Court judges or persons qualified to be appointed as HC judges—reports before expiry of three months that **sufficient cause** exists for extended detention. *(Devastating Prelims Trap: The **44th Amendment Act, 1978** amended Article 22(4) to cut this initial cap from **3 months to 2 months** and require Advisory Board appointment on the Chief Justice of the HC's recommendation—**but Section 3 of the 44th CAA has NEVER been notified into force** by any Union Government! Thus, **3 months** remains the operative constitutional law.)*
- **Communication & Representation (Art 22(5)–(6)):** The detaining authority must communicate the grounds of detention *"as soon as may be"* and afford the **earliest opportunity of making a representation** against the order—subject to **Article 22(6)** allowing non-disclosure of facts whose revelation is contrary to the **public interest**.
- **Exclusive Parliamentary Override Power (Art 22(7)):** **Only Parliament** (never a State Legislature) may by law prescribe: (a) the circumstances and classes of cases where detention may exceed 3 months **without** Advisory Board opinion; (b) the **maximum period** of detention in any class of cases; and (c) the procedure followed by Advisory Boards.

**Major Statutes — Crucial Classification Distinction:**
- **True Preventive Detention Statutes (Art 22(4)–(7)):** **National Security Act (NSA), 1980** (maximum 12 months); **COFEPOSA, 1974** (foreign exchange/smuggling); PITNDPS Act, 1988; and State preventive detention laws (e.g., UP Gangsters/Telangana PD/Tamil Nadu Goondas Acts).
- **NOT Preventive Detention — UAPA, 1967:** Frequently conflated in coaching notes, the **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967** is a **punitive criminal anti-terror statute** governed by **Article 22(1)–(2)** (*Prabir Purkayastha*, 2024), NOT a preventive detention law under Article 22(4)—though its stringent bail threshold (Sec 43D(5)) and 180-day pre-chargesheet custody period create prolonged *de facto* pre-trial incarceration (*K.A. Najeeb*, 2021, allowing constitutional courts to grant bail under Art 21 when trials face inordinate delay despite Sec 43D(5)).

**Judicial Tightening — *Ameena Begum v. State of Telangana* (Sept 4, 2023, `2023 INSC 788`):**
Quashing a preventive detention order issued under the Telangana Prevention of Dangerous Activities Act against a repeat criminal accused, the Supreme Court issued a rigorous constitutional rebuke against the "routine weaponization" of preventive detention by States:
- **Reaffirming *Ram Manohar Lohia*'s (1966) Concentric Circles:** Courts must strictly differentiate three nested domains—**"Law and Order"** (widest circle: individual crimes affecting specific victims), **"Public Order"** (middle circle: acts disturbing the even tempo of community life and evoking public panic/insecurity), and **"Security of the State"** (narrowest core: existential threats to governance). Preventive detention is an extraordinary weapon reserved strictly for **public order or security**, never for ordinary law-and-order offences or to **bypass bail granted by ordinary criminal courts**.
- **No Mechanical Maximum Confirmation:** Confirming detention automatically for the statutory **12-month maximum** without recording individualized, reasoned justification violates Article 21's fairness standard and vitiates the order.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Citizens vs. All Persons Split:** **Article 19** is available **only to citizens** (not foreigners, not companies). **Articles 20, 21, 21A, and 22** extend to **all persons** (except Art 22(1)–(2) which exclude *enemy aliens* and preventive detainees under Art 22(3)).
> - **Article 19(2) Missing Ground Trap:** **"Interests of the general public"** is a restriction ground under **Art 19(5)** (movement/residence) and **Art 19(6)** (profession/trade)—it is **NOT** a valid ground under **Article 19(2)** (speech & expression)! Also recall that **1st CAA (1951)** added *public order, friendly relations, incitement to an offence* (plus *reasonable*), while **16th CAA (1963)** added *sovereignty and integrity of India* to Arts 19(2), (3), and (4).
> - **Travel Abroad vs. Internal Movement:** Free movement *within* India is **Article 19(1)(d)** (citizens only); the right to travel *abroad* and hold a passport resides under **Article 21** (*Satwant Singh*, 1967; *Maneka Gandhi*, 1978).
> - **Article 20(1)–(3) Precision Traps:** (1) **Art 20(1)** bars retrospective *criminal* laws/enhanced penalties, **NOT** retrospective civil/tax laws or *lenient* criminal laws. (2) **Art 20(2)** uses conjunctive *"prosecuted **and** punished"*—protecting only against double punishment (*autrefois convict*), **NOT** retrial after acquittal (statutory protection lies in Sec 337 BNSS) and **NOT** departmental/customs penalties. (3) **Art 20(3)** bars involuntary *testimonial* brain/narco/polygraph tests (*Selvi*, 2010), **NOT** physical biometrics/handwriting/voice samples (*Kathi Kalu Oghad*, 1961; *Ritesh Sinha*, 2019).
> - **44th CAA Unnotified 2-Month Trap (Art 22(4)):** Although the **44th CAA, 1978** passed an amendment shortening preventive detention without Advisory Board approval from **3 months to 2 months**, it was **never brought into force by notification**—so **3 months** remains the active constitutional limit! Also remember **ONLY Parliament** (not State Legislatures) can prescribe circumstances waiving Advisory Board review or fixing maximum detention periods under **Article 22(7)**.
> - **UAPA vs. NSA Trap:** **NSA (1980)** is a *preventive detention* law under Art 22(4)–(7); **UAPA (1967)** is a *punitive* anti-terror law governed by Art 22(1)–(2) (requiring written grounds of arrest under *Prabir Purkayastha*, 2024).
> - **BNSS Section 479 Subtle Trap:** While Sec 479 BNSS lowers the undertrial bail clock for **first-time offenders** from 1/2 to **1/3rd** (applied retrospectively by SC in Aug 2024), it **excludes life-imprisonment offences** (tighter than old Sec 436A CrPC, which excluded only death-penalty offences) and bars release where **multiple cases** are pending (Sec 479(2)).
