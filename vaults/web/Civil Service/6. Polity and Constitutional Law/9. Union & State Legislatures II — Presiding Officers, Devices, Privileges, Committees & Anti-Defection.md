# Chapter 9 - Union & State Legislatures II — Presiding Officers, Devices, Privileges, Committees & Anti-Defection

If Chapter 8 examined the statutory mechanics of lawmaking and the budget, this chapter dissects the **institutional nervous system** that determines whether a legislature genuinely checks the executive or deteriorates into a rubber stamp. Five internal institutions govern that equilibrium: **Presiding Officers**, **floor accountability devices**, **Parliamentary Privileges**, **Parliamentary Committees**, and the **Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule)**.

Between 2023 and 2026, nearly every one of these institutions faced acute constitutional stress—and transformative Supreme Court intervention. The unanimous 7-Judge verdict in ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (March 2024)** stripped bribed legislators of constitutional immunity; the **March 2026 removal motion against the Lok Sabha Speaker** activated the rare mechanics of Article 96; the **seven-year Deputy Speaker vacancy** tested textual mandates under Article 93; and the Supreme Court’s **July 2025 Telangana Anti-Defection mandate** (*Padi Kaushik Reddy*) underscored how partisan Speaker inertia threatens representative democracy.

## Presiding Officers, Leadership & Parliamentary Secretariats (Articles 89–98, 178–187)

Parliamentary autonomy depends on the impartial refereeing of floor debate, agenda allocation, Money Bill certification, and disqualification adjudication. Part V (**Articles 89–98**) and Part VI (**Articles 178–187**) construct an elaborate architecture of neutral offices—whose constitutional independence has increasingly collided with party-whip realpolitik.

### The Lok Sabha Speaker — Constitutional Autonomy vs. Indian Partisanship (Articles 93–96, 100)

Elected by the Lok Sabha from among its own members on a date fixed by the **President** (**Article 93**), the Speaker is the supreme guardian of the House’s rights, privileges, and procedure—combining authority derived from the Constitution, Rules of Procedure, and parliamentary conventions. *(Prior to the Speaker’s election in a newly constituted House, the President conventionally appoints the senior-most MP as **Speaker Pro Tem** under **Article 95(1)**—a term absent from the constitutional text—strictly to administer oaths and conduct the Speaker’s election.)*

- **Post-Dissolution Continuity (Second Proviso to Article 94):** Unlike ordinary MPs, the Speaker does **not** vacate office upon dissolution of the Lok Sabha; they remain in office until **immediately before the first meeting of the newly constituted Lok Sabha**, ensuring institutional continuity of the Secretariat and House property.
- **Removal Mechanics & Article 96 Live Precedent (March 2026):** Under **Article 94(c)**, the Speaker may be removed only by a Lok Sabha resolution passed by an **Effective Majority** (*"a majority of all the then members of the House"*, i.e., >50% of total strength minus actual vacancies), requiring **14 days’ advance written notice** and support of at least **50 members** for leave to move. **Article 96** enforces strict procedural fairness while such a resolution is under consideration—demonstrated live during the **March 10–11, 2026** Lok Sabha debate (moved by Congress MP Dr. Mohammad Jawed alongside K. Suresh and Mallu Ravi, chaired by Jagdambika Pal from the Panel of Chairpersons in the absence of a Deputy Speaker, and defeated by voice vote on **March 11, 2026** against Speaker Om Birla):
  1. The Speaker **cannot preside** over the sitting, though present in the House;
  2. The Speaker retains the constitutional right to **speak and otherwise take part in proceedings**;
  3. The Speaker is entitled to vote **ONLY in the first instance**—and is barred from exercising a **casting vote** in the event of an equality of votes!
- **Casting Vote (Article 100(1)):** In ordinary proceedings, the Speaker does **not** vote in the first instance, exercising a **casting vote** solely to break a tie. Beyond floor management, the Speaker certifies **Money Bills (Art 110(3))**, presides over **Article 108 Joint Sittings**, appoints Chairpersons of Lok Sabha committees (and ex-officio chairs the Business Advisory, Rules, and General Purposes Committees), and adjudicates **Tenth Schedule** petitions.
- **UK Non-Partisan Convention vs. Indian Partisan Reality:** Under British parliamentary convention (*"Once a Speaker, always a Speaker"*), the Westminster Speaker **resigns from their political party** upon election, contests re-election unopposed in their constituency, and abstains from partisan politics. Although **Paragraph 5 of India’s Tenth Schedule** explicitly exempts a Presiding Officer from disqualification if they voluntarily resign from their party upon election and rejoin afterward, **Indian Speakers rarely sever party ties**—contesting elections on party tickets and returning to ministerial or gubernatorial roles. This institutional dependency directly fuels recurring charges of bias in Money Bill certification, Opposition suspensions, and Tenth Schedule delays.

### The Deputy Speaker & the Seven-Year Article 93 Vacancy Crisis

Under **Article 93**, the Lok Sabha *"shall, **as soon as may be**, choose two members of the House to be respectively Speaker and Deputy Speaker."* Unlike the Speaker’s election date (fixed by the President), the **Deputy Speaker’s election date is fixed by the Speaker**. Crucially, the Deputy Speaker is **NOT subordinate to the Speaker**—they are directly responsible to the House, automatically become Chairperson of any parliamentary committee to which they are appointed, vote as an ordinary member when not presiding, and enjoy a casting vote only while in the Chair. *(Whenever both Speaker and Deputy Speaker are merely **absent**, a member from the Speaker-nominated **Panel of Chairpersons**—up to 10 members—presides; however, if both offices are **vacant**, the Panel cannot preside and the President appoints a member under **Article 95(1)**.)*

From the 11th Lok Sabha (1996) through the 16th Lok Sabha, healthy parliamentary convention allotted the Deputy Speakership to the **Main Opposition Party** (while the ruling coalition held the Speakership). That convention collapsed completely after May 2019: the **17th Lok Sabha (2019–2024)** became the **first Lok Sabha in Indian history to complete a full five-year term without a Deputy Speaker**, and as of **November 2026**, the post remains **vacant in the 18th Lok Sabha**—stretching the constitutional hiatus beyond **seven consecutive years**. A pending Supreme Court PIL (on which notices were issued to the Centre and five States in 2023) squarely interrogates whether the phrase *"as soon as may be"* in **Articles 93 and 178** imposes a justiciable constitutional obligation or remains an unenforceable parliamentary silence.

### Rajya Sabha Presiding Officers, LoP & Independent Secretariats

- **Rajya Sabha Chairman & Deputy Chairman (Articles 89–92):** The Vice-President is ex-officio Chairman (**Art 89(1)**) and is **not a member of the House**. Observe a sharp Prelims contrast between **Article 92** and **Article 96**: while a removal resolution is under consideration against the **RS Chairman**, the Chairman cannot preside, may speak and participate, yet **CANNOT vote even in the first instance** (because they are not an RS member)—whereas the LS Speaker under Article 96 *can* vote in the first instance! The **Deputy Chairman** is elected by the Rajya Sabha from among its members and removed by an **Effective Majority** after 14 days’ notice (**Art 90(c)**).
- **Leader of the House & Leader of Opposition (LoP):** **Neither office is mentioned in the Constitution.** The Leader of the House is defined in the Rules of Procedure (usually the PM in LS if an LS member, or a nominated minister). The **Leader of Opposition** enjoys **statutory recognition** under the **Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977** (conferring Cabinet Minister rank and facilities). Crucially, the **10% minimum seat threshold** (55 seats in LS; 25 in RS) is **NOT in the 1977 Act**—it derives from the **Mavalankar Rule** (1956) and **Direction 121 of the Speaker’s Directions**. Following a ten-year vacuum across the 16th and 17th Lok Sabhas (2014–2024), the **18th Lok Sabha restored an official LoP in June 2024** (**Rahul Gandhi**, INC with 99 seats; **Mallikarjun Kharge** in RS)—reactivating opposition participation across statutory appointment panels (**CBI Director, CVC, CIC, NHRC, Lokpal, and the CEC/ECs selection committee under the 2023 Act**).
- **Independent Parliamentary Secretariat (Article 98; State Art 187):** Each House maintains a separate secretarial staff—headed by a **Secretary-General** (a permanent civil servant appointed by the Presiding Officer)—whose recruitment and service conditions are insulated from the Union Executive to guarantee administrative independence.

## Parliamentary Accountability Devices & Delegated Legislation

Parliament extracts day-to-day ministerial accountability through structured floor devices rooted in the **Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business** (framed under **Article 118 / State Art 208**), alongside informal Indian conventions:

- **Question Hour (11:00 AM–12:00 PM):** The opening hour of every sitting, weaponized to expose administrative lapses. **Starred Questions** require an **oral answer** and permit **supplementary questions**; **Unstarred Questions** receive a **written answer** with **zero supplementaries**; **Short Notice Questions** address urgent public matters with fewer than 10 days’ notice (oral answer + supplementaries). Questions may also be addressed to Private Members regarding Bills/resolutions under their charge.
- **Zero Hour:** An entirely **Indian parliamentary innovation (since 1962)**—mentioned **neither in the Constitution nor in the Rules of Procedure**. Beginning immediately after Question Hour (noon), it enables MPs to raise sudden matters of urgent public importance **without prior formal notice**.
- **Confidence, Censure & Urgent Floor Motions:**
  - **No-Confidence Motion (LS Rule 198):** Exclusive to the **Lok Sabha** (operationalizing **Art 75(3)**); requires support of **50 MPs** for admission; **need NOT state any reasons**; moved strictly against the **entire Council of Ministers**; passage forces immediate Cabinet resignation. *(Contrast **Confidence Motion**, moved by a minority/coalition government at the President’s or House’s direction.)*
  - **Censure Motion:** Exclusive to the **Lok Sabha**; **must state specific reasons**; may target an **individual minister, a group of ministers, or the entire Council**; passage reprimands policy failure without automatically forcing resignation (though politically necessitating an early confidence test).
  - **Adjournment Motion:** Exclusive to the **Lok Sabha** (barred in Rajya Sabha because it carries an **element of censure**); requires **50 MPs’ support**; interrupts normal business for at least **2 hours 30 minutes** to debate a **definite, factual, urgent, recent matter** of public importance (cannot raise sub-judice issues, privilege questions, or matters already discussed in the same session).
  - **Calling Attention Motion:** Another **Indian innovation (since 1954)**, formally codified in the **Rules of Procedure** and available in **both Houses**. Draws a minister’s attention to an urgent public matter requesting an authoritative statement—**no voting and no element of censure**.
  - **Other Devices:** **Short Duration Discussion** (LS Rule 193 / RS Rule 176 — max 2 hours, no formal motion or vote); **Half-an-Hour Discussion** (clarifying factual points from recent Question Hour answers); **Special Mention (RS) / Rule 377 (LS)** (matters outside normal procedural categories); **Point of Order** (extraordinary intervention by an Opposition or ruling MP halting proceedings to enforce Rules/Constitution—zero debate); and **Closure Motions** (Simple, Closure by Compartments, Kangaroo Closure, and Guillotine Closure).

### Delegated (Subordinate) Legislation & the Executive Scrutiny Gap

Modern technical governance renders it impossible for Parliament to draft every granular regulatory detail. Consequently, Parliament increasingly passes **"skeleton" parent Acts**—conferring power upon the Executive (Ministries and statutory regulators) to issue **rules, regulations, notifications, and by-laws** carrying the force of law (**Delegated / Subordinate Legislation**).

In the foundational landmark ***In Re: The Delhi Laws Act, 1912* (1951, 7-Judge Bench)**, the Supreme Court drew the constitutional red line: Parliament **cannot abdicate its "essential legislative functions"**—namely, declaring core legislative policy and laying down intelligible standards. Any blanket delegation lacking guiding principles is void for **excessive delegation**.

Yet operational oversight remains alarmingly weak. Most Indian statutes employ **"negative laying"**—subordinate rules take immediate legal effect upon Gazette notification and survive unless both Houses affirmatively vote to annul or modify them within 30 sitting days (a rare occurrence under party-whip dominance). Meanwhile, the **Committee on Subordinate Legislation** (15 members in each House) lacks technical staffing and reviews rules strictly *ex-post*, often years after enforcement—leaving consequential regulatory regimes (such as IT intermediary rules, DPDP rules, and GST notifications) virtually immune from democratic floor scrutiny.

## Parliamentary Privileges & the 2024 *Sita Soren* Constitutional Reset (Articles 105 & 194)

**Parliamentary Privileges** are special institutional rights, immunities, and exemptions conferred upon each House, its committees, and its members—and upon non-members constitutionally entitled to speak and participate in proceedings, notably the **Attorney General (Art 88)** and Union Ministers (**NOT the President**, despite the President being a constituent part of Parliament). Their sole constitutional purpose is functional: safeguarding fearless legislative deliberation against executive coercion or vexatious litigation.

### Codified Text vs. Uncodified Privileges & the 44th CAA Shift

- **Two Expressly Enumerated Privileges:** (1) **Freedom of speech in Parliament** (**Art 105(1) / State Art 194(1)**), subject only to constitutional provisions (such as **Art 121/211**, barring discussion of the judicial conduct of SC/HC Judges except upon a removal motion) and House Rules; and (2) **Immunity from court proceedings** regarding anything said or any vote given in Parliament or any committee thereof, as well as authorized publication of parliamentary reports, papers, votes, or proceedings (**Art 105(2) / Art 194(2)**).
- **Uncodified Residue (Article 105(3)):** Originally defined residual privileges as those enjoyed by the **British House of Commons** on January 26, 1950, until defined by parliamentary law. The **44th CAA (1978)** deleted the explicit textual mention of *"House of Commons"* while retaining its frozen substantive content (*"those of that House... immediately before the coming into force of Section 15 of the 44th Amendment Act"*). Parliament has **never enacted a comprehensive law codifying privileges**—primarily because a codified statute would become **"law" under Article 13**, immediately exposing parliamentary privileges to direct Fundamental Rights scrutiny!
- **Individual vs. Collective Privileges:**
  - **Individual:** Absolute freedom of speech inside the House (**Art 105(1)–(2)**); **freedom from arrest in CIVIL cases ONLY** during a parliamentary session and **40 days before and 40 days after** a session (**ZERO immunity in criminal offences or preventive detention!**); and exemption from jury service or appearing as a court witness while the House sits.
  - **Collective:** Exclusive right to publish reports/debates and prohibit unauthorized publication *(qualified by **Article 361A**, inserted by the **44th CAA**, shielding press media from civil/criminal liability for publishing substantially true reports of parliamentary proceedings unless actuated by malice)*; right to exclude strangers and hold secret sittings; autonomy to regulate internal proceedings free from judicial inquiry into mere procedural irregularity (**Art 122**); right to receive immediate intimation of the arrest, detention, conviction, or release of any member; and power to punish members or outsiders for **breach of privilege or contempt of the House** (via reprimand, admonition, imprisonment, suspension, or expulsion).

### Chronological Jurisprudence — From *Searchlight* (1959) to *Sita Soren* (2024)

Because uncodified privileges periodically collide with Fundamental Rights, judicial independence, and anti-corruption statutes, the Supreme Court has progressively narrowed immunity to its democratic core:

1. ***M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha* (*Searchlight I*, 1959, 5-Judge Bench):** Held via harmonious construction that uncodified legislative privileges under **Article 194(3)** prevail over a citizen’s free-speech right under **Article 19(1)(a)** (upholding a State Assembly’s power to prohibit publication of expunged proceedings), while remaining subject to **Article 21** (life and personal liberty).
2. ***In Re: Powers, Privileges and Immunities of State Legislatures* (*Keshav Singh*, 1964, 7-Judge Advisory Opinion):** Sparked when the UP Assembly ordered the arrest of two Allahabad High Court Judges who had granted bail to citizen Keshav Singh (imprisoned by the House for contempt). The 7-Judge Bench decisively curbed legislative absolutism: Indian legislatures are **not sovereign like the British Parliament**; House contempt committals are **subject to Article 226/32 judicial review** for violating **Article 21**; and a House can **never** summon or punish High Court Judges for discharging judicial duties (upholding **Article 211**).
3. ***Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha* (2007, 5-Judge Bench — "Cash-for-Query" Case):** Upheld Parliament’s power under Article 105(3) to **expel sitting MPs** caught accepting money to table questions—distinguishing self-cleansing *expulsion* from statutory *disqualification* under Article 102—while simultaneously confirming **limited judicial review** of privilege resolutions for *mala fides*, gross illegality, or violation of constitutional mandates.
4. ***State of Kerala v. K. Ajith* (2021):** Stripped MLAs of Article 194(2) immunity after they hurled microphones and destroyed Assembly furniture during the 2015 Kerala Budget speech—ruling that **vandalism and destruction of public property are criminal offences**, never protected legislative protest or speech.
5. ***Sita Soren v. Union of India* (March 4, 2024, 7-Judge Constitution Bench — Unanimous Landmark Reset):** Delivering a decisive blow against political corruption, Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud’s unanimous 7-Judge Bench **expressly overruled** the 3:2 majority verdict in ***P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (CBI/SPE)*** (*JMM Bribery Case*, 1998)—which had perversely shielded MPs who took bribes and *actually voted* as promised while prosecuting an MP who took a bribe and *abstained*! Four binding pillars define active 2026 law:
   - **Zero Immunity for Bribery:** Legislators enjoy **NO immunity under Articles 105(2) or 194(2)** from criminal prosecution for accepting or agreeing to accept bribes in connection with a vote or speech.
   - **The Twofold Necessity Test:** Any claim of privilege must be strictly (i) **tethered to the collective functioning of the House**, and (ii) **necessary to the discharge of essential duties** of a legislator. Bribery fails both tests—it corrodes probity and destroys deliberative democracy.
   - **Offence Complete Upon Acceptance:** Under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, the crime of bribery is **complete the moment the legislator agrees to accept or accepts an illegal gratification**—wholly independent of whether they subsequently deliver the promised vote or speech inside the House.
   - **Applicability to Rajya Sabha / Indirect Elections & Parallel Jurisdiction:** Bribery in **Rajya Sabha elections** (the exact factual matrix of Sita Soren’s 2012 Jharkhand prosecution) and Presidential/Vice-Presidential elections is equally unshielded, and criminal prosecution by courts operates **independently** of—and is not barred by—internal House privilege action.

## Parliamentary Committees & the Declining Scrutiny Crisis

Parliamentary Committees—the *"mini-Parliaments"* conducting bipartisan, camera-free technical scrutiny—are **mentioned in the Constitution** (Articles 105(2), 118, 122), yet their exact composition, tenure, and mandate are governed entirely by the **Rules of Procedure** of each House. They divide into **Standing Committees** (permanent, reconstituted annually) and **Ad hoc Committees** (temporary—such as Inquiry Committees, Select Committees, and Joint Parliamentary Committees on Bills).

- **The Three Financial Committees (Zero Ministerial Membership):**
  1. **Public Accounts Committee (PAC):** Established **1921** (under the GoI Act, 1919); **22 members** (**15 LS + 7 RS**) elected annually via **PR-STV**; **1-year term**; **no Minister** can join. By convention since **1967**, the Chairperson (appointed by the LS Speaker) is invariably from the **Opposition**. Examines CAG audit reports (Appropriation Accounts, Finance Accounts, and PSU accounts) for legality, economy, and propriety—with the **CAG acting as "friend, philosopher, and guide."**
  2. **Estimates Committee:** Originated **1921** and reconstituted **1950** (on Finance Minister John Mathai’s recommendation); **30 members — ALL 30 exclusively from Lok Sabha (ZERO Rajya Sabha members!)**, elected annually via **PR-STV**; **1-year term**; **no Minister**; Chairperson invariably from the **Ruling Party**. India’s largest parliamentary committee, dubbed the **"Continuous Economy Committee"**—examines budget estimates and suggests economies, organizational improvements, and alternative policies (*strictly post-voting* and without questioning Parliament-approved policy).
  3. **Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU):** Created **1964** on the **Krishna Menon Committee’s** recommendation; **22 members** (**15 LS + 7 RS**) elected annually via **PR-STV**; **1-year term**; **no Minister**; Chairperson appointed by the LS Speaker **exclusively from among Lok Sabha members** (Rajya Sabha members cannot chair COPU). Examines the reports, accounts, and commercial efficiency of Central PSUs alongside CAG’s PSU audit reports.
- **24 Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs):** Introduced with 17 committees in **1993** and expanded to **24 in 2004** (**16 under the LS Speaker**, **8 under the RS Chairman**). Each DRSC comprises **31 members** (**21 LS + 10 RS**) **nominated** (not elected!) by the respective Presiding Officers for a **1-year term**, excluding Ministers. DRSCs scrutinize Ministry Demands for Grants (during the Budget recess), examine Bills referred to them, review annual reports, and evaluate long-term policy documents; their recommendations remain **advisory/non-binding**.
- **Committee on the Welfare of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Tested Prelims 2026):** A permanent Joint Standing Committee comprising **30 members** (**20 elected by Lok Sabha + 10 elected by Rajya Sabha** via **PR-STV**), serving a **1-year term**. Its **Chairperson is appointed by the Lok Sabha Speaker** from amongst its members (not the RS Chairman), and **no Minister** can be a member (with **no exception** for the Minister of Social Justice and Empowerment). It examines reports of the NCSC and NCST and monitors the implementation of constitutional and statutory reservation safeguards across Union ministries, PSUs, and UTs.
- **Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs) & the Declining Bill-Referral Crisis:** Ad hoc JPCs investigate major governance controversies or examine landmark legislation (e.g., the JPC on the **Waqf (Amendment) Bill, 2024–25** and the ongoing JPC examining the **Constitution (129th Amendment) / One Nation, One Election Bill, 2024–26**). However, PRS Legislative Research data exposes a sharp structural erosion in legislative scrutiny: the share of Bills referred to Parliamentary Committees plunged from **60% in the 14th Lok Sabha (2004–09)** and **71% in the 15th Lok Sabha (2009–14)** down to **27% in the 16th Lok Sabha (2014–19)** and just **16% in the 17th Lok Sabha (2019–24)**. Although the coalition-era **18th Lok Sabha (2024–26)** witnessed a modest recovery (referring contentious bills like the Waqf Bill, ONOE Bill, and Income-Tax Bill 2025 to JPC/Select Committees), structural flaws persist: **optional (non-mandatory) bill referrals**, **non-binding reports**, **short 1-year tenures** obstructing specialized expertise, chronically low member attendance, and acute scarcity of independent technical research staff.

## The Anti-Defection Law — Tenth Schedule Mechanics & 2023–2025 Jurisprudence

Enacted via the **52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985** (amending **Articles 101, 102, 190, 191** and inserting the **Tenth Schedule**) and tightened by the **91st CAA, 2003**, India’s Anti-Defection Law aimed to eradicate the epidemic of *"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram"* floor-crossing that destabilized elected governments across the 1960s–1980s.

### Grounds of Disqualification, Exemptions & Adjudicatory Authority

Under **Paragraph 2** of the Tenth Schedule, a legislator incurs disqualification across **four distinct membership categories**:

1. **Elected Party Member — Voluntary Resignation / Conduct (Para 2(1)(a)):** Disqualified if they **voluntarily give up membership** of the political party on whose ticket they were elected. As settled in *Ravi S. Naik* (1994), this sweeps far wider than submitting a formal resignation letter and may be inferred directly from external anti-party conduct.
2. **Elected Party Member — Whip Defiance (Para 2(1)(b)):** Disqualified if they **vote or abstain from voting** in the House **contrary to any direction (whip)** issued by their political party without prior permission, **and** such act is **not condoned by the party within 15 days**. *(Note: Because whips apply to all parliamentary votes—not just confidence votes—this clause curtails independent legislative deliberation.)*
3. **Independent Member (Para 2(2)):** Disqualified immediately if they **join any political party** at any time after election.
4. **Nominated Member (Para 2(3)):** Disqualified if they join any political party **AFTER the expiry of six months** from the date they take their seat in the House. *(Crucial Prelims distinction: A nominated member **MAY** lawfully join a political party **within the first six months** without incurring disqualification!)*

**Exemptions & the 91st CAA Overhaul:**

- **Repeal of One-Third Split (Paragraph 3 — DELETED by 91st CAA, 2003):** Originally immunized a *"split"* by one-third of a legislature party; Parliament scrapped Paragraph 3 because it encouraged engineered factional breakups.
- **Two-Thirds Merger Exemption (Paragraph 4 — ACTIVE):** No disqualification applies if a political party merges with another party **and not less than two-thirds of the members of the legislature party** agree to the merger (protecting both those who merge and any dissenting minority forming a separate group). In practice, this has incentivized **wholesale defections**—poaching two-thirds of smaller opposition legislature parties rather than curbing defection.
- **Presiding Officer Exemption (Paragraph 5):** Exempts a Speaker, Deputy Speaker, or Deputy Chairman who resigns from their party upon election to preserve chair neutrality and rejoins after demitting office.

**Adjudicatory Authority (Paragraph 6) — Critical Prelims Split:** Disqualifications under **Articles 102(1) / 191(1)** (Office of Profit, unsound mind, insolvency, foreign citizenship, RPA conviction) are decided by the **President or Governor** acting on the **binding opinion of the Election Commission of India** (**Articles 103 & 192**). By stark contrast, **Tenth Schedule disqualifications (Articles 102(2) / 191(2))** are adjudicated **exclusively by the Presiding Officer** (Speaker or Chairman)—**NOT** the President, Governor, or ECI!

| ***Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur*** (Jan 2020, 3-Judge Bench) | Unlimited delay by partisan Speakers refusing to decide disqualification petitions | Absent exceptional circumstances, a Speaker must decide Tenth Schedule petitions within a **reasonable outer period of 3 months**. Strongly **urged Parliament to amend the Constitution** to replace the partisan Speaker with an **independent permanent tribunal** headed by a retired SC Judge or retired HC Chief Justice |
| ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra*** (May 11, 2023, 5-Judge Constitution Bench) | Political Party vs. Legislature Party Whip; Speaker’s role amid party splits; *Nabam Rebia* | **Sharply separated Political Party from Legislature Party:** ONLY the **Political Party** (organizational wing)—**NOT the Legislature Party** (MLAs)—can appoint the **Whip** and **Leader of the House**; a Speaker recognizing a breakaway MLA faction’s whip acts **contrary to law**. Distinguished ECI’s prospective symbol adjudication (Para 15) from Speaker’s retrospective Tenth Schedule inquiry. **Referred *Nabam Rebia* (2016) to a 7-Judge Bench** on whether a pending removal notice disables a Speaker from deciding defection petitions (**pending Nov 2026**) |
| ***Padi Kaushik Reddy v. State of Telangana*** (`2025 INSC 912`, July 31, 2025, 2-Judge Bench — CJI B.R. Gavai & J. A.G. Masih) | Telangana Speaker stalling disqualification of 10 BRS-to-Congress defecting MLAs for >1 year | Enforced a binding **3-month judicial mandate** directing the Speaker to decide pending petitions (invoking *Keisham Meghachandra*). Expressly held that when a Speaker acts as an adjudicating tribunal under **Paragraph 6 of the Tenth Schedule**, those proceedings are quasi-judicial—not *"proceedings in the Legislature"*—so the Speaker enjoys **NO constitutional procedural immunity under Articles 122 or 212**, castigated partisan inaction as *"making a mockery of the Tenth Schedule,"* and warned Parliament to reform the adjudicatory mechanism |

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Article 96 (LS Speaker) vs. Article 92 (RS Chairman) Voting Inversion:** When a removal resolution is under consideration against the **Lok Sabha Speaker (Art 96)**, they cannot preside, may speak, and **CAN vote in the first instance** (no casting vote). When a removal resolution is under consideration against the **Rajya Sabha Chairman / Vice-President (Art 92)**, they cannot preside, may speak, and **CANNOT vote even in the first instance** (because they are not a member of the RS!).
> - **Deputy Speaker & LoP Traps:** The **Deputy Speaker’s election date** is fixed by the **Speaker** (NOT the President—President fixes the Speaker’s election date!). The Deputy Speaker is **NOT subordinate to the Speaker**. Neither the **Leader of the House** nor the **Leader of Opposition (LoP)** is mentioned in the Constitution; the **10% LoP seat rule** is a Speaker’s convention (**Mavalankar Rule / Direction 121**), *not* codified in the **1977 LoP Act**.
> - **Devices & Innovations Traps:** **Zero Hour** is an Indian innovation (1962) found **neither in the Constitution nor in the Rules of Procedure**; **Calling Attention Motion** (1954) is an Indian innovation **explicitly codified in the Rules of Procedure** and available in **both Houses**. **No-Confidence Motion, Censure Motion, and Adjournment Motion** can be moved **ONLY in the Lok Sabha** (never Rajya Sabha!). A No-Confidence Motion needs **zero stated reasons**; a Censure Motion **must state specific reasons**.
> - **Parliamentary Privilege Traps (Post-*Sita Soren*, March 2024):** Privileges extend to the **Attorney General** and Ministers, **NOT to the President**. Freedom from arrest (40 days before/after session) applies **strictly to CIVIL cases**—NEVER to criminal offences or preventive detention! Under ***Sita Soren* (2024, 7-Judge, overruling *JMM Bribery* 1998)**, bribery is **NEVER protected** under Art 105(2)/194(2) (whether or not the MP actually votes, and including in Rajya Sabha elections). The **44th CAA** deleted the textual mention of "House of Commons" in Art 105(3) and added **Art 361A** protecting substantially true press reporting of House proceedings.
> - **Parliamentary Committees Traps:** **Estimates Committee (30 members)** consists **exclusively of Lok Sabha MPs (ZERO RS members)** and is chaired by a **Ruling Party** member. **PAC (15+7=22)** and **COPU (15+7=22)** include RS members, yet **COPU’s Chair must be strictly from Lok Sabha** and **PAC’s Chair is from the Opposition** (convention since 1967). The **Committee on the Welfare of SCs and STs** (*Prelims 2026*) comprises **30 members (20 LS + 10 RS, elected via PR-STV)** for a **1-year term**, with its Chairperson appointed by the **LS Speaker** (not RS Chairman). **NO Minister** can be a member of PAC, Estimates, COPU, the SC/ST Welfare Committee (no exception for the Social Justice Minister), or any of the **24 DRSCs**. Financial Committees and the SC/ST Welfare Committee are **elected via PR-STV**; **24 DRSCs (21 LS + 10 RS = 31 each)** are **nominated** by Speaker/Chairman.
> - **Tenth Schedule Traps:** Tenth Schedule disqualifications (**Art 102(2)/191(2)**) are decided by the **Speaker/Chairman** (NOT President/Governor/ECI!). Under ***Padi Kaushik Reddy* (`2025 INSC 912`, July 31, 2025)**, a Speaker acting as a Paragraph 6 Tribunal enjoys **no procedural immunity under Articles 122 or 212**. A **Nominated Member** CAN join a political party **within 6 months** of taking their seat without disqualification (disqualified only *after* 6 months)—whereas an **Independent Member** is disqualified immediately upon joining any party. The **1/3rd Split** exemption (Para 3) was **deleted by the 91st CAA (2003)**; only **2/3rd Merger** (Para 4) survives. Under ***Subhash Desai* (2023)**, ONLY the **Political Party** (NOT the Legislature Party) appoints the Whip.

> [!info] Mains Arsenal
> - **Three Structural Pathologies of Modern Indian Parliament (GS2 Core):**
>   1. **The Partisan Referee Dilemma:** Concentrating Money Bill certification (**Art 110(3)**), agenda control, Opposition suspensions, and Tenth Schedule adjudication (**Para 6**) in a Speaker who retains party membership creates a structural conflict of interest—evidenced by the seven-year **Article 93 Deputy Speaker vacuum**, the March 2026 Speaker removal clash, and chronic Tenth Schedule paralysis (*Padi Kaushik Reddy*, July 2025).
>   2. **The Committee & Delegated Legislation Deficit:** With Bill referrals dropping from **71% (15th LS)** to **16% (17th LS)**, routine guillotining of Demands for Grants, and unscrutinized executive rulemaking via **"negative laying"** of subordinate legislation, legislative deliberation is progressively hollowing out.
>   3. **The Anti-Defection Paradox:** Designed to preserve stability, the Tenth Schedule has morphed into an instrument of **elite party autocracy** (suppressing MP conscience via universal whips on non-confidence votes) while failing to stop **wholesale defections** (2/3rd mergers under Para 4 and mass resignations triggering bye-elections).
> - **Precision Committee & Reform Toolkit (Way Forward):**
>   - **Presiding Officer Neutrality:** Adopt the **UK convention** (mandatory resignation from party upon election via Para 5 + uncontested re-election or guaranteed post-retirement pension/RS nomination) and make **Article 93 Deputy Speaker** election time-bound (within the first session).
>   - **Anti-Defection Reform (Dinesh Goswami Committee, 170th Law Commission, *Keisham Meghachandra* 2020 & *Padi Kaushik Reddy* 2025):** (i) Transfer Tenth Schedule adjudication from the Speaker to an **independent permanent tribunal** chaired by a retired SC Judge/HC Chief Justice (or President/Governor on binding ECI advice); (ii) **Restrict whip-based disqualification** solely to **No-Confidence Motions, Confidence Votes, and Money Bills** to restore intra-party policy debate; (iii) Plug the **Paragraph 4 merger loophole** by requiring verifiable *organizational* merger (not mere legislative headcount) and barring resigning defectors from ministerial office until full term expiry.
>   - **Strengthening Scrutiny:** Mandate automatic referral of all non-emergency Bills to **DRSCs** (with structured public consultation), extend DRSC tenures from **1 year to 2–3 years** for domain specialization, fund dedicated parliamentary research fellowships, and shift critical delegated rules to **"affirmative laying."**

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature

- **Adjudicator Swap on Disqualifications:** UPSC routinely claims that Tenth Schedule defection disputes are decided by the **President/Governor on ECI advice** (False—exclusively **Speaker/Chairman** under Para 6; President/Governor + ECI decide *non-defection* disqualifications under Art 103/192) or that the **Speaker’s decision is final and immune from judicial review** (False—Para 7 struck down in *Kihoto Hollohan*, 1992).
- **Nominated vs. Independent Member & Split vs. Merger Inversions:** Expect statements claiming *"a nominated member is disqualified immediately if they join a political party"* (False—allowed within **6 months**), *"an independent member has 6 months to join a party"* (False—barred immediately), or *"a 1/3rd split is still protected under the Tenth Schedule"* (False—repealed by 91st CAA, 2003).
- **Committee Composition & Election Traps:** Watch for claims that the **Estimates Committee** includes Rajya Sabha MPs (False—all 30 from LS), that **Ministers** can sit on PAC/COPU/DRSCs or the SC/ST Welfare Committee (False—strictly barred), that **DRSCs** are *elected* via PR-STV (False—*nominated* by Speaker/Chairman), or that **COPU** can be chaired by a Rajya Sabha MP (False—LS member only).
- **Privilege Scope & Arrest Immunity Traps:** Classic traps assert that parliamentary privilege shields MPs from arrest in **criminal cases** during sessions (False—**civil cases only**), that the **President** enjoys Article 105 privileges (False—only MPs, AG, and Ministers), or that **taking a bribe to vote** in the House remains immunized post-1998 (False—overruled unanimously in ***Sita Soren*, March 2024**).

### 3. Cross-Hub AIR-1 Provocations

1. **Tenth Schedule vs. Representative Democracy (GS2 Polity × GS4 Ethics):** By binding every MP and MLA to party whips on ordinary legislation—while failing to deter orchestrated two-thirds mergers and mass resignations—has India’s Anti-Defection Law inverted Edmund Burke’s trusteeship theory of representation into **party-high-command feudalism**? Evaluate whether removing the Speaker as adjudicator (*Keisham Meghachandra* / *Padi Kaushik Reddy*) is sufficient without simultaneously dismantling whip tyranny on non-confidence votes.
2. **Constitutional Silences & the Limits of Judicial Cure (GS2 Institutional Architecture):** Compare the Supreme Court’s divergent responses to two parallel constitutional silences—enforcing a hard **three-month deadline** on Speakers acting as Tenth Schedule tribunals (*Keisham Meghachandra* 2020 & *Padi Kaushik Reddy* July 2025) while **refusing fixed timelines** on Governors acting under Article 200 (**16th Presidential Reference, Nov 2025**). What doctrinal distinction (tribunal adjudication vs. sovereign executive assent) explains this divergence, and where does judicial intervention risk collapsing Separation of Powers?
