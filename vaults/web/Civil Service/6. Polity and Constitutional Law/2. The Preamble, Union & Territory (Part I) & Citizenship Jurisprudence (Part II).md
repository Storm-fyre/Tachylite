# Chapter 2: The Preamble, Union & Territory (Part I) & Citizenship Jurisprudence (Part II)

Before assigning a single right or institutional power, a constitution must answer three existential questions: *Why* does the polity exist (the **Preamble**), *where* do its sovereign borders run (**Part I**), and *who* belongs to the political community (**Part II**). Across 2024–2026, all three foundational planks returned to the centre of Indian constitutional adjudication—through Supreme Court rulings on the 42nd Amendment’s Preamble insertions, Assam’s Section 6A citizenship cut-off, pending challenges to the CAA 2019, and Parliament’s wholesale replacement of colonial foreigners statutes in 2025–2026.

---

## 1. The Preamble - Sovereign Philosophical Matrix & Legal Status

Drafted on the spine of Jawaharlal Nehru’s **Objectives Resolution** (adopted January 22, 1947), the Preamble was enacted by the Constituent Assembly **after** the rest of the Constitution had been settled, ensuring exact textual symmetry between philosophy and operative clauses. N.A. Palkhivala styled it the **"identity card of the Constitution,"** while K.M. Munshi called it the **"horoscope of our sovereign democratic republic."**

### Deconstructing the Four Structural Pillars

1. **Source of Authority ("We, the People of India"):** Anchors popular sovereignty directly in the Indian citizenry—breaking cleanly from colonial grants of Westminster.
2. **Nature of the Indian State (Exact Constitutional Sequence):**
   - **Sovereign:** Independent external diplomacy and supreme internal authority; India’s voluntary **Commonwealth membership (May 1949)** or UN obligations do not impair sovereignty.
   - **Socialist *(42nd CAA, 1976)*:** Denotes **democratic socialism** (a mixed-economy welfare state aimed at ending poverty, ignorance, and inequality of opportunity), *not* state-monopoly communism. Even post-1991 liberalization, the Court has read socialism through social-welfare equity (*D.S. Nakara*, 1983; *Dr. Balram Singh*, 2024).
   - **Secular *(42nd CAA, 1976)*:** Embodies **positive secularism** (*Sarva Dharma Sambhava* / Rajeev Bhargava’s **"principled distance"**)—no State religion, equal respect for all faiths, and calibrated state intervention to eradicate oppressive religious practices (contrast with French negative *laïcité* or America’s rigid wall of separation).
   - **Democratic:** Representative parliamentary democracy rooted in universal adult franchise, periodic elections, and rule of law—encompassing political, social, and economic democracy (Ambedkar’s *trinity*).
   - **Republic:** An **elected Head of State** (President, indirectly elected for a fixed term) coupled with the absence of any privileged hereditary class and universal public office access (unlike the UK’s constitutional monarchy).
3. **Objectives of the Republic (Exact Order & Scope):**
   - **Justice:** **Social, Economic, and Political** *(drawn from the 1917 Russian Revolution)*—operationalized through Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.
   - **Liberty:** **Thought, Expression, Belief, Faith, and Worship** *(paired with Equality and Fraternity from the 1789 French Revolution)*—qualified by reasonable restrictions, not absolute license.
   - **Equality:** **Status and Opportunity**—spanning civic equality (Arts 14–18), political equality (Arts 325–326), and economic parity (Art 39).
   - **Fraternity:** Assuring the **dignity of the individual** and the **unity and integrity *(42nd CAA)* of the Nation**—combating both social hierarchy (*dignity*) and territorial/psychological fragmentation (*unity and integrity*).
4. **Date of Adoption:** **November 26, 1949**—fixed inside the enacting clause itself (distinct from the **January 26, 1950** commencement date in Art 394).

### The 42nd Amendment (1976) & *Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India* (Nov 2024)

The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** amended the Preamble once—and only once—inserting three words: **Socialist**, **Secular**, and **Integrity** (transforming *"Sovereign Democratic Republic"* into *"Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic"* and *"unity of the Nation"* into *"unity and integrity of the Nation"*).

Nearly five decades later, writ petitions (*Dr. Balram Singh* and *Subramanian Swamy*) challenged these insertions on three grounds: (i) procedural illegitimacy of an extended Emergency-era Lok Sabha, (ii) logical impossibility of retroactively altering a text bearing the historical date November 26, 1949, and (iii) ideological fettering of elected governments’ economic and religious policies.

On **November 25, 2024** (`2024 INSC 893`), a Bench of **CJI Sanjiv Khanna and Justice Sanjay Kumar** summarily dismissed the challenge, settling four doctrines:
- **Plenary Scope of Article 368:** Parliament’s constituent amending power under Article 368 unquestionably extends to the Preamble; the historical adoption date of November 26, 1949 does not freeze the Preamble against subsequent prospective amendments.
- **Democratic Ratification by Acquiescence:** The Janata government’s **44th CAA (1978)** dismantled authoritarian provisions of the 42nd Amendment yet consciously **retained** *Socialist*, *Secular*, and *Integrity*—reflecting enduring cross-party consensus.
- **Indianized Meaning of "Socialist":** In India’s constitutional lexicon, *"socialist"* denotes a **welfare state committed to equality of opportunity**, imposing zero prohibition on private entrepreneurship, market reforms, or disinvestment.
- **Implicit Secular Ethos:** Even before 1976, the Preamble’s original text (equality of status/opportunity, liberty of belief/faith/worship, fraternity) alongside Arts 14–16 and 25–28 embedded secularism into the **Basic Structure** (*Kesavananda* 1973; *S.R. Bommai* 1994).

### Judicial Trajectory: Legal Status & Amendability

- ***In Re: Berubari Union* (1960):** The Supreme Court praised the Preamble as *"a key to the minds of the makers"* useful in resolving textual ambiguity, yet erroneously held that it is **NOT a part of the Constitution** (and therefore neither enforceable nor amendable).
- ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973, 13-Judge):** Expressly **overruled *Berubari*** on this point. The Court held that the Preamble **IS a part of the Constitution**, reflects its foundational vision, and **IS amendable under Article 368**—subject to the iron ceiling that **Basic Structure features embedded in the Preamble cannot be destroyed**.
- ***S.R. Bommai* (1994) & *LIC of India v. Consumer Education & Research Centre* (1995):** Reaffirmed that the Preamble is an **integral part** of the Constitution and serves as a live interpretive compass.
- **Operational Legal Boundary (Prelims 2020 Gold Standard):** Despite being an integral part of the Constitution, the Preamble is **non-justiciable**—it is neither an independent source of legislative power nor an independent prohibition upon the legislature, and carries **no legal effect independently of other constitutional provisions**. A statute cannot be struck down solely for violating the Preamble without invoking an operative Article or Basic Structure principle.

## 2. Union and Its Territory (Part I - Articles 1 to 4)

### Article 1: "Union of States" vs. "Territory of India"

**Article 1(1)** declares: *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."* The dual nomenclature resolved a Constituent Assembly debate between traditionalists favouring *Bharat* and modernists favouring *India*.

More structurally decisive was Dr. Ambedkar’s choice of **"Union"** over **"Federation"** (November 4, 1948), grounded in two constitutional axioms:
1. **No Compact Origin:** Unlike the United States, the Indian Union was not forged by a treaty or agreement among pre-existing sovereign states.
2. **No Right to Secede:** States hold no legal exit option. India is therefore an **"indestructible Union of destructible States"**—whereas the United States is an *"indestructible Union of indestructible States"*.

**Atomic Distinction:** **"Union of India"** is narrower—it encompasses **only the 28 States** participating in the federal division of powers. **"Territory of India" (Article 1(3))** is wider, comprising three categories: (a) territories of the States, (b) the **8 Union Territories** specified in the First Schedule, and (c) **such other territories as may be acquired** in the future.

### Articles 2, 3 & 4: External Admission vs. Internal Redrawing

- **Article 2 (External Intake):** Empowers Parliament by law to **admit** into the Union, or **establish**, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. It operates strictly on territories **not previously part of the Union of India**—exemplified by **Sikkim**, first admitted as an *"Associate State"* via the **35th CAA (1974)** (Art 2A, 10th Schedule) and then integrated as India’s 22nd full State via the **36th CAA (1975)** (repealing Art 2A).
- **Article 3 (Internal Reorganisation):** Empowers Parliament by law to: (a) form a new State by separation, uniting two or more States/parts, or uniting territory to a State; (b) increase a State’s area; (c) diminish a State’s area; (d) alter boundaries; or (e) alter the name of any State.
  - **Two Mandatory Conditions (Proviso to Art 3):** (1) The Bill requires the **prior recommendation of the President** before introduction in either House; (2) Where the Bill affects the area, boundaries, or name of any **State**, the President must refer it to that **State Legislature** for expressing its views within a specified (or extended) timeframe *(timeframe power added by the **5th CAA, 1955**)*.
  - **Non-Binding Views (*Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay*, 1960):** The Supreme Court confirmed that Parliament is **not bound** to accept the State Legislature’s views, and need **not** make a fresh reference to the State Legislature every time substantive amendments are moved to the Bill during parliamentary passage (upholding the split of Bombay into Maharashtra and Gujarat).
  - **18th CAA (1966) — Explanations I & II:** Clarified that **"State" in clauses (a)–(e) includes a Union Territory**, *except* in the Proviso—meaning **no presidential reference to a UT Legislature is constitutionally required** when altering a UT. Also clarified that forming a new State/UT includes uniting parts of any State or UT.
  - **Unresolved Frontier — State-to-UT Downgrade (*In Re: Article 370*, Dec 2023):** While upholding the carving out of the UT of Ladakh under Article 3, the 5-Judge Constitution Bench **deliberately left open** the core federal question of whether Article 3 empowers Parliament to extinguish an entire State (Jammu & Kashmir) into a Union Territory, relying instead on the Solicitor General’s solemn assurance that statehood would be restored.
- **Article 4 (Ordinary Legislative Route):** Declares that laws enacted under Articles 2 or 3—including consequential amendments to the **First Schedule** (names/territories) and **Fourth Schedule** (Rajya Sabha seats)—are **NOT to be deemed amendments of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368**. They pass by **ordinary simple majority**.

### Cession of Territory vs. Settlement of Boundary Disputes

A sharp doctrinal wall separates internal boundary redrawing from external territorial adjustment:
- **Cession of Indian Territory (*In Re: Berubari Union*, 1960):** Parliament’s power under Article 3(c) to *"diminish the area of any State"* applies solely to internal restructuring; it does **NOT** authorize ceding Indian territory to a foreign nation. Sovereign cession requires a **Constitutional Amendment under Article 368** amending Article 1 / First Schedule—requiring the **9th CAA (1960)** for transferring Berubari Union to East Pakistan, and the **100th CAA (2015)** to operationalize the **India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement (1974 & 2011 Protocol)** (transferring 111 enclaves to Bangladesh, acquiring 51 enclaves for India, and settling adverse possessions and a 6.1 km un-demarcated border across Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya, and Tripura).
- **Settlement of a Boundary Dispute (*Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India*, 1969):** Implementing an international arbitral award (the 1968 **Rann of Kutch Tribunal** award) that merely demarcates a **disputed border** without ceding *de jure* Indian territory does **NOT** amount to cession—it requires **no Article 368 CAA** and can be executed by **Union executive action alone**.

### Post-1950 Territorial Integration Arc (Architectural Summary)

Resisting immediate linguistic division, the **Dhar Commission (1948)** and **JVP Committee (1948)** prioritized administrative convenience and national consolidation. Following Potti Sriramulu’s fast and the creation of **Andhra (1953)**, the **Fazl Ali States Reorganisation Commission (1953–55)** rejected a dogmatic *"one language, one state"* formula while proposing four balanced criteria: national unity/security, linguistic-cultural homogeneity, financial-administrative viability, and citizen welfare. The resulting **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** and **7th CAA (1956)** dismantled the asymmetric Part A/B/C/D classification, creating **14 States and 6 UTs**—evolving through subsequent reorganizations (1960–2020) into today’s **28 States and 8 Union Territories**.

## 3. Citizenship Architecture (Part II - Articles 5 to 11 & Citizenship Act, 1955)

Citizenship marks the constitutional boundary between **full members of the political community** and **aliens** (friendly or enemy). In India, **Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, and 30**, universal suffrage (**Art 326**), contesting Parliamentary/State elections, and eligibility for top constitutional offices (President, Vice-President, SC/HC Judges, Governor, Attorney General, Advocate General) belong exclusively to **citizens**. Conversely, unlike the U.S. *"natural-born citizen"* barrier, **both citizens by birth and naturalized citizens** are constitutionally eligible to become President of India.

### Part II (Articles 5–11): Commencement Rules & Plenary Parliamentary Mandate

Crucially, Part II does **not** establish a permanent, comprehensive citizenship code; it merely resolved who stood as a citizen on **January 26, 1950** amidst Partition’s mass displacement, delegating all future regulation to Parliament:

- **Article 5 (Domicile at Commencement):** Every person domiciled in India on January 26, 1950 who satisfied **any one** of three criteria: (a) born in India, (b) either parent born in India, or (c) ordinarily resident in India for **at least 5 years** immediately preceding commencement. *(India recognizes **one citizenship and one all-India domicile** under its single constitutional polity—validated in UPSC Prelims 2021—even though States impose statutory residence criteria for education or under Art 16(3)).*
- **Article 6 (Migrants from Pakistan to India):** Anchored on **July 19, 1948** (introduction of the inter-Dominion permit system). A person (or parent/grandparent born in undivided India under the GOI Act 1935) who migrated *before* July 19, 1948 became a citizen if ordinarily resident since migration; those migrating *on or after* July 19, 1948 required **registration** by an appointed officer after **6 months’ residence** in India prior to application.
- **Article 7 (Migrants from India to Pakistan):** Any person migrating to Pakistan **after March 1, 1947** ceased to be a citizen—*except* those who returned to India under a valid **permit for resettlement or permanent return**, treated on par with post-July 19, 1948 migrants under Art 6.
- **Article 8 (PIOs Residing Abroad):** Persons of Indian origin (self/parent/grandparent born in undivided India) ordinarily residing outside India could register as citizens through Indian diplomatic or consular representatives.
- **Article 9 (Single Citizenship Bar):** Automatically disqualifies anyone who **voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship** from claiming citizenship under Articles 5, 6, or 8. *(Post-1950 foreign acquisition is governed statutorily by Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955).*
- **Article 10 (Continuance):** Citizens under Part II continue as citizens subject to any law made by Parliament.
- **Article 11 (Plenary Parliamentary Power):** Declares that *"nothing in the foregoing provisions of this Part shall derogate from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship."* Paired with **List I Entry 17**, Article 11 grants Parliament sweeping authority—even to craft region-specific cut-offs deviating from Articles 6–7 (*In Re: Section 6A*, Oct 2024).

### The Citizenship Act, 1955: Structural Shift from *Jus Soli* to *Jus Sanguinis*

Exercising Article 11, Parliament enacted the **Citizenship Act, 1955** (amended in 1957, 1960, 1985, 1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, and 2019). Its deepest doctrinal arc is a steady **retreat from birthright territorial citizenship (*jus soli*) toward lineage-gated citizenship (*jus sanguinis*)**, driven by cross-border migration pressures in Eastern India:
- **1950–1987:** Pure ***jus soli***—birth on Indian soil conferred automatic citizenship regardless of parental nationality.
- **1986 Amendment (effective July 1, 1987):** Added a parentage filter—at least **one parent** had to be an Indian citizen at the time of birth.
- **2003 Amendment (Act 6 of 2004; assented Jan 7, 2004, notified effective Dec 3, 2004):** Introduced the statutory category **"illegal migrant"** (Section 2(1)(b): entering without valid travel documents or overstaying permitted time) and tightened birth citizenship further—requiring **both parents to be citizens, OR one citizen parent while the other is NOT an illegal migrant**.

## 4. Contemporary Citizenship, Refugee & Diaspora Jurisprudence (2024–2026)

### *In Re: Section 6A of the Citizenship Act* (Oct 17, 2024) — Assam Accord Validated

Inserted in 1985 to codify the **Assam Accord** following the six-year Assam Agitation, **Section 6A** carved an asymmetric citizenship cut-off for persons of Indian origin entering Assam from East Pakistan/Bangladesh: (i) **pre-January 1, 1966** entrants were deemed full citizens; (ii) **January 1, 1966 – March 24, 1971** entrants were required to be detected by Foreigners Tribunals, registered, removed from electoral rolls for **10 years**, and thereafter vested with full citizenship; and (iii) entrants **on or after March 25, 1971** were declared illegal migrants liable to deportation.

Petitioners (*Assam Sanmilita Mahasangha* and indigenous bodies) attacked Section 6A for violating Articles 6–7 (altering the national July 19, 1948 Partition cut-off via ordinary statute), Article 14 (arbitrarily singling out Assam), Article 29(1) (diluting indigenous Assamese cultural identity), and Article 355 (failing to protect Assam against *"external aggression"*).

On **October 17, 2024** (`2024 INSC 789`), a **5-Judge Constitution Bench** upheld Section 6A by a **4:1 majority** (Justice Surya Kant authoring the primary majority opinion for himself, Justices M.M. Sundresh, and Manoj Misra; CJI D.Y. Chandrachud concurring; Justice J.B. Pardiwala dissenting):
- **Parliament’s Plenary Art 11 Competence:** Articles 6–7 fixed baseline citizenship strictly at the Constitution’s commencement; **Article 11** explicitly preserves Parliament’s plenary power to prescribe tailored post-1950 citizenship regimes without amending Part II.
- **Reasonable Geographic Classification (Art 14):** Singling out Assam passed constitutional muster given its acute share of cross-border influx compared to larger border states and the historical watershed of **March 25, 1971** (when Pakistan launched *Operation Searchlight*, triggering the Bangladesh Liberation War).
- **Inclusive Reading of Article 29(1):** Rejecting demographic exclusivity, the Court ruled that **the mere presence of diverse ethnic or linguistic groups within a State does NOT violate the cultural conservation rights of local communities under Article 29(1)**—provided the local population retains freedom to conserve its own script, language, and culture.
- **Art 355 & Enforcement Directions:** While holding that legislation cannot be invalidated solely by invoking Article 355, the majority directed robust executive enforcement and adequate tribunal infrastructure to detect and deport **post-March 24, 1971** illegal migrants.
- **Justice Pardiwala’s Sole Dissent (Temporal Unreasonableness):** Held Section 6A unconstitutional **prospectively**, reasoning that the absence of any sunset clause in Section 6A(3) left detection and registration perpetually open-ended nearly forty years after 1985—turning a temporary humanitarian compromise into an indefinite legal absurdity.

### NRC, Foreigners Tribunals & Electoral Roll Citizenship Verification (2025–2026)

- **The *Sarbananda Sonowal* Doctrine (2005 & 2006):** In ***Sarbananda Sonowal I* (2005)**, the Supreme Court struck down the **Illegal Migrants (Determination by Tribunals) [IMDT] Act, 1983**—applicable exclusively to Assam—because it shifted the burden of proving foreign nationality onto the complainant/State, impeding detection and violating Articles 14 and 355 (equating large-scale illegal influx to *"external aggression"*). In ***Sonowal II* (2006)**, the Court struck down an executive attempt to re-tilt the burden of proof, firmly anchoring Assam proceedings under the **reverse burden of proof** (Section 9 of the Foreigners Act, 1946—carried forward under the 2025 statutory framework), requiring the *accused individual* to prove Indian citizenship.
- **Assam NRC Stalemate & Foreigners Tribunals (FTs):** Monitored by the Supreme Court (*Assam Public Works*), Assam’s updated **National Register of Citizens (August 31, 2019)** excluded ~19.06 lakh of 3.3 crore applicants. As of November 2026, the list remains **unnotified by the Registrar General of India (RGI)** amidst pending re-verification pleas, leaving excluded persons in protracted adjudication before quasi-judicial **Foreigners Tribunals**—widely scrutinized for high rates of *ex-parte* orders, minor spelling discrepancies in legacy documents, and weak appellate safeguards.
- **2026 Flashpoint — ECI’s Special Intensive Revision (SIR) & Voter Citizenship:** During 2025–2026 Special Intensive Revisions of electoral rolls across States (triggering *ADR*, WP(C) 640/2025 and tested directly in **UPSC Mains 2026 GS-II**), the constitutional line between **voter registration** and **citizenship determination** sharpened. While **Article 326** and Section 16 of the **RPA, 1950** restrict electoral rolls to citizens—empowering Electoral Registration Officers (EROs) to verify eligibility and exclude non-citizens during roll revision (referencing the 2003 Citizenship Act cut-offs)—adjudicating definitive citizenship status or ordering deportation remains strictly the statutory province of the **Central Government and Foreigners Tribunals**, *not* the Election Commission.

### Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (CAA) & 2024 Rules

Enacted in December 2019 and operationalized via the **Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024** (notified **March 11, 2024**), the CAA amends the 1955 Act through the **Proviso to Section 2(1)(b)**, **Section 6B**, and the **Third Schedule**:
- **Substantive Pathway:** Exempts **six non-Muslim religious communities**—**Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians**—who fled persecution from **Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan** and entered India **on or before December 31, 2014** from being classified as *"illegal migrants,"* and fast-tracks their **naturalisation** by reducing aggregate residency from **11 years to 5 years** (plus 12 months continuous stay immediately prior). Pending proceedings regarding illegal migration abate upon grant of citizenship.
- **Northeastern Statutory Carve-Outs (Section 6B(4)):** To mitigate indigenous demographic anxieties, Section 6B expressly does **NOT apply** to: (i) **Sixth Schedule tribal areas** of **Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Tripura**, and (ii) areas governed by the **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** under the Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation, 1873—covering **Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Nagaland, and Manipur** *(Manipur brought under ILP in Dec 2019)*.
- **Administrative Mechanics (2024 Rules):** Applications run digitally through MHA portals, verified by **District Level Committees** (headed by designated central officers such as Postal Superintendents) and decided by **Empowered Committees** (headed by State Directors of Census Operations)—structurally bypassing State governments and provoking cooperative-federalism friction.
- **Constitutional Challenges & Live Judicial Status (Nov 2026):** Over **240 writ petitions** led by ***Indian Union Muslim League v. Union of India*** assail the CAA as violating **Article 14** (under-inclusive religious and territorial classification excluding persecuted Muslim sects like Ahmadiyyas/Hazaras/Shias, Rohingyas from Myanmar, Sri Lankan Tamils, and Tibetans), **Secularism (Basic Structure)**, and the **1985 Assam Accord** (shifting Assam’s non-6th Schedule cut-off from 1971 to 2014). The Union defends the classification as grounded in the **historical legacy of Partition** and the state-religion constitutions of those three neighbours, emphasizing that the Act **strips no existing citizen** and leaves regular naturalisation open to Muslims. Having refused interim stays (2020; March 2024), a Bench led by **CJI Surya Kant** heard final arguments scheduled in **May 2026** (separating pan-India from Assam–Tripura petitions); **the final Supreme Court judgment remains awaited as of November 2026**.

### Overseas Citizen of India (OCI — Sections 7A–7D): Rights, Limits & 2025–2026 Tightening

Introduced in 2005 (and merged with the PIO card scheme in **January 2015**), the **OCI card** is a **statutory diaspora privilege**, *strictly not* dual citizenship (which Article 9 prohibits). OCI cardholders remain **foreign nationals** lacking Article 19 fundamental freedoms.

- **Eligibility (Sec 7A):** Foreign nationals who were Indian citizens (or eligible on Jan 26, 1950), their children/grandchildren/great-grandchildren, or foreign spouses of Indian citizens/OCIs (marriage subsisting **≥ 2 years**)—**strictly excluding** anyone who is or ever was a citizen of **Pakistan or Bangladesh** (or whose parents/grandparents/great-grandparents were).
- **Rights vs. Exclusions (Sec 7B & March 4, 2021 MHA Notification):** Grants a **lifelong multiple-entry, multi-purpose visa**, exemption from FRRO police reporting regardless of stay duration, and parity with NRIs in non-agricultural property acquisition, domestic airfares, national parks, inter-country adoption, and appearing in All-India entrance tests (NEET/JEE) *for NRI/supernumerary seats only*. Crucially, in ***Anushka Rengunthwar v. Union of India* (Feb 2023, `2023 INSC 99`)**, the Supreme Court invoked non-retrogression to hold that the March 2021 bar on OCI students competing for general citizen seats operates **prospectively only** (sparing OCIs registered before March 4, 2021).
  - **Hard Constitutional & Regulatory Exclusions:** No voting, no contesting elections, no constitutional offices (President, VP, SC/HC Judge, etc.), no Article 16 public employment, no purchase of agricultural/farmhouse/plantation land, and **prior special permission required** for missionary, *Tabligh*, mountaineering, journalistic, or research activities, or visiting Protected/Restricted Areas.
- **Cancellation Grounds & 2025–2026 Overhaul (Sec 7D):** CAA 2019 inserted **Section 7D(da)** enabling cancellation for violating any notified law, paired with a mandatory statutory right to a **reasonable opportunity of being heard** (Proviso to Sec 7D). Two major 2025–2026 developments reshaped the regime:
  1. **MHA Gazette Notification `S.O. 3662(E)` (August 11, 2025):** Exercising Section 7D(da), MHA tightened revocation norms—making OCI registration liable to cancellation if the cardholder is **sentenced to imprisonment for ≥ 2 years** (without the older 5-year post-registration cap under Sec 7D(d)) **OR is formally charge-sheeted for an offence carrying punishment of ≥ 7 years’ imprisonment** (conviction not required), regardless of whether the offence occurred in India or abroad (if recognized under Indian law).
  2. **Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2026 (April 2026):** Modernized diaspora administration by introducing paperless **e-OCI registration/cards** alongside physical booklets.

### India’s Statutory Framework for Refugees & Stateless Persons (2025–2026 Overhaul)

- **International Treaty Stance vs. Domestic Constitutional Shield:** India is **NOT a signatory** to the **1951 UN Refugee Convention**, its **1967 Protocol**, or the **1954/1961 Statelessness Conventions**—citing Eurocentric framing, porous South Asian demographics, and security imperatives. Nevertheless, **Article 21** protects every *"person"* (citizen or alien) against arbitrary deprivation of life and dignity (*NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh*, 1996—protecting Chakma refugees from forced eviction), while the Supreme Court’s interim order in ***Mohammad Salimullah* (2021)** clarified that the right not to be deported flows from Article 19(1)(e) (citizen-only) and deportation of undocumented Rohingyas must follow procedure established by law.
- **No Dedicated Domestic Asylum Law → *Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025*:** Lacking a unified refugee statute, India historically managed asylum seekers through executive discretion under colonial laws. On **September 1, 2025**, Parliament’s **Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025 (Act No. 13 of 2025)** came into force—**repealing four older enactments under Section 36**: (i) *Passport (Entry into India) Act, 1920*, (ii) *Registration of Foreigners Act, 1939*, (iii) *Foreigners Act, 1946*, and (iv) *Immigration (Carriers’ Liability) Act, 2000* *(crucially, it does **NOT** repeal the **Passports Act, 1967**)*. It establishes a statutory Bureau of Immigration, tightens carrier/institution reporting, empowers refusal of entry/stay on grounds of sovereignty, security, foreign relations, or public health, and renders the Immigration Officer’s admissibility decision final.
- **The 2025 & November 2026 Exemption Orders (High-Yield Cut-Off Distinction):** Exercising **Section 33** of the 2025 Act, MHA notified the **Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) Order, 2025 (Sept 2025)** and amended it via the **Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) (Amendment) Order, 2026 (November 4–5, 2026)**:
  - **2014 vs. 2024 Cut-Off Split:** Extends passport/visa *entry* exemptions to the same **six persecuted non-Muslim minorities** from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan who entered India on or before **December 31, 2024**. Critically, **December 31, 2024** governs *immigration/entry exemption* under the 2025 Act, whereas **December 31, 2014** remains the statutory cut-off for *fast-track citizenship under CAA 2019*.
  - **End of Blanket Stay Waiver (Nov 2026):** While preserving entry exemption without valid papers, the **November 2026 Amendment** makes holding an **"appropriate visa" mandatory for continued lawful stay**, converting blanket waivers into individually regulated permissions.
  - **Tailored Refugee Carve-Outs:** Reorganizes standing executive exemptions for **Tibetan refugees** (post-1959 Entry/Special Entry Permits) and registered **Sri Lankan Tamil refugees** who arrived up to **January 9, 2015**—illustrating that India’s refugee protection remains an **ad-hoc executive regime**, leaving stateless persons vulnerable to shifting gazette notifications.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Preamble Legal Effect Trap (Prelims 2020):** The Preamble **IS** an integral part of the Constitution (*Kesavananda*, *LIC*, *Dr. Balram Singh*), **CAN** be amended under Art 368 (without damaging Basic Structure), yet has **NO legal effect independently of other constitutional provisions**.
> - **Article 3 vs. Article 368 Territorial Traps:** (1) Internal creation/renaming of States needs only a **Simple Majority** (Art 4), NEVER Art 368; (2) **UT Legislatures** need **NOT** be consulted under the Art 3 Proviso (18th CAA Explanation I); (3) **Ceding Indian territory** abroad requires an **Art 368 CAA** (*Berubari* 1960; 9th & 100th CAAs), whereas **settling a disputed border** requires only **Executive Action** (*Maganbhai Patel* 1969).
> - **Citizenship Bare-Statute Traps (Prelims 2021 + 2025–26):** (1) India has **one citizenship and one domicile**; **naturalized citizens CAN become President of India**; (2) **Section 10 Deprivation** does **NOT** apply to citizens by **birth or descent**; (3) **Aadhaar** is proof of identity/residence, **NEVER** proof of citizenship or domicile (Prelims 2018); (4) **CAA 2019 cut-off = Dec 31, 2014** (citizenship), whereas **Immigration Exemption Order 2025/2026 cut-off = Dec 31, 2024** (entry/visa exemption).
> - **Immigration & Foreigners Act, 2025 Repeal Trap:** Section 36 repealed the **1920, 1939, 1946, and 2000** Acts—it did **NOT** repeal the **Passports Act, 1967** (which governs issuance of passports to Indian citizens).
> - **OCI Revocation Trap (Aug 2025):** Under **Section 7D(da)** notification (`S.O. 3662(E)`), OCI registration is now cancellable not only upon a **≥ 2-year prison sentence**, but merely upon being **charge-sheeted** for an offence punishable with **≥ 7 years** (conviction not required).

UoI* (Nov 2024)** to demonstrate that Indian *Socialism* (welfare-state equality, not market hostility) and *Secularism* (principled distance, *Bommai* 1994) represent settled constitutional identity rather than partisan Emergency relics.

> - **Diaspora Rights vs. Sovereign Security:** Balance the **Anushka Rengunthwar (2023)** doctrine of *non-retrogression* and *e-OCI* digitization (2026) against heightened administrative discretion in **Aug 2025 charge-sheet-based OCI cancellations** (requiring procedural due process and proportionality under Arts 14 & 21).
