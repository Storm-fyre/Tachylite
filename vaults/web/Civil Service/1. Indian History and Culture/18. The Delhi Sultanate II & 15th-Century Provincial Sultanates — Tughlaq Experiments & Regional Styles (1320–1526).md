# Chapter 18 - The Delhi Sultanate II & 15th-Century Provincial Sultanates — Tughlaq Experiments & Regional Styles (1320–1526)

If the 13th-century Delhi Sultanate was an exercise in constructing a centralized northern garrison state, the 14th and 15th centuries exposed the physics of **imperial overstretch**. Under the Tughlaqs, Delhi pushed its direct administrative perimeter from the Indus to Madurai—outstripping pre-modern communication speeds, silver supply, and bureaucratic audit capacity.

When Timur shattered Delhi in 1398, political fragmentation did not produce a cultural dark age. Instead, fiscal surpluses once siphoned into the capital remained inside thriving regional ecologies—from the monsoon deltas of Bengal and Gujarat's maritime ports to Malwa's Vindhyan plateau and Kashmir's snowbound valley—triggering India's most inventive era of climate-adapted architecture and vernacular synthesis.

## 1. Tughlaq Statecraft - Overstretch, Rationalism, and Hydraulic Appeasement (1320–1414)

### Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq (1320–1325) - Moderation and Direct Annexation
Following the chaotic murder of Qutb-ud-din Mubarak Shah and the brief usurpation of Khusrau Khan, the veteran Warden of the Marches at Dipalpur, **Ghazi Malik**, seized Delhi and assumed the throne as **Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq**. His five-year reign balanced conservative agrarian recovery in the north with aggressive territorial annexation in the south.

- **Agrarian Pragmatism (*Rasm-i-Miyan*):** Rejecting Alauddin Khalji's harsh fixed *Masahat* assessment, Ghiyas-ud-din reverted to **crop-sharing (*Batai* / *Hukm-i-Hasili*)** so the crown shared harvest risk during droughts, capping annual revenue hikes to one-tenth or one-eleventh. He restored the customary grazing and cultivation exemptions of hereditary village chiefs (*Khuts* and *Muqaddams*) while forbidding them from levying unauthorized cesses on peasants.
- **Hydraulics and the Postal Relay (*Barid*):** Ghiyas-ud-din became the **first Delhi Sultan to excavate state irrigation canals** and perfected the imperial intelligence-and-postal relay network later marveled at by Ibn Battuta: two parallel lines comprising the royal horse-post (***Ulaq***, stationed every four miles) and the even faster foot-runner relay (***Dawa***, spaced every one-third of a mile with couriers ringing copper bells to alert the next runner).
- **The Fatal Deccan Reversal:** Abandoning Alauddin's realist policy of indirect Deccan vassalage, Ghiyas-ud-din dispatched his heir **Jauna Khan (Ulugh Khan)** to **directly annex** Warangal (1323, renamed *Sultanpur*, toppling Kakatiya Prataparudra II) and Madurai (*Ma'bar*). Returning from a punitive march to Bengal and Tirhut—amid a tense standoff with Chishti saint Nizamuddin Auliya, who famously remarked *"Hunuz Dilli Dur Ast"* ("Delhi is still far off")—Ghiyas-ud-din died in 1325 when a wooden reception pavilion erected by Jauna Khan collapsed at Afghanpur.

### Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351) - The Rationalist Autocrat
No medieval Indian monarch is more caricatured in popular lore—or more illuminating for studying institutional failure—than **Muhammad bin Tughlaq (Jauna Khan)**. Far from an erratic madman, he was a formidable scholar of logic, astronomy, medicine, and Persian poetry who attempted to solve genuine structural problems through top-down administrative decrees that far exceeded medieval state capacity.

Two eyewitness sources anchor his reign from sharply contrasting viewpoints. **Ziauddin Barani** (*Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi*), an orthodox aristocratic bureaucrat, condemned him as a "mixture of opposites" for bypassing the *Ulema*, recruiting rationalist philosophers, and elevating non-noble "low-born" administrators (such as Aziz Khummar the vintner, Pira Mali the gardener, and Ladha). By contrast, the Moroccan jurist **Ibn Battuta** (*Kitab-ul-Rihla*, arriving in India in 1333 and serving roughly eight years as Qazi of Delhi before his abortive embassy to Yuan China) captured both his terrifying unpredictability and his extraordinary cosmopolitanism—celebrating Holi, debating Jain scholars like **Jinaprabha Suri**, and presiding over one of Eurasia's wealthiest capitals.

His reign is structured around **five interlocking policy experiments**, each rooted in sound strategic logic yet undone by execution, ecology, or institutional fragility:

1. **The Daulatabad Relocation (1327):** Once Delhi directly annexed the Deccan and Tamil country, ruling a subcontinental empire from its exposed northwestern rim became logistically untenable. Muhammad designated Devagiri—renamed **Daulatabad**—as a **second imperial capital** situated near the empire's geographic center. Modern scholarship (K.A. Nizami, Satish Chandra) debunks Ibn Battuta's exaggerated tale of a totally emptied Delhi: the Sultan relocated primarily the **Muslim ruling elite, Sufi *shaikhs*, and *ulema*** (buying their Delhi homes and constructing shaded highway stations) to anchor an Islamicate administrative nucleus in the south, leaving Delhi garrisoned. The gamble collapsed when homesick northern elites balked, the Chagatai Mongol **Tarmashirin** raided the Punjab–Doab frontier (c. 1327–28)—note that unlike later myth, Muhammad did *not* permanently cede northwestern territory to Mongols—and managing the Indus frontier from the Deccan proved impossible. Returning the court to Delhi (c. 1335) left behind an alienated southern Muslim nobility that soon founded the Bahmani Sultanate.
2. **The Token Currency Crisis (1329–1332):** Confronted by a **14th-century Eurasian silver shortage** and spiraling military bills, Muhammad took inspiration from Kublai Khan's paper *chao* in Yuan China and Ilkhanid Gaykhatu's *chav* in Iran. He issued **copper and brass token coins** decreed at full legal parity with the 175-grain silver *tanka*. The monetary physics were ruined by **Gresham's Law** coupled with zero anti-counterfeiting technology: the state lacked a monopoly over bronze smelting or intricate die-stamping. As Barani acidly observed, *"every Hindu's house turned into a mint."* Counterfeit copper flooded revenue offices while pure silver vanished into private hoards, freezing long-distance trade. Demonstrating surprising fiscal integrity, the Sultan canceled the experiment and redeemed *all* copper tokens—genuine and forged alike—for silver and gold at Tughlaqabad, exhausting the treasury.
3. **Khurasan (c. 1329–30) and Qarachil (c. 1333–34) Campaigns:** Sensing political instability in Ilkhanid Persia, Muhammad recruited a colossal expeditionary force of 3,70,000 cavalrymen paid a full year's advance to invade Khurasan; when Persian and Chagatai alliances stabilized, he abruptly disbanded the unpaid army, unleashing armed turbulence across Hindustan. Immediately afterward, the **Qarachil expedition**—an effort to pacify refractory chiefs in the **Kumaon–Garhwal Himalayan foothills** (not a quixotic attempt to conquer China, as later folklore claimed)—secured initial surrender before monsoon downpours, mountain ambushes, and epidemic disease wiped out the retreating force.
4. **Doab Famine & the *Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi* (c. 1326–1342):** To replenish the drained treasury, Muhammad escalated land taxes and extra cesses (*abwabs*) across the Ganga–Yamuna Doab precisely as a catastrophic **seven-year drought and famine** struck (c. 1334–41). Starving peasants torched their crops and fled to the forests, met initially with brutal punitive hunts. Recognizing that violence cannot tax barren dust, Muhammad pivoted to India's first state-directed agrarian recovery program: establishing the ***Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi*** (Ministry of Agriculture), relocating his court temporarily to a Ganges relief camp at **Swargadwari** near Kannauj, and disbursing state relief loans (***Sondhar* / *Taqavi***) for wells, bullocks, and seeds. He even funded a **70-lakh *tanka* model agricultural tract** (60 by 60 *karoh*) enforcing progressive **crop rotation** (barley to wheat, wheat to sugarcane, sugarcane to grapes and dates). Embezzlement by corrupt contractors and uncultivable wastelands doomed the experiment, yet *taqavi* credit became a permanent pillar of Mughal fiscalism.
5. ***Amiran-i-Sada* Revolts and Imperial Implosion (1335–1351):** To tighten provincial control, Muhammad relied on foreign military-revenue officers titled ***Amiran-i-Sada*** ("Centurions," commanding groups of 100 villages or troopers) across Gujarat, Malwa, and the Deccan. Terrified by his ruthless purges and resentful of his non-noble appointees, the *Amiran-i-Sada* triggered a domino chain of secessions: **Ma'bar (Madurai)** broke away (1335), **Vijayanagara** rose in Karnataka (1336), **Bengal** turned independent (1338–42), **Gujarat** erupted under the cobbler-rebel Taghi (1345), and Deccani *Amiran-i-Sada* seized Daulatabad to crown **Alauddin Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu)**, founding the **Bahmani Sultanate (1347)**. Pursuing Taghi into Sindh, Muhammad died of fever near Thatta in March 1351—inspiring Badauni's celebrated epitaph: *"The king was freed from his people, and they from their king."*

### Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388) - Hydraulics, Orthodoxy, and Feudal Decay
Elevated by nobles and *ulema* in the Sindh camp to prevent total disintegration, **Firoz Shah Tughlaq** pursued the exact inverse of his cousin's centralism: **appeasement of the aristocracy, rigid Sunni orthodoxy, and intensive northern hydraulic welfare**. His reign is documented by his own inscribed autobiography ***Futuhat-i-Firoz Shahi*** and two separate court histories both titled ***Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi*** (by Ziauddin Barani and Shams-i-Siraj Afif).

- **Re-Feudalizing the *Iqta* and Army:** Abandoning reconquest of the Deccan and Bengal (after two indecisive Bengal marches against Haji Ilyas and Sikandar Shah, limiting offensive campaigns to Jajnagar/Puri, Nagarkot/Kangra—where he commissioned Persian translations of 300 Sanskrit manuscripts as *Dalail-i-Firoz Shahi*—and Thatta), Firoz wrote off all *Sondhar* agrarian debts. Fatally for central statecraft, he **made *Iqtas* and military posts hereditary** and substituted direct cash pay for ordinary soldiers with assignable revenue rights (*wajh*)—reversing a century of anti-feudal discipline built by Iltutmish, Balban, and Alauddin.
- **Orthodox Fiscalism and *Jizya* on Brahmins:** Firoz abolished **24 non-canonical cesses (*abwabs*)** (including *Ghari* and *Charai*) and aligned taxation strictly with the **four Quranic levies**: *Kharaj*, *Khums* (restoring 4/5th war booty to troops and 1/5th to the state), *Zakat*, and ***Jizya***. For the first time in Sultanate history, he decoupled *Jizya* from *Kharaj* and **imposed *Jizya* directly on Brahmins**, refusing to yield even when Delhi Brahmins staged hunger strikes until local Hindu merchants paid the levy on their behalf.
- **The Canal Revolution and *Haqq-i-Shurb*:** Firoz built the **largest pre-Mughal irrigation canal grid in India**, most notably the **Sutlej-to-Ghaggar (*Ulughkhani*, ~150 km)** and **Yamuna-to-Hissar (*Rajabwah*, ~240 km)** arteries. Transforming semi-arid Haryana and the Doab into double-cropped wheat, sugarcane, and fruit-orchard belts (planting 1,200 state gardens near Delhi), he secured *Ulema* approval to levy a **10% irrigation tax (***Haqq-i-Shurb***)** on canal-watered fields, which flowed directly into his personal privy purse. To lubricate petty urban commerce, he introduced small fractional coins (*Adha*, *Bikh*, and the 6-*jital* *Shashgani*).
- **Urbanism, Welfare Bureaus, and *Diwan-i-Bandagan*:** Firoz founded planned towns including **Firozabad** (Delhi's fifth city), **Jaunpur** (honoring cousin Jauna Khan), **Hissar-i-Firoza**, **Fatehabad**, and **Firozpur**. He created India's earliest medieval welfare departments—***Dar-ul-Shafa*** (free public hospital), ***Diwan-i-Khairat*** (endowment office funding dowries for indigent Muslim girls), and an employment bureau—while controversially expanding the royal slave bureau, ***Diwan-i-Bandagan***, to **1,80,000 slaves** (employed across 36 state *karkhanas* and as palace guards). After his death in 1388, this politicized slave corps plunged the capital into a decade of destructive civil war. As a remarkable feat of antiquarian engineering, Firoz also dismantled and transported two monolithic **Ashokan sandstone pillars**—from **Topra** (Haryana) and **Meerut**—by custom 42-wheel carriages and river rafts to crown Kotla Firoz Shah and Delhi Ridge.

## 2. The Timurid Rupture, Sayyids, and Afghan Kingship (1398–1526)

### Timur's Sack of Delhi (1398)
By 1398, the Tughlaq state had fractured into two rival courts contesting Delhi itself. Into this vacuum marched **Amir Timur (Tamerlane)** of Samarkand, crossing the Indus, ravaging Multan and Bhatner, and routing the reigning Tughlaq Sultan **Nasir-ud-din Mahmud Shah** outside Delhi in December 1398.

Following a fierce urban uprising, Timur unleashed a five-day sack and massacre of Delhi before marching back to Central Asia with unimaginable bullion and thousands of Indian stone-masons and artisans to build Samarkand's Bibi-Khanym Mosque. Timur neither annexed Hindustan nor stayed—yet his blow severed Delhi's imperial aura permanently, formalizing the independence of Jaunpur, Gujarat, and Malwa.

### The Sayyid Interlude (1414–1451)
Before departing India, Timur appointed **Khizr Khan** as governor of Multan, Lahore, and Dipalpur. Seizing Delhi in 1414 after the last Tughlaq died, Khizr Khan founded the **Sayyid Dynasty**, prudently declining the sovereign title *Sultan* in favor of ***Rayat-i-Ala*** ("Exalted Standard") while striking coins and reading the *khutba* in the name of Timur and his son Shah Rukh.

Claiming Prophetic descent and wearing distinctive pointed caps, the Sayyids were known socially as ***Kulah-Daran*** ("Cap-wearers"), distinguishing themselves from turbanned religious jurists (*Dastar-bandan*). Khizr Khan's successors—**Mubarak Shah** (whose reign is chronicled in Yahya bin Ahmad Sirhindi's ***Tarikh-i-Mubarak Shahi***), **Muhammad Shah**, and **Alauddin Alam Shah**—presided over a shrinking regional principality scarcely extending 200 miles from Delhi, until Alam Shah voluntarily retired to Badaun in 1451.

### The Lodi Dynasty (1451–1526) - Afghan Tribal Confederation vs. Autocracy
The **Lodis** inaugurated the **first Afghan (Pashtun) dynasty** of Delhi, transforming the structural ideology of kingship:

- **Bahlul Lodi (1451–1489):** Seizing Delhi peacefully, Bahlul systematically re-annexed the formidable Sharqi Sultanate of Jaunpur (1479–86). To rally Pashtun warriors from Roh against rival regional sultans, Bahlul institutionalized **Afghan tribal confederation kingship (*primus inter pares*)**: abandoning the Turkish elevated throne, he sat on a flat carpet alongside his clan chiefs, addressed them as *Masnad-i-Ali* ("Exalted Lordship"), and treated the kingdom as a shared tribal patrimony rather than a Sultan's personal estate. His copper coin, the ***Bahluli***, remained northern India's everyday exchange medium well into Akbar's reign.
- **Sikandar Lodi (1489–1517):** The ablest Lodi sovereign recognized that excessive tribal equality paralyzed fiscal administration. While respecting Pashtun sensibilities, he reasserted formal royal hierarchy, enforced strict *iqta* revenue audits via spies, annexed Bihar (1495–96), concluded a boundary treaty with Bengal's Alauddin Husain Shah, and **founded Agra in 1504** (making it his administrative capital in 1506) to dominate Gwalior, Rajasthan, and Malwa. Economically, he introduced a standardized 32-digit land-measurement yard—the ***Gaz-i-Sikandari*** (~30–32 inches, later adopted by Sher Shah and Akbar)—and abolished internal transit duties (*zakat*) on grain. A refined Persian poet under the pen-name ***Gulrukhi*** (composing a *diwan* of 9,000 verses), he commissioned the Ayurvedic medical digest *Tib-i-Sikandari* (*Ma'dan-us-Shifa*) and the music treatise *Lahjat-i-Sikandar Shahi*, though he simultaneously enforced harsh orthodox bans on Hindu shrines at Mathura and Nagarkot.
- **Ibrahim Lodi (1517–1526):** Ibrahim fatally shattered Bahlul's tribal compact by declaring *"kingship knows no kinship."* Forcing proud Afghan clan chiefs to stand with folded arms before his throne and executing senior dissenters (like Mian Bhua), he drove Punjab governor **Daulat Khan Lodi** and his own uncle **Alam Khan Lodi**—alongside Rajput challenger Rana Sanga of Mewar—to invite **Babur** from Kabul. At the **First Battle of Panipat (April 21, 1526)**, Babur's Timurid field artillery and *Tulghuma* flanking tactics crushed Ibrahim's vastly larger Afghan host, extinguishing the Delhi Sultanate after 320 years.

## 3. Tughlaq, Sayyid, and Lodi Architecture - Austerity, Octagons, and Double Domes

### The Tughlaq "Puritanical" Phase (1320–1414)
Depleted treasuries after Muhammad bin Tughlaq's fiscal shocks forced builders to abandon the ornate carved red-sandstone vocabulary of the Khaljis. Tughlaq engineering pivoted to austere, militant **local grey Delhi quartzite rubble** covered with thick lime plaster—prioritizing monumental structural durability over surface filigree:

- **Battered (Sloping) Walls:** Thick exterior walls leaned dramatically inward (*batter*), visually amplifying fortress solidity while physically countering the outward lateral thrust of heavy masonry domes and vaults.
- **Arch-and-Beam Synthesis:** To harmonize indigenous trabeate craft with West Asian vaulting, Tughlaq architects inserted a horizontal flat **stone lintel and brackets** directly across the springing line of a **four-centered pointed arch**—eliminating the Seljuq horseshoe arch and marrying Hindu beam aesthetics with Islamic compression.
- **Tughlaqabad & Ghiyas-ud-din's Tomb (1321–25):** Delhi's third medieval city, built atop a rocky ridge by Ghiyas-ud-din, features massive cyclopean sloping ramparts. Linked to the fort via a fortified causeway across a former artificial lake stands **Ghiyas-ud-din's Tomb**: enclosed within a pentagonal bastioned wall, its sharply battered red-sandstone chamber is inlaid with white marble bands, pierced by arch-and-lintel doorways, and crowned by a pointed white-marble dome carrying a Hindu *amalaka-kalasha* finial.
- **Jahanpanah & Firozabad (Kotla Firoz Shah):** Muhammad bin Tughlaq linked earlier settlements into **Jahanpanah** (Delhi's fourth city, containing Bijai Mandal, the seven-arched **Satpula** weir-bridge, and Begumpur Masjid). Firoz Shah Tughlaq built **Firozabad / Kotla Firoz Shah** (fifth city along the Yamuna), anchored by a three-tiered pyramidal vaulted rubble terrace crowned with the relocated **Topra Ashokan Pillar**, alongside his sober plastered **Tomb and L-shaped *Madrasa* at Hauz Khas** overlooking Alauddin's desilted tank. His Wazir, **Khan-i-Jahan Jauna Shah**, erected distinctive multi-domed hypostyle mosques including **Khirki Masjid** (a rare enclosed double-storey mosque lit by four interior open courts) and **Kalan Masjid**.
- **Tomb of Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul Tilangani (1368–69, Nizamuddin):** Constructed by Jauna Shah for his father—a converted Telugu Brahmin from Warangal who served as Firoz Shah's Wazir for 18 years—this monument stands as an architectural milestone: **Delhi's earliest Octagonal Tomb**. Surrounded by a verandah with three pointed arches on each of its eight sides, shaded by deep overhanging *chhajja* eaves, and ringed above by eight rooftop cupolas (*chhatris*) around a central dome, Tilangani's octagonal geometry—echoing Jerusalem's Dome of the Rock—established the dominant royal funerary template for the next two centuries. *(Geographic Precision Watchout: While Tilangani is **Delhi's** earliest octagonal tomb, the **earliest surviving freestanding octagonal Islamic mausoleum in the Indian subcontinent / Tughlaq Empire** is the monumental brick-and-glazed-tile **Tomb of Shaikh Rukn-i-Alam in Multan**, c. 1320–24, built by Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq four decades earlier.)*

### Sayyid and Lodi Funerary Engineering (1414–1526)
With contracted territory and modest revenues, Sayyid and Lodi patronage centered overwhelmingly on mausoleums (prompting Percy Brown to dub it the "Age of the Macabre"), dividing cleanly into **octagonal royal tombs** and **square noble tombs (*gumbads*)**:

- **Octagonal Royal Lineage:** Direct descendants of Tilangani's design appear in the tombs of **Mubarak Shah Sayyid** and **Muhammad Shah Sayyid (c. 1444, Lodi Gardens)**—the latter refining the silhouette with taller dome drums, sloped corner buttresses, triple-arched verandahs on double square pillars, and eight lotus-crowned *chhatris*.
- **Square *Gumbads* — Bara Gumbad & Shish Gumbad (Lodi Gardens):** Lodi nobles favored tall, un-verandahed square cube-and-dome chambers whose facades were broken visually into faux two- or three-storey blind arches. Critically, the towering **Bara Gumbad (1490–94)** contains **no graves**—it functioned as a colossal domed gateway for the adjoining three-domed mosque lavishly carved in floral stucco. Facing it stands **Shish Gumbad (c. 1489–1517)**, named for the bright blue and turquoise glazed tile bands (*shisha*) decorating its quartzite facade. Separately, **Moth ki Masjid (c. 1505)**—built by Sikandar's Wazir Miyan Bhoiya—represents the mature Lodi mosque synthesis of red sandstone, white marble, and balanced three-dome rhythm.
- **Sikandar Lodi's Tomb (1517–18) — Double-Dome & Garden-Tomb Prototype:** Built by Ibrahim Lodi in Lodi Gardens, this octagonal mausoleum achieved **two structural breakthroughs** that directly bridged Sultanate and Mughal architecture. First, it stood inside a vast, battlemented **walled garden enclosure**—making it **India's earliest surviving garden-tomb** prior to Humayun's *Charbagh*. Second, building on early structural experiments (such as Taj Khan's Tomb, 1501), it canonized the **True Double Dome** in royal Indian architecture: two concentric masonry shells separated by a hollow void. This solved a classic geometric dilemma—allowing the **outer dome** to soar skyward for commanding exterior proportions while keeping the **inner ceiling** low enough to prevent a cavernous, echoing tomb interior.

## 4. 15th-Century Provincial Sultanates and Regional Architectural Styles

Post-1398 decentralization acted as an extraordinary cultural catalyst. Regional dynasties competing for legitimacy rooted themselves in local languages, artisanal guilds, and ecological building materials—producing five unmistakably distinct Indo-Islamic civilizations across Hindustan and its borderlands.

### Jaunpur - The Sharqi "Shiraz of the East" (1394–1479)
Founded by Firoz Shah Tughlaq on the Gomti River in memory of Jauna Khan, Jaunpur became autonomous in 1394 when eunuch Wazir **Malik Sarwar** assumed the title ***Malik-us-Sharq*** ("Lord of the East"). Under **Ibrahim Shah Sharqi (1402–1440)** and **Husain Shah Sharqi (1458–1479)**, Jaunpur earned renown as ***Shiraz-i-Hind*** ("Shiraz of India"), attracting refugee scholars from Timur-ravaged Delhi, fostering Sufi-Awadhi literary circles, and catalyzing Hindustani music—with Husain Shah celebrated as a key pioneer of **Khayal** vocal idioms (*Jaunpuri* and *Husaini* ragas) before Bahlul Lodi reconquered the kingdom.

Architecturally, Jaunpur adapted Tughlaq battered masonry (specifically Delhi's Begumpur Masjid) into a striking regional hallmark: **complete elimination of minarets**, replaced by **colossal, tapering rectangular front pylons (*pishtaqs*)** flanked by battered towers that rise 23 meters or more to completely conceal the prayer hall's central dome from the courtyard. This grammar crystallized in the **Atala Masjid (completed 1408 by Ibrahim Shah** over Firoz Tughlaq's late-14th-century foundation, featuring double-storeyed trabeate cloisters and screened *zenana* galleries), followed by the **Jhanjhiri Masjid**, **Lal Darwaza Masjid**, and Husain Shah's monumental **Jama Masjid (1470)** raised on an elevated basement plinth.

### Bengal - Alluvial Terracotta, *Bangla* Roofs, and Vernacular Renaissance (1342–1538)
Unified by **Shams-ud-din Ilyas Shah (1342–1358)** and his son **Sikandar Shah**, independent Bengal weathered an interlude under Hindu zamindar **Raja Ganesha** and his converted son Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Shah (builder of the iconic single-domed **Eklakhi Mausoleum** at Pandua) before ascending to a golden age under the **Husain Shahi Dynasty**—specifically **Alauddin Husain Shah (1494–1519)** and **Nasir-ud-din Nusrat Shah (1519–1532)**.

Remembered in Bengali tradition as *Nripati Tilak* ("Crown of Kings") and *Jagat Bhushan*, Alauddin Husain Shah appointed Hindus to apex state posts—including his chief ministers **Rupa and Sanatana Goswami**, who became principal theologians of **Chaitanya Mahaprabhu**, whose Vaishnava *Sankirtana* movement flourished freely under Husain Shahi protection. Crucially, the Bengal Sultans broke Sanskrit courtly exclusivity by patronizing **vernacular Bengali literature**: **Krittivas Ojha** rendered the Bengali *Ramayana*, **Maladhar Basu** translated the *Bhagavata Purana* as *Sri Krishna Vijaya* (winning the title *Gunaraj Khan* from Rukn-ud-din Barbak Shah), **Kavindra Parameshvara** and **Srikara Nandi** translated the *Mahabharata*, and **Vijay Gupta** composed *Manasamangal*.

Because the Gangetic delta lacked stone quarries and suffered torrential monsoons, Bengal architecture mastered **kiln-fired red brick**, sparse **Rajmahal black basalt facing**, delicate **carved and glazed terracotta plaques**, and **drooping curved roof cornices** translated directly from rural Bengali bent-bamboo thatched roofs—yielding both ***Do-chala*** (two-sloped curved gable) and ***Char-chala*** (four-sloped curved pavilion, later adopted by Mughals as the *Bangla* roof). Landmark monuments span the vast **Adina Mosque at Pandua (1375, Sikandar Shah)** (then the subcontinent's largest mosque, covering a colossal courtyard under 370+ small domes split by a Sasanian-style barrel-vaulted central nave); Gaur's towering brick-and-terracotta **Dakhil Darwaza** ("Saluting Gate," flanked by twelve-sided five-storey corner towers, variously assigned by scholars to **Barbak Shah** c. 1465 and later inscriptions of **Nusrat Shah**); polychrome **Tantipara** and **Lotan Masjids**; **Chota Sona Masjid** ("Small Golden Mosque," built by Wali Muhammad in present-day Chapai Nawabganj during the reign of **Alauddin Husain Shah**, 1494–1519) and **Bara Sona Masjid (*Baroduari*, "Large Golden Mosque," at Gaur, completed in 1526 by Nasir-ud-din Nusrat Shah)**; and **Qadam Rasul (Gaur, 1530, Nusrat Shah)**, enclosing the Prophet's footprint stone beneath a dome flanked by curved *Bangla* corner roofs.

### Gujarat - Maritime Opulence and Maru-Gurjara Synthesis (1407–1573)
Breaking from post-Timur Delhi under Tughlaq governor **Zafar Khan (Muzaffar Shah I, 1407)**, the Muzaffarid Sultanate harnessed western India's premier maritime commercial corridor—**Cambay (Khambhat)**, **Surat**, **Bharuch**, and **Diu**—channeling Indian cottons, silk brocades, indigo, and spices across the Arabian Sea to Aden, Hormuz, and Malacca. **Ahmad Shah I (1411–1442)** founded **Ahmedabad (1411)** on the Sabarmati, while his grandson **Mahmud Begada (1458–1511)**—nicknamed for conquering two mighty hill forts (*gadhs*), **Girnar/Junagadh** and **Champaner-Pavagadh (1484)**—built Gujarat into a maritime powerhouse that allied with Mamluk Egypt to contest early Portuguese naval raids off Chaul (1508) and Diu (1509). Under **Bahadur Shah (1526–1537)**, Mughal Emperor Humayun's 1535 invasion forced Gujarat to **surrender Diu to the Portuguese** in exchange for military aid—before Portuguese treachery led to Bahadur Shah's drowning off Diu in 1537.

Gujarat produced India's most seamlessly refined synthesis of Islamic congregational plans and **Solanki / Maru-Gurjara Hindu-Jain stonecraft**. Employing hereditary guild masons who had sculpted Dilwara and Modhera, Gujarat mosques feature richly carved stone pillars, multi-tiered corbelled domes, overhanging *jharokha* balconies, twin richly carved slender minarets framing the central arch, and peerless pierced-stone ***jali*** screens. Its crowning sites include Ahmad Shah's **Jama Masjid of Ahmedabad (1423)**; the **Champaner–Pavagadh Archaeological Park (UNESCO WHS, 2004)**—Mahmud Begada's completely preserved pre-Mughal planned capital (*Muhammadabad-Champaner*, 1484), uniting Pavagadh's Kalikamata hill shrine, Chauhan fortifications, rainwater-harvesting stepwells, and the sublime **Jama Masjid of Champaner** (twin minarets, seven *mihrabs*, carved *jalis*); **Sarkhej Roza** and **Adalaj Stepwell**; and Ahmedabad's **Sidi Saiyyed Mosque (1572–73)**—erected by Abyssinian noble Sidi Saiyyed under general Bilal Jhajar Khan in the very last year of Sultan **Muzaffar Shah III** right before Akbar's conquest, globally celebrated for its ten semicircular sandstone *jali* tympani carved into intertwining **"Tree of Life" (*Kalpavriksha*)** palm-and-foliage tracery.

### Malwa - Mandu's Plateau Pavilions and the *Nimatnama* (1401–1562)
Following **Dilawar Khan Ghuri's** independence at Dhar (1401), his son **Hoshang Shah Ghuri (1406–1435)** fortified the dramatic 634-meter Vindhyan plateau of **Mandu**—christened ***Shadiabad*** ("City of Joy"). In 1436, the **Malwa Khaljis** took power under warrior-statesman **Mahmud Khalji I (1436–1469)** (locked in relentless rivalry with Rana Kumbha of Mewar), succeeded by pleasure-loving **Ghiyas-ud-din Khalji (1469–1500)** and **Nasir-ud-din Khalji (1500–1511)**, before Gujarat (1531) and Mughal Akbar (1561–62, defeating musician-prince Baz Bahadur of Roopmati legend) absorbed Malwa.

Malwa's environmental architecture dispensed entirely with minarets, pairing **lofty plinths approached by dramatic stairways** with steeply battered Tughlaq walls, pointed spearhead-fringed arches, turquoise/yellow glazed tiles, airy cantilevered balconies, and cooling artificial lakes. Key monuments at **Mandu** include the grand **Jama Masjid** (begun by Hoshang Shah, completed by Mahmud Khalji I in 1454); **Hoshang Shah's Tomb (completed c. 1440)**—**India's earliest mausoleum built entirely in white marble**, displaying balanced Afghan dome proportions and delicate marble *jalis*, later surveyed by Shah Jahan's four architects (including Ustad Ahmad Lahori) as a design precursor to the Taj Mahal; **Hindola Mahal** ("Swinging Palace," whose sharply battered 77-degree sidewalls create an optical illusion of swaying); **Jahaz Mahal** ("Ship Palace," built under Ghiyas-ud-din Khalji on a slender strip between the Kapur and Munj Talao reservoirs with sculpted rooftop pleasure pools); and **Ashrafi Mahal**.

Equally decisive in Indian art history is Malwa's illuminated Persian culinary treatise, the ***Nimatnama-i-Nasiruddin-Shahi*** ("Book of Delights," c. 1495–1505, British Library)—compiled for **Ghiyas-ud-din Khalji** and completed under **Nasir-ud-din Khalji**. Alongside some of India's earliest written recipes for *samosas* (*sanbusa*), *wadas*, sherbets, betel (*pan*), and perfumes, its **50 opaque watercolor miniatures** fuse Iranian **Shirazi (Turkman) landscapes** with indigenous **Western Indian/Jain angular brushwork**, forming a vital bridge toward early Mughal and Rajput painting.

### Kashmir - Zain-ul-Abidin *Budshah* and Himalayan Timber Mosques (1339–1561)
Established by **Shams-ud-din Shah Mir (1339)**, the Kashmir Sultanate suffered acute sectarian trauma under **Sultan Sikandar *Butshikan* (1389–1413)**, whose iconoclasm triggered a mass exodus of Kashmiri Pandits. His younger son **Zain-ul-Abidin (1420–1470)**—universally hailed as ***Budshah*** ("The Great King") and **"the Akbar of Kashmir"**—reversed every intolerant decree a full century before Akbar:

- **Pluralism and Literary Synthesis:** He **abolished *Jizya*** and cow slaughter, recalled exiled Brahmins into top administrative offices, restored damaged temples, opened free pilgrim kitchens (*Jogi Lanker* at Rainawari), and established a royal **translation bureau** rendering the *Mahabharata* and Kalhana's *Rajatarangini* into Persian (*Bahr-ul-Asmar* by Mulla Ahmad). Simultaneously, his Sanskrit court historians **Jonaraja** (*Dvitiya Rajatarangini*, continuing Kalhana down to 1459) and his pupil **Srivara** (*Jaina-Rajatarangini*) kept Kashmir's ancient Sanskrit historiographical chain unbroken.
- **Hydraulics, Crafts, and *Zaina Lank*:** Continuing Kashmir's ancient hydraulic tradition, *Budshah* excavated major **irrigation canals** (such as the Lachham Kul and Mar canals), erected Srinagar's first wooden bridge (**Zaina Kadal**), and engineered the fortified artificial stone island ***Zaina Lank*** in **Wular Lake** (alongside *Sona Lank* and *Rupa Lank* in Dal Lake). By importing master artisans from Samarkand and Khurasan, he seeded Kashmir's globally celebrated craft industries: **papier-mâché, carpet weaving, *pashmina* shawl-making, wood-carving, and bookbinding**.
- **Seismic Timber Pagoda Architecture:** Heavy Himalayan snowfalls, frequent earthquakes, and abundant ***Deodar* cedar forests** rendered rigid brick-and-stone domes structurally vulnerable in the valley. Kashmiri builders fused Sufi/Rishi liturgy with indigenous timber temple and Buddhist pagoda engineering: flexible nail-less timber joinery, intricate geometric ***Khatamband*** ceilings, carved ***Pinjra*** lattice windows, and **tiered pyramidal pagoda-style roofs** crowned by open wooden pavilions and slender spires (*brangh*) built to shed snow. This idiom culminates in **Khanqah-e-Moula (Shah-e-Hamdan Shrine)** along the Jhelum (founded c. 1395 by Sikandar for Persian Sufi Mir Sayyid Ali Hamadani and repeatedly restored in authentic timber-and-papier-mâché craft) and Srinagar's monumental **Jama Masjid** (begun c. 1400 by Sikandar, expanded with turrets by Zain-ul-Abidin, later restored under Aurangzeb)—supported around a serene square courtyard by **370 massive *deodar* wooden pillars** beneath four pagoda-spired halls. (Notably, **Budshah's Tomb**—erected for Zain-ul-Abidin's mother—provides a rare brick-and-dome exception, importing Timurid Central Asian glazed-brick craftsmanship atop an ancient carved stone base.)

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Timur & Mongol Synchronism Traps:** Timur crossed the Indus and sacked Delhi in **1398** during **Nasir-ud-din Mahmud Shah Tughlaq**, **NOT** Muhammad bin Tughlaq (UPSC Prelims 2021). Similarly, Muhammad bin Tughlaq did **NOT** temporarily lose northwestern territory to Mongols (UPSC Prelims 2022).
> - **Tughlaq Irrigation Nuance:** **Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq** was the *first* Delhi Sultan to dig irrigation canals, whereas **Firoz Shah Tughlaq** built the *biggest network* of canals (Sutlej–Ghaggar and Yamuna–Hissar) and introduced the 10% irrigation tax ***Haqq-i-Shurb*** (UPSC Prelims 1998).
> - **Who Were *Kulah-Daran*?:** The **Sayyids** (pointed cap-wearers claiming Prophetic descent), **NOT** Qalandars or Arab merchants (UPSC Prelims 2022).
> - **Double-Dome & Octagonal Tomb Ladder:** Earliest surviving freestanding octagonal tomb in the **Indian subcontinent** = **Tomb of Shaikh Rukn-i-Alam (Multan, c. 1320–24, Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq)** $\rightarrow$ Earliest octagonal tomb in **Delhi** = **Khan-i-Jahan Tilangani (1368–69, Firoz Tughlaq era)** $\rightarrow$ Earliest garden-tomb + canonical **True Double Dome** prototype = **Sikandar Lodi's Tomb (1517–18, built by Ibrahim Lodi)** (whereas **Bara Gumbad** contains *no graves* and serves as a mosque gateway).
> - **Regional "No-Minaret" & "First Marble" Traps:** Both **Jaunpur** (massive sloping pylons) and **Malwa** (Mandu pavilions) omit minarets—whereas **Gujarat** celebrates towering twin carved minarets. **Hoshang Shah's Tomb at Mandu (c. 1440)** is India's **first all-marble tomb** (predating Mughal Itimad-ud-Daulah and Taj Mahal by nearly two centuries).
> - **Gujarat Final-Phase Chronology:** **Bahadur Shah** (NOT Ahmad Shah or Mahmud Begada) surrendered **Diu** to the Portuguese in 1535 (UPSC Prelims 2023). **Sidi Saiyyed Mosque (1572–73)** belongs to the final year of the Gujarat Sultanate (**Muzaffar Shah III**), right before Akbar's annexation—not the early 15th century.
> - **Kashmir *Rajatarangini* Continuity:** Kalhana wrote *Rajatarangini* in 1148–49 (Lohara era), while **Jonaraja** (*Dvitiya Rajatarangini*) and **Srivara** (*Jaina-Rajatarangini*) continued it in the 15th century under **Zain-ul-Abidin *Budshah***.

### Prelims Trap Signature
- **Ruler–Reform Misattribution:** Swapping Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq (first canals, *Batai* moderation) with Firoz Shah Tughlaq (largest canal grid, *Haqq-i-Shurb*, hereditary *Iqtas*, *Jizya* on Brahmins), or confusing Muhammad bin Tughlaq's *Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi* (agriculture) with Firoz's *Diwan-i-Khairat* (charity) and *Diwan-i-Bandagan* (slaves).
- **Architectural Prototype Inversion:** Crediting the **Mughals** (Humayun's Tomb) with inventing the octagonal tomb, garden-tomb, double dome, or white-marble mausoleum—all four actually debuted in Sultanate and provincial strata (**Rukn-i-Alam Multan** c. 1320–24 & **Tilangani's Tomb** 1368–69, **Hoshang Shah's Tomb** c. 1440, and **Sikandar Lodi's Tomb** 1517–18).
- **Regional Style Swaps:** Mixing up the signature ecological adaptations across the five provincial schools—especially **no-minaret pylons** (Jaunpur) vs. **curved *Bangla* terracotta** (Bengal) vs. **twin carved minarets & *jalis*** (Gujarat) vs. **lofty lake-plinth pavilions** (Malwa) vs. **wooden pagoda roofs** (Kashmir).
- **Chronicle & Patron Pairing:** Confusing the **two distinct works** titled *Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi* (Ziauddin Barani vs. Shams-i-Siraj Afif) with Firoz's autobiography *Futuhat-i-Firoz Shahi*, or misattributing the *Nimatnama* to the Mughals instead of the Malwa Khaljis.
