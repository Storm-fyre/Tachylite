# Chapter 23 - Regional Statecraft & Resistance - The Maratha Swarajya and the Ahom Paik Kingdom (1630–1761)

Imperial centralization in Mughal India encountered its two most formidable regional limits at opposite geographical extremes of the subcontinent: the fractured volcanic ridges of the **Western Ghats (Sahyadris)** and the flooded alluvial corridors of the **Upper Brahmaputra Valley**. Neither the **Maratha *Swarajya*** nor the **Ahom Kingdom** survived merely through tactical bravery; both engineered bespoke ecological-institutional states calibrated to neutralize heavy Mughal cavalry and fiscal hegemony.

Comparing them side by side reveals two radically distinct models of pre-colonial Indian state capacity: a **monetized, mobile, fortress-and-tribute state** forged in western Maharashtra, and a **non-monetized, collective-labor, hydraulic-riverine monarchy** that ruled Assam unbroken for six centuries. Both traditions sit at the forefront of contemporary Indian heritage after back-to-back UNESCO World Heritage inscriptions in **2024** (*Moidams of Charaideo*) and **2025** (*Maratha Military Landscapes of India*).

---

## 1. Chhatrapati Shivaji (1630–1680) and the Architecture of Maratha *Swarajya*

### Ecological Determinism, Bhakti Solidarity and *Mavali* Mobilization

The rise of Maratha sovereignty sprang from a convergence of terrain, cultural cohesion, and political apprenticeship. Unlike the flat Indo-Gangetic alluvium where heavy imperial cavalry swept unobstructed, the **Sahyadri** escarpments fractured Maharashtra into narrow mountain passes (*ghats*), basaltic tablelands, and narrow coastal Konkan strips—favoring nimble light forces over ponderous imperial armies.

Socially, three centuries of the ***Varkari* Bhakti movement** (Jnaneshwar, Namdev, Eknath, Tukaram) had forged an egalitarian Marathi vernacular identity bridging Brahmin, Kunbi peasant, and artisanal strata, reinforced by **Samarth Ramdas's** activist *Dharakari* discipline. Politically, Maratha chieftains (*Deshmukhs* and *Bargirs*)—foremost Shivaji's father **Shahji Bhonsle**—had mastered guerrilla warfare (*Bargigiri*) under Ahmadnagar's Malik Ambar and held administrative *jagirs* under Bijapur's Adil Shahi court.

Born at **Shivneri Fort (1630)** and raised in his father's Pune *jagir* by his mother **Jijabai** and guardian-administrator **Dadaji Kondadev**, **Shivaji Bhonsle** bypassed aristocratic warlords to recruit hardy hill peasants from the **Maval** valleys (***Mavalis***) as his core infantry. Exploiting Bijapur's internal weakness and Mughal–Deccan rivalries, he captured **Torna (1646)**, Kondhana (Sinhagad), Purandar, and the strategic hill principality of **Javali (1656)** from Chandra Rao More—opening access to the Konkan coast.

When Bijapur dispatched veteran commander **Afzal Khan** to crush him, Shivaji lured him into the forested slopes below **Pratapgad (November 1659)**, killing him in a preemptive close-quarters encounter (*Bagh Nakh* tiger-claws) and routing the Bijapuri army. Confronting Aurangzeb next, Shivaji executed a daring night raid on Mughal viceroy **Shaista Khan's** camp in Pune (**1663**) and mounted the lucrative **First Sack of Surat (1664)** (repeated in 1670), systematically starving the imperial exchequer while bankrolling *Swarajya*.

### *Ganimi Kava*, Fortification Triads and the Maratha Navy

Recognizing that his lean agrarian base could never match Mughal heavy artillery in pitched plains battles, Shivaji perfected ***Ganimi Kava*** (asymmetric guerrilla warfare): rapid cavalry strikes without baggage trains, relentless severance of imperial supply convoys, night ambushes, and strategic retreats into impenetrable hill strongholds.

His standing army was professionally salaried in cash and organized strictly along decimal units (*Havaldar* [25] → *Jumladar* [125] → *Ek-Hazari* [1,000] → *Panch-Hazari* [5,000] → *Sar-i-Naubat*). Cavalry divided into two clear classes: elite royal horsemen funded, mounted, and equipped directly by the state (***Bargirs***), supplemented by contracted troopers supplying their own horses and gear (***Silahdars***).

Central to *Swarajya's* survival was a distributed network of roughly **240–300 hill, forest, and marine forts** (*"Forts are the very essence of the kingdom,"* notes the *Ajnapatra* attributed to Ramachandra Pant Amatya). To prevent any single commandant from selling a strategic fort to Mughal besiegers, Shivaji split every fort's authority across a **tri-partite check-and-balance drawn from three distinct social communities**:

1. ***Havaldar*** (typically Maratha): Held military command and physical custody of the fortress keys.
2. ***Sabnis*** (Brahmin): Maintained muster rolls, troop pay, and civil correspondence.
3. ***Karkhanis*** (Prabhu/Kayastha): Controlled granaries, water cisterns, gunpowder magazines, and ordnance supplies.

Uniquely among 17th-century Indian land empires, Shivaji grasped **maritime sovereignty**. Confronting the Abyssinian **Sidis of Janjira** (Mughal maritime allies), the Portuguese at Goa/Bassein, and the English at Bombay, he constructed a coastal navy of agile, shallow-draft gunboats (***Gurabs***) and swift row-galleys (***Galbats***) built in Kalyan, Bhivandi, and Malvan shipyards under admirals **Maynak Bhandari** and **Daulat Khan**. In the early 18th century, legendary Maratha Grand Admiral (*Sarkhel*) **Kanhoji Angre (1669–1729)**—operating from **Kolaba (Alibag)**, **Suvarnadurg**, and **Vijaydurg**—enforced the Maratha maritime passport (***Dastak***) system across the Konkan littoral, repulsing joint English–Portuguese naval assaults and dying undefeated at sea.

### UNESCO Heritage Anchor - Maratha Military Landscapes of India (44th WHS, July 2025)

Inscribed on **July 11, 2025**, at the 47th Session of the UNESCO World Heritage Committee in Paris under Criteria (iv) and (vi), the **Maratha Military Landscapes of India** became India's **44th UNESCO World Heritage Site**. Spanning the late 17th to early 19th centuries, this serial nomination recognizes **12 extraordinary fortifications** demonstrating terrain-adaptive military engineering across five distinct ecological zones—comprising **11 forts in Maharashtra and 1 fort in Tamil Nadu**:

- **Hill Forts (5 — Maharashtra):** **Salher** (site of the 1672 open-field Maratha victory over Mughals), **Shivneri** (Shivaji's birthplace), **Lohagad** (controlling the Bhor Ghat trade corridor), **Raigad** (coronation capital), and **Rajgad** (primary capital for 26 years).
- **Hill-Forest Fort (1 — Maharashtra):** **Pratapgad** (dense Sahyadri valley stronghold guarding the Konkan-Deccan passes).
- **Hill-Plateau Fort (1 — Maharashtra):** **Panhala** (commanding the southern Kolhapur trade routes).
- **Coastal Fort (1 — Maharashtra):** **Vijaydurg** (triple-walled maritime dockyard and naval headquarters with an underwater submerged stone wall thwarting deep-draft European ships).
- **Island Forts (3 — Maharashtra):** **Khanderi** (controlling Mumbai harbor approaches), **Suvarnadurg** (naval shipbuilding hub), and **Sindhudurg** (constructed on rocky Kurte Island off Malvan using molten-lead-reinforced basalt foundations and camouflaged zigzag gateways).
- **Southern Outpost Hill Fort (1 — Tamil Nadu):** **Gingee Fort (*Senji*)** (Villupuram district, Tamil Nadu)—fortified by Shivaji during his 1677–78 Karnataka expedition and celebrated as **"the Troy of the East"** after sheltering Chhatrapati Rajaram against an eight-year Mughal siege (1689–98).

## 2. The Maratha Fiscal-Institutional Grid

### Treaty of Purandar (1665) and the Raigad Coronation (1674)

Alarmed by the sack of Surat, Aurangzeb dispatched his ablest Rajput statesman-general, **Mirza Raja Jai Singh of Amber**, who methodically besieged Purandar Fort and forced the **Treaty of Purandar (June 1665)**. Shivaji ceded **23 of his 35 forts** (yielding 4 lakh *huns* annually), retained **12 forts** (1 lakh *huns*), accepted a 5,000 *mansab* for his young son **Sambhaji**, and agreed to aid imperial campaigns against Bijapur. Persuaded by Jai Singh to visit Agra (May 1666), Shivaji protested being placed among junior 5,000 *mansabdars*, suffered house arrest under Ram Singh, and executed a celebrated escape back to the Deccan—reclaiming nearly all ceded forts by 1670–74.

To elevate his movement from a rebel *zamindari* into legally recognized Kshatriya sovereignty (*Chhatrapati*), Shivaji orchestrated a grand Vedic coronation (*Rajyabhisheka*) at **Raigad Fort in June 1674**, officiated by renowned Varanasi scholar **Gaga Bhatta (*Vishveshvara Bhatta*)** (followed in September 1674 by a Tantric consecration performed by **Nischal Puri Goswami**). Assuming the titles ***Chhatrapati***, ***Haindava Dharmoddharaka***, and ***Kshatriya Kulavatansa***, Shivaji inaugurated the *Rajyabhisheka Shaka* calendar, struck gold ***Hon (Shivarai)*** and copper coins, and commissioned the ***Rajyavyavahara Kosha***—a Sanskrit–Marathi administrative dictionary replacing Persian bureaucratic terminology with indigenous equivalents. In **1677–78**, he mounted his final strategic masterstroke—the **Karnataka Expedition**—capturing **Gingee and Vellore** to build a deep southern fallback shield ahead of anticipated Mughal offensives.

### The *Ashtapradhan* Council and Administrative Standardization

Shivaji centralized executive oversight around an eight-member advisory council called the ***Ashtapradhan***, directly modeled on prior Deccan Sultanate ministerial offices (such as Ahmadnagar's *Peshwa* and *Dabir*) before being Sanskritized. Crucially, each minister reported **individually to the *Chhatrapati*** (never functioning as a collective cabinet), served on **non-hereditary cash salaries** (no *jagirs* under Shivaji), and—except the judicial and religious heads (*Nyayadhish* and *Panditrao*)—was expected to lead military expeditions in the field:

1. ***Peshwa / Mukhya Pradhan*** — Prime Minister overseeing general administration, civil coordination, and affixing the royal seal.
2. ***Amatya / Mazumdar*** — Finance Minister and Accountant-General auditing all public receipts and expenditure.
3. ***Waqia-navis / Mantri*** — Head of intelligence, royal household security, and daily court chronicles.
4. ***Sachiv / Shuru-navis*** — Royal Secretary in charge of state correspondence, auditing *pargana* ledgers, and verifying edicts drafted in the swift cursive ***Modi script***.
5. ***Sumant / Dabir*** — Foreign Minister managing diplomatic intelligence and external envoys.
6. ***Sar-i-Naubat / Senapati*** — Commander-in-Chief responsible for military recruitment, discipline, and campaign deployment.
7. ***Panditrao / Danadhyaksha*** — Minister for religious affairs, charitable grants, and moral supervision.
8. ***Nyayadhish*** — Chief Justice administering civil and criminal law per classical *Shastras* and customary norms.

### Annaji Datto's *Kathi* Settlement, *Watandar* Containment and *Chauth–Sardeshmukhi*

Territorially, *Swarajya* was partitioned into provinces (***Prants***, later consolidated under three regional viceroys), districts (***Tarafs***), and villages (***Mauzas***), staffed by salaried transferable officials (*Subahdars*, *Kamavisdars*, *Karkuns*).

Building directly upon **Malik Ambar's** Deccani *ryotwari* survey model, revenue minister **Annaji Datto** carried out a comprehensive cadastral land settlement in **1679**. Land was measured precisely using a standardized rod called the ***Kathi*** (5 cubits and 5 closed fists; 20 square *kathis* = 1 *bigha*; 120 *bighas* = 1 *chavar*). State land tax was fixed initially at 30% and consolidated at **40% of actual produce** after abolishing arbitrary feudal cesses—payable in cash or kind, supported by interest-free *tagai* recovery loans for bullocks and seed.

Politically, Shivaji's boldest domestic reform was **breaking the entrenched autonomy of hereditary rural *Watandars* (*Mirasdars*)**—the powerful ***Deshmukhs***, ***Deshpandes***, ***Patils***, and ***Kulkarnis*** who had long acted as petty rural tyrants under the Deccan Sultanates. Shivaji prohibited *Deshmukhs* from erecting fortified towers, stripped them of unilateral revenue collection, reduced them to fixed customary dues under imperial *karkun* supervision, and paid his own officers strictly in cash rather than hereditary land grants (*Mokasa / Saranjam*).

Because rugged Sahyadri agriculture could never independently finance prolonged anti-Mughal warfare, Shivaji supplemented domestic *Swarajya* taxes with two distinct **external tributary levies** exacted exclusively from **non-*Swarajya* (Mughal and Bijapuri) territories**:

- ***Chauth* (25% — One-Fourth of Standard Revenue):** Extracted from neighboring territories as a protection levy insulating them from Maratha military raids. Historians remain divided on its character: **M.G. Ranade** equated *Chauth* with a proto-Subsidiary Alliance buying defense against third-party invaders, whereas **Jadunath Sarkar** critiqued it as war-finance tribute exacting cash without assuming regular civil governance.
- ***Sardeshmukhi* (10% — Additional One-Tenth Levy):** Demanded by Shivaji on a **legal-hereditary claim**—asserting his status as the supreme *Sardeshmukh* (overlord headman) of all Maharashtra, giving the Marathas a durable constitutional foothold inside Mughal Deccan provinces.

## 3. Peshwa Ascendancy, the Maratha Confederacy and Panipat III (1713–1761)

### From War of Independence to the Hereditary *Peshwaship*

Shivaji's death in 1680 plunged *Swarajya* into a grueling **27-year War of Independence (1680–1707)** against Aurangzeb's full imperial host. Following **Chhatrapati Sambhaji's** valiant defiance and brutal execution (**1689**), his half-brother **Rajaram (1689–1700)** shifted resistance southward to **Gingee Fort**. Facing empty treasuries and cut-off supply lines, Rajaram reversed Shivaji's cash-salary principle—reviving hereditary ***Saranjam / Mokasa*** land assignments to incentivize autonomous Maratha generals (*Santaji Ghorpade*, *Dhanaji Jadhav*) to raise their own armies. After Rajaram's death, his formidable widow **Maharani Tarabai (1700–07)** directed relentless mobile offensives into Malwa and Gujarat, exhausting Aurangzeb's empire.

Aurangzeb's successor Bahadur Shah I released Sambhaji's son **Chhatrapati Shahu (1707)** to divide Maratha ranks, sparking a dynastic split settled between the **Satara branch (Shahu)** and **Kolhapur branch (Tarabai/Sambhaji II)** (formalized by the 1731 Treaty of Warna). To consolidate Shahu's precarious authority, a sharp Chitpavan Brahmin revenue specialist rose to become *Peshwa*—transforming the office into a hereditary executive dynasty centered at **Pune**:

- **Balaji Vishwanath (1713–1720):** Architect of the *Peshwa* state. In **1719**, he negotiated a landmark treaty with Mughal kingmaker **Sayyid Husain Ali Khan** (ratified by Emperor Muhammad Shah), winning imperial *sanads* granting Shahu full *Swarajya* recognition plus legal rights to collect ***Chauth* and *Sardeshmukhi* across the six Mughal *Subas* of the Deccan** in exchange for 15,000 Maratha horsemen. To bind restless warlords to Shahu, he institutionalized the **partition of *Chauth***—allocating 25% (*Babti*) to the king, **66% (*Mokasa*) to autonomous Maratha *Sardars*** for troop upkeep, 6% (*Sahotra*) to the *Pant Sachiv*, and 3% (*Nadgaunda*) to royal discretion—cementing the feudal *Saranjam* architecture.
- **Baji Rao I (1720–1740):** Arguably 18th-century India's finest cavalry tactician, Baji Rao pivoted Maratha ambition decisively northward (*"Let us strike at the trunk of the withering tree, and the branches will fall of themselves"*), framing expansion through *Hindu-Pad-Padshahi* alongside pragmatic alliance politics. Outmaneuvering Nizam-ul-Mulk without a single major pitch-battle defeat at **Palkhed (1728)** (studied centuries later by Field Marshal Montgomery as a classic of strategic mobility) and **Bhopal (1737)**, he wrested Malwa, Gujarat, and Bundelkhand (aiding Chhatrasal Bundela), raided the gates of Delhi (1737), and seized **Salsette and Bassein (1739)** from the Portuguese (under brother Chimaji Appa). Under his leadership, Maratha military expansion crystallized into the **Maratha Confederacy**—five semi-autonomous hereditary houses anchored on regional *Saranjam* domains: **Peshwas (Pune)**, **Gaekwads (Baroda/Gujarat)**, **Scindias/Shindes (Gwalior/Malwa)**, **Holkars (Indore/Malwa)**, and **Bhonsles (Nagpur/Berar)**.
- **Balaji Baji Rao / Nana Saheb (1740–1761):** Following Shahu's death (1749) and the **Sangola Agreement (1750)**, constitutional power transferred openly to the *Peshwa* at Pune, reducing the *Chhatrapati* at Satara to a ceremonial suzerain. Maratha power hit its territorial apex: **Raghuji Bhonsle** forced Bengal's Alivardi Khan to cede **Odisha (1751)** and pay *Chauth*; the **1752 Ahmadiya Treaty** contracted the Marathas as formal defenders of the Mughal throne against Afghan raids; and by **1758**, Raghunath Rao raised Maratha banners at **Lahore and Attock on the Indus**.

### The Third Battle of Panipat (January 14, 1761)

Expelling Afghan ruler **Ahmad Shah Abdali's** son Timur Shah from Punjab in 1758 triggered an existential reckoning. Returning across the Indus, Abdali forged an Indo-Afghan coalition with Rohilla chief **Najib-ud-Daulah** and **Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh**. By contrast, aggressive Maratha *Chauth* extractions had alienated every potential northern ally—the **Rajputs**, **Jats (Suraj Mal of Bharatpur)**, and **Sikhs** stood aloof.

Compounding diplomatic isolation was a fatal doctrinal blunder. Abandoning Shivaji and Baji Rao's lightweight *Ganimi Kava*, commander **Sadashivrao Bhau** and heir **Vishwasrao** marched north encumbered by tens of thousands of pilgrims and non-combatants, banking on heavy European-trained infantry and artillery under **Ibrahim Khan Gardi**. Trapped inside an entrenched camp at **Panipat** after Abdali severed their Yamuna supply lines, the starving Maratha army sallied forth on **January 14, 1761**—fighting heroically until Abdali's fresh reserve camel-swivel guns (*zamburaks*) shattered their depleted ranks.

Both Vishwasrao and Sadashivrao Bhau perished alongside tens of thousands of soldiers; Nana Saheb died brokenhearted months later. While **Peshwa Madhavrao I (1761–72)** and **Mahadji Scindia** engineered a remarkable northern resurgence within a decade, Panipat III shattered the illusion of centralized *Peshwa* supremacy, accelerated confederacy infighting, and handed the **British East India Company**—fresh from Plassey (1757)—an unobstructed decade to entrench British rule over Bengal.

## 4. The Ahom Kingdom (1228–1826) - Hydraulic Agrarianism, *Paik* Statecraft and *Moidams*

### Tai-Shan Genesis, Wet-Rice Reclamation and Cultural Assimilation

While western India forged a monetized fortress confederacy, Assam witnessed one of South Asia's longest-lived indigenous dynasties. In **1228 CE**, Tai-Shan prince **Chaolung Sukapha** crossed the rugged **Patkai Hills** from Mong Mao (upper Myanmar–Yunnan frontier) into the Upper Brahmaputra Valley, establishing his first permanent royal seat at **Charaideo (1253)** (later capitals shifted to Garhgaon, Rangpur/Sivasagar, and Jorhat). Commanding the valley unbroken for nearly **600 years (1228–1826)** until internal rebellions, Burmese invasions, and the **Treaty of Yandabo (1826)** ushered in British annexation, the Ahoms gave the region both political continuity and its modern name (*Assam*).

The material engine of Ahom state-building was **intensive wet-rice (*Sali*) hydraulic reclamation**. Unlike existing shifting-cultivation (*jhum*) communities along the foothills, the Ahoms imported advanced Tai valley-drainage engineering—constructing extensive earthen flood embankments (***mathauris***), irrigation channels, and iron-tipped plough agriculture to convert dense, waterlogged Brahmaputra marshes into hyper-productive rice bowls.

Rather than ruling as an insular foreign military caste, the Ahoms practiced pragmatic **demographic and cultural assimilation**. Incorporating local Tibeto-Burman groups (Morans, Borahis, Chutias, Kacharis) and old *Bhuyan* landlords into state cadres, they gradually adopted the **Assamese language** and integrated indigenous Tai ancestor worship (*Phuralung*, *Me-Dam-Me-Phi*) with royal **Shakta–Shaiva Hinduism** (from 16th-century Suhungmung *Dihingia Raja* through Pratap Singha and Rudra Singha, patronizing the Kamakhya temple while preserving core Tai coronation rituals).

Politically, the monarch (***Swargadeo*** — "Lord of Heaven") operated within an aristocratic constitutional check: the hereditary **Council of Great Ministers (***Patra Mantris***)** comprising the ***Burhagohain***, ***Borgohain***, and (added by Suhungmung) ***Borpatragohain***, who collectively held authority to select or even depose an unfit sovereign. To counterbalance these hereditary clans as the empire expanded westward, **Pratap Singha (1603–41)** created two powerful non-hereditary imperial viceroyalties: the ***Borbarua*** (chief judicial-executive officer east of Kaliabor) and the ***Borphukan*** (western viceroy based at Guwahati, overseeing defense against the Mughals).

### The Non-Monetized *Paik* and *Khel* Mobilization Grid

Because Upper Assam remained lightly monetized compared to the silver-flush Gangetic plains, the Ahom state solved military logistics and public infrastructure through an extraordinary **non-monetized corvée labor system**—systematized in the early 17th century by minister **Momai Tamuli Borbarua** under King Pratap Singha:

- **Universal Registration & Rotational *Gots*:** Every able-bodied adult male (ages 15–50) outside the nobility, priesthood, and bonded serfs was registered as a ***Paik***. Four *paiks* (tightened to three during peak Mughal wars) formed an interdependent cooperative squad called a ***Got***.
- **Zero-Disruption Labor Exchange:** Each *Got* owed the state **one *paik's* uninterrupted year-round service**—meaning each individual served **3 to 4 months per year** in rotation (building flood dykes, clearing forests, excavating royal tanks, forging boats/artillery, or fighting on campaign) while his fellow *Got* members cultivated his fields during his absence.
- **Land-for-Service Social Contract:** In return for corvée duty, every *paik* received **2 *puras* (~2.66 acres) of prime tax-free wet-rice land (***Ga-mati***)** (state-owned and non-hereditary, reverting upon death/disability) alongside hereditary personal homestead plots (***Bari-mati***).
- **Functional *Khels* & Decimal Command:** *Paiks* were deliberately grouped across ethnic lines into occupational guilds called ***Khels*** (boatmen, archers, cannon-founders, silk-weavers, gold-washers) to dissolve tribal separatism and streamline mobilization. Every *Khel* ran along a tight numerical hierarchy: ***Bora*** (20 *paiks*) → ***Saikia*** (100) → ***Hazarika*** (1,000) → ***Phukan*** (6,000).
- **Internal Stratification & Vulnerability:** Regular soldier-cultivators formed the ***Kanri Paiks***, whereas skilled artisans and literate clerks (***Chamua Paiks***) could commute physical service into cash tax, and nobles held dependent attendants (***Liksous***). Though phenomenally resilient against external invasion, the system fractured in the late 18th century when royal oppression and tax/corvée flight into egalitarian Neo-Vaishnavite **Moamoria *Sattras*** ignited the devastating **Moamoria Rebellion (1769–1805)**.

### Riverine Warfare - The Battle of Saraighat (1671) and *Buranji* Chronicles

Between 1615 and 1682, the Ahom Kingdom repelled **seventeen imperial Mughal invasions**—making Assam the sole eastern frontier that permanently halted Mughal territorial expansion. Even after Aurangzeb's formidable Bengal viceroy **Mir Jumla** occupied Garhgaon (**1662**) and extracted the punishing **Treaty of Ghilajharighat (1663)**, monsoon inundation, guerrilla ambush, and disease forced Mir Jumla's fatal retreat.

Determined to avenge the humiliation, **Swargadeo Chakradhwaj Singha** rebuilt arsenals, appointed **Lachit Borphukan** as supreme commander, and recaptured Guwahati (1667). When Aurangzeb dispatched veteran Rajput general **Ram Singh I of Amber** with a vast imperial cavalry and artillery host, **Lachit Borphukan** and strategic mastermind Prime Minister **Atan Burhagohain** refused open-field battle on dry plains.

Instead, they weaponized Assam's monsoon hydrography—throwing up formidable interlocking earthen ramparts (*garhs*) around Guwahati's narrow **Saraighat** gorge (where Ahom lore recalls Lachit beheading his own negligent maternal uncle with the immortal vow, *"My uncle is not greater than my country"* / *Deshotkoi momai dangor nohoi*). Forced onto the river, the Mughal fleet entered the decisive **Battle of Saraighat (March 1671)**. Rising from a debilitating high fever aboard his war-boat (*bachari nao*), Lachit rallied retreating Assamese sailors into a converging three-pronged naval counterattack—shattering the imperial armada, forcing Ram Singh's withdrawal, and fixing the **Manas River** as the permanent Mughal–Ahom boundary (sealed finally at the **1682 Battle of Itakhuli**).

Complementing this statecraft was South Asia's finest secular indigenous historiographical tradition: the ***Buranjis*** (a Tai-Ahom term meaning *"a store of knowledge that teaches the ignorant"*). Inscribed on cured ***Sanchi-pat*** (agarwood bark, *Aquilaria agallocha*) initially in the **Tai-Ahom script/language** and from the 16th century onward in crisp, unembellished **Assamese prose**, *Buranjis* recorded dated diplomatic exchanges, military reverses, administrative censuses, and candid judgments of kings without mythical exaggeration—extending even to external intelligence digests (*Padshah Buranji*, *Tripura Buranji*).

### Sivasagar Brick Masonry and the Moidams of Charaideo (43rd UNESCO WHS, 2024)

During their 18th-century cultural zenith at **Rangpur (modern Sivasagar)** under **Rudra Singha (1696–1714)**, **Siba Singha**, **Pramatta Singha (1744–51)**, and **Rajeswar Singha (1751–69)**, Ahom builders perfected distinctive flat-brick masonry bonded with ultra-durable indigenous organic mortar (***Karal*** — mixing sticky *bora* rice, duck eggs, lime, snail shells, jaggery, and pulses), blending Mughal arches and Bengal curved *chala* roofs with Tai-Assamese spatial plans:

- ***Rang Ghar* (Sivasagar, 1744–51 — Pramatta Singha):** Iconic two-storey elliptical brick royal pavilion crowned by an inverted boat-shaped roof with twin crocodile (*makara*) motifs; used by *Swargadeos* to spectate Bihu festivals and buffalo fights (*moh juj*), widely celebrated as Asia's oldest surviving masonry amphitheatre.
- ***Talatal Ghar* (Rangpur/Joysagar, Sivasagar):** The largest surviving Ahom monument, standing beside the Dikhow River. Initiated in timber by **Rudra Singha** (c. 1698–1707) and rebuilt in permanent brick masonry by **Rajeswar Singha (1751–69)** under Cooch Behar architect **Ghanashyam**, this palatial army base featured open terraced ceremonial decks, vaulted subterranean levels, and legendary secret escape tunnels connecting to the Dikhow River and the four-storey royal palace ***Kareng Ghar*** at Garhgaon.
- **Sacred Hydraulic Tanks & *Dols*:** Colossal excavated tanks (*Joysagar*, *Sivasagar*, *Gaurisagar*) framed towering brick temples including the ***Sivadol*** (the tallest Shiva temple in Northeast India), *Vishnudol*, and *Devidol*.

Crowns of Ahom cultural heritage stand at **Charaideo**, inscribed on **July 26, 2024** (46th WHC Session, New Delhi) as **India's 43rd UNESCO World Heritage Site** and **Northeast India's first cultural World Heritage Site**: ***Moidams – the Mound-Burial System of the Ahom Dynasty*** (Criteria iii & iv). Nestled against the Patkai foothills, over **90 royal tumuli (*Moidams* — Tai *Phrang-Mai-Dam*, "home of the spirit")** span 600 years of Tai-Ahom royal necropolis tradition. Each *Moidam* conceals a vaulted subterranean brick, stone, or timber burial chamber (*maidam-garbha*) beneath a lush hemispherical earthen mound capped by an open shrine (*chow-chali*) and ringed by an octagonal boundary wall (*garh*) with a western gateway—enshrining royal deceased (prior to Hindu cremation adoption under Rajeswar Singha) alongside ceremonial regalia, weapons, ivory, and ancestor rituals (*Me-Dam-Me-Phi*).

## 5. Maratha vs. Ahom Institutional Matrix

| Analytical Dimension | Maratha *Swarajya* & Confederacy (1630–1761) | Ahom *Paik* Kingdom (1228–1826) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ecological & Terrain Spine** | Basaltic Western Ghats (*Sahyadris*), narrow hill passes (*ghats*) & Konkan coast | Alluvial Upper Brahmaputra floodplains, riverine islands & Patkai foothills |
| **Sovereign Legitimation** | Vedic-Kshatriya consecration (*Chhatrapati*, 1674 Raigad) + *Varkari/Dharakari* Marathi solidarity | *Swargadeo* ("Lord of Heaven") fusing Tai ancestor cosmology (*Phuralung*) with royal Shakta–Shaiva Hinduism |
| **Central Executive Check** | ***Ashtapradhan*** (8 salaried non-hereditary ministers under Shivaji) → evolved into hereditary *Peshwa* & Confederacy | Hereditary ***Patra Mantris*** (*Burhagohain, Borgohain, Borpatragohain*) balanced by non-hereditary *Borbarua* & *Borphukan* |
| **Fiscal & Resource Engine** | **Monetized:** Direct *Kathi* cash/kind assessment (40%) inside *Swarajya* + external ***Chauth* (25%) & *Sardeshmukhi* (10%)** | **Non-Monetized Corvée:** Universal rotational ***Paik*** labor (*Got* of 4/3 men) exchanged for 2 *puras* tax-free wet-rice ***Ga-mati*** |
| **Local Intermediary Control** | Shivaji curbed hereditary *Watandars* (*Deshmukhs/Patils*) via salaried *Karkuns*; later weakened by *Saranjam* revival | Functional ***Khels*** cut across tribal/clan lines under *Bora–Saikia–Hazarika–Phukan* decimal hierarchy |
| **Military & Naval Doctrine** | ***Ganimi Kava*** light cavalry (*Bargirs/Silahdars*), *Mavali* infantry, 3-officer fort triad & blue-water/coastal Navy (**Kanhoji Angre**) | Riverine choke-point defense (**Battle of Saraighat, 1671**, *bachari nao* war-boats), earthen *garhs* & mass *Paik* mobilization |
| **Archival & Linguistic Record** | Cursive ***Modi script*** revenue records; *Rajyavyavahara Kosha* Sanskrit–Marathi administrative lexicon; *Bakhars* | Empirical prose ***Buranji*** chronicles inscribed on ***Sanchi-pat*** (agarwood bark) in Tai-Ahom & Assamese |
| **UNESCO World Heritage Anchor** | **Maratha Military Landscapes of India** (**44th WHS, July 2025**: 11 forts in Maharashtra + **Gingee Fort** in Tamil Nadu) | **Moidams of Charaideo** (**43rd WHS, July 2024**: 90+ royal earthen mound burials — NE India's first cultural WHS) |

---

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **Gingee Fort inside the 2025 UNESCO Listing:** The **Maratha Military Landscapes of India (44th WHS, July 2025)** includes **12 forts**, NOT all inside Maharashtra—**11 are in Maharashtra** (Salher, Shivneri, Lohagad, Khanderi, Raigad, Rajgad, Pratapgad, Suvarnadurg, Panhala, Vijaydurg, Sindhudurg) and **1 is in Tamil Nadu (Gingee Fort)**. Note also that Torna, Purandar, and Sinhagad are **NOT** among the 12 inscribed forts!
> - **Charaideo Moidams Function & Year (Live 2026 UPSC Trap):** Inscribed in **July 2024** (43rd WHS, NOT 2025), **Moidams** were **royal and noble mound-burial necropolises** ("home of the spirit")—NOT army fortresses, battle drill centers, or common soldiers' graves.
> - ***Chauth* & *Sardeshmukhi* Jurisdiction Trap:** Neither *Chauth* (25%) nor *Sardeshmukhi* (10%) was a domestic land tax inside Shivaji's *Swarajya* (which paid ~40% *Kathi* land revenue); both were levied on **non-*Swarajya* (Mughal/Deccan) territories**.
> - **Origin of *Ashtapradhan* & *Peshwa* Titles:** The office of ***Peshwa*** was **NOT** a Maratha invention—it originated in the **Deccan Sultanates** (e.g., Malik Ambar served as *Peshwa* of Ahmadnagar). Similarly, Shivaji's *Ashtapradhan* was **neither hereditary nor a collective cabinet** under Shivaji—hereditary *Peshwaship* emerged only under Shahu and Balaji Vishwanath.
> - ***Bargirs* vs. *Silahdars*:** ***Bargirs*** were regular cavalry mounted, armed, and salaried directly by the state; ***Silahdars*** were contracted horsemen who brought their own horses and arms.
> - **Confederacy Seat Inversion:** **Scindias (Shindes) = Gwalior**, **Holkars = Indore**, **Gaekwads = Baroda (Gujarat)**, **Bhonsles = Nagpur**, **Peshwas = Pune**. (UPSC frequently swaps Gaekwads to Gwalior or Holkars to Nagpur as an elimination trap.)
> - ***Rang Ghar* vs. *Talatal Ghar* Patrons:** The permanent brick ***Rang Ghar*** amphitheatre was built by **Pramatta Singha (1744–51)**; the permanent brick ***Talatal Ghar*** was constructed by **Rajeswar Singha (1751–69)** over an earlier timber base started by **Rudra Singha**.
> - **Battle of Saraighat (1671) Commander:** Lachit Borphukan defeated **Ram Singh I of Amber** (son of Mirza Raja Jai Singh), **NOT** Mir Jumla (Mir Jumla invaded earlier in 1662–63 and died on the return march).

---

> [!tip] Whole-UPSC Spillover
> - **GS1 & GS3 (Ecology-Driven State Capacity & Disaster-Resilient Engineering):** Both polities weaponized local geography rather than fighting it—Maratha basalt hill-island forts engineered rainwater cisterns and triple-walled coastal breakwaters (Vijaydurg), while Ahom wet-rice reclamation and earthen ***mathauri*** embankments tamed Brahmaputra floods. This provides high-altitude indigenous context for GS3 answers on **climate-adaptive infrastructure and river-basin management**.
> - **GS2 (Institutional Design, Decentralization & Checks-and-Balances):** Shivaji's **three-caste fort command triad** (*Havaldar–Sabnis–Karkhanis*) and Ahom **cross-ethnic *Khels*** + *Patra Mantri* council checks illustrate sophisticated indigenous mechanisms for preventing institutional capture and ethnic separatism. Conversely, the slide from Shivaji's cash-salaried bureaucracy to hereditary ***Saranjam*** warlordism warns how short-term fiscal expediency can permanently erode central state authority.
> - **GS3 Internal Security & Defense Strategy (Maritime & Asymmetric Doctrine):** Shivaji and **Kanhoji Angre's** early recognition of coastal-littoral denial (controlling chokepoints via island forts and agile *gurabs*) alongside **Lachit Borphukan's** riverine asymmetric defense at Saraighat remain foundational references in modern Indian Navy maritime doctrine and territorial defense thinking.
> - **Essay & GS1 Heritage Diplomacy:** The sequential UNESCO recognition of **Charaideo Moidams (2024)** and **Maratha Military Landscapes (2025)** reframes India's cultural geography beyond the Indo-Gangetic Mughal–Sultanate corridor—anchoring essays on **regional pluralism, Northeast integration, and decentralized civilizational resilience**.

> - **Maratha Military Landscapes Circuit (12 UNESCO Forts — North to South):** **Salher** (Nashik, northernmost Sahyadri peak) → **Shivneri** (Junnar, Pune) → **Lohagad** (Lonavala/Bhor Ghat pass) → **Khanderi Island** (off Alibag/Mumbai harbor) → **Raigad** & **Rajgad** (Sahyadri heartland core) → **Pratapgad** (Satara hills) → **Suvarnadurg Island** (Harnai coast) → **Panhala** (Kolhapur plateau) → **Vijaydurg** (Sindhudurg coast) → **Sindhudurg Island** (Malvan) → leaping southeast across the Deccan to **Gingee Fort** (Villupuram, Tamil Nadu).

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Serial UNESCO Fort Enumeration Trap:** Sneaking iconic non-listed Maratha forts (Sinhagad, Torna, Purandar) into the 12-fort **Maratha Military Landscapes (2025)** list, or claiming all 12 lie within Maharashtra (forgetting **Gingee Fort in Tamil Nadu**).
- **Monetized vs. Non-Monetized Fiscal Inversion:** Conflating the Ahom ***Paik*** system (rotational corvée labor rewarded with tax-free *Ga-mati*) with cash land taxation or feudal serfdom, or misclassifying Maratha ***Chauth/Sardeshmukhi*** as internal *Swarajya* land taxes rather than external tributary levies.
- **Institutional Attribution Swaps:** Crediting Shivaji with inventing the office of *Peshwa* (Deccan Sultanate origin), calling the *Ashtapradhan* hereditary under Shivaji, or confusing **Rajaram's** revival of *Saranjam* with Shivaji's cash-salaried *Bargir* model.
- **Ahom Chronology & Monument Mix-Ups:** Swapping **Mir Jumla (1662–63)** with **Ram Singh I (Saraighat, 1671)**, or misattributing **Rang Ghar** (Pramatta Singha) and **Talatal Ghar** (Rajeswar Singha brick masonry over Rudra Singha's timber origin).
