# Chapter 7 - Tribal Societies (Adivasis) — Continuum Debates, Displacement & Indigenous Assertions

The Indian Constitution conspicuously avoids defining the term "tribe." While **Article 366(25)** defines **Scheduled Tribes (STs)** strictly by procedural reference to Presidential notification under **Article 342**, administrative practice continues to rely on the five non-statutory **Lokur Committee (1965)** indicators: primitive traits, distinctive culture, geographical isolation, shyness of contact with the community at large, and backwardness.

Sociologists—most forcefully **André Béteille** and **Virginius Xaxa**—have long challenged this colonial-evolutionary checklist. Unlike Australia or the Americas, India’s tribal populations were never hermetically sealed from agrarian civilizations; they interacted across millennia with kingdoms, markets, and caste societies while retaining distinct linguistic, territorial, and political identities.

Treating India’s Adivasis as a uniform "pre-modern" stratum collapses radically divergent social worlds into a single administrative box. Understanding tribal sociology requires mapping their geographical and institutional diversity, interrogating the classical assimilation-versus-isolation debates, and analyzing how contemporary extractivism, internal class stratification, and indigenous movements are reshaping tribal citizenship.

## Geographical and Institutional Mosaic of Scheduled Tribes

As per **Census 2011**, India’s Scheduled Tribe population stood at **10.43 crore** (**10,42,81,034** in the original Census 2011 release and the Xaxa Committee Report 2014; updated to **10.45 crore** / **10,45,45,716** in revised ORGI/MoTA Primary Census Abstract tables following post-2011 notifications—both constituting **8.6%** of the total population), characterized by a predominantly rural residence (**~90% rural**) and a comparatively egalitarian sex ratio of **990 females per 1,000 males** (well above the national average of 943). Maintaining dual-standard parity on official tallies is essential: while Census 2011 and the Ministry of Tribal Affairs (MoTA) Year-End Review (January 2025) anchor the baseline at **over 705 distinct ST groups** across 30 States and Union Territories, post-2022–2024 parliamentary amendments under Article 342 pushed the notified tally to **over 730 Scheduled Tribes** (MoTA Year-End Review, January 2024). Five jurisdictions—**Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Chandigarh, and Puducherry**—have no notified Scheduled Tribes.

### Regional Ecological and Structural Zones

Following **B.S. Guha**, **L.P. Vidyarthi**, and **Virginius Xaxa**, India’s tribal mosaic divides into three structurally distinct socio-geographical regions whose historical trajectories bear little resemblance to one another:

- **The Central Indian Heartland (~80%+ of ST Population):** Stretching across Madhya Pradesh (highest absolute ST population), Maharashtra, Odisha (highest number of notified communities at 62), Rajasthan, Gujarat, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and parts of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and West Bengal. Home to India’s largest tribes—the **Bhils** (~1.7 crore) and **Gonds** (~1.3 crore)—alongside **Santhals, Oraons, Mundas, Hos, Kharias, Baigas, and Kondhs**. Governed primarily under the **Fifth Schedule**, these communities inhabit India’s richest forest and mineral belts, placing them at the epicenter of caste-society contact, land alienation, agrarian distress, and industrial displacement.
- **The Northeast Frontier Polities (~12% of ST Population):** Covering Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, Mizoram (highest ST share among states at 94.4%), Nagaland, Manipur, Tripura, Sikkim, and Assam. Encompassing **Nagas, Mizos, Khasis, Garos, Apatanis, Bodos, Karbis, and Kuki-Zo** communities. Crucially, unlike Central India, most Northeastern tribes **constitute demographic majorities in their own states**, enjoy robust constitutional self-governance under the **Sixth Schedule** and **Articles 371A/371G**, remained historically outside the Hindu *varna–jati* hierarchy, and display substantially higher literacy and human development indicators alongside acute ethnic-territorial boundary politics.
- **Southern Pockets and Island Tribes:** Small, ecologically specialized enclaves across the Western and Eastern Ghats (**Todas, Kotas, Irulas, Kurumbas, Chenchus, Soligas, Paniyas**) and the **Andaman & Nicobar Islands** (**Great Andamanese, Onge, Jarawa, Sentinelese, Shompen**, and **Nicobarese**) plus **Lakshadweep** (highest ST share overall at 94.8%). The Andamanese hunter-gatherers represent extreme demographic fragility, with groups like the Sentinelese maintaining deliberate isolation.

### Customary Institutions — Tenure, Dormitories and Cosmology

Classical tribal societies did not merely lag behind caste society economically; they operated on qualitatively different institutional grammars rooted in **Karl Polanyi’s** principles of **reciprocity and redistribution** rather than individualized market accumulation.

- **Communal Land Tenure and Self-Governance:** Land was historically held as lineage or village commons rather than alienable private real estate. Examples include the Munda ***Khuntkatti*** (descendants of original forest clearers holding collective tenure), Oraon ***Bhuinhari***, Khasi ***Ri-Raid*** (communal land allocated for use without absolute sale rights), and shifting cultivation (*Jhum* / *Podu* / *Bewar*) mediated through cooperative labor exchange. Customary dispute resolution functioned via decentralized councils such as the ***Manki–Munda*** (Ho), ***Parha*** (Oraon), ***Manjhi–Pargana*** (Santhal), and Northeastern village ***Durbars*** or ***Nokmas***.
- **Youth Dormitories (Socialization Institutions):** Across Central and Northeastern India, youth dormitories operated as autonomous institutions of civic training, gender socialization, craft transmission, cooperative labor mobilization, and cultural memory. **Verrier Elwin’s** classic ethnography *The Muria and their Ghotul* (1947) documented the bi-sexual Muria Gond ***Ghotul*** of Bastar as an egalitarian space fostering premarital companionship and discipline without puritanical shame. Other major forms included the Oraon monosexual ***Dhumkuria*** (*Jonkherpa* for boys, *Pelerpa* for girls), the Naga ***Morung*** (a fortified guardhouse and artistic academy), the Juang ***Majang***, and the Adi ***Moshup***. Formal residential schooling, missionary and Hindu-reformist moral policing, and cash-wage out-migration have largely eroded these institutions.
- **Cosmology and the Nature–Man–Spirit Complex:** Rather than transcendent scriptural orthodoxy, indigenous tribal religion was anchored in **animism, totemism, and ancestor veneration**. In *The Maler* (1963), anthropologist **L.P. Vidyarthi** conceptualized this worldview as the ***Nature–Man–Spirit Complex***—an indivisible ecological and moral triad linking forest landscapes (*Sarna* or *Jaherthan* sacred groves in Chotanagpur; *Law Kyntang* in Meghalaya), human kinship clans, and protective spirits (*Bongas* / ancestors). Colonial census officers initially counted tribes as "Animists" (1881–1921) and "Tribal Religions" (1931–1941) before post-1951 enumerations dropped the independent column, pushing most non-Christian tribes into either "Hindu" or an undifferentiated "Others" bucket.

### 75 PVTGs and Denotified Tribes (DNTs) — Layered Vulnerability

Within and around the Scheduled Tribe fold exist two severely marginalized categories whose sociological predicaments defy generic tribal policy.

**Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs):** Recognizing extreme intra-tribal disparity, the **Dhebar Commission (1960–61)** recommended carving out a sub-category for the most isolated communities. The Government of India designated **52 Primitive Tribal Groups (PTGs)** in 1975, added **23 more in 1993** (bringing the total to **75 PVTGs** across **18 States and 1 Union Territory — Andaman & Nicobar Islands**), and renamed them **PVTGs in 2006**. Identification rests on four criteria: **pre-agricultural level of technology, stagnant or declining population, extremely low literacy, and subsistence-level economy**. Sociologically, PVTGs range from tiny, endangered island foragers (Sentinelese, Onge, Great Andamanese) and forest bands (Birhor, Korwa, Chenchu) to larger shifting cultivators (Maria Gond, Saura, Baiga, Dongria Kondh, Irular, Konda Reddi)—all acutely vulnerable to ecological shock and predatory market entry.

**Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Tribes (DNTs/NTs/SNTs):** Under the colonial **Criminal Tribes Act, 1871**, the British state biologized mobile pastoralists, bards, salt-traders, acrobats, and forest-fringe communities (such as **Pardhis, Sansis, Kanjars, Bawarias, Banjaras, and Lodhas**) as "hereditary criminals" born to crime—subjecting entire families to forced settlements and biometric surveillance. Although independent India repealed the Act in **1952** (following the Ayyangar Committee), state governments promptly enacted **Habitual Offenders Acts**, keeping policing stigma, custodial profiling, and residential ostracism alive.

The **Renke Commission (2008)** estimated over 10.74 crore DNT/NT/SNT citizens, while the **Bhiku Ramji Idate Commission (2014–2018)** mapped **1,262 communities** across states and exposed a glaring constitutional blind spot: **269 DNT communities are excluded from SC, ST, and OBC lists altogether**. Lacking fixed addresses, ancestral land titles, or recognized caste certificates, DNTs suffer **documentary statelessness**—which the **Development and Welfare Board for Denotified, Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Communities (DWBDNC, constituted 2019)** and ongoing Anthropological Survey of India / NITI Aayog ethnographic verifications have only partially begun to redress.

## Classical Tribal Policy and Sociological Continuum Debates

From the 1930s through the 1960s, Indian sociology and statecraft waged an intense intellectual debate over a deceptively simple question: *Are tribes distinct autonomous societies needing protection, or merely unintegrated segments of Hindu civilization awaiting absorption?*

### Isolation, Assimilation and Integration — Elwin, Ghurye and Nehru

- **Verrier Elwin — Early "National Park" Thesis and Later Revision:** In *The Baiga* (1939), missionary-turned-anthropologist **Verrier Elwin** witnessed Central Indian tribes stripped of land, enslaved by usurious *dikus* (outsiders/moneylenders), demoralized by colonial forest laws, and suffering a profound psychological **"loss of nerve."** He controversially proposed establishing a protective **"National Park"**—an administrative buffer shielding tribes from predatory commercial and missionary intrusion. Critics fiercely caricatured Elwin as wanting to preserve Adivasis as "museum specimens" in an anthropological zoo. Crucially, UPSC answers must register **Elwin’s own later revision**: working closely with Jawaharlal Nehru as Adviser for Tribal Affairs in NEFA (modern Arunachal Pradesh), Elwin explicitly disowned permanent isolation in *A Philosophy for NEFA* (1957), championing cautious, self-paced cultural integration instead.
- **G.S. Ghurye — The "Backward Hindus" Assimilationist Thesis:** Leading the nationalist rebuttal in *The Aborigines—'So-Called'—and Their Future* (1943; later republished as *The Scheduled Tribes*), **G.S. Ghurye** accused British administrators and Elwin of engineering a colonial "divide-and-rule" wedge via Excluded and Partially Excluded Areas. Ghurye argued that most Central Indian tribes shared linguistic, ritual, and agrarian histories with Hindu society and were best understood as **"Backward Hindus"** whose primary affliction was economic deprivation, not cultural difference. His prescription was rapid assimilation into the agrarian Hindu mainstream. Sociologically, Ghurye’s thesis drew sharp fire for erasing distinct non-Hindu tribal cosmologies (particularly in the Northeast and Chotanagpur) and ignoring **structural caste humiliation**: when tribes assimilate into Hindu society, they rarely enter as equals—they are slotted near the bottom of the *jati* ladder.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru’s *Tribal Panchsheel* (1958):** Seeking a middle course between museumizing isolation and coercive assimilation, Nehru articulated five foundational principles of **Integration** in his Foreword to Elwin’s *A Philosophy for NEFA*:
  1. People should develop along the lines of their own genius without external imposition.
  2. Tribal rights in land and forests should be respected.
  3. Tribal teams should be trained for administration and development, avoiding excessive influx of outsiders.
  4. Tribal areas should not be over-administered or overwhelmed with a multiplicity of schemes.
  5. Results should be judged not by statistics or money spent, but by the quality of human character evolved.
  
  While philosophically enlightened, Nehruvian *Panchsheel* collided head-on with Nehruvian heavy industrialization: throughout the 1950s–1970s, mega-dams (Hirakud, Rihand, Bhakra), steel plants (Rourkela, Bhilai, Bokaro), and coal mines systematically hollowed out Principle 2 across Fifth Schedule India.

### N.K. Bose and Surajit Sinha — Absorption and Continuum Sociology

Moving beyond normative policy prescriptions, two pioneering Bengali anthropologists theorized the *actual empirical mechanics* through which tribes interacted with caste society.

In his seminal 1941 essay ***The Hindu Method of Tribal Absorption*** (grounded in fieldwork among the Juangs of Odisha), **Nirmal Kumar Bose** argued that tribes entered the Hindu fold not through forcible religious conversion, but through **asymmetric economic gravity**. Plough-based caste agriculture generated far higher, stabler surpluses than tribal foraging or swidden cultivation. Drawn into the regional economy for survival, tribes accepted subordination within the *varna–jati* division of labor as low-ranking occupational specialists (basket-makers, rope-twisters, agricultural laborers). The Hindu social order granted them marginal economic security and allowed them to retain minor gods and customs—at the steep price of surrendering internal egalitarianism and political autonomy.

**Surajit Sinha** (*Tribal Cultures of Peninsular India*, 1959; *Tribe–Caste and Tribe–Peasant Continua in Central India*, 1965), studying the **Bhumij** of Barabhum (West Bengal/Jharkhand) and **Raj Gonds**, refined Bose by dismantling the binary wall between "tribe" and "caste." Sinha mapped Indian communities along an empirical **Tribe–Caste / Tribe–Peasant Continuum** across four axes—ecological isolation, demographic scale, social stratification, and ideological worldview:

- At the **isolated tribal pole** stood small, kin-based, egalitarian foraging bands (e.g., Birhor, Hill Maria).
- Along the **peasantized middle** stood settled agricultural tribes (Santhal, Munda, Oraon) integrated into local markets yet fiercely guarding autonomous ethnic solidarity.
- At the **caste-state pole**, tribal elites who acquired surplus agrarian land underwent **Rajputization / Kshatriyaization**—patronizing Brahmin priests, fabricating royal genealogies, and forming medieval tribal chiefdoms and states (Bhumij, Gond, Chero, Malla kingdoms).

### Virginius Xaxa’s Modern Paradigm Shift

Contemporary sociologist **Virginius Xaxa** (*Tribes as Indigenous People of India*, 1999; *State, Society, and Tribes*, 2008) exposed the hidden evolutionary bias shared by both Ghurye’s assimilationism and careless readings of the continuum model. Tribes, Xaxa demonstrated, are not merely "backward castes" awaiting inevitable absorption into Hindu society. Even when Central Indian tribes adopt settled plough cultivation, modern education, or Christianity/Hinduism, they do **not** melt into caste society—they retain **distinct collective ethnic identity, territorial consciousness, and linguistic solidarity**. Treating "tribe" strictly as a society with its own historical integrity—rather than a developmental deficit—laid the intellectual blueprint for India’s twenty-first-century rights-based tribal discourse.

## Virginius Xaxa Committee, Internal Stratification and Development-Induced Displacement

Constituted by the Prime Minister’s Office in 2013 and submitting its landmark report in May 2014, the **High-Level Committee on Socio-Economic, Health and Educational Status of Tribal Communities of India**—chaired by **Prof. Virginius Xaxa**—performed the most rigorous state-commissioned audit of post-Independence tribal policy since the Dhebar Commission.

### Key Diagnostic Findings of the Xaxa Committee (2014)

Examining five interlocking domains—livelihood, education, health, involuntary displacement, and legal-constitutional governance—the Xaxa Committee concluded that tribal marginalization is not a product of "cultural backwardness" or "geographical remoteness," but of **systemic institutional failure and asymmetric resource extraction**:

- **Disproportionate Displacement Burden:** Drawing on longitudinal displacement studies (including Walter Fernandes), Xaxa highlighted that while Scheduled Tribes constitute **8.6%** of India’s population, they account for **over 40% of all persons displaced by post-Independence development projects** (dams, mines, steel plants, thermal stations, and sanctuaries)—with fewer than one-third receiving meaningful rehabilitation. Roughly **one in four tribal citizens** has been directly displaced or project-affected at least once.
- **Erosion of Protective Shields:** Governors routinely failed to exercise their discretionary Fifth Schedule powers to block harmful parliamentary/state statutes or file substantive annual reports; Tribal Advisory Councils remained politically subordinated; and **Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP)** funds were routinely diverted toward general infrastructure rather than targeted tribal human development.
- **Multidimensional Human Development Deficits:** Severe institutional gaps in tribal health—disproportionate malaria burden, tuberculosis, sickle-cell anemia, stunting, and elevated ST Infant Mortality (**44.4 per 1,000** vs. national average of ~35 in NFHS-5)—were compounded by culturally alienating school curricula delivered in non-tribal state languages, driving steep secondary dropout rates.
- **Right to Say "No":** Crucially, Xaxa recommended curbing the colonial doctrine of **Eminent Domain**, recognizing *Gram Sabhas’* statutory **right to refuse land acquisition**, restoring illegally alienated tribal land, and extending Fifth Schedule coverage to sizeable tribal blocks currently excluded in West Bengal, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Goa.

### Internal Class Stratification and Elite Capture Within Tribes

Tribal societies today are no longer the egalitarian kin-communities of nineteenth-century ethnography. Seven decades of affirmative action, modern higher education, electoral democracy, commercial agriculture, and state contracting have produced pronounced **intra-tribal class differentiation**:

- **Emergence of a Tribal Middle Class and Political Elite:** Communities with early access to missionary schooling, administrative proximity, or irrigated agriculture—such as **Meenas** in Rajasthan, **Mizos, Khasis, and Nagas** in the Northeast, and urbanized sections of **Oraons, Mundas, Santhals, and Gonds**—have forged a substantial bureaucratic, professional, and business stratum.
- **Privatization of Communal Land:** Even within Sixth Schedule states where non-tribal land purchase is constitutionally barred, traditional communal tenure is quietly concentrating in indigenous elite hands. In Meghalaya, for instance, communal ***Ri-Raid*** lands have increasingly been converted into privately titled ***Ri-Kynti*** holdings controlled by wealthy tribal politicians, coal barons, and urban contractors—creating a novel class of **landless tribals inside tribal-majority states**.
- **The Sub-Classification and Creamy-Layer Fault Line:** The Supreme Court’s landmark 7-Judge Constitution Bench verdict in ***State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (August 2024)** constitutionally upheld states’ power to sub-classify Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to target backward sub-groups, with four concurring judges explicitly flagging the desirability of identifying a **creamy layer** within SC/ST quotas. While Parliament and the Union Government have **not** enacted a creamy layer for STs as of November 2026, the sociological tension is acute: urbanized, third-generation ST bureaucrats capture disproportionate shares of reserved seats, while **75 PVTGs** and remote forest-dwelling tribes remain largely absent from higher education and Class-I services.

### Development-Induced Displacement, Customary Land Alienation and Fortress Conservation

India’s tribal heartland embodies the classic **resource-curse paradox**: over 80% of India’s coal, iron ore, bauxite, copper, and hydropower reserves sit directly beneath Central Indian Fifth Schedule forests (Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Madhya Pradesh).

Sociologist **Michael Cernea’s Impoverishment Risks and Reconstruction (IRR) model** identifies eight interlocking risks triggered by **Development-Induced Displacement (DID)**: *landlessness, joblessness, homelessness, marginalization, food insecurity, increased morbidity, loss of access to common property resources, and social disarticulation*. Cernea’s framework explains why **monetary cash compensation structurally fails Adivasis**. Cash payouts compensate only individually titled arable plots—erasing unrecorded forest commons (fuelwood, mahua, tendu, bamboo, medicinal herbs, grazing pastures), collective labor reciprocity, and sacred ancestral geography (*jal, jangal, zameen*). Lacking financial literacy or industrial skills, displaced tribes rapidly exhaust cash awards and join **Jan Breman’s** footloose urban proletariat as brick-kiln and construction migrants.

Alongside mining (Hasdeo Arand coal blocks in Chhattisgarh, Niyamgiri bauxite struggles in Odisha) and mega-infrastructure (such as the ongoing **Great Nicobar** strategic development debates involving Shompen and Nicobarese ecological vulnerability, where the NGT declined to halt clearances in 2026 citing strategic importance), tribes face a second displacement vector: **Fortress Conservation and "Green Grabbing."** Colonial forestry laws (Indian Forest Act, 1927) and exclusionary protected-area management historically branded indigenous forest dwellers as "encroachers" rather than ecological stewards.

This tension flared sharply between **June 2024 and 2025**: after the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) directed states to expedite the relocation of roughly **591 villages (~64,801 families)** inside notified tiger-reserve core zones, tribal organizations protested widespread coercive evictions bypassing legal consent. Responding directly, the Ministry of Tribal Affairs issued a formal **January 2025 directive** affirming that no eviction may precede settlement of forest rights and written *Gram Sabha* consent, followed by an October–November 2025 policy framework (*Reconciling Conservation and Community Rights*) explicitly advocating a transition from exclusionary **fortress conservation** to community-centered human–wildlife coexistence.

Sociologically, statutes such as **PESA (1996)** and the **Forest Rights Act (FRA, 2006)** represent attempts to **decolonize forest governance** by relocating legitimate authority from the forest bureaucracy to the village *Gram Sabha*—replacing top-down state trusteeship with democratic indigenous stewardship. Conversely, targeted infrastructure saturation drives like **PM-JANMAN** and the **Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (launched October 2024)** seek to close material deprivation gaps among PVTGs and tribal villages, though sociologists caution that welfare delivery without secured land/forest tenure risks reproducing bureaucratic paternalism. For detailed statutory clauses, individual vs. community forest title mechanisms, and welfare-delivery architectures, see [[10. Marginalized Groups, Social Defense & Correctional Admin#Tribal Welfare FRA 2006 and PM-JANMAN|Tribal Welfare, FRA 2006 & PM-JANMAN]].

## Contemporary Indigenous Assertions - Sarna Code, Pathalgadi and Identity Politics

Rather than passively accepting marginalization or cultural absorption, contemporary Adivasi movements deploy constitutional instruments, archival memory, and symbolic mobilization to assert political and religious autonomy.

### The Sarna Religious Code Demand and Census 2027 Politics

Across Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Bihar, and Assam, Adivasi communities—principally **Santhals, Oraons, Mundas, Hos, and Kharias**—have waged a sustained campaign demanding an independent seventh religious column (**"Sarna"**, also articulated as *Adi Dharam* or *Gondi* in neighboring regions) in the national Census. Derived from the Mundari word for sacred *sal*-tree groves (*Sarna Sthal* / *Jaherthan*), Sarnaism centers on non-idolatrous nature reverence (*Dharti Aayo*, *Singbonga*, *Dharmes*), ancestor veneration, and egalitarian rituals devoid of the *varna–jati* hierarchy.

- **Empirical & Political Momentum:** Even without a dedicated code, **~49.57 lakh (~5 million) respondents** across 21 states voluntarily wrote "Sarna" under "Other Religions and Persuasions" in **Census 2011** (83.3% in Jharkhand, ~8.1% each in Odisha and West Bengal)—outnumbering officially enumerated Jains nationwide. The Jharkhand Legislative Assembly passed a formal resolution supporting the Sarna Code in **November 2020**, and Chief Minister Hemant Soren again wrote to the Prime Minister and President in **May 2026** pressing for its inclusion ahead of Census 2027. The National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST) has likewise backed independent recognition.
- **The November 16–18, 2026 Census 2027 Portal Controversy (287 Drop-Down $\rightarrow$ 76 Predictive-Text Entries):** When India’s first digital **Census 2027** self-enumeration portal went live on **16 November 2026** (opening in UP, Uttarakhand, Goa, and Punjab before pan-India expansion in January 2027), the portal initially displayed **287 entries as a drop-down menu** under the "Others" religion field—including *Sarna*, alongside sects and denominations of the six major religions such as *ISKCON*, *Sanatan*, *Shia*, *Sunni*, *Pentecostal*, and *Ramakrishna Paramhans*. Within 48 hours (**18 November 2026**), following widespread controversy, the Union government and Registrar General & Census Commissioner (RG&CCI) removed the drop-down, deleted over 200 sect/denomination entries, and replaced it with **76 predictive-text suggestions**—consisting primarily of Scheduled Tribe faiths recorded in the 2011 Census (*Sarna, Donyi-Polo, Tana Bhagat, Birsa, Munda, Oraon, Santal*) plus **five new tribal entries** (*Adibasi Dharma, Bura Deo, Karupa, Tingkao Rangong, and Tingwang Hingde*)—while clarifying that only six major religions plus "Others" remain official categories. The episode vividly illustrated the classic sociological **"design effect"** through which state census menus actively manufacture, merge, or erase minority identities.
- **Sociological Fault Line:** Sociologically, the Sarna movement executes a **double refusal**—resisting both Hinduization (*Ghar Wapsi* / Ghurye’s "Backward Hindu" framing) and Christian missionary conversion narratives, while asserting that tribal identity is anchored in ecological distinctiveness rather than mere socio-economic backwardness. Counter-mobilizations—including demands by some Hindu-aligned tribal organizations to strip ST benefits from converted Christian tribals (delisting campaigns)—underscore how religion has become a sharp political cleavage inside Adivasi society.

### The Pathalgadi Movement — Constitutional Counter-Inscription

Emerging prominently during **2017–2018** across Khunti, West Singhbhum, Gumla, and Simdega districts of Jharkhand (with ripples in Chhattisgarh and Odisha), the **Pathalgadi movement** demonstrated an extraordinary fusion of ancient custom and modern constitutionalism.

Historically, *Pathalgadi* (literally "erecting a stone") is a sacred Munda and Ho megalithic tradition used to mark ancestral burial sites (*Sasan Diri*), genealogical land boundaries, and village founding deeds. Triggered by proposed state amendments to dilute Jharkhand’s protective colonial-era tenancy shields—the **Chotanagpur Tenancy (CNT) Act, 1908** and **Santhal Pargana Tenancy (SPT) Act, 1949**—which barred tribal-to-non-tribal land transfers, Adivasi activists repurposed the ancient monolith into **constitutional counter-inscription**.

Massive green-painted stone slabs erected at village entrances bore verbatim quotes from the **Fifth Schedule**, **Article 244(1)**, **PESA 1996**, and the Supreme Court’s **Samatha (1997)** and **Niyamgiri (2013)** rulings—declaring the *Gram Sabha* the supreme sovereign over village territory, prohibiting non-tribal outsiders (*dikus*) from acquiring land or minerals without consent, and asserting indigenous self-rule.

Sociologically, Pathalgadi revealed a **dual trajectory**:

1. **Subaltern Constitutional Literacy:** Mainstream Pathalgadi mobilization reclaimed the Indian Constitution itself as a weapon of indigenous defense—moving tribal resistance beyond extralegal insurgency toward public constitutional invocation.
2. **Fringe Radicalization and State Coercion:** A splinter faction (*Adivasi Mahasabha* / *Adivasi Kutumb Parivar* elements) radicalized the doctrine—rejecting Aadhaar, voter IDs, parliamentary elections, and state schools as "unconstitutional" in Scheduled Areas, prompting massive police crackdowns and sedition charges against thousands of villagers (many subsequently withdrawn after Jharkhand’s 2019 regime change). Even after its street peak subsided, Pathalgadi permanently shifted rural Jharkhand’s normative baseline toward assertive *Gram Sabha* territorialism.

### ST-List Contestation and Ethnic Boundary Politics

Finally, because **Scheduled Tribe status unlocks territorial protection, legislative seats, and affirmative action**, state classification itself has become a high-stakes trigger of contemporary ethnic mobilization. Nowhere is this sharper than in the Northeast—vividly illustrated by the prolonged **2023–2026 Manipur ethnic conflict**, catalyzed by disputes over extending ST status to the demographically and politically dominant valley **Meitei** community against fierce resistance from hill **Kuki-Zo and Naga** tribes fearing erosion of hill-land protection and job quotas, alongside long-pending ST-inclusion demands by six major communities in Assam (Ahom, Koch-Rajbongshi, Moran, Matak, Chutia, and Tea Tribes). In twenty-first-century India, "tribe" is simultaneously an ancestral ecological lifeworld, a constitutional shield, and a fiercely contested arena of resource politics.

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature
- **Constitutional Silence vs. Lokur Criteria:** The Constitution of India **does NOT specify criteria** for identifying Scheduled Tribes (Articles 342 and 366(25) are purely procedural). The five textbook traits (primitive traits, distinctive culture, geographical isolation, shyness of contact, backwardness) derive strictly from the non-statutory **Lokur Committee (1965)**.
- **PVTG Tally, Origin & Nodal Ministry Traps:** There are **75 PVTGs** (not 95) across **18 States and 1 UT (Andaman & Nicobar Islands)**, rooted in the **Dhebar Commission (1960–61)** (originally named PTGs in 1975; renamed PVTGs in 2006). The nodal ministry for FRA 2006 is the **Ministry of Tribal Affairs (MoTA)**—never MoEFCC, even inside tiger reserves.
- **ST Count Dual-Standard & Zero-ST Jurisdictions:** Keep both **10.43 crore** (`10,42,81,034`, original Census 2011 / Xaxa Report) and **10.45 crore** (`10,45,45,716`, updated ORGI/MoTA PCA) anchored for ST population (**8.6%**), alongside **705** (Census 2011 baseline) and **over 730** (post-2022–24 Article 342 amendments) for notified ST groups, and remember the five jurisdictions with **no notified STs**: **Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Chandigarh, and Puducherry**.
- **Fifth vs. Sixth Schedule & Section 10(26) Income-Tax Exemption Trap (Prelims 2025 & 2026):** The Tribal Areas of **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram** are governed under the **Sixth Schedule** (Article 244(2)), NOT the Fifth Schedule; inside Fifth Schedule Areas (Article 244(1)), the executive power of the State **continues to extend** subject to the Governor’s special powers. Furthermore, under **Section 10(26) of the Income Tax Act, 1961**, members of Scheduled Tribes residing in specified Sixth Schedule areas, Northeastern states, and Ladakh are statutorily **exempt from paying Income Tax** on income accruing/arising within those areas or from dividends/interest.
- **DNTs Are Not Automatically Scheduled Tribes:** Despite carrying the colonial word "Tribes" in *Denotified Tribes*, DNTs are **not** uniformly classified as STs; they are scattered across SC, ST, and OBC lists—and **269 DNT communities** mapped by the Idate Commission remain outside all reserved categories.
