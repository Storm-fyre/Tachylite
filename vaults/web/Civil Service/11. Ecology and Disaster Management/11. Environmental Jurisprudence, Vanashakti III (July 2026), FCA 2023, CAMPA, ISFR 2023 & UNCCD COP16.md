# Chapter 11 - Environmental Jurisprudence, Vanashakti III (July 2026), FCA 2023, CAMPA, ISFR 2023 & UNCCD COP16

Indian environmental law is unusual among major economies: its doctrinal backbone was forged as much in Supreme Court courtrooms as in parliamentary chambers. Over four decades, constitutional courts read ecological duties into Article 21, invented **Absolute Liability**, defined "forest" by dictionary meaning, and repeatedly stepped in to referee the sharpest structural tension of Indian governance—accelerating infrastructure while upholding the **Precautionary Principle**.

Between 2023 and late 2026, that equilibrium underwent a sweeping statutory and judicial recalibration: decriminalization under the **Jan Vishwas Act**, border exemptions under the **FCA (Amendment) Act, 2023**, the Supreme Court's landmark **July 29, 2026 *Vanashakti III* verdict** on retrospective Environmental Clearances, the **February 2026 NGT Great Nicobar ruling**, **ISFR 2023**, and the **UNCCD COP16 Riyadh–COP17 Ulaanbaatar** land-restoration arc. This chapter unpacks every statutory provision, judicial doctrine, and live 2026 development with exam-room precision.

## 1. Constitutional Ecology & Foundational Judicial Doctrines

### Constitutional Architecture & Article 21's Climate Expansion

The original 1950 Constitution contained no explicit environmental clause. Following the **1972 Stockholm Conference**, the **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act (1976)** embedded environmental stewardship across three constitutional planes:

- **Article 48A (Directive Principle of State Policy):** Mandates that the State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and safeguard the forests and wildlife of the country.
- **Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty):** Places an affirmative duty upon every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment—including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife—and to have compassion for living creatures.
- **Seventh Schedule Shift:** Transferred **"Forests"** (Entry 17A) and **"Protection of Wild Animals and Birds"** (Entry 17B) from the State List to the **Concurrent List**, creating the legislative foundation for the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980. Separately, **Article 253** (legislation to implement international treaties) enabled Parliament to enact nationwide umbrella statutes like the Air Act (1981) and **Environment (Protection) Act (1986)**.

Converting non-justiciable Directive Principles into enforceable rights, the Supreme Court expanded **Article 21 (Right to Life)**—beginning with *Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar* (1991) recognizing the right to pollution-free air and water. That arc reached a historic global milestone on **March 21, 2024**, when a 3-Judge Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud in ***M.K. Ranjitsinh & Ors. v. Union of India*** explicitly recognized a standalone **Fundamental Right to be Free from the Adverse Effects of Climate Change** anchored jointly in **Article 21** (life and health) and **Article 14** (equality—since climate disasters disproportionately harm marginalized communities). Notably, the Court invoked this new right to **balance** Great Indian Bustard habitat protection against India's clean-energy obligations, modifying an earlier blanket underground-powerline ban.

### Five Core Doctrines of Indian Environmental Jurisprudence

1. **Absolute Liability (*M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*, 1987 — Oleum Gas Leak Case):** Departing decisively from English common law's **Strict Liability** (*Rylands v. Fletcher*, 1868), Chief Justice P.N. Bhagwati ruled that an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity owes an **absolute, non-delegable duty** to the community. Unlike strict liability, **zero exceptions apply** (no defense of Act of God, third-party sabotage, statutory authority, or victim fault), and compensation scales proportionally with the **magnitude and financial capacity of the enterprise** to achieve real deterrence.
2. **Polluter Pays Principle (*Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. UoI*, 1996 — Bichhri Case & *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum*, 1996):** Establishes that liability for environmental harm extends beyond compensating individual victims to bearing the **full financial cost of restoring degraded ecosystems** (soil remediation, aquifer cleanup, ecological restitution)—internalizing negative externalities rather than socializing them onto taxpayers.
3. **Precautionary Principle (*Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. UoI*, 1996 & *A.P. Pollution Control Board v. Prof. M.V. Nayudu*, 1999):** Where threats of serious and irreversible ecological harm exist, **lack of full scientific certainty cannot excuse postponing preventive measures**. Crucially, Indian jurisprudence inverted the traditional burden of proof: the **onus of proof lies on the industrial proponent/developer** to demonstrate that its proposed activity is environmentally benign.
4. **Public Trust Doctrine (*M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath*, 1997 — Beas River / Span Motels Case):** Holds that vital natural resources—rivers, air, forests, seashores, and ecologically fragile lands—are held by the State strictly as a **fiduciary trustee** for unimpeded public use, *not* as private sovereign property alienable for commercial resort or real-estate exploitation.
5. **Intergenerational Equity & Sustainable Development (*State of Himachal Pradesh v. Ganesh Wood Products*, 1995):** Grounded in the 1987 Brundtland Commission and 1992 Rio Declaration, affirming that the present generation holds Earth's ecological capital in trust for unborn generations—requiring developmental planning to weigh carrying capacity before sanctioning irreversible resource extraction.

## 2. National Green Tribunal (NGT) Act, 2010

Enacted on **October 18, 2010** in consonance with India's Rio (1992) commitments and the **Article 21** right to a healthy environment, the **NGT Act, 2010** made India only the **third nation globally** (after Australia and New Zealand) and the **first developing country** to establish a dedicated statutory environmental tribunal.

### Composition, Procedure & *Suo Motu* Powers

- **Bench Architecture:** Comprises a full-time **Chairperson** (retired Supreme Court Judge or retired High Court Chief Justice), **10 to 20 full-time Judicial Members**, and **10 to 20 full-time Expert Members** (scientists/environmental administrators)—mandating equal-footing multidisciplinary adjudication. The **Principal Bench sits in New Delhi**, supported by four Zonal Benches in **Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, and Chennai**.
- **Procedural Flexibility & Speed:** NGT is **not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure (CPC), 1908** or strict rules of evidence; it operates under the **Principles of Natural Justice** and is statutorily required under **Section 20** to apply **Sustainable Development, Precautionary, and Polluter Pays principles**. It is mandated to finally dispose of applications or appeals within **6 months**. Appeals against NGT orders lie directly to the **Supreme Court within 90 days (Section 22)**, though High Courts retain constitutional writ jurisdiction under Articles 226/227 (*M.P. High Court Advocates Bar Association*, 2022).
- ***Suo Motu* Cognizance (*Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. Ankita Sinha*, October 7, 2021):** Ending years of jurisdictional dispute, a 3-Judge Supreme Court Bench declared the NGT a *sui generis* guardian of environmental rights vested with inherent ***suo motu* powers** to initiate proceedings on its own motion based on media reports, letters, or ecological emergencies—without waiting for an aggrieved petitioner.

### The Schedule I Jurisdictional Boundary (Golden Prelims Filter)

NGT exercises civil jurisdiction over "substantial questions relating to environment" arising **exclusively out of seven statutes listed in Schedule I**:

1. **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974**
2. **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977** *(repealed via the Taxation Laws Amendment Act, 2017 alongside GST, though retained in Schedule I text)*
3. **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980**
4. **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981**
5. **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**
6. **Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991**
7. **Biological Diversity Act, 2002**

**Three Major Exclusions:** NGT has **NO jurisdiction** under the **Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972**, the **Indian Forest Act, 1927**, or the **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 (FRA)** (nor over state-specific tree-protection statutes). Disputes solely under those laws must go before High Courts or civil courts.

## 3. EPA 1986, EIA 2006, PARIVESH 2.0 & *Vanashakti III* (July 29, 2026)

### Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 & Jan Vishwas Decriminalization

Enacted in the wake of the 1984 Bhopal Gas Tragedy under **Article 253**, the **EPA 1986** functions as India's overarching "umbrella legislation." Rather than detailing every technical standard in the parent text, **Sections 3, 5, and 6** confer sweeping delegated rulemaking authority on the Central Government—enabling it to set emission/discharge norms, issue binding closure/power-cut directions, constitute specialized authorities (such as the **Central Ground Water Authority [CGWA]**), and notify subsidiary regimes governing EIA, CRZ, Eco-Sensitive Zones, Wetlands, Solid/Plastic/E-Waste, and Green Credits.

Under the **Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023** (effective for EPA from **April 1, 2024**), Parliament decriminalized routine regulatory violations under the EPA 1986 (and Air Act 1981)—replacing criminal imprisonment with graded **monetary penalties (₹10,000 to ₹15 lakh, plus daily continuing fines)** adjudicated by senior executive **Adjudicating Officers** (Joint Secretary / Secretary rank), while retaining **imprisonment (up to 3 years)** specifically for *failure to pay assessed penalties*. Fines flow into a newly created **Environmental Protection Fund (Section 16A)** split **75% to States/UTs and 25% to the Centre** for environmental remediation. Policy critics warn that shifting adjudication from courts to executive officers dilutes judicial independence and risks reducing statutory penalties to a predictable "cost of doing business" for capital-rich violators.

### EIA Notification 2006 & PARIVESH 2.0

Issued under Section 3 of EPA 1986 (replacing the 1994 Notification), the **EIA Notification, 2006** mandates **Prior Environmental Clearance (EC)** before any construction or site preparation begins for listed projects. It bifurcates proposals into two tiers:

- **Category A (Central Appraisal):** High-impact projects (large thermal/nuclear plants, major mining, chemical complexes, ports) appraised directly by **MoEFCC** on the recommendation of sector-specific **Expert Appraisal Committees (EACs)**. They skip screening and require mandatory full EIA reports and Public Consultation.
- **Category B (State Appraisal):** Medium-scale projects appraised by the **State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA)** aided by the **SEAC**. After Screening, they divide into **Category B1** (requiring full EIA and Public Consultation) and **Category B2** (exempt from EIA reports and public hearings). Under the **General Condition**, any Category B project located within **5 km** (earlier 10 km) of a Protected Area (National Park/Sanctuary), Critically Polluted Area (CPCB), Eco-Sensitive Area, or inter-state/international border is automatically escalated to **Category A**.

The statutory clearance sequence comprises **four stages**: **Screening** (Category B only) → **Scoping** (setting Terms of Reference [ToR]) → **Public Consultation** (public hearing within 45 days conducted by SPCB + written objections, with exemptions for B2 and defense/strategic projects) → **Appraisal**. Operational processing has migrated to **PARIVESH 2.0**—MoEFCC's GIS-integrated single-window portal embedding a Decision Support System (overlaying wildlife corridors, protected forests, and CRZ boundaries) across Environmental, Forest, Wildlife, and CRZ clearances to compress approval timelines.

### The *Vanashakti* Ex-Post Facto EC Trilogy — Culminating in *Vanashakti III* (July 29, 2026)

For nearly a decade, executive attempts to regularize projects that started construction *without* Prior EC ignited fierce constitutional litigation. Two MoEFCC instruments stood at the centre of the storm: the **March 14, 2017 Statutory Notification** (creating a one-time, six-month window to appraise pre-existing violators) and the **July 7, 2021 Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) Office Memorandum (OM)** (creating an open-ended administrative pathway for ongoing and future violators).

The Supreme Court resolved this saga across three dramatic rulings in 2025–2026:

1. ***Vanashakti I* (May 16, 2025 — Justices Abhay S. Oka & Ujjal Bhuyan):** Struck down *both* the 2017 Notification and the 2021 OM as completely illegal under EPA 1986 and barred the Union Government from ever introducing future retrospective EC schemes.
2. ***Vanashakti II* Review Order (November 18, 2025 — CJI B.R. Gavai, Justices K. Vinod Chandran & Ujjal Bhuyan):** In a **2:1 split decision** (with Justice Bhuyan dissenting sharply), recalled *Vanashakti I*—noting that demolishing completed public infrastructure worth roughly **₹20,000 crore** (hospitals, airports, public housing) would cause irreversible public waste and that earlier coordinate-bench precedents (*Common Cause*, *Alembic Pharmaceuticals*, *Electrosteel*) had moulded relief rather than mandating blanket demolition. The petitions were restored for fresh adjudication.
3. ***Vanashakti III* — Final Binding Verdict (July 29, 2026 — `2026 INSC 761`, 3-Judge Bench of CJI Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi [author], and Justice Vipul M. Pancholi):** Delivered a landmark unanimous ruling balancing ecological law with **"eco-centric proportionality"**:
   - **Mandatory Prior EC Reaffirmed:** Held unambiguously that Prior EC under the EIA Notification, 2006 remains the inviolable rule; any unit commencing work without Prior EC is *void ab initio* and cannot ordinarily claim retrospective amnesty.
   - **July 7, 2021 Office Memorandum Quashed:** Struck down the 2021 SOP OM as illegal and *ultra vires*—holding that a mere executive administrative instruction cannot override delegated statutory legislation (EIA 2006) and unlawfully created a **"perpetual amnesty"** violating **Articles 14 and 21**.
   - **March 14, 2017 Statutory Notification Upheld:** Distinguished the 2017 Notification as valid, narrowly tailored delegated legislation under Section 3 of EPA 1986 addressing a closed class of past violators within a strict six-month window.
   - **Narrow Statutory Exception Guardrail:** Clarified that the Central Government retains strictly circumscribed power under **Section 3 of EPA 1986** to issue a fresh, time-bound **statutory Notification** (never an executive OM) *solely* in rare cases of **overriding/supervening public interest**—subject to rigorous EIA appraisal, deterrent environmental compensation, full ecological remediation, and zero immunity for violators.
   - **Operational Follow-Up (September 30, 2026):** MoEFCC instructed EACs and SEIAAs to resume processing *existing eligible pending applications* submitted prior to the cutoff, while strictly enforcing the Court's ban on fresh applications under both the closed 2017 Notification and the quashed 2021 OM.

## 4. Forest Law, CAMPA, ISFR 2023 & Global Land Degradation Regimes

### FCA 1980, *Godavarman* (1996), FCA (Amendment) Act 2023 & Great Nicobar Litigation

Enacted to arrest rapid deforestation, the **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 (FCA)** mandates **prior approval of the Central Government** before any State de-reserves forest land or diverts it for non-forest purposes. In the historic ***T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India* (December 12, 1996)** order, the Supreme Court held that "forest" under Section 2 of the FCA is not confined to formally notified Reserved or Protected Forests—it must be understood in its **broad dictionary meaning**, covering **all areas recorded as forest in any government record irrespective of ownership** (protecting millions of hectares of unclassed **"deemed forests"**).

**Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 2023 (FCA Amendment Act, 2023 — effective December 1, 2023):** Renamed the 1980 statute and introduced two deeply contested changes:

- **Section 1A Narrowing Scope:** Restricted FCA coverage solely to (a) lands notified as forest under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 or any other statute, and (b) lands recorded as forest in government records **on or after October 25, 1980** (exempting lands officially converted to non-forest use before December 12, 1996). Petitioners warned this stripped statutory protection from roughly **1.97 lakh sq km** of unrecorded deemed forests, community forests, and sacred groves.
- **Sweeping Statutory Exemptions:** Exempted forest land within **100 km of international borders, Line of Control (LoC), or Line of Actual Control (LAC)** for "strategic linear projects of national importance and concerning national security"—encompassing virtually the entire **Northeast India biodiversity hotspot**, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand. It further exempted up to **10 hectares** for security infrastructure, up to **5 hectares** in Left-Wing Extremism districts, 0.10 ha along rail/roads for access, and excluded **zoos, safaris, and eco-tourism facilities** from "non-forest purposes."

**Supreme Court Interim Order (*Ashok Kumar Sharma, IFS [Retd.] v. Union of India*, February 19, 2024):** While refusing to stay the 2023 Amendment outright pending final constitutional adjudication (still *sub judice* as of November 2026), a Bench led by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud directed all States and UTs to **continue strictly applying the 1996 *Godavarman* dictionary meaning** of "forest" until consolidated state forest records are compiled and published, and **barred establishing any new zoos or safaris inside forest areas** (outside Protected Areas) without prior Supreme Court permission. Extending this protective logic, in **December 2024** the Supreme Court explicitly classified Rajasthan's community sacred groves (**Orans**) as "forests" under *Godavarman*/FCA.

**Great Nicobar Island Mega-Infrastructure Litigation (Live 2026 Update):** The ₹72,000–₹92,000 crore holistic project—combining an International Container Transshipment Terminal at **Galathea Bay**, greenfield airport (updated ₹13,000 cr clearance in June 2026), township, and 450 MVA power plant—requires diverting **130.75 sq km (~14–15% of the island)** of pristine old-growth tropical rainforest (~9.64 lakh trees) inside the **Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve** (UNESCO MAB, 2013). Ecologists and anthropologists flagged threats to **Giant Leatherback Turtle** nesting sites, **Nicobar Megapode** habitats, **Shompen** (PVTG) and Nicobarese tribal autonomy, proximity to the seismically active **Sunda Subduction Zone** (which sank Indira Point in 2004), and ecologically non-equivalent Compensatory Afforestation sited thousands of kilometres away in Haryana and Madhya Pradesh.

On **February 16, 2026**, a 6-member Special Bench of the **NGT (Eastern Zone, Kolkata)** in ***Ashish Kothari v. MoEFCC*** **upheld the November 2022 Environmental Clearance**—citing the project's strategic national significance, findings of a High-Powered Committee, ZSI coral-translocation protocols, and retention of **82% of Great Nicobar** under protected forest/biosphere cover—while directing strict perpetual compliance with clearance conditions (leaving appellate remedies open under Section 22 of the NGT Act and parallel FRA/tribal litigation).

### CAMPA (CAF Act, 2016) & Net Present Value (NPV)

Born out of Supreme Court orders in *Godavarman* (2002) to stop states from misappropriating user-agency afforestation levies, Parliament enacted the **Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016** (Rules notified 2018) establishing non-lapsable, interest-bearing **National CA Fund (10% share)** and **State CA Funds (90% share)** within the Public Accounts of India and the States, overseen by **National and State CAMPA Authorities**.

When forest land is diverted under FCA, the user agency must deposit: (i) **Compensatory Afforestation (CA)** costs (normally over equivalent non-forest land, or double area of degraded forest land for central PSUs/exempted projects), and (ii) **Net Present Value (NPV)**—a monetary valuation of lost ecosystem services (carbon, watershed, biodiversity, pollination) stratified across 6 eco-classes and 3 canopy tiers (revised upwards ~53% by MoEFCC in January 2022 to **₹9.57–15.95 lakh/ha**). Positively, in **2026 National CAMPA approved ₹3,000 crore (2025–2030)** for targeted recovery of four Schedule I flagship species: **Ganges River Dolphin, Snow Leopard, Greater One-horned Rhinoceros, and Wild Water Buffalo**. Structurally, however, CAG audits and ecologists critique CAMPA for under-utilization, administrative diversions, monoculture plantations failing to replicate old-growth biodiversity, and tension with Gram Sabha consent under FRA 2006.

### India State of Forest Report (ISFR) 2023 — 18th Biennial Edition (Released Dec 21, 2024)

Published biennially since 1987 by the **Forest Survey of India (FSI, Dehradun)**, the **18th edition (ISFR 2023)** sets the authoritative baseline for Prelims 2027:

- **Total Forest & Tree Cover:** **8,27,357 sq km = 25.17%** of India's geographical area (up **1,445 sq km** vs ISFR 2021's 24.62%)—still trailing the **National Forest Policy (1988) goal of 33%**.
- **Forest Cover vs. Tree Cover Disaggregation:** **Forest Cover** = **7,15,343 sq km (21.76%)** (modest gain of **+156.41 sq km**); **Tree Cover** = **1,12,014 sq km (3.41%)** (surge of **+1,289 sq km**, driving **~89%** of the total net gain due partly to refined mapping of Trees Outside Forests and agroforestry).
- **Carbon Stock & Paris NDC Progress:** Total forest carbon stock reached **7,285.5 million tonnes** = **30.43 billion tonnes $\text{CO}_2\text{e}$**—meaning India has generated an **additional carbon sink of 2.29 billion tonnes $\text{CO}_2\text{e}$ since 2005**, tracking close to its 2.5–3.0 $\text{BtCO}_2\text{e}$ 2030 Paris NDC goal. **Soil Organic Carbon (55.06%)** constitutes the single largest carbon pool (ahead of Above Ground Biomass at 32.69%); state-wise, **Arunachal Pradesh (1,021 Mt)** holds the largest forest carbon stock, followed by **Madhya Pradesh (608 Mt)**, **Chhattisgarh (505 Mt)**, and **Maharashtra (465 Mt)**.
- **Mangroves & Bamboo:** **Mangrove cover** stood at **4,992 sq km (0.15% of GA)**—registering a **net decline of 7.43 sq km** vs 2021 (driven by losses in **Gujarat [$-36.39\text{ sq km}$]** and **Andaman & Nicobar Islands [$-4.65\text{ sq km}$]**, partially offset by gains led by **Andhra Pradesh [$+13.01\text{ sq km}$]** and **Maharashtra [$+12.39\text{ sq km}$]**). Bamboo-bearing area expanded by 5,227 sq km to **1,54,670 sq km**.
- **State Rankings & Regional Red Flags:**
  - *Largest Area — Forest Cover Alone vs. Combined (Forest + Tree):* **Forest Cover alone:** **Madhya Pradesh** (77,073 sq km) > **Arunachal Pradesh** (65,882 sq km) > **Chhattisgarh** (55,812 sq km). **Combined Forest + Tree Cover:** **Madhya Pradesh** (85,724 sq km) > **Arunachal Pradesh** (67,083 sq km) > **Maharashtra** (65,383 sq km).
  - *Highest Forest Cover (% of GA):* **Lakshadweep** (91.33%) > **Mizoram** (85.34%) > **Andaman & Nicobar Islands** (81.62%) *(19 States/UTs exceed 33%; 8 exceed 75%)*.
  - *Top Net Gainers — Combined (Forest + Tree) vs. Forest Cover Alone:* **Combined (Forest + Tree):** **Chhattisgarh (+684 sq km)**, Uttar Pradesh (+559), Odisha (+559), Rajasthan (+394). **Forest Cover Alone (excluding Tree Cover):** **Mizoram (+242 sq km)** > **Gujarat (+180 sq km)** > **Odisha (+152 sq km)**.
  - *Critical Losses:* **Madhya Pradesh ($-612\text{ sq km}$)**, Karnataka ($-459$), Ladakh ($-159$), Nagaland ($-125$), alongside continued net losses across **Northeast India ($-327.30\text{ sq km}$)** and the Western Ghats ESA.
- **Methodological Critique (Mains Differentiator):** FSI defines **"Forest Cover"** strictly by **canopy density $\ge 10\%$ over $\ge 1\text{ ha}$** on *any* land regardless of ownership or species—meaning commercial **teak, rubber, tea, coffee, and oil-palm monocultures** count equally alongside natural biodiversity-rich forests, masking internal degradation within Recorded Forest Areas (RFA).

### UNCCD (COP16 Riyadh → COP17 Ulaanbaatar), LDN 2030, Bonn Challenge & Aravalli Green Wall

- **UNCCD (Adopted 1994; in force 1996; 197 Parties; HQ: Bonn):** One of the three 1992 Rio Conventions and the **sole legally binding international agreement** linking environment and development directly to **sustainable land management**, operating through a bottom-up community-participatory framework across arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid regions. Globally, up to **40% of Earth's land** is already degraded.
- **Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN 2030 — SDG Target 15.3) & Bonn Challenge:** LDN aims to stabilize or enhance land productivity via an *"avoid > reduce > reverse"* hierarchy. Complementing the voluntary **Bonn Challenge** (launched 2011 by Germany & IUCN targeting 150 Mha by 2020 and **350 Mha by 2030**), India—where ~29.77% (97.85 Mha, ISRO SAC Atlas) of land faces degradation—upgraded its **COP14 New Delhi (2019)** pledge from 21 Mha to restoring **26 million hectares of degraded land by 2030**.
- **UNCCD COP16 Riyadh (December 2–13, 2024) → COP17 Ulaanbaatar (August 17–28, 2026):**
  - **COP16 Riyadh ("Our Land. Our Future."):** Launched the **$12.15 billion Riyadh Global Drought Resilience Partnership (RGDRP)** shifting 80 vulnerable nations from reactive drought relief to proactive resilience and advanced decisions on Sand and Dust Storms and pastoralists—but **failed to adopt a legally binding Global Drought Protocol**.
  - **COP17 Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia (August 17–28, 2026 — "Restoring Land. Restoring Hope."):** Convened on the rangelands frontier (54% of Earth's terrestrial surface); announced a **$1.3 billion restoration/drought finance portfolio** ($644.5 million new finance) plus a **$400 million Drought Resilience Investment Facility**, yet **again deferred a binding Drought Protocol to COP18** following US opposition. At COP17 (August 24, 2026), Union Minister Bhupender Yadav showcased India's Sovereign Green Bonds, revised 5-year/40%-canopy Green Credit Programme, and **India's First Guide to Grasslands and Other Open Natural Ecosystems (ONEs)**.
- **Aravalli Green Wall Project & the 2025–2026 SC "100-Metre" Definition Flashpoint:** Inspired by Africa's Great Green Wall, MoEFCC launched the **Aravalli Green Wall Project (March 2023; scaled in May 2025)** to restore a **5-km ecological buffer** across **4 States/UTs (Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana, Delhi)** covering **29 districts** (~1.15 Mha initial phase using native *Khejri, Dhok, Rohida*) to block eastward Thar desertification, recharge NCR aquifers, and trap dust. Crucially, environmental governance of the Aravallis faced a seismic legal turn in 2025–2026: on **November 20, 2025**, the Supreme Court initially accepted a uniform mining-regulation definition restricting "Aravalli Hills" to landforms rising **$\ge 100\text{ metres}$ above local relief** (which threatened to exclude over 90% of Rajasthan's low ridges—leaving only 1,048 of 12,081 hills protected). Taking *suo motu* cognizance, on **December 29, 2025**, the Supreme Court **stayed its own November 2025 order**, paused mining leases without SC approval, and constituted a **High-Powered Committee (May 2026)**—which in **September 2026** rejected any single elevation yardstick and proposed a holistic **"Aravalli Ecosystem Landscape"** framework integrating hydrology, biodiversity, and corridors.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions — Environmental Law, Forests & ISFR 2023
> - **NGT Schedule I Exclusions Trap:** NGT covers **Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980**, **EPA 1986**, **Air/Water Acts**, **PLIA 1991**, and **Biological Diversity Act, 2002**—it has **ZERO jurisdiction** over the **Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972**, **Indian Forest Act, 1927**, and **Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006**.
> - **EPA 1986 Public Participation Trap:** The parent **EPA 1986** empowers the Centre to lay down emission/discharge standards and constitute bodies like **CGWA**—it does **NOT** expressly mandate public participation in the Act's text (public hearings arise via the delegated **EIA Notification, 2006**).
> - ***Vanashakti III* (July 29, 2026) Binary Trap:** Do **not** fall for extreme statements claiming either "SC completely banned all future statutory regularization" OR "SC upheld the 2021 Office Memorandum." *Vanashakti III* **quashed the 2021 OM**, **upheld the closed 2017 Notification**, reaffirmed **Prior EC as the rule**, and restricted any exceptional future regularization strictly to a **Section 3 EPA statutory Notification** for overriding public interest.
> - **ISFR 2023 Metric Swaps:** Total Forest + Tree Cover = **25.17%** (**21.76% Forest** + **3.41% Tree**)—do NOT swap 21.76% with total cover. Separate **Combined (Forest + Tree)** rankings from **Forest Cover alone**: top gainers in *Combined* cover are **Chhattisgarh (+684), UP (+559), Odisha (+559), Rajasthan (+394)**, whereas top gainers in *Forest Cover alone* are **Mizoram (+242), Gujarat (+180), Odisha (+152)**; similarly, **Maharashtra** ranks #3 in *Combined* area while **Chhattisgarh** ranks #3 in *Forest Cover alone*. **Mangrove cover DECREASED** by 7.43 sq km (Gujarat $-36.39\text{ sq km}$, offset partially by **Andhra Pradesh $+13.01$** and **Maharashtra $+12.39$**). Top forest-cover *area* state is **Madhya Pradesh**, top *carbon stock* state is **Arunachal Pradesh (1,021 Mt)** (**Soil Organic Carbon = 55.06%** largest pool), and top *percentage* entity is **Lakshadweep (91.33%)** (and **Mizoram [85.34%]** among states).
> - **UNCCD Drought Protocol & CAMPA Traps:** Both **COP16 Riyadh (2024)** and **COP17 Ulaanbaatar (Aug 2026)** **failed** to adopt a legally binding Global Drought Protocol (deferred to COP18). Under the **CAF Act, 2016**, CAMPA operates at **both National (10%) and State (90%) levels**—and does **not** contain a mandatory statutory public-participation clause.

> [!info] Mains Arsenal — GS3 & GS2 Environmental Governance Architecture
> - **Eco-Centric Proportionality vs. Regulatory Capture (*Vanashakti III* & Jan Vishwas):** Pairing *Vanashakti III*'s **"eco-centric proportionality"** (distinguishing administrative OMs from parliamentary/delegated statutory notifications) with **Jan Vishwas (2023)** decriminalization exposes India's central governance dilemma: easing compliance for infrastructure must never normalize *fait accompli* violations or transfer deterrent adjudication away from independent tribunals to executive officers.
> - **Ecological Non-Equivalence in Forest Accounting (FCA 2023, CAMPA & ISFR 2023):** India's headline 25.17% green cover and 30.43 $\text{BtCO}_2\text{e}$ carbon sink obscure three structural fractures: (i) FSI's $\ge 10\%$ canopy metric conflates commercial plantations with biodiverse natural forests, (ii) Northeast India and natural hill forests continue shrinking while outside-forest tree cover drives gains, and (iii) spatial/ecological mismatch in **CAMPA** (e.g., felling endemic Nicobar rainforests while afforesting arid mainland zones) violates the scientific principle of **"like-for-like" ecological restoration**.
> - **Strategic Geography vs. Constitutional Ecology (100-km LAC Exemption & Great Nicobar):** Both the **FCA 2023 100-km border exemption** and the **Feb 2026 NGT Great Nicobar clearance** crystallize the tension between geopolitical security (Malacca Strait transshipment, Himalayan border roads) and irreplaceable biodiversity/tribal rights—demanding independent Strategic Environmental Assessments (SEAs), cumulative carrying-capacity audits, and enforceable Gram Sabha/tribal consent rather than project-by-project clearances.
> - **From Tree-Obsession to Landscape Ecology (Aravallis & UNCCD COP17):** India's **First Guide to Grasslands & Open Natural Ecosystems** unveiled at **UNCCD COP17 (Aug 2026)** and the SC Expert Panel's **Sept 2026 "Aravalli Ecosystem Landscape"** model mark an overdue scientific paradigm shift—recognizing that savannas, scrublands, and low-relief ridges under 100 m are vital aquifers and desertification barriers rather than "wastelands" awaiting mining or monoculture afforestation.

### 2. Prelims Trap Signature

- **Statutory Parentage & Tribunal Boundary Swaps:** UPSC consistently tests whether a body was created under **EPA 1986** (CGWA, GEAC, National Ganga Council, Green Credit Rules) vs. Water/Air/WPA statutes, and whether a law falls *inside* or *outside* **NGT Schedule I** (WPA 1972, IFA 1927, and FRA 2006 are outside!).
- **Delegated Legislation vs. Executive OM Ambush:** Post-*Vanashakti III* (July 29, 2026), expect sharp questions testing why a **Section 3 EPA Notification** can carry statutory force while an **Office Memorandum (OM)** cannot override the EIA Notification, 2006.
- **ISFR 2023 Metric & Trend Inversions:** Examiners trap rote readers by quoting **21.76%** as total forest-and-tree cover (actual: **25.17%**), swapping **Forest Cover alone** gainers (**Mizoram > Gujarat > Odisha**) with **Combined Forest + Tree** gainers (**Chhattisgarh > UP = Odisha > Rajasthan**), claiming mangrove cover *increased* (it **declined** by 7.43 sq km), or equating FSI "Forest Cover" strictly with legally notified Recorded Forest Area.
- **Judicial Status Distortion:** Treating stayed or *sub judice* measures as settled law—such as asserting the SC *permanently upheld* the 100-metre Aravalli elevation rule (**stayed Dec 29, 2025**) or *struck down* the FCA Amendment 2023 (**interim *Godavarman* directive issued Feb 2024; constitutional challenge pending**).
