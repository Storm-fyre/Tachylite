# Chapter 15 - The Contested Multipolar Order — US–China Rivalry, BRICS+ (India's 2026 Presidency) & Frontier Norm Governance

The post-Cold War liberal international order—defined by American unipolarity, tariff-free globalization, and universal multilateral institutions—has structurally expired. Yet the world of late 2026 has neither stabilized into a clean Cold War II bipolarity nor matured into a harmonious concert of powers.

What has emerged is an **asymmetric, contested multipolar order**—what scholar Amitav Acharya labels a **"Multiplex World."** At the apex sits an acute **US–China structural duopoly** over defense spending, frontier compute, and capital markets, even as Washington under Trump 2.0 retreats from multilateral treaties (announcing withdrawals from 66 international bodies in January 2026). Beneath that apex, major swing powers—**India, the European Union, Japan, Brazil, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE**—refuse binary bloc discipline, hedging across overlapping minilaterals while racing to write the rules for three ungoverned frontiers: **outer space, cyberspace, and artificial intelligence**.

## 1. From Unipolarity to Contested Multipolarity - Rivalry, Taiwan & Minilaterals

### 1.1 US–China Tech Containment - Thucydides Trap vs. the G2 Duopoly Risk
Unlike the US–Soviet ideological rivalry between two largely disconnected economies, the US–China contest is waged through **weaponized interdependence** across deeply entangled supply chains. Washington escalated from tariffs into structural **technology containment**—"small yard, high fence" export controls barring advanced EUV lithography (ASML), Electronic Design Automation (EDA) software, and frontier GPUs (Nvidia) from reaching China, paired with Entity List blacklisting and Trump 2.0 reciprocal tariffs. Beijing retaliated symmetrically through **midstream critical mineral export curbs** (gallium, germanium, graphite, rare earths) while accelerating indigenous state-backed breakthroughs in semiconductors (Huawei/SMIC) and open-weight AI models (*DeepSeek*), bifurcating the global tech ecosystem into incompatible standards.

For Indian grand strategy, US–China dynamics present **two inverse nightmares**:
- **The Thucydides Trap (Systemic War):** Coined by Graham Allison from Sparta's fear of Athens' rise, structural rivalry risks tipping into kinetic conflict over Taiwan or the South China Sea. A US–China war would shatter Indo-Pacific maritime trade, spike energy prices, freeze capital flows, and force New Delhi into an agonizing binary choice while its northern land border remains militarized.
- **The G2 Condominium (Spheres-of-Influence Bargain):** Equally threatening to India—and sharply debated amid Washington's transactional 2025–2026 deal-making—is a tactical **US–China grand bargain**. If Washington accommodates Beijing economically in exchange for hemispheric restraint, China could gain an unchecked free hand across mainland Asia and the Indian Ocean. New Delhi's counter-doctrine, repeatedly articulated by EAM S. Jaishankar, is categorical: **"A multipolar world is impossible without a multipolar Asia."**

### 1.2 The Taiwan Strait, "Porcupine Strategy" & the First Island Chain
Nowhere does structural geography collide more violently with economic interdependence than across the **130-km Taiwan Strait**. Strategically, Taiwan anchors the geometric center of the **First Island Chain**—the maritime barrier running from the **Japanese archipelago through the Ryukyu Islands, Taiwan, and the Philippines to Borneo**. As long as Taiwan remains outside Beijing's military control, the PLA Navy's Eastern and Southern Theater fleets face monitored chokepoints (Miyako Strait, Bashi Channel) when attempting to break out into the open Western Pacific or establish deep-water nuclear ballistic missile submarine (SSBN) bastions.

Economically, Taiwan is the **silicon jugular of the global economy**. Producing **over 60% of the world's semiconductors** and **over 90% of cutting-edge sub-5nm logic chips** (via TSMC), a Chinese amphibious invasion or customs quarantine/blockade would trigger an estimated **$10 trillion global GDP shock** (~10% of world output), dwarfing the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic combined.

Facing overwhelming PLA quantitative superiority and relentless gray-zone **Air Defense Identification Zone (ADIZ)** incursions and *"Joint Sword"* encirclement drills, Taipei—guided by former Chief of General Staff Admiral Lee Hsi-min's **Overall Defense Concept** and US security assistance—has pivoted toward a **"Porcupine Strategy"** (asymmetric denial deterrence):
- **Core Operational Logic:** Abandoning vulnerable, prestige legacy platforms (large surface combatants, exposed fighter runways) that PLA rocket forces would destroy in the opening hours of war, in favour of large numbers of cheap, mobile, dispersed, and survivable asymmetric systems.
- **Key Capabilities:** Truck-mounted **Harpoon coastal anti-ship missiles**, **HIMARS** precision artillery, man-portable **Stinger** air defenses, smart sea mines choking shallow invasion beaches, autonomous aerial/naval **drone swarms**, and resilient low-Earth-orbit satellite communications—designed to make amphibious occupation prohibitively bloody rather than winning a conventional force-on-force clash.
- **India's Calibrated Posture:** New Delhi maintains formal diplomatic ties with Beijing while dropping explicit endorsement of China's "One China Principle" from joint communiqués since 2010. Quietly, India has expanded high-tech economic ties with Taipei—partnering with **PSMC** on Tata's Dholera semiconductor fab, scaling **Foxconn** electronics manufacturing, signing the **February 2024 India–Taiwan Labour Mobility Pact**, and allowing discreet military-contingency assessments without crossing Beijing's formal diplomatic red line.

### 1.3 Proliferation of Issue-Based Minilaterals ("Plurilateralism")
As universal UN Security Council, WTO, and UNFCCC consensus grinds to a halt, major and middle powers have migrated toward nimble, interest-specific **minilaterals**—small coalitions of 3 to 4 states united by functional problems rather than binding Cold War mutual-defense treaties. Five architectures illustrate this structural shift:

- **QUAD (India, USA, Japan, Australia):** Eschewing formal NATO-style treaty obligations, the Quad focuses on Indo-Pacific maritime public goods—**IPMDA** maritime domain awareness, subsea cables, Malabar naval interoperability, and the **May 2026 $20 billion Quad Critical Minerals Framework**.
- **AUKUS (Australia, UK, USA):** A hard-defense Anglosphere technology pact split into **Pillar I** (transferring nuclear-powered conventionally armed attack submarines, SSNs, to Australia—triggering IAEA Article 14 naval-propulsion safeguard debates) and **Pillar II** (hypersonics, quantum computing, AI, undersea autonomy, electronic warfare—selectively exploring Japan/partner cooperation).
- **I2U2 (India, Israel, UAE, USA):** Non-military geo-economic minilateral leveraging Abraham Accords normalization across six civilian sectors (food parks, hybrid renewables, water, space, transport, health).
- **India–France–UAE Trilateral:** Anchored in Western Indian Ocean maritime security, defense co-production, and solar/green hydrogen diplomacy—extending India's strategic autonomy into European and Gulf naval architectures simultaneously.
- **Camp David Trilateral (USA, Japan, South Korea):** Institutionalized in August 2023 to overcome decades of Tokyo–Seoul historical animosity, creating real-time North Korean missile warning sharing, annual trilateral summits, and joint Freedom Edge multi-domain exercises across Northeast Asia. *(Complemented by the US–Japan–Australia–Philippines "Squad" contesting Chinese coercion in the South China Sea).*

Minilateralism is the operational engine of Indian **multi-alignment**: it enables New Delhi to balance China with Washington and Tokyo in the Quad, deepen European ties via Paris, and shape Eurasian/Global South rules inside BRICS+ without accepting subordinate alliance discipline from anyone.

## 2. BRICS+ Expansion & India's 2026 Presidency (18th Summit)

### 2.1 From Acronym to Geopolitical Heavyweight - The 2024–2025 Expansion Waves
Coined as an investment thesis by Goldman Sachs economist **Jim O'Neill (2001)** and formalized at the **2006 BRIC Foreign Ministers' meeting** before its inaugural **Yekaterinburg Summit (2009)**, BRIC added **South Africa (2010/2011)** and built concrete Bretton Woods alternatives at the **2014 Fortaleza Summit**: the **New Development Bank (NDB, Shanghai)** and the **$100 billion Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA)**.

Between 2023 and 2025, BRICS executed its most consequential structural expansion—transforming from a five-nation emerging-market club into a transcontinental Global South mega-platform accounting for **49.5% of world population, 40% of global GDP, and 26% of global trade** (PIB, September 2026):

- **Full Membership & Dual-Standard Parity (11 Official vs. 10 Active Ratified):** Following the **15th Summit (Johannesburg, August 2023)** invitations, **Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and the UAE** formally acceded on **January 1, 2024**, followed by Southeast Asia's heavyweight **Indonesia on January 6, 2025** (*Argentina*, under President Javier Milei, declined its invitation in December 2023). **Crucial Epistemic Parity:** Official **Government of India / PIB / BRICS 2026 protocol documents list 11 full members**—*Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Indonesia*. However, **Saudi Arabia has not formally ratified full membership** and continues domestically to describe itself as an "invited country" (notably absent from the ceremonial leaders' photo at New Delhi in September 2026 to hedge US security ties), leading several international datasets to count **10 active full members**. Aspirants must retain both the official **11-member GoI tally** and the **Riyadh ratification nuance**.
- **The New "Partner Country" Tier (2025):** To manage 30+ membership applications without diluting consensus, BRICS created a formal **Partner Country** tier (attending ministerial and outreach sessions without full veto/decision-making status), inducting **10 Partner Countries in 2025**: **Belarus, Bolivia, Cuba, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Nigeria, Thailand, Uganda, Uzbekistan, and Vietnam**. *(Note: distinct from ad hoc **BRICS Outreach / BRICS Plus** summit invitees).*

### 2.2 India's 2026 BRICS Presidency & the 18th New Delhi Summit (September 12–13, 2026)
Assuming its **fourth BRICS Chairship** on January 1, 2026 (after **2012, 2016, and 2021**) in BRICS's **20th anniversary year**, India hosted the **18th BRICS Summit at Bharat Mandapam, New Delhi on September 12–13, 2026**—culminating over **350 meetings across 25+ Indian cities** under the theme **"Building for Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation and Sustainability"** (guided by PM Modi's *"Humanity First"* vision).

The summit unfolded amid peak geopolitical polarization: an ongoing US–Iran war, Russia's continuing Ukraine war, and explicit US threats of **100% punitive tariffs** against any bloc attempting to replace the US dollar. New Delhi confronted an existential strategic challenge inside BRICS+: **Moscow and Beijing actively sought to weaponize expanded BRICS+ (bolstered by Iran) into an overtly anti-Western, anti-dollar revisionist camp.**

Through meticulous consensus-building, India's Presidency anchored a disciplined **"Non-Western, Not Anti-Western"** trajectory—keeping BRICS **reformist rather than revisionist**—crystallized in the unanimous **140-paragraph New Delhi Declaration (September 12, 2026)**:
1. **Defusing the Anti-Western & Common-Currency Trap:** The New Delhi Declaration **contained zero explicit naming or condemnation of the United States** despite intense lobbying by Tehran and Moscow. Crucially, while advancing **local-currency trade settlement**, interoperable fast-payment connectivity, and NDB local-currency lending, India successfully ensured BRICS **rejected any proposal for a common BRICS currency**—shielding Indian exporters from Trump 2.0 tariff retaliation and protecting RBI monetary autonomy.
2. **Reclaiming Multilateral Reform & Global South Agency:** Building directly on India's 2023 G20 Presidency and South Africa's 2025 G20, New Delhi kept the spotlight on **UN Security Council reform** (backing greater representation for India, Brazil, and Africa), IMF quota recalibration, WTO dispute-settlement restoration, and opposition to unilateral green trade barriers (CBAM).
3. **Practical Development Deliverables:** Shifting focus from geopolitical polemics to tangible cooperation across **Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)**, startup innovation (*Bharat Innovates* exposition), resilient agricultural and critical-mineral value chains, Middle East de-escalation, and climate resilience—cementing India alongside Brazil, Indonesia, the UAE, and Egypt as the pragmatic **swing-power caucus** preventing Sino–Russian ideological capture of BRICS+.

## 3. Global Norm Generation Across Frontier Domains

Traditional international law was written for sovereign territories, maritime straits, and industrial-age weapons. Across **outer space, cyberspace, and artificial intelligence**, technological velocity has outpaced binding treaties—turning **norm-setting** itself into a premier theater of great-power rivalry. Whoever writes the technical standards and legal rules today locks in economic rents and military advantages for the next half-century.

### 3.1 Outer Space - Obsolescence of 1967 OST, Artemis vs. ILRS & Orbital Weaponization
The constitutional bedrock of space law—the **1967 Outer Space Treaty (OST)** (ratified by India, US, China, Russia)—was drafted for a Cold War state duopoly. While **Article II** prohibits national appropriation of celestial bodies (*res communis*) and **Article IV** bans placing **nuclear weapons or other WMD** in orbit or on celestial bodies, the OST suffers glaring 21st-century voids: it imposes **no ban on conventional anti-satellite weapons (ASATs)**, sets **no binding ceilings on space debris or LEO mega-constellations** (like Starlink crowding orbital slots), ignores dual-use commercial constellations aiding live combat, and leaves **commercial extraction of lunar/asteroid water-ice and Helium-3** legally ambiguous. *(The **1979 Moon Agreement**, which declared lunar resources the "Common Heritage of Mankind," failed geopolitically—ratified by fewer than 20 states; India signed in 1982 but never ratified; the US, Russia, and China rejected it outright).*

Into this legal vacuum stepped **two competing lunar norm blocs**:
- **The US-Led Artemis Accords (2020):** Non-binding bilateral political commitments grounded in the OST, explicitly affirming that **extracting and owning space resources does NOT constitute prohibited "national appropriation"** and introducing **"safety zones"** around lunar landing sites and mining assets (which critics warn could harden into de facto territorial exclusion zones). By January 2026 (with Oman signing as the 61st member), **61 nations** had joined—including **India (27th signatory, June 21, 2023)**, unlocking deep ISRO–NASA interoperability (NISAR radar satellite, Indian astronaut flight to the ISS, Gaganyaan synergy).
- **The Sino–Russian International Lunar Research Station (ILRS, 2021):** Spearheaded by China's **CNSA** and Russia's **Roscosmos** to build a permanent lunar south-pole base in the 2030s, recruiting roughly **17 partner nations and organizations**—including **Pakistan, Belarus, Venezuela, South Africa, Egypt, Thailand, Azerbaijan, Senegal, and Serbia**—positioning itself as an alternative non-Western deep-space coalition.
- **UN PAROS Deadlock & Orbital Arms Race:** Efforts at the Geneva Conference on Disarmament to conclude a **Prevention of an Arms Race in Outer Space (PAROS)** treaty remain paralyzed along rival proposals. China and Russia champion the draft **PPWT (Treaty on the Prevention of the Placement of Weapons in Outer Space)**—which Western states reject because it lacks verification and omits *ground-based direct-ascent ASAT missiles* (like China's 2007 and Russia's 2021 debris-creating tests). Conversely, Washington promotes a non-binding **UNGA moratorium on destructive direct-ascent ASAT tests** (backed by 155+ countries). India—having validated indigenous kinetic deterrence via **Mission Shakti (2019)**—**abstained** on the US moratorium, arguing that selective voluntary bans lock in first-mover advantages while advocating a **comprehensive, legally binding, and verifiable PAROS treaty**. Tensions escalated sharply in **April 2024**, when Russia vetoed a US–Japan UNSC resolution simply reaffirming the OST's ban on orbital nuclear weapons amid Western intelligence warnings of Russian space-nuclear ASAT capabilities.

### 3.2 Cyberspace - Open Internet vs. Cyber Sovereignty & the 2024 UN Cybercrime Convention
Cyberspace governance is fractured across an ideological fault line over **who governs the internet's logical and content layers**:
- **Western "Multi-Stakeholder Open Internet" Model:** Champions decentralized governance involving governments, private tech firms, and technical bodies (**ICANN, IETF**), protecting free cross-border data flows and human rights. Its criminal-law anchor is the Council of Europe's **2001 Budapest Convention on Cybercrime** (~70+ parties). **India is NOT a signatory to the Budapest Convention**—both because New Delhi was excluded from its drafting and because **Article 32(b)** permits foreign law-enforcement agencies unilateral trans-border access to stored computer data without host-government consent, violating Indian digital sovereignty.
- **Sino–Russian "Cyber Sovereignty" Model:** Asserts absolute state territorial jurisdiction over national internet segments (*Great Firewall*, Russian *RuNet*), seeks to shift technical internet governance from multi-stakeholder ICANN to the state-run **UN International Telecommunication Union (ITU)**, and defines "information security" broadly to legitimize political censorship.
- **The 2024 UN Cybercrime Convention (Hanoi Convention):** Originating from a 2019 Russian UNGA initiative, the **United Nations Convention against Cybercrime**—the first global, legally binding UN treaty on cybercrime—was adopted by UNGA consensus on **December 24, 2024** and opened for signature in **Hanoi, Vietnam on October 25–26, 2025**, drawing **72 initial signatories** (including China, Russia, the EU, Brazil, and ASEAN states; requiring **40 ratifications** to enter into force). **Crucial 2025–2026 Nuance:** Neither the **United States** (citing weak human-rights safeguards and authoritarian surveillance abuse) nor **India** signed at Hanoi. While India engaged actively in the Ad Hoc Committee to seek fairer global e-evidence cooperation than Budapest offered, New Delhi held back signature to examine intrusive cross-border electronic-evidence sharing provisions (Articles 23 and 35–42), domestic privacy compatibility under the **DPDP Act, 2023**, and operational fit with its new criminal codes—while simultaneously contributing to the **UN Open-Ended Working Group (OEWG)** on ICT security (transitioning into a permanent **UN Global Mechanism on ICT Security in 2026**).

### 3.3 Global AI Governance & Autonomous Warfare (LAWS)
Between 2023 and 2026, global AI diplomacy pivoted rapidly from Anglo-American existential fear toward **economic sovereignty and Global South diffusion** across four landmark summits:

1. **Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit (November 2023, UK):** Convened 28 countries + EU around frontier "existential risks" and bio/cyber misuse (*Bletchley Declaration*).
2. **Seoul AI Summit (May 2024):** Expanded the agenda to innovation and inclusivity, networking national **AI Safety Institutes**.
3. **Paris AI Action Summit (February 10–11, 2025):** Co-chaired by **France and India**, shifting the narrative toward open-source ecosystems, public-interest AI, and energy sustainability. Exposed stark transatlantic rupture when **the United States and the United Kingdom refused to sign** the Paris statement on "Inclusive and Sustainable AI" over regulatory and governance language.
4. **India AI Impact Summit (February 16–20, 2026, Bharat Mandapam, New Delhi):** The **first global AI summit hosted in the Global South**, structured around **Three Sutras (*People, Planet, Progress*)** and **Seven Chakras** (*Human Capital, Inclusion, Safe & Trusted AI, Resilience, Science, Democratizing AI Resources, Economic Growth*). In a major diplomatic triumph after Paris's fracture, India brokered the **New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact**, endorsed by **91 countries and international organizations—explicitly including both the United States and China!** Key institutional deliverables included PM Modi's **MANAV Vision** (*Moral & ethical systems, Accountable governance, National sovereignty, Accessible & inclusive, Valid & legitimate*), the **Charter for the Democratic Diffusion of AI**, the **Global AI Impact Commons**, the **Trusted AI Commons**, and the **International Network of AI for Science Institutions**.

#### Three Competing AI Regulatory Archetypes vs. India's DPI-Sovereign Third Way
- **EU AI Act (2024; phased enforcement through August 2026–2027):** Comprehensive, statutory **risk-based hard law** categorizing AI into *Unacceptable (banned)*, *High-Risk*, *Limited*, and *Minimal* tiers, plus strict rules for General-Purpose AI (GPAI)—leveraging the **"Brussels Effect"** to set global compliance floors, though criticized for slowing European startup innovation.
- **US Market-Led Deregulation:** Following President Trump's **January 20, 2025 revocation** of Biden's October 2023 Executive Order 14110 (which had imposed safety reporting and NIST red-teaming), Washington pivoted to aggressive deregulation, private compute megaprojects (*Stargate*), and export denial against China.
- **China's State-Controlled Open-Weight Hybrid:** Enforces binding vertical regulations under the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) requiring generative models to reflect "core socialist values," while encouraging Chinese labs (*DeepSeek*, *Qwen*) to release cheap open-weight models globally to undermine Western proprietary moats.
- **India's DPI-Inclusive Sovereign AI Model:** Deliberately avoids premature heavy EU-style omnibus legislation—relying on agile sectoral regulation, the IT Act, DPDP Act 2023, and an indigenous AI Safety Institute—while using the **IndiaAI Mission** (38,000+ subsidized GPU compute cluster, **AIKosh** public datasets, and sovereign multilingual models like *BharatGen*) to treat AI compute and models as **Digital Public Goods** exportable across the Global South.

#### Autonomous Warfare - Geneva CCW Talks on LAWS
At the sharp military edge of AI sit **Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)**—weapons capable of selecting and engaging targets without human intervention. Under the **1980 UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)** in Geneva, the **Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS** operates under a **2026 mandate** to deliver a rolling text of regulatory principles (backed by UNGA Resolution 79/62).

A structural divide blocks any binding treaty: over 100 **Global South nations, Austria, and civil-society coalitions** demand a **legally binding two-tier instrument** outright banning fully autonomous anti-personnel systems and codifying **"Meaningful Human Control"** across all other systems. Conversely, major military powers—**the US, Russia, Israel, China, and India**—resist a preemptive binding ban, favouring non-binding norms grounded in existing **International Humanitarian Law (IHL)**. India's realist calculus balances ethical commitment to human accountability and IHL compliance against hard operational deterrence: New Delhi refuses unilateral technological disarmament while the PLA aggressively fields AI-enabled drone swarms and autonomous unmanned platforms along the Himalayan LAC.
