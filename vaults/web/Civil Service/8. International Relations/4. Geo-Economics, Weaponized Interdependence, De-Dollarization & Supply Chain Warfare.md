# Chapter 4 - Geo-Economics, Weaponized Interdependence, De-Dollarization & Supply Chain Warfare

For three decades after 1991, liberal globalization operated on a comforting premise: dense cross-border networks of finance, manufacturing, and energy would create symmetric interdependence and pacify great-power rivalry. Instead, hyper-efficiency produced highly centralized **hub-and-spoke networks**—dollar clearing in New York, EUV lithography in the Netherlands, heavy rare-earth refining in China—that major states now systematically turn into instruments of coercion.

By late 2026, geo-economics has moved to the center of grand strategy. Sovereign reserve freezes, extraterritorial chip bans, heavy rare-earth export licensing, rival connectivity corridors, and the January 2026 fracture in the OECD Global Minimum Tax reveal a structural shift from **efficiency-first globalization** to **resilience-first economic statecraft**. For India, sustaining high domestic growth requires hedging simultaneously against Western financial/tech chokepoints and Chinese industrial/mineral monopolies.

---

## 1. Weaponized Interdependence - Asymmetric Network Topology

Formulated by political scientists **Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman** (2019; expanded in *Underground Empire*, 2023), **Weaponized Interdependence** exposes the structural flaw in classic Complex Interdependence theory. Global economic networks are *not* flat, decentralized meshes of equal nodes; driven by economies of scale, network effects, and **preferential attachment**, they evolve into **scale-free asymmetric topologies** where vast global flows funnel through a tiny handful of monopolistic hubs.

States that exercise sovereign legal jurisdiction over these central hubs can exploit asymmetric vulnerability through **two distinct strategic mechanisms**:

- **The Panopticon Effect (Information Extraction):** Named after Jeremy Bentham's surveillance prison, this effect allows a hub-controlling state to harvest granular global intelligence by tapping central informational relays. Classic deployments include Washington's post-9/11 access to **SWIFT** financial messaging logs under the Terrorist Finance Tracking Program (TFTP), NSA interception of global undersea fiber-optic cables landing on US/allied shores, and regulatory access to data routed through US hyperscale cloud providers.
- **The Chokepoint Effect (Coercive Exclusion):** A hub state can deny adversaries access to vital global systems by severing their connectivity at the bottleneck node—without firing a single missile. Examples include disconnecting Iranian and Russian banks from **SWIFT** and New York **CHIPS** dollar clearing, deploying the US **Foreign Direct Product Rule (FDPR)** to bar third-country firms from shipping advanced semiconductors to China, and Beijing licensing **heavy rare-earth magnets** essential for global defense and electric-vehicle motors.

**The Domestic Institutional Capacity Condition:** Geographic control over a hub is necessary but *insufficient* for weaponization. A state must also possess robust **domestic legal-regulatory institutions** capable of compelling private gatekeepers (banks, lithography firms, miners) to comply against their own commercial interests—such as the US Treasury's **OFAC**, the US Commerce Department's **BIS**, or China's **2020 Export Control Law** and **December 2025 Foreign Trade Act** amendments. Critically, overuse of chokepoints triggers **counter-network migration**, incentivizing rivals and neutral states alike to build slower, costlier, sanctions-proof alternative rails.

## 2. Financial Weaponization and the Pragmatic Reality of De-Dollarization

### The Architecture of Dollar Primacy and Sanctions Coercion

The US dollar's hegemony rests on interlocking network effects across four roles—reserve asset, global trade invoicing currency, commodities pricing benchmark, and foreign-exchange vehicle currency—supported by the unmatched depth and liquidity of the US Treasury bond market. Two distinct pieces of financial plumbing sustain US coercive leverage:

- **SWIFT vs. CHIPS (Crucial Technical Distinction):** **SWIFT** (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, headquartered in La Hulpe, **Belgium**) is a cooperative **financial messaging network** linking 11,000+ institutions across 200+ territories—it sends standardized payment instructions, *not* actual money. Actual cross-border US dollar clearing and final settlement occur via **CHIPS** (Clearing House Interbank Payments System, New York) and **Fedwire** inside US sovereign jurisdiction. Even if two foreign banks bypass SWIFT messaging, any dollar-denominated transaction must clear through US correspondent accounts, handing Washington extraterritorial reach.
- **OFAC and Extraterritorial Secondary Sanctions (CAATSA & Beyond):** While *primary sanctions* bind US persons and entities, **secondary sanctions** administered by the US Treasury's **Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)** force *third-country* firms and banks to choose between transacting with a sanctioned target or losing access to the US financial system. Under **CAATSA (Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act, 2017)**, Section 231 threatened third nations procuring major Russian defense hardware (creating protracted US waiver friction over India's S-400 acquisition). Under Trump 2.0, financial coercion escalated further—encompassing repeated **100% tariff threats against any BRICS rival currency** and passage of the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026** (`H.R. 5334` / `Public Law 119-111`, signed **September 18, 2026**), which authorized secondary tariffs of up to 100% on major purchasers of Russian energy and extended the Iran Sanctions Act of 1996 to 2031—though President Trump invoked statutory waiver authority on **October 16, 2026** to suspend the Russia tariff/sanctions provisions before they took effect.
- **G7 Price Caps and the Sovereign Reserve Freeze Rubicon (2022–2026):** Following Russia's February 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the G7 and EU immobilized roughly **$280–$300 billion in Central Bank of Russia (CBR) sovereign reserves**—the bulk (**€193 billion**) held at Belgium's **Euroclear** central securities depository. While European legal concerns over sovereign immunity and Euro stability blocked outright confiscation of the principal, G7 leaders agreed in June 2024 to leverage future windfall interest (~€3–5 billion annually) to service a **$50 billion (€45 billion) Extraordinary Revenue Acceleration (ERA) loan** for Ukraine (largely disbursed through late 2025–26), supplemented by the EU's **€90 billion Ukraine Support Loan** agreed in December 2025. Simultaneously, the G7 **$60/barrel Russian oil price cap**—enforced by barring Western maritime insurers (P&I Clubs) from covering above-cap cargoes—prompted Moscow to assemble an unregulated **"shadow fleet"** of aging tankers. For non-Western reserve managers in New Delhi, Beijing, and Riyadh, freezing sovereign reserves converted a theoretical tail-risk into an operational precedent.

### Counter-Strategies and the Empirical Limits of De-Dollarization

Evaluating de-dollarization demands separating summit rhetoric from central-bank balance-sheet data:

- **Empirical Reality Check (IMF COFER & BIS 2025–2026):** According to **IMF COFER** data for **Q1 2026**, the US dollar's share of allocated global foreign-exchange reserves declined gradually from ~72% in 2001 to **57.13%** (followed by the Euro at 20.0%, Japanese Yen at 5.4%, and Chinese Renminbi at just **2.0%**). More tellingly, the **BIS 2025 Triennial Central Bank Survey** recorded the US dollar on one side of **89.2% of $9.6 trillion in daily global FX turnover**—actually *higher* than 88.4% in 2022. No alternative currency offers comparable capital-account openness, rule-of-law asset depth, or zero-friction convertibility. Consequently, what is underway is not sudden dollar collapse, but **incremental multi-rail hedging ("plumbing diversification")**:
- **Local Currency Settlement (LCS) & Special Rupee Vostro Accounts (SRVAs):** In **July 2022**, the RBI authorized international trade invoicing and settlement in Indian Rupees via **SRVAs** (rupee accounts held by foreign correspondent banks with Indian Authorized Dealer banks). By 2025–26, the RBI approved **123 correspondent banks across 30 partner countries** (spanning Russia, the UAE, UK, Germany, Singapore, Malaysia, Oman, Sri Lanka, Kenya, and Tanzania) to open **156 SRVAs** with 26 Indian banks. To fix early friction—notably billions in trapped Russian rupee surpluses caused by India's massive bilateral oil import deficit—the RBI issued **Circular `RBI/2025-26/71` (August 5, 2025)** allowing AD Category-I banks to open SRVAs **without prior RBI approval**, followed in **October 2025** by allowing surplus SRVA balances to be invested in **non-convertible corporate debt securities** beyond government securities/T-bills. Parallel **Local Currency Settlement Agreements (LCSAs)**—such as India–UAE INR–AED oil settlements—and cross-border **UPI payment linkages** (Singapore, UAE, Mauritius, France, Sri Lanka) reduce dollar intermediation costs without risking sudden capital-account volatility.
- **Bilateral Swap Lines & Alternative Clearing Rails:** Central banks expanded emergency currency swap networks (e.g., RBI's SAARC Currency Swap Framework and $75B India–Japan swap; China's PBOC 5-year RMB swap with Brazil in May 2025). Concurrently, China's **CIPS (Cross-Border Interbank Payment System)** expanded direct and indirect links across **119 countries** for RMB clearing (though still relying partially on SWIFT for indirect messaging), while Russia relies domestically and bilaterally on **SPFS** (launched post-2014).
- **Central-Bank Gold Accumulation & Sovereign Vault Repatriation:** Because physical gold carries **zero counterparty or sovereign-freeze risk**, global central banks purchased **over 1,000 tonnes annually for three consecutive years (2022–2024; 1,045 tonnes in 2024)** and ~863 tonnes in 2025. Reflecting heightened caution over foreign custodianship, the **RBI held 880.52 tonnes of gold at end-March 2026**—repatriating physical bullion from London (Bank of England) so that **77% (680.05 tonnes)** now resides in domestic Indian vaults, up sharply from 58% a year earlier.
- **BRICS Pay & BRICS Bridge (Sept 2026 New Delhi Summit Calibration):** Building on blockchain and Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) interoperability concepts (modeled loosely on the BIS-incubated **mBridge** project), Moscow and Beijing championed **BRICS Pay** and **BRICS Bridge** to bypass Western payment intermediaries. During India's **18th BRICS Summit (New Delhi, September 12–13, 2026)**, RDIF, JSC BRICS Pay, and BRICS Pay India signed a development agreement alongside an India–Russia MoU linking **UPI with Russia's Faster Payments System (FPS)** and RBI–CBR CBDC settlement talks. Yet New Delhi firmly kept a **single BRICS common currency** *off* the summit agenda—with EAM S. Jaishankar clarifying that India seeks trade-payment resilience, *"not a campaign to replace the dollar"*.

## 3. Supply-Chain Geopolitics - Semiconductors, Critical Minerals, and Connectivity

### Semiconductor Chokepoints - The Silicon War

No modern industry exhibits sharper weaponizable chokepoints than advanced semiconductors—the foundational substrate of AI compute, guided munitions, and modern telecom. Rather than vertical integration within one state, the global chip value chain splits into four hyperspecialized choke nodes:

1. **Electronic Design Automation (EDA) & Architecture (United States):** US firms (Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens EDA) dominate chip-design software, while US fabless giants (Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm) lead AI accelerators and high-end logic design.
2. **Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) Lithography (Netherlands & Japan):** Dutch firm **ASML** commands a **100% global monopoly on EUV and High-NA EUV lithography machines** required for sub-7nm chips, while Japanese suppliers (Tokyo Electron, Shin-Etsu, SUMCO) dominate photoresists, ultra-pure etching chemicals, and silicon wafers.
3. **Leading-Edge Foundry Fabrication (Taiwan & South Korea):** Taiwan's **TSMC** fabricates >90% of the world's most advanced logic nodes (3nm/2nm), creating Taiwan's geopolitical **"Silicon Shield,"** while South Korea's **Samsung and SK Hynix** anchor global advanced memory (DRAM/High-Bandwidth Memory).
4. **Assembly, Testing, and Packaging — ATP/OSAT (China & Southeast Asia):** China retains large legacy-node fabrication and packaging capacity, though firms are steadily diversifying ATP toward Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand, and India.

**US Containment vs. Industrial Reshoring:** In **October 2022** (tightened in 2023–25), the US Commerce Department's BIS deployed the extraterritorial **Foreign Direct Product Rule (FDPR)** to block advanced AI chips, EUV/advanced DUV lithography tools, and US-citizen engineering support from reaching Chinese fabs (forcing SMIC to rely on costlier multi-patterning DUV workarounds for 7nm chips). Simultaneously, Washington backed reshoring via the **$52.7 billion US CHIPS and Science Act (2022)**—amplified under Trump's **2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA)** raising the advanced manufacturing investment tax credit from **25% to 35%**—alongside the **€43 billion EU Chips Act** (and early 2026 "Chips Act 2.0" proposals). Plurilaterally, the US organized the **Chip 4 Alliance (US, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan)**—controlling **~82% of the global semiconductor market and ~99% of memory chips**—supplemented by the **Quad Semiconductor Supply Chain Contingency Network** and the US-led **Pax Silica** AI-and-chip supply-chain bloc—launched on **December 12, 2025** with core US allies (expanding to 26+ signatories plus the EU by late 2026), which **India formally joined on February 20, 2026** by signing the Pax Silica Declaration at the India AI Impact Summit in New Delhi (followed by India co-signing the 10-nation **San Francisco Declaration** on AI supply chains in November 2026).

### Critical Mineral Export Curbs vs. Friend-Shoring Coalitions

Confronted with US semiconductor containment, Beijing systematically weaponized its **asymmetric midstream monopoly in critical minerals**—controlling roughly **60% of global rare earth mining**, nearly **90–100% of heavy rare-earth refining/separation**, and **~91% of defense-grade NdFeB permanent magnet production**. Between 2023 and 2026, China deployed a calibrated escalation ladder of dual-use export licensing:

- **2023–2024 Opening Salvos:** Export licensing on **gallium, germanium, and graphite** (2023), followed by **antimony** (August 2024—collapsing Chinese exports by ~97% and tripling global prices) and an explicit December 2024 ban on US-bound gallium, germanium, and antimony.
- **2025 Heavy Rare Earth Escalation:** In February 2025, Beijing restricted **tungsten, indium, bismuth, tellurium, and molybdenum**. On **April 4, 2025** (following US "Liberation Day" tariffs), China imposed sweeping export controls on **seven medium and heavy rare earths**—*samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium*—plus SmCo and NdFeB magnets containing dysprosium/terbium. Shipment-level licensing triggered global automaker slowdowns and opened a **250% non-China price premium** in H2 2025, handing Chinese downstream EV/wind/defense manufacturers a massive structural cost advantage.
- **Extraterritoriality & Tactical Pause (Oct 2025 – Nov 2026):** On **October 9, 2025**, China unveiled a mirror image of the US FDPR—proposing extraterritorial licensing on *any third-country product* containing even trace Chinese-sourced rare earths or processing tech, alongside battery/graphite anode curbs. Following the **October 2025 Trump–Xi Busan summit**, Beijing paused the October 9 controls (and its explicit US gallium/germanium ban) until November 10, 2026—and in **November 2026 extended that suspension into 2027**—yet deliberately left its **April 2025 heavy rare-earth licensing** and 2023–24 base controls in force while adding targeted **January 2026 dual-use curbs against Japan**.

To break Beijing's leverage, India and Indo-Pacific partners erected a four-layer **friend-shoring counter-grid**:

- **SCRI (Supply Chain Resilience Initiative):** Launched **April 27, 2021** by **India, Japan, and Australia** to diversify Indo-Pacific supply chains through policy coordination, investment promotion, and buyer–seller matching—though constrained by limited dedicated sovereign funding.
- **IPEF Supply Chain Pillar (Pillar II):** Within the 14-nation US-led Indo-Pacific Economic Framework (2022), India opted out of Pillar I (Trade) to guard domestic policy space while joining Pillars II–IV. The **IPEF Supply Chain Agreement entered into force on February 24, 2024**, establishing a **Supply Chain Council** (with **India as Vice-Chair**), a **Crisis Response Network**, and a Labor Rights Advisory Board—though Trump 2.0's tariff unilateralism blunted US momentum.
- **MSP → FORGE (February 2026 Institutional Upgrade):** Launched by the US in June 2022 (14 countries + EU; **India became the 14th member in June 2023** and joined the **MSP Finance Network** in September 2024 to crowd in DFI/ECA co-financing), the **Minerals Security Partnership (MSP)** was formally succeeded on **February 4, 2026**—at Washington's 54-nation Critical Minerals Ministerial attended by EAM S. Jaishankar—by the **Forum on Resource Geostrategic Engagement (FORGE)**. Guided by consensus principles adopted July 10, 2026 (countering non-market dumping and mobilizing blended finance) alongside the **May 26, 2026 India–US bilateral critical minerals pact** and **Quad Critical Minerals Initiative Framework**, FORGE aims to build resilient mine-to-magnet supply chains outside China.
- **KABIL & Overseas Resource Diplomacy:** Incorporated in August 2019 as a 40:30:30 PSU joint venture among **NALCO, Hindustan Copper Ltd., and MECL** under the Ministry of Mines, **Khanij Bidesh India Ltd. (KABIL)** leads overseas equity acquisitions—signing a landmark **January 15, 2024 agreement with Argentina's CAMYEN SE** for exploration and mining of **five lithium brine blocks (15,703 hectares) in Catamarca province**, complemented by critical mineral pacts with Australia, Chile, Brazil, and France.

Domestically, India pairs this resource diplomacy with a transition from *mineral security* (ore access) to *material security* (midstream refining)—anchored by its 30-mineral critical list, the **2023 MMDR Act amendment**, the **National Critical Mineral Mission (NCMM, Jan 2025)**, the ₹7,280 crore Rare Earth Permanent Magnet scheme, and **Dedicated Rare Earth Corridors** announced in Union Budget 2026–27. For detailed geological reserves, domestic corridors, and global mineral geography (Lithium Triangle, DRC cobalt, Australian spodumene), cross-reference [[16. Mineral & Energy Cartography and Critical Minerals#Strategic Critical Minerals and Rare Earth Cartography|Critical Minerals & Rare Earth Cartography]].

### Connectivity Geopolitics - China's BRI Debt-Trap Debate vs. G7 PGII & EU Global Gateway

- **China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI, 2013) and the Debt-Trap Debate:** Linking ~140+ countries across the overland *Silk Road Economic Belt* and *21st Century Maritime Silk Road*, BRI deployed over $1 trillion in state-backed loans through China Exim Bank and China Development Bank (CDB). **India consistently boycotts BRI** on core sovereignty grounds because the flagship **China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC)** traverses Indian territory in Pakistan-Occupied Kashmir (Gilgit-Baltistan), alongside objections to opaque financing and ecological standards. Analytically, the **"debt-trap diplomacy" thesis** requires sharp nuance: while strategic critics point to Sri Lanka's 99-year **Hambantota Port lease (2017)**, Djibouti, Laos, and Pakistan's CPEC power arrears as deliberate asset capture, empirical scholarship demonstrates that most defaults stemmed from commercially reckless Chinese policy-bank over-lending colliding with recipient elite governance failures and high-interest Western Eurobonds—*not* premeditated foreclosure plots. Yet regardless of intent, the *geopolitical effect* is asymmetric leverage via non-disclosure clauses, escrow accounts, and dual-use port access. Following sovereign defaults and high-profile withdrawals—**Italy (December 2023, the sole G7 signatory)** and **Panama (February 2025, the first Latin American exit amid US Panama Canal pressure)**—Beijing pivoted toward **"BRI 2.0" ("small and beautiful")**, prioritizing greener energy, EVs, and targeted critical-mineral mining over mega-rail white elephants.
- **Western Counter-Corridors — G7 PGII and EU Global Gateway:** To offer transparent, ESG-compliant alternatives, the G7 launched the **Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment (PGII)** in 2022 (rebranding Biden's B3W) pledging **$600 billion by 2027**, paired with the **EU Global Gateway (€300 billion by 2027)** backed by the EIB and EBRD. Flagship bets include Africa's **Lobito Corridor** (upgrading rail links from Angola's Atlantic coast to DRC's Katanga cobalt belt and Zambia's Copperbelt, attracting $320M from post-BRI Italy) and the **India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)** announced at the 2023 New Delhi G20. Structural weakness: because PGII and Global Gateway rely on **crowding in private capital** rather than state-directed policy banks—and face reduced US enthusiasm under Trump 2.0—actual disbursements lag dramatically behind headline pledges (low- and middle-income states still capture only ~20% of private infrastructure capital).

## 4. Sovereign Debt Restructuring and Global Tax Architecture (BEPS 2.0)

### G20 Common Framework - Paris Club vs. Chinese Bilateral Lending

Historically, sovereign defaults by poor nations were resolved cleanly inside the **Paris Club** (an informal group of 22 predominantly Western official bilateral creditors founded in 1956). Over the 2010s, however, China overtook the entire Paris Club combined as the world's largest bilateral sovereign lender, while private Eurobond investors surged—shattering old restructuring coordination.

- **Architecture of the G20 Common Framework (November 2020):** Endorsed jointly by the **G20 and the Paris Club** to provide structural debt treatments beyond COVID-19's temporary Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI), the Common Framework covers **73 DSSI-eligible Low-Income Countries (LICs)**. Its core innovation was seating **non-Paris Club G20 creditors (China, India, Saudi Arabia)** alongside Paris Club lenders inside a single **Official Creditor Committee (OCC)** bound by IMF Debt Sustainability Analyses and a **"Comparability of Treatment"** clause requiring private bondholders to accept equivalent relief.
- **Structural Gridlock and 2026 Case Scorecard:** Uptake remained sluggish—only **four LICs (Chad, Zambia, Ghana, Ethiopia)** applied initially, with **Senegal** becoming a new applicant in late 2026. Restructurings dragged out (averaging 2.5 years post-2020 vs. 1.1 years pre-2020) due to three clashes with Beijing: (1) China's refusal to grant nominal **principal haircuts** (favoring maturity extensions); (2) Beijing classifying **China Development Bank (CDB)** as a "commercial" lender outside official restructuring; and (3) Chinese demands that **World Bank/MDBs take haircuts** despite their Preferred Creditor Status. Per the **October 14, 2026 Seventh Progress Report of the Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable (GSDR)** (which also published a Restructuring Playbook in April 2025/2026), older cases have finally neared closure:
  - **Zambia:** Defaulted Nov 2020; took 4.1 years amid OCC (co-chaired by China and France) vs. bondholder comparability clashes; >94% of in-scope debt resolved by Oct 2026.
  - **Ghana:** Defaulted Dec 2022; moved faster (1.9 years), securing a 37% nominal haircut on $13B Eurobonds and completing its final IMF program review in July 2026.
  - **Ethiopia:** Signed 11 of 14 bilateral OCC deals and reached a Eurobond Agreement in Principle on June 29, 2026.
- **Sri Lanka — India's Middle-Income Innovation:** Because **Sri Lanka** is a Middle-Income Country (MIC) excluded from the LIC-only Common Framework, India spearheaded an unprecedented parallel **Official Creditor Committee co-chaired by India, Japan, and France**, coordinating alongside China Exim Bank's separate bilateral relief to restructure **~94% of Sri Lanka's in-scope debt** by 2026. India continues to press G20 partners—reinforced by the **2025 UN Sevilla Commitment** (4th FfD Conference)—to build predictable restructuring mechanisms for distressed MICs.

### OECD/G20 BEPS 2.0 - Digital Taxation and the January 2026 Pillar Two Fracture

Multinational Enterprises (MNEs) routinely exploit cross-border tax mismatches—booking intellectual property in zero-tax havens while generating billions in revenue across high-population digital markets—draining **$100–$240 billion annually** (4–10% of global corporate tax revenue). To halt this race to the bottom, the **OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on BEPS** (now **147 member jurisdictions**) endorsed a landmark **Two-Pillar Solution (BEPS 2.0)** in October 2021:

- **Pillar One (Reallocating Market Taxing Rights — Stalled in 2026):** Designed to replace outdated physical **"Permanent Establishment" (PE)** rules for the digital economy, **Amount A** targets the world's largest, most profitable MNEs (**global revenue > €20 billion and profit margin > 10%**), reallocating **25% of residual profits above the 10% threshold** to **market jurisdictions** where users/consumers reside—paired with **Amount B** (simplified arm's-length transfer pricing for baseline marketing/distribution). **2026 Reality:** Pillar One Amount A remains **deadlocked** without a signed Multilateral Convention owing to US Congressional hostility (protecting US Big Tech) and disputes over withholding-tax double-counting.
  - **India's Strategic Digital Tax Reset (2024–2025):** India had pioneered unilateral digital taxation via the **Equalisation Levy (EL)**—a 6% levy on non-resident online ads (Finance Act, 2016) expanded to a 2% levy on non-resident e-commerce supplies (Finance Act, 2020). To de-escalate US Section 301 trade friction and simplify compliance, New Delhi phased out **both components entirely**: abolishing the **2% e-commerce EL from August 1, 2024** (Finance [No. 2] Act, 2024) and abolishing the **6% online advertising EL from April 1, 2025** under the **Finance Act, 2025**. Non-resident digital transactions now revert to ordinary Income-tax Act / **Significant Economic Presence (SEP)**, DTAA provisions, and OIDAR GST.
- **Pillar Two (15% Global Minimum Tax — GloBE Rules):** Targeting MNE groups with consolidated annual revenues **>= €750 million**, Pillar Two guarantees a **minimum 15% Effective Tax Rate (ETR)** in every jurisdiction through three interlocking Global Anti-Base Erosion (GloBE) mechanisms (implemented across 50+ jurisdictions including EU, UK, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and Canada since 2024–25):
  1. **QDMTT (Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-Up Tax):** First-mover right allowing the *host/source jurisdiction* to collect the top-up tax domestically rather than surrendering revenue abroad.
  2. **IIR (Income Inclusion Rule):** Empowers the MNE's *headquarters (Ultimate Parent Entity) jurisdiction* to levy a top-up tax on low-taxed foreign subsidiaries.
  3. **UTPR (Undertaxed Profits Rule):** A potent backstop allowing *other jurisdictions* where the MNE operates to deny deductions/collect top-up tax if neither the source nor parent jurisdiction imposes 15%.
  4. **STTR (Subject to Tax Rule):** A treaty-based override permitting developing source states to levy a **9% minimum tax** on specified intra-group payments (interest, royalties) routed to low-tax treaty partners.
- **The January 5, 2026 "Side-by-Side" Package (Multilateral Convergence Fractures):** Following Trump 2.0's withdrawal of US support for the OECD Global Tax Deal and enactment of the 2025 *One Big Beautiful Bill Act* (threatening retaliatory taxes against foreign UTPR levies), all **147 Inclusive Framework members agreed on January 5, 2026 to the OECD "Side-by-Side Package"**. Under its new **Side-by-Side (SbS) Safe Harbour**, MNEs headquartered in a jurisdiction maintaining qualifying parallel domestic/international minimum-tax rules—currently **ONLY the United States** on the OECD Central Record (via its GILTI/OBBBA framework)—receive **full exemption from Pillar Two's IIR and UTPR**, though **remaining subject to local QDMTTs** in host countries. Crucially for developing economies, the package also introduced a **Substance-Based Tax Incentive Safe Harbour** protecting genuine manufacturing/R&D tax incentives from being wiped out by top-up taxes. While averting a transatlantic tax war, this American carve-out exposed OECD asymmetry—accelerating Global South momentum behind the rival **UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation** for fairer source-based taxing rights.
- **Net Impact on India:** Because India's standard corporate tax rate is 22% (and 15% for eligible new manufacturing), Pillar Two erodes the lure of zero-tax conduits (Cayman Islands, Mauritius, Singapore) without undercutting India's core fiscal competitiveness—though Indian global majors (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) above €750M face heavier GloBE compliance and New Delhi must align domestic **PLI, SEZ, and GIFT City** incentives carefully with QDMTT and the 2026 Substance-Based Safe Harbour.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **SWIFT vs. CHIPS & De-Dollarization Traps:** **SWIFT** (Belgium) is strictly a *messaging network*—it neither holds funds nor clears dollars (settlement occurs via New York's **CHIPS/Fedwire**). Watch also for exaggerated de-dollarization claims: per BIS 2025 data, the USD still features in **89.2% of global FX trades** and **~57.1% of IMF COFER reserves** (while RMB sits near ~2%), and India has **not** endorsed a common BRICS currency.
- **Equalisation Levy Repeal & Pillar Two Side-by-Side Traps:** Outdated notes still treat India's 6% Equalisation Levy as active—in fact, **BOTH the 2% e-commerce EL (Aug 2024) and the 6% online ads EL (April 1, 2025 via Finance Act 2025) have been completely abolished**. Under OECD BEPS 2.0, remember the **January 5, 2026 Side-by-Side Safe Harbour** exempts **US-parented MNEs** from Pillar Two's **IIR and UTPR**, *not* from local host-country **QDMTTs**.
- **Critical Minerals Coalitions & Country-Membership Traps:** India opted **OUT of IPEF Pillar I (Trade)** while joining **Pillars II, III, and IV** (and serves as Vice-Chair of the IPEF Supply Chain Council). Furthermore, the **Minerals Security Partnership (MSP)** (which India joined in June 2023) was formally succeeded by **FORGE (Forum on Resource Geostrategic Engagement)** in **February 2026**; and **KABIL** is a JV of **NALCO, Hindustan Copper, and MECL** (NOT Coal India or NMDC). Watch also for the **Pax Silica "founding member" trap**: launched by the US on December 12, 2025 with core allies, **India was NOT a founding signatory**, formally joining two months later on **February 20, 2026** at the New Delhi India AI Impact Summit.
- **G20 Common Framework Eligibility & BRI Exit Traps:** The **G20 Common Framework** applies exclusively to **Low-Income Countries (DSSI-eligible LICs)**—that is *why* Middle-Income **Sri Lanka** had to restructure *outside* the Common Framework via an ad-hoc OCC co-chaired by India, Japan, and France. On BRI exits, **Italy (Dec 2023)** was the sole G7 exit, while **Panama (Feb 2025)** was the **first Latin American country** to withdraw.
