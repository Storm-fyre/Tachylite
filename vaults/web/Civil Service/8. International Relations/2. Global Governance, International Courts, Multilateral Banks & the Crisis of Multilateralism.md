# Global Governance, International Courts, Multilateral Banks & the Crisis of Multilateralism

Post-1945 multilateralism was engineered around a frozen geopolitical snapshot—the victors of World War II writing the constitutional rules of security (UNSC), finance (IMF and World Bank), and commerce (GATT/WTO). Eighty-one years later, those institutions suffer an acute twin crisis of **legitimacy** (gross under-representation of the Global South) and **efficacy** (veto paralysis, judicial boycotts, and unilateral funding withdrawals).

By late 2026, this institutional erosion has moved from chronic gridlock to acute contraction: the United States completed its formal exit from the WHO in January 2026, initiated exits from UNESCO and 66 international bodies, stalled IMF quota realignment, and watched WTO multilateralism fracture at MC14 Yaoundé. For India—a founding member of the UN, IMF, World Bank, and WTO now operating as the world's most populous democracy and a rising economic pole—mastering these institutional rules is indispensable for navigating both Prelims traps and GS2 reformed-multilateralism diplomacy.

## 1. The UN System and the UNSC Reform Deadlock

Founded under the San Francisco Charter (signed June 1945; effective October 24, 1945), the United Nations now encompasses **193 Member States** (South Sudan being the latest in 2011). India signed the Charter as an **original founding member** in 1945 prior to formal independence. Yet operational authority remains starkly concentrated inside a single organ—the Security Council—creating persistent friction between sovereign equality in the General Assembly and great-power hierarchy.

### UN Principal Organs - Institutional Architecture

| UN Principal Organ | Seat & Composition | Core Mandate & Decision Rule | Legal Binding Status & Key Prelims Distinction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **General Assembly (UNGA)** | New York; all 193 Member States (1 state = 1 vote) | Deliberative plenary; approves UN budget, admits new members (on UNSC recommendation), elects non-permanent UNSC & ICJ judges; **2/3 majority** for "important questions" | Resolutions are **recommendatory (non-binding)** except internal budgetary/administrative decisions |
| **Security Council (UNSC)** | New York; **15 members** = **P5** (US, UK, France, Russia, China) + **10 elected (E10)** for 2-year non-consecutive terms | Primary responsibility for international peace & security (Art 24); substantive votes need **9/15 with zero P5 negative votes** (abstention/absence is *not* a veto) | **Only UN organ** empowered to pass **legally binding** resolutions (Art 25, Chapter VII) |
| **ECOSOC** | New York/Geneva; **54 members** elected by UNGA for 3-year staggered terms | Coordinates economic, social, environmental work, SDGs, and functional commissions (Commission on Narcotic Drugs, UN Statistical Commission) + 15 Specialized Agencies | Policy coordination and recommendations; grants consultative status to NGOs |
| **Trusteeship Council** | New York; P5 members | Supervised administration of 11 UN Trust Territories toward self-government/independence | **Suspended operations on November 1, 1994** following independence of **Palau** (still formally exists in UN Charter) |

### P5 Veto Paralysis, Chapter VI vs. Chapter VII, and Procedural Workarounds

The UN Charter splits conflict management across two sharply distinct legal regimes, both repeatedly stalled by P5 geopolitics:

- **Chapter VI (*Pacific Settlement of Disputes*, Arts 33–38):** Authorizes the UNSC to investigate friction and recommend peaceful mechanisms—negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, or ICJ referral. Resolutions adopted under Chapter VI (such as early Kashmir resolutions) are **legally non-binding recommendations**.
- **Chapter VII (*Threats to the Peace, Breaches of the Peace, Acts of Aggression*, Arts 39–51):** Empowers the Council to determine threats (Art 39), impose mandatory non-military sanctions such as embargoes and asset freezes (Art 41), and authorize collective military force (Art 42), while preserving inherent self-defense (Art 51). Under Article 25, Chapter VII directives are **legally binding on all UN Member States**.
- **UNSC Counter-Terrorism Architecture (1267 Committee, CTC, and India's CCIT):** Operating under Chapter VII, the **UNSC 1267/1988/1989/2253 Sanctions Committee** maintains the consolidated global terror list (triggering mandatory asset freezes, travel bans, and arms embargoes against Al-Qaida, ISIL, and affiliates), complemented by the **Counter-Terrorism Committee (CTC)** created post-9/11 under **Resolution 1373 (2001)** and assisted by CTED. Because the 1267 Committee operates strictly by **consensus**, any single member can impose a "technical hold" or block listings—an instrument Beijing repeatedly weaponized to shield Pakistan-based terrorists (delaying Jaish-e-Mohammed chief Masood Azhar's designation for a decade until May 2019, and blocking subsequent listings of Lashkar-e-Taiba/JeM operatives like Sajid Mir and Abdul Rauf Azhar). To cure this selective politicization, India proposed the **Comprehensive Convention on International Terrorism (CCIT)** in **1996**, seeking a universal legal definition of terrorism denying safe haven (*extradite or prosecute*); thirty years on, CCIT remains deadlocked over three disputes—OIC insistence on exempting "national liberation movements," Western resistance to covering state armed forces, and Latin American concerns over international humanitarian law overlap.
- **UNGA Resolution 377A (V) — *Uniting for Peace* (November 3, 1950):** Crafted during the Korean War to bypass Soviet vetoes, Resolution 377A empowers the General Assembly to convene an **Emergency Special Session (ESS)** within 24 hours whenever P5 disunity prevents the UNSC from acting on a breach of peace. Crucially, referring a crisis from the UNSC to an ESS requires only a **procedural vote of 9/15 UNSC members—immune to the P5 veto**. Invoked **11 times** in history—most notably the **10th ESS (Palestine/Gaza)** and the **11th ESS (Ukraine, February 2022)**—*Uniting for Peace* carries immense moral weight but cannot override Charter limits: its recommendations remain **legally non-binding**.
- **UNGA Resolution 76/262 — *The Veto Initiative* (April 26, 2022):** Adopted by consensus following Russia's Ukraine vetoes, this standing mandate requires the UNGA President to convene a formal General Assembly debate **within 10 working days** whenever any P5 state casts a veto in the UNSC, compelling the vetoing power to explain its conduct before 193 nations. While raising diplomatic costs across Gaza, Syria, DPRK, and outer-space vetoes, it leaves Article 27's legal veto untouched.

### UN80 Initiative and UN Peacekeeping Reforms

Marking the UN's 80th anniversary amid an acute liquidity crunch—projected to cut UN system-wide resources by roughly **25% in 2026** following sharp US contribution cuts and Washington's **January 7, 2026 executive order exiting 66 international bodies (31 UN-affiliated)**—Secretary-General António Guterres launched the **UN80 Initiative in March 2025**. UN80 operates across **three reinforcing workstreams**:

1. **Secretariat Efficiency:** Cutting Secretariat staff positions by **21% in 2026**, consolidating administrative platforms, and relocating ~230 posts (within ~2,300 system-wide) from high-cost hubs like New York/Geneva to lower-cost duty stations.
2. **Mandate Lifecycle Discipline:** Auditing over 40,000 accumulated resolutions since 1946 to prune duplication, institutionalized via **UNGA Resolution 80/251 (adopted April 1, 2026)** establishing structured mandate review.
3. **Structural & Programme Realignment:** Reconfiguring UN Country Teams, piloting integrated humanitarian supply chains under the New Humanitarian Compact (Afghanistan, Sudan, Gaza, Haiti, Somalia), and conducting a flexible peace-operations review.

Simultaneously, **UN Peacekeeping**—informally termed **"Chapter Six-and-a-Half"** (a phrase coined by Dag Hammarskjöld because peacekeeping does not explicitly appear in the UN Charter)—faces severe operational strain across 11 active missions ($5.4 billion budget for 2025–26). **India** is a foundational pillar of Blue Helmet operations: over **2,90,000 Indian troops** have served across 50+ UN missions (5,000+ deployed across 9 missions in 2025–26), India deployed the world's **first all-women Formed Police Unit (Liberia, 2007)**, hosted the Global South Women Peacekeepers Conference (New Delhi, February 2025 via CUNPK), and bears the **highest cumulative fatality toll** of any Troop Contributing Country (179+ personnel honored with Dag Hammarskjöld Medals). India's reform agenda focuses on three imperatives: (i) **mandatory consultation with Troop Contributing Countries (TCCs)** before UNSC non-contributors draft ambitious, under-resourced mandates; (ii) **legal accountability for crimes against peacekeepers** (reinforced by India's UNSC Res 2589 and the **UNITE AWARE** geospatial situational-awareness platform); and (iii) transitioning from static buffer missions to realistic, politically anchored peacebuilding.

### The UNSC Reform Deadlock - G4, UfC, Ezulwini, L.69, and the IGN Impasse

Since the sole historical Charter amendment enlarging the UNSC from 11 to 15 members (**adopted 1963, effective August 31, 1965**), every reform proposal has collided with **Article 108 of the UN Charter**: any amendment requires a **two-thirds vote of the UNGA** *plus* ratification by **two-thirds of UN Member States including all five Permanent Members**. That constitutional lock interacts with four competing negotiating blocs inside the General Assembly's **Intergovernmental Negotiations (IGN)** process (running since 2008–09 without a single agreed negotiating text):

- **G4 Nations (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan — est. 2004):** Support mutual bids for permanent seats in an expanded Council of **25–26 members** (adding 6 permanent seats—2 Africa, 2 Asia-Pacific, 1 Latin America/Caribbean, 1 Western Europe—plus 4–5 non-permanent seats). Seeking to break the veto impasse, in **April 2026** India (via Permanent Representative P. Harish) rejected any permanent **"two-tier" membership without veto**, while backing the **G4 compromise deferring exercise of veto rights by new permanent members for 15 years** pending review. India has already served **8 elected non-permanent terms** (most recently 2021–22) and has launched its campaign for **2028–29**.
- **Uniting for Consensus (UfC / "Coffee Club"):** Led by regional rivals of G4 aspirants—**Italy, Pakistan, Spain, South Korea, Mexico, Argentina, Canada, and Türkiye**—UfC vehemently opposes creating *any* new permanent seats, advocating solely an expansion of longer-term, immediately re-electable non-permanent seats.
- **African Union (*Ezulwini Consensus* & *Sirte Declaration*, 2005 — represented by C-10):** Anchoring Africa's 54-vote bloc, the Common African Position demands **at least 2 permanent seats with full veto powers** (if the veto is not abolished altogether) plus 5 non-permanent seats, with the AU itself selecting African representatives. In **April 2026**, the African Group formally tabled its consolidated reform model inside IGN (proposing a Council of "not less than 26" members), prompting strong G4 convergence.
- **L.69 Group:** A cross-regional coalition of **42 developing countries** (including India) across Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean, and Pacific Small Island Developing States (SIDS), demanding simultaneous expansion in both permanent and non-permanent categories alongside improved working methods.
- **Why IGN Remains Stalled:** Unlike standard UN negotiations, IGN lacks formal **text-based negotiations** or an official record; China and the UfC consistently block rolling competing models into a single negotiating text—ensuring that despite rhetorical backing for India from the US, France, UK, and Russia, and the September 2024 *Pact for the Future* pledge to redress African under-representation, structural deadlock endures.

## 2. International Judicial and Arbitral Architecture

International law lacks a compulsory supreme court. Jurisdiction rests almost entirely on sovereign consent, producing a fragmented ecosystem of four major adjudicatory and arbitral institutions—frequently conflated in UPSC Prelims options.

### International Court of Justice (ICJ)

Seated at the Peace Palace in **The Hague**, the ICJ (operational 1946) is the **sole judicial principal organ of the UN**. Its **15 judges** serve **9-year terms** (one-third elected every three years; eligible for re-election), requiring simultaneous **absolute majorities in both UNGA and UNSC** voting independently—with **no P5 veto** applicable in judicial elections. Four Indians have served on the ICJ bench: Sir Benegal Narsing Rau, Nagendra Singh (also ICJ President), R.S. Pathak, and **Justice Dalveer Bhandari** (re-elected in 2017 after a landmark diplomatic contest against the UK's nominee, serving through early 2027).

- **Dual Jurisdiction:** The ICJ hears **contentious cases solely between sovereign States** (never individuals, corporations, or NGOs) based on express consent (special agreement, treaty compromissory clause, or Optional Clause declaration under Article 36(2) of the ICJ Statute; India maintains broad national-security/Commonwealth reservations on its declaration). Judgments are final and binding under **UN Charter Article 94**, yet enforcement relies solely on the UNSC—where a P5 veto can neutralize compliance. Conversely, **Advisory Opinions** (requested by UNGA, UNSC, or authorized agencies) are **non-binding**, though they powerfully codify customary international law.
- **Landmark Precedents Affecting India and Global Law:**
  - ***Kulbhushan Jadhav* (India v. Pakistan, July 2019):** By a **15–1 majority**, the ICJ ruled Pakistan violated Article 36 of the **1963 Vienna Convention on Consular Relations** by denying India consular access to Jadhav and ordered an effective review and reconsideration of his military-court death sentence while staying execution.
  - ***Chagos Archipelago* Advisory Opinion (February 2019):** Advised that Britain's 1965 excision of the Chagos Islands (including Diego Garcia) from Mauritius rendered decolonization unlawful—providing the legal lever that drove the **2024–25 UK–Mauritius treaty transferring Chagos sovereignty** while preserving the 99-year US–UK base lease.
  - ***Palestine* Advisory Opinions (2024 & 2025):** Beyond its 2004 *Wall* opinion, the ICJ delivered two transformative rulings: on **July 19, 2024**, holding Israel's prolonged occupation and settlement policies in the Occupied Palestinian Territory (OPT) unlawful and obligating third states to non-recognition; and on **October 22, 2025**, ruling on Israel's legal duties as an occupying power to facilitate UN/UNRWA humanitarian relief in Gaza.
  - ***Climate Change* Advisory Opinion (July 2025):** Following UNGA's Vanuatu-led request, the ICJ declared climate change an **"urgent and existential threat,"** anchoring state climate mitigation duties in both treaty obligations and customary international law.

### International Criminal Court (ICC - 2002 Rome Statute)

Established by the **1998 Rome Statute** (entered into force **July 1, 2002**; seated in The Hague), the ICC is an **independent treaty-based organization—NOT an organ of the United Nations**. Unlike the ICJ, the ICC prosecutes **natural persons (individuals)**—including sitting heads of state without sovereign immunity—for four core international crimes: **Genocide, Crimes Against Humanity, War Crimes, and the Crime of Aggression**.

- **Complementarity and Jurisdiction Triggers:** The ICC operates as a court of *last resort* under the **Principle of Complementarity**, intervening only when domestic judicial systems are genuinely *unwilling or unable* to prosecute. Jurisdiction activates through three routes: (i) referral by a State Party, (ii) Prosecutor *proprio motu* investigation over crimes committed on the territory or by nationals of a State Party, or (iii) **UNSC Chapter VII referral**—which controversially extends ICC jurisdiction over *non-party states* (as occurred for Sudan/Darfur and Libya).
- **Why India, the US, China, and Russia Remain Non-Signatories:** Roughly **125 countries** are States Parties, yet four major powers—**India, the United States, China, and Russia** (plus Israel and Pakistan)—stand outside. New Delhi refused to sign the Rome Statute in 1998 on four principled grounds: (1) granting the political **UNSC referral (Art 13b) and deferral (Art 16) powers** violates the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties by binding non-parties while allowing non-party P5 states (US, China, Russia) to shield themselves via veto; (2) the Statute **omitted cross-border terrorism** and the **use of nuclear weapons** from core crimes; (3) expansive definitions of non-international armed conflict risked politicized scrutiny of domestic counter-insurgency operations (e.g., J&K/Northeast under AFSPA) despite India's independent constitutional judiciary; and (4) granting the Prosecutor *proprio motu* trigger power undermined sovereign consent.
- **2025–2026 Legitimacy Fracture:** Following ICC arrest warrants against Russian President Vladimir Putin (2023) and Israeli PM Benjamin Netanyahu / former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant (November 2024), Washington escalated diplomatic sanctions and pressure against the Court. While **Hungary withdrew its exit notice on May 29, 2026** to remain a State Party, the military-led **Alliance of Sahel States (Burkina Faso, Mali, Niger)** formally deposited withdrawal notifications with the UNSG in **June 2026** (effective June 2027 under Article 127), followed by **Venezuela and Chad in July 2026**—denouncing selective Global South prosecution.

### Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) and ITLOS

- **Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA):** Established in **1899** (First Hague Peace Conference) and housed in the Peace Palace alongside the ICJ, the PCA is **neither a UN organ nor a standing judicial court with fixed judges**. It functions as an intergovernmental **arbitral registry** administering ad-hoc tribunals for state-to-state disputes, investor-state BIT disputes (e.g., *Vodafone* and *Cairn Energy* retrospective tax awards against India), and **UNCLOS Annex VII arbitrations**. Landmark decisions include:
  - ***Enrica Lexie* Award (Italy v. India, May 2020):** Held that while India's arrest of two Italian marines who fatally shot Indian fishermen off Kerala did not breach India's rights, Italy breached Indian freedom of navigation; crucially, the tribunal granted the marines **functional immunity** as state officials (transferring criminal prosecution to Italy) while awarding India compensation (₹10 crore later settled).
  - ***South China Sea Arbitration* (Philippines v. China, July 2016):** Unanimously invalidated Beijing's "Nine-Dash Line" historic-rights claims as incompatible with UNCLOS—a ruling Beijing boycotted and rejected.
  - ***Indus Waters* Proceedings:** Following its 2013 *Kishenganga* partial award, PCA-administered Court of Arbitration supplemental awards in **June/August 2025** were formally rejected by India as procedurally illegal once New Delhi placed the IWT in abeyance and insisted on Neutral Expert jurisdiction.
- **International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS):** Seated in **Hamburg, Germany** (operational 1996), ITLOS is an independent **21-judge** judicial body created by the **1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS)**—again, **not a UN organ**. It hears maritime delimitation, deep-seabed mining (Seabed Disputes Chamber), prompt-release vessel disputes, and provisional measures (such as ordering the 2015 stay in *Enrica Lexie*). In a landmark **May 2024 Advisory Opinion**, ITLOS unanimously ruled that anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions absorbed by oceans legally constitute **"marine pollution" under UNCLOS Article 1(1)(4)**, imposing binding due-diligence mitigation duties beyond the Paris Agreement.

## 3. Bretton Woods Twins vs. Non-Western Multilateral Development Banks

The July 1944 Bretton Woods Conference erected two complementary pillars—the **IMF** as macroeconomic fire-brigade and the **World Bank** as long-term reconstruction financier. Both operate on a **capital-weighted ("one dollar, one vote")** governance model reinforced by an informal Transatlantic "Gentlemen's Agreement" reserving the World Bank Presidency for an American (**Ajay Banga**, since June 2023) and the IMF Managing Directorship for a European (**Kristalina Georgieva**).

### International Monetary Fund (IMF) - Quotas, Veto, SDRs, and SAPs

Comprising **191 members** headquartered in Washington, D.C., the IMF monitors global macroeconomic stability (Article IV consultations; flagship publications *World Economic Outlook* and *Global Financial Stability Report*) and extends short-to-medium-term **Balance of Payments (BoP)** liquidity via instruments like the Extended Fund Facility (EFF), Stand-By Arrangement (SBA), **Rapid Financing Instrument (RFI)**, and concessional **Rapid Credit Facility (RCF)**. India has tapped IMF support seven times (most decisively during the 1991 BoP crisis) and has been a net creditor since 2003.

- **Quota Distortion and the US Blocking Veto:** Every member's **Quota** (denominated in SDRs, weighted roughly on GDP 50%, openness 30%, economic variability 15%, and reserves 5%) dictates four levers: capital subscription, **voting power**, borrowing ceiling, and SDR allocation share. Because major IMF structural decisions require an **85% supermajority**, the **United States (~17.43% quota / ~16.5% voting share)** wields a **unilateral blocking veto**—while **India holds just ~2.75%–2.76% quota (8th largest)** and China ~6.4% (3rd largest), wildly misaligned with Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) GDP reality.
- **Stalemate of the 16th General Review of Quotas (2023–2026):** Concluded in December 2023, the 16th Review approved a **50% equiproportional quota increase** (raising total quotas to SDR 715.7 billion)—explicitly leaving *relative quota shares unchanged* and postponing real realignment to a future quota-formula negotiation. Even this status-quo increase remains stalled: effectiveness requires written consent from members holding **>=85% of total quotas**, and with US Congressional consent delayed, the IMF Executive Board approved yet another six-month extension on **November 10, 2026, pushing the consent deadline to May 15, 2027**.
- **Special Drawing Rights (SDRs — "Paper Gold"):** Created in **1969**, SDRs are an **international reserve asset** (part of India's Forex reserves alongside foreign currency assets, gold, and Reserve Tranche Position)—**neither a sovereign currency nor a direct claim on the IMF**, but a potential claim on freely usable currencies of IMF members. Valued daily on a five-currency basket reviewed quinquennially (next review due **July 2027**), current weights (effective August 1, 2022) rank: **US Dollar (43.38%) > Euro (29.31%) > Chinese Renminbi (12.28%, inducted Oct 1, 2016) > Japanese Yen (7.59%) > Pound Sterling (7.44%)**.
- **Structural Adjustment Programmes (SAPs):** IMF bailouts historically tie disbursements to stringent Washington Consensus conditionalities—fiscal austerity, subsidy abolition, currency depreciation, and SOE privatization. While stabilizing sovereign solvency, SAPs routinely provoke pro-cyclical recession and domestic political backlash (evident across 2023–26 rescues in Sri Lanka, Pakistan, Egypt, and Kenya).

### World Bank Group - The Five-Institution Architecture

The **World Bank Group (WBG)** comprises **five distinct legal institutions**, though the term **"World Bank"** strictly denotes only the first two:

1. **IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, est. 1944):** Initially financed post-WWII European reconstruction; today provides market-rate sovereign loans and guarantees to **middle-income and creditworthy low-income countries** (funding itself primarily via AAA bond markets). India is a founding member and historically IBRD's largest cumulative borrower.
2. **IDA (International Development Association, est. 1960):** The Bank's "soft-loan window" extending zero- or ultra-low-interest credits and grants to **78 low-income nations**. Financed via triennial donor replenishments leveraged in capital markets, **IDA21 secured a historic $100 billion package in December 2024** (from $23.7B donor pledges). Crucially, **India graduated from IDA eligibility in 2014** and now contributes as an IDA donor.
3. **IFC (International Finance Corporation, est. 1956):** Largest global development institution exclusively funding **private-sector** enterprises in developing economies via equity, loans, and advisory services (also issues INR-denominated **Masala Bonds**).
4. **MIGA (Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency, est. 1988):** Provides **political-risk insurance** (covering expropriation, currency inconvertibility, war/civil disturbance) to unlock cross-border private FDI into developing states.
5. **ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes, est. 1966):** Arbitrates investor–state disputes (ISDS). **Atomic Prelims Distinction:** India is a member of **IBRD, IDA, IFC, and MIGA**, but **India is NOT a member of ICSID**, rejecting its perceived bias toward foreign multinational investors and the absence of domestic judicial review over arbitral awards.

### Non-Western Challengers - NDB (BRICS Bank) and AIIB

Frustrated by stalled Bretton Woods quota reforms and intrusive SAP conditionalities, emerging economies launched two major non-Western MDBs in 2014–2016:

- **New Development Bank (NDB — Shanghai):** Agreed at the **6th BRICS Summit (Fortaleza, 2014)** and operational from July 2015, NDB carries **$100 billion authorized capital** ($50B initial subscribed capital split **equally** among Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa). Governance explicitly rejects hegemonic dominance: **no single country holds a veto**, though the five founding BRICS members' combined voting power **can never fall below 55%**. Open to all UN members, NDB reached **10 full member countries as of June 5, 2026**—the five founders plus **Bangladesh (2021), UAE (2021), Egypt (2023), Algeria (May 19, 2025), and Uzbekistan (June 5, 2026 — first Central Asian member)**, with prospective members admitted by the Board (Uruguay, Colombia, Ethiopia, Angola, Zimbabwe, Belarus, Indonesia) awaiting deposit of accession instruments. Unanimously reappointed through **July 2030**, President **Dilma Rousseff** steered cumulative approvals to **$42.9 billion across 140 projects by Q1 2026**, notably executing **46% of 2025 lending in local currencies** (including NDB's maiden private Indian rupee loan).
- **Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB — Beijing):** Operational since **January 2016** ($100B authorized capital), AIIB rapidly scaled from 57 founding members to **111 approved members globally** (surpassing the Japan/US-led ADB's ~69 members), including major European economies (UK, Germany, France, Italy) while **the US and Japan remain non-members**. Former Chinese Vice Finance Minister **Zou Jiayi** succeeded founding President Jin Liqun on **January 16, 2026**. Regional members must retain **>=75% voting share**; **China holds ~26.4%–26.5% voting power** (giving Beijing a **unilateral veto** on 75% supermajority matters), while **India is the #2 shareholder (~7.58% voting power / ~8.6% capital subscription) AND AIIB's single largest cumulative borrower**. Despite New Delhi boycotting China's unilateral BRI, India pragmatically engages AIIB because its multilateral oversight, European board presence, and WB/ADB co-financing restrain overt Chinese geopolitical capture.

## 4. WTO Institutional Paralysis and Specialized Global Regimes

### WTO Institutional Architecture and Systemic Paralysis

Established on **January 1, 1995** via the **1994 Marrakesh Agreement** replacing GATT (1947), the Geneva-based **World Trade Organization (166 members; DG Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala)** rests on a **consensus-based ("one country, one vote")** rule-making system—making it structurally more democratic than the Bretton Woods twins yet chronically prone to deadlock. Three institutional fractures now threaten its survival:

- **Appellate Body Freeze (Since December 2019):** The WTO's binding two-tier **Dispute Settlement Mechanism (DSM)**—its historic "crown jewel"—lost appellate quorum on **December 11, 2019** after the United States systematically blocked appointments and reappointments to the 7-member **Appellate Body** (which requires 3 judges per appeal), alleging judicial overreach and missed 90-day deadlines. Losing nations now routinely **"appeal into the void,"** leaving panel rulings legally unadoptable. While the EU, China, Brazil, and ~50 members set up a stopgap **Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA)** under DSU Article 25, **India refuses to join MPIA**, insisting on full restoration of the permanent multilateral Appellate Body.
- **Erosion of MFN and S&DT:** Foundational rules of **Most Favoured Nation (MFN — non-discrimination across WTO members)** and **National Treatment** have been hollowed out by unilateral US Section 301/national-security tariffs, China's opaque state-capitalist subsidies, and green border barriers (EU CBAM). Simultaneously, developed economies demand dismantling **Special and Differential Treatment (S&DT)** self-designation so major developing economies like India and China graduate out of policy flexibilities—a red line for New Delhi given its vast rural livelihood base.
- **Plurilateral Joint Statement Initiatives (JSIs):** Unable to secure 166-nation consensus, coalitions launched plurilateral JSIs covering **Investment Facilitation for Development (IFD)**, E-Commerce, and MSMEs. **India (backed by South Africa)** legally blocks incorporating JSIs into WTO Annex 4 (which under **Article X.9 of the Marrakesh Agreement** requires *exclusive consensus* of all members), warning that plurilateral cherry-picking sidelines agriculture/development mandates and fragments the multilateral trading order.

At the **14th Ministerial Conference (MC14, March 26–30, 2026, Yaoundé, Cameroon)**, these institutional fissures converged into a visible breakdown: Ministers adjourned **without an overarching Ministerial Declaration**. Most consequentially, the **1998 multilateral moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions expired on March 30, 2026** (alongside the TRIPS Non-Violation and Situation Complaints moratorium), prompting 66 members representing ~70% of global trade to pivot toward plurilateral interim e-commerce implementation while India blocked IFD incorporation and defended consensus rules. With core negotiations on agricultural Public Stockholding (PSH), Special Safeguard Mechanism (SSM), fisheries overcapacity subsidies, and digital customs duties deferred back to Geneva, the detailed sector-specific trade mechanics are analyzed in [[10. WTO Agreement on Agriculture & MC13-MC14 Deadlocks#WTO MC13 and MC14 Yaounde Deadlocks|WTO MC14 Yaounde Deadlocks]].

### Specialized Agencies - WHO, ILO, UNESCO, and UNHCR

Beyond security and trade, four UN specialized/affiliated agencies illustrate the friction between universal technical mandates and sovereign national-interest red lines:

- **World Health Organization (WHO — Geneva, est. 1948):** Directs international health norms and the legally binding **International Health Regulations (IHR, 2005; amended 2024)**. Already battered by COVID-19 politicization, WHO suffered a historic funding and geopolitical blow when the **United States completed its formal withdrawal on January 22, 2026** (one year after President Trump's January 20, 2025 executive order). Defying this retreat, remaining WHO Member States adopted the landmark, legally binding **WHO Pandemic Agreement at the 78th World Health Assembly (WHA78) on May 20, 2025**, embedding a *One Health* approach and voluntary tech-transfer commitments. Crucially, however, under **Article 31** the treaty **cannot open for signature** until states finalize a supplementary **Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing (PABS) Annex** governing rapid pathogen-data sharing in exchange for equitable vaccine/therapeutic access (requiring 60 ratifications thereafter)—a deadline negotiators on **November 13, 2026** pushed toward **May 2027 (WHA80)**. India backs mandatory benefit-sharing while safeguarding TRIPS flexibilities for its generic pharmaceutical industry.
- **International Labour Organization (ILO — Geneva, est. 1919):** Predating the UN (Treaty of Versailles), the ILO is the **sole UN agency with a tripartite governing structure** uniting Governments, Employers, and Workers. India is a founding member holding a permanent non-elective Governing Body seat. **Critical Dual-Standard Ratification Status:** Historically comprising **8 Core/Fundamental Conventions**, the ILO elevated **Occupational Safety and Health (Conventions No. 155 and No. 187)** into the fundamental category in **June 2022**, expanding the tally to **10 Fundamental Conventions**. India has ratified **6 of 8 (legacy) / 6 of 10 (current)**—**C029 & C105** (forced labor), **C100 & C111** (equal pay & non-discrimination), and **C138 & C182** (minimum age & worst forms of child labor, ratified in 2017). India has **NOT ratified C087 (Freedom of Association)** or **C098 (Right to Organize & Collective Bargaining)**—because Central Civil Services Conduct Rules bar government servants from striking, open policy criticism, or joining foreign unions—nor the two post-2022 OSH conventions (**C155, C187**).
- **UNESCO (Paris, est. 1945/46):** Advances education, science, cultural heritage (1972 World Heritage Convention, Intangible Cultural Heritage, Man and the Biosphere reserves), and press freedom. Chronically exposed to US–Middle East politicization (having withdrawn in 1984 and 2017–18 before rejoining in 2023), **Washington announced its third withdrawal from UNESCO on July 22, 2025 (effective December 31, 2026)**. Nuanced legal distinction: exiting UNESCO does **not** automatically terminate a state's party status under the separate **1972 World Heritage Convention** or strip its inscribed sites. India remains an active cultural pillar, having hosted the 46th World Heritage Committee session in New Delhi (2024).
- **UNHCR (Geneva, est. 1950) & India's Non-Signatory Refugee Logic:** Custodian of the **1951 Refugee Convention** and its **1967 Protocol** (removing the 1951 Eurocentric geographic/temporal restrictions), UNHCR enforces the bedrock principle of ***non-refoulement*** (Article 33—forbidding expulsion of refugees to territories where life or freedom faces persecution, recognized as customary international law). **India is NOT a party to either the 1951 Convention or the 1967 Protocol**, nor has it enacted a standalone Refugee Act—governing asylum administratively under the Foreigners Act (1946), Passport Act, Standard Operating Procedures, and Supreme Court **Article 21** jurisprudence. India's strategic non-signatory rationale rests on four calculations: (1) South Asia's porous borders produce massive *mixed flows* blurring persecution and economic migration; (2) signing invites external legal oversight over acute internal-security and demographic sensitivities (Northeast, J&K, Rohingya); (3) the Convention imposes rigid socio-economic duties on developing host states without binding Northern burden-sharing; and (4) non-ratification preserves sovereign discretion while allowing India to maintain a generous *de facto* humanitarian record (hosting Tibetans, Sri Lankan Tamils, 1971 Bangladeshis, and Afghans, alongside selective UNHCR India registration in New Delhi).

### Global Commons Regimes - Antarctic Treaty System and 1967 Outer Space Treaty

- **Antarctic Treaty System (ATS — signed December 1, 1959; in force June 23, 1961):** Governs all landmass and ice shelves **south of 60°S latitude** (58 parties; 29 Consultative Parties with voting rights based on substantial scientific research). Key rules: **freezes all pre-existing territorial claims** without recognizing or extinguishing them (Article IV); reserves Antarctica exclusively for peaceful scientific purposes; prohibits military bases, nuclear explosions, and radioactive waste disposal; and under the **1991 Madrid Protocol on Environmental Protection**, designates Antarctica a natural reserve **banning commercial mineral exploitation** (reviewable only after **2048**).
  - **India's Antarctic Footprint:** Acceding in **1983** (gaining Consultative Party status the same year), India enacted the **Indian Antarctic Act, 2022** extending domestic judicial, permit, and environmental oversight over Indian expeditions, coordinated by the **National Centre for Polar and Ocean Research (NCPOR), Goa** under the **Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES)**. After decommissioning its first base (*Dakshin Gangotri*, 1983), India operates two year-round stations—**Maitri** (1989, **Schirmacher Oasis**, Queen Maud Land, East Antarctica) and **Bharati** (2012, **Larsemann Hills**, ~3,000 km east of Maitri). To replace the aging 1989 base, the Finance Ministry granted in-principle approval in **October 2025** for **Maitri-II**—a ~₹2,000 crore solar-and-wind-powered "green research base" (housing 140 summer / 40 winter personnel; ₹29.2 crore approved for DPR/design) in the Schirmacher Oasis, with MoES updating Parliament in **December 2025** that operational completion is projected for **2032** (revised from an initial 2029 estimate).
- **1967 Outer Space Treaty (OST):** Adopted under **UN COPUOS** (supported by Vienna-based **UNOOSA**) and effective **October 10, 1967**, the OST serves as the constitutional foundation of space law. Core mandates: outer space is the **"province of all mankind"** open to free exploration (Art I); strict **non-appropriation** prohibits sovereign claims over the Moon or celestial bodies (Art II); **Article IV prohibits placing nuclear weapons or any WMDs in Earth orbit** or stationing military bases/weapons on celestial bodies; and **Articles VI–VII** render launching states internationally responsible and **liable** for national space activities—including those conducted by private companies (e.g., SpaceX or Indian NewSpace startups).
  - **The Five UN Space Treaties & India's Status:** Alongside OST (1967), the UN regime comprises the **Rescue Agreement (1968)**, **Liability Convention (1972)** (imposing *absolute liability* for damage on Earth/aircraft, *fault-based liability* in orbit), **Registration Convention (1975/76)**, and **Moon Agreement (1979)** (declaring lunar resources "common heritage of mankind"). **India has ratified the first four treaties and signed—but NOT ratified—the 1979 Moon Agreement** (which only ~18 states have ratified, enabling major space powers including India to engage commercial/lunar resource norms under the 2020 US-led Artemis Accords).

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **UN Organ vs. Independent Body Trap:** **ICJ** IS a Principal Organ of the UN (and the *only* one outside New York—in The Hague). By contrast, **ICC** (Rome Statute, The Hague), **PCA** (1899 Convention, The Hague), **ITLOS** (UNCLOS, Hamburg), and **WTO** (Marrakesh Agreement, Geneva) are **NOT UN organs**.
> - **ICSID & Moon Agreement Membership Traps:** India is a member of **IBRD, IDA, IFC, and MIGA**, but **NOT ICSID** (the 5th World Bank Group arm). Similarly, India ratified 4 of the 5 UN space treaties (OST, Rescue, Liability, Registration) and signed—but **did NOT ratify**—the **1979 Moon Agreement**.
> - **ILO Core Conventions Dual-Standard Trap:** India has ratified **6 of 8 (legacy)** / **6 of 10 (post-June 2022)** ILO Fundamental Conventions. India has **NOT ratified C087** (Freedom of Association), **C098** (Collective Bargaining), nor the two 2022 OSH conventions (**C155, C187**).
> - **AIIB vs. NDB Veto & Shareholder Trap:** In **AIIB**, **China (~26.4–26.5% votes)** holds a unilateral blocking veto on 75% supermajorities, while **India is #2 shareholder (~7.58% votes)** and #1 borrower—and AIIB allows **non-Asian members** (111 total; US & Japan excluded). In **NDB**, **no country holds a veto**, founding BRICS share cannot fall below **55%**, and membership reached **10 full members** with Uzbekistan (June 2026).
> - **Outer Space Treaty Article IV Trap:** OST bans **nuclear weapons / WMDs in orbit** and weapons/bases *on celestial bodies*—it does **NOT** ban conventional weapons in Earth orbit, ground-launched anti-satellite (ASAT) missiles, or ballistic missiles briefly transiting outer space.
> - **UNSC Procedural vs. Substantive Vote Trap:** P5 **abstention or absence** does **NOT** constitute a veto. Furthermore, invoking UNGA Res 377A (*Uniting for Peace*) from the UNSC is a **procedural vote** (9/15) **immune to P5 veto**, though resulting UNGA ESS resolutions remain **non-binding**.
> - **WHO Pandemic Agreement Status (Nov 2026):** Adopted at WHA78 (May 20, 2025), it is **NOT yet open for signature or in force**—opening awaits adoption of the **PABS Annex** (targeted May 2027) followed by **60 ratifications**.

> [!info] Mains Arsenal
> - **Reformed Multilateralism Framework (India's Core GS2 Thesis):** Avoid merely chanting *"UNSC reflects 1945."* Diagnose the **three structural deadlocks**: (1) **Constitutional Lock** (UN Charter Art 108 P5 ratification + IMF 85% US supermajority veto stalling even the 16th GRQ through May 2027); (2) **Intra-South Fragmentation** (G4 vs. UfC/Coffee Club vs. Africa's Ezulwini Consensus blocking a unified IGN text); and (3) **Great-Power Weaponization & Retreat** (US exits from WHO/UNESCO/66 bodies in 2025–26, WTO Appellate Body freeze & MC14 e-commerce lapse, China's 1267 terror holds, and the Nov 2026 UNSG selection deadlock).
> - **India's Pragmatic Reform Playbook:** Pair **insider reform pressure** (G4 April 2026 15-year veto-deferral compromise, CCIT advocacy, UN80 mandate discipline, TCC consultation in peacekeeping) with **hedge-building via alternative plurilaterals** (co-founding NDB & AIIB for non-conditional capital, leading BRICS 2026 Presidency, securing G20 African Union permanent membership, and defending WTO consensus/S&DT against plurilateral JSIs).
> - **Normative Consistency Check (High-Scoring Balance):** Acknowledge the realist paradox of non-Western institutions—while NDB eliminates individual vetoes and scales local-currency lending (46% in 2025), both NDB and AIIB remain embedded in dollar bond markets and Western credit ratings, proving that **non-Western banks supplement rather than replace Bretton Woods capital**. Similarly, India's principled non-signatory posture on ICC, ICSID, and the 1951 Refugee Convention reflects deliberate **sovereignty preservation**, not isolationism.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Membership & Ratification Inversion:** UPSC swaps India's exact treaty status across institutional clusters—asserting India is a member of **ICSID** (False; member of only 4 WBG bodies), party to the **1951 Refugee Convention** or **Rome Statute/ICC** (False), ratified all **8/10 ILO Fundamental Conventions** (False; 6 ratified, missing C087/C098/C155/C187), or ratified the **1979 Moon Agreement** (False; signed only).
- **Quota, SDR & Bank Governance Traps:** Claiming the **16th IMF General Review of Quotas** increased India's relative voting share (False; it is an *equiproportional* 50% increase leaving relative shares unchanged, and remains unratified through May 2027), claiming SDR is a tradable sovereign currency or includes the Indian Rupee (False; 5-currency basket only: USD > EUR > RMB > JPY > GBP), or stating **India is the largest shareholder in AIIB** (False; China is #1, India is #2 shareholder and #1 borrower).
- **Judicial Jurisdiction & Binding Nature Traps:** Confusing **ICJ** (states only; UN principal organ; binding contentious rulings, non-binding advisory opinions) with **ICC** (individuals only; non-UN treaty body; complementarity) or **PCA** (arbitral registry with no permanent judges), or claiming UNGA **Uniting for Peace (Res 377A)** resolutions are legally binding like UNSC Chapter VII orders.
- **Polar & Specialized Treaty Traps:** Confusing **Maitri-II** (East Antarctica, Schirmacher Oasis, MoES/NCPOR, revised 2032 target) with Arctic station *Himadri* (Svalbard), or assuming US exit from **UNESCO** (effective Dec 2026) automatically cancels US obligations under the separate **1972 World Heritage Convention**.
