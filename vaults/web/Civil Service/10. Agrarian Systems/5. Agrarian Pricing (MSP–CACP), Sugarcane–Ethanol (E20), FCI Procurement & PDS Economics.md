# Chapter 5 - Agrarian Pricing (MSP–CACP), Sugarcane–Ethanol (E20), FCI Procurement & PDS Economics

India’s post-1965 food-management architecture was engineered to solve an acute calorie deficit: coax risk-averse farmers into adopting high-yielding dwarf wheat and rice by guaranteeing a price floor, procure the resulting surplus through a public monopoly, and distribute it cheaply to urban and poor consumers. Six decades later, that same machinery presides over a **structural surplus of 357–376 million tonnes of foodgrains** while locking public finance, aquifers, and cropping patterns into an increasingly expensive equilibrium.

Every major agrarian tension in contemporary India—from statutory Minimum Support Price (MSP) agitations and frozen sugar-mill margins to groundwater collapse in Punjab, food-versus-fuel ethanol trade-offs, and a **₹2.27 lakh crore** food subsidy bill—traces directly to how the Indian state sets agricultural prices and clears markets. Understanding this chapter requires tracking price signals from the CACP cost ledger through state procurement godowns all the way to the Fair Price Shop and the fuel pump.

---

## 1. The Minimum Support Price (MSP) & CACP Pricing Architecture

### Institutional Origins & Non-Statutory Mandate

The institutional trinity of India’s grain regime emerged from the **L.K. Jha Foodgrains Prices Committee (1964)**, which led to the creation of the **Agricultural Prices Commission (APC)** and the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)** in January 1965. In 1985, the APC was renamed the **Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP)** to signal a broader remit covering input-output parity and long-term agricultural investment.

CACP operates as an **attached office under the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare (MoA&FW)**. Crucially, it is an **expert advisory body, not a statutory commission**—its recommendations are neither legally binding on the Union government nor enforceable in court.

Every agricultural year, CACP submits **five separate Price Policy Reports** covering Kharif crops, Rabi crops, Sugarcane, Raw Jute, and Copra. Final approval rests strictly with the **Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA)**, chaired by the Prime Minister, which announces MSPs ahead of sowing so farmers can allocate acreage rationally.

### Crop Coverage Taxonomy - Dual-Standard Numeric Parity

UPSC routinely exploits ambiguity in the exact headcount of MSP crops, which shifts depending on whether statutory sugarcane and derived prices are bundled together. Both official framings must be retained simultaneously:

- **Standard CACP Mandate (22 MSPs + 1 FRP = 23 Commodities):** CACP recommends MSPs for **22 mandated crops** and a statutory **Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP)** for **Sugarcane**.
  - **14 Kharif Crops:** 5 Cereals (*Paddy, Jowar, Bajra, Ragi, Maize*), 3 Pulses (*Tur/Arhar, Moong, Urad*), 5 Oilseeds (*Groundnut, Sunflower Seed, Soyabean [Yellow], Sesamum, Nigerseed*), and 1 Commercial crop (*Cotton*).
  - **6 Rabi Crops:** 2 Cereals (*Wheat, Barley*), 2 Pulses (*Gram/Chana, Masur/Lentil*), and 2 Oilseeds (*Rapeseed & Mustard, Safflower*).
  - **2 Year-Round Commercial Crops:** *Copra* (Milling and Ball) and *Raw Jute*.
  - **1 Statutory Crop:** *Sugarcane* (FRP, not MSP).
- **Extended PIB Headcount (24 MSP Crops + 1 FRP):** Official MoA&FW releases frequently cite **24 MSP crops** by adding two derived crops—**Toria** (fixed on the basis of Rapeseed & Mustard MSP) and **De-husked Coconut** (fixed on the basis of Copra MSP)—whose prices are set administratively by the Ministry rather than via separate CACP cost studies.
- **Excluded Categories:** Horticultural perishables (*tomato, onion, potato, fruits*), spices (*turmeric, pepper*), and plantation crops (*tea, coffee, rubber, areca nut*) carry **no MSP**.

### CACP Cost Accounting & Multi-Factor Price Determination

While public debate obsesses over cost-plus margins, CACP formally weighs **multiple macroeconomic determinants** beyond cultivation cost: domestic and global supply-demand balances, market price trends, **inter-crop price parity** (to prevent cereal dominance from crowding out pulses), **terms of trade** between agriculture and non-agriculture, likely impact on consumer food inflation, and rational utilization of land and water resources.

To anchor its cost baseline, CACP relies on farm-level data from the Directorate of Economics and Statistics under the Comprehensive Scheme for Studying the Cost of Cultivation of Principal Crops. It computes three hierarchical cost tiers (**A2**, **A2 + FL**, and **C2**), alongside auxiliary analytical layers (**A1**, **C1**, and **C3**), where the exact accounting boundary separates routine farming expenses from imputed opportunity costs of land and capital.

#### Matrix 1 - CACP Cost Concepts (A2 / A2+FL / C2) & Pricing Benchmarks

### Swaminathan (`C2 + 50%`) vs. Active Policy (`1.5× A2 + FL`) - 2026–27 Reality

Since the **Union Budget 2018–19**, the Centre has operationalized a rule of guaranteeing at least a **50% margin over All-India weighted average Cost A2 + FL**. Actual margins vary sharply by crop, deliberately favouring Rabi cereals, oilseeds, and pulses over Kharif paddy to nudge diversification:

- **Kharif Marketing Season (KMS) 2026–27** (approved 13 May 2026): **Paddy (Common)** MSP was set at **₹2,441/qtl** (Grade A: ₹2,461/qtl) against an A2 + FL cost of ₹1,627/qtl (exact **50%** margin); **Maize** stood at ₹2,410/qtl (56%); **Tur (Arhar)** at **₹8,450/qtl** (54%); **Moong** at ₹8,780/qtl (61%); **Urad** at ₹8,200/qtl (51%); and **Yellow Soybean** at ₹5,708/qtl (50%).
- **Rabi Marketing Season (RMS) 2027–28** (approved 30 September 2026): **Wheat** MSP rose to **₹2,610/qtl** (up ₹25 from ₹2,585 in RMS 2026–27) against an A2 + FL cost of ₹1,264/qtl—delivering a **106% margin** over A2 + FL. **Rapeseed & Mustard** stood at **₹6,613/qtl** (96% margin), **Lentil (Masur)** at **₹7,390/qtl** (92%), **Gram** at ₹5,958/qtl (59%), and **Safflower** at ₹7,215/qtl (50%).

Notice two structural tensions hidden inside these figures. First, an **All-India weighted average cost** masks wide inter-state cost dispersion: paddy cultivation costs in West Bengal, Maharashtra, or Tamil Nadu frequently exceed Punjab’s highly mechanized, free-power-subsidized cost structure, squeezing farmers in higher-cost states even when the national average clears 50%.

Second, while wheat and mustard MSPs already approach or occasionally cross `C2 + 50%`, paddy, cotton, soybean, and pulses typically sit well below `1.5× C2` because imputed land rent in irrigated tracts adds ₹450–₹800 per quintal to the C2 base. Farmer unions therefore view the `A2 + FL` pivot as a fiscal dilution of the Swaminathan formula.

### The Statutory MSP Guarantee Debate - Hard Fiscal, Trade & Ecological Math

Following the 2020–21 farm-law repeal and renewed 2024–26 farmer agitations, the demand for a **legally enforceable MSP guarantee across all 23 mandated crops**—ideally at `C2 + 50%`—has become India’s most polarising agrarian policy question. Evaluating it requires disaggregating the three distinct institutional ways a statutory guarantee could be legislated:

1. **Model A - Criminalizing Private Purchases Below MSP:** Making it illegal for private traders to buy below MSP mirrors sugarcane’s FRP model. Unlike sugarcane (which must be crushed within 24 hours by a licensed factory with fixed catchment), grains, pulses, and oilseeds trade across fragmented *haats* and mandis. When global or domestic prices drop below statutory MSP, private traders simply withdraw from bidding—triggering **inventory pile-ups, informal cash kickbacks ("black-market discounts"), and severe distress sales** among smallholders lacking storage.
2. **Model B - Open-Ended Public Procurement of All Marketed Surplus:** If the state steps in as buyer of last resort for all 23 crops, the **gross capital outlay** for acquiring the full marketed surplus is estimated at **₹10–11 lakh crore annually** (CRISIL / NITI Aayog order-of-magnitude estimates). Even if the state recovers 75–85% upon resale, the **net fiscal loss** (procurement, storage, deterioration, and disposal losses) plus carrying costs could run **₹2–4 lakh crore per year**—dwarfing the entire MoA&FW Union Budget of **₹1.30 lakh crore (2026–27)** and crowding out rural irrigation, R&D, and cold-chain capital formation.
3. **Model C - Statutory Price Deficiency Payments (PDP):** Letting markets clear freely and reimbursing farmers via DBT for the gap between MSP and mandi modal price avoids physical warehousing bloat. Independent empirical estimates (e.g., CRISIL / ICRIER) place the net annual PDP fiscal bill far lower—roughly **₹21,000–₹45,000 crore** in normal price years, spiking above **₹1 lakh crore** during synchronized commodity gluts—yet PDP faces severe vulnerability to **trader-farmer collusion** in thin mandis.

Beyond fiscal math, three macroeconomic constraints bind any legal MSP mandate:

- **Inflation & Export Competitiveness:** Fixing statutory floors above international parity prices pushes up domestic CPI food inflation (where food carries ~45.9% weight in the legacy basket) and prices Indian cotton, maize, soybean meal, and non-basmati rice out of export markets without export subsidies—which are banned under the **2015 WTO Nairobi Ministerial Decision**.
- **WTO Amber Box Collision:** Under the **WTO Agreement on Agriculture (AoA)**, India’s trade-distorting **Aggregate Measurement of Support (AMS)** is capped at **10% *De Minimis*** of the value of production, measured against frozen **1986–88 External Reference Prices**. Extending statutory price-support procurement across 23 crops—especially at `C2 + 50%`—would trigger massive Amber Box breaches across oilseeds, pulses, and cotton where India lacks the **Bali Peace Clause** shield (which covers only traditional food-security crops like rice and wheat; see Chapter 10).
- **Ecological Lock-In:** Unless statutory floors are tightly conditioned on agro-ecological zoning (e.g., capping paddy procurement in over-exploited groundwater blocks), a legal guarantee risks cementing water-guzzling monocultures rather than financing climate-resilient diversification.

## 2. Revamped PM-AASHA (2024–26) - Price Assurance Beyond Cereals

Because FCI’s open-ended procurement effectively covers only paddy and wheat, non-cereal farmers historically received paper MSPs with negligible market defence. To close this gap without building a second FCI for oilseeds and perishables, the Union Government launched the **Pradhan Mantri Annadata Aay SanraksHan Abhiyan (PM-AASHA)** in **September 2018**.

On **18 September 2024**, the Union Cabinet approved a comprehensive **continuation and integration of PM-AASHA** for the 15th Finance Commission cycle up to 2025–26 with a total financial outlay of **₹35,000 crore** (plus a renewed and enhanced **₹45,000 crore central government guarantee** to lender banks for working capital, and a **₹7,200 crore** allocation in Union Budget 2026–27).

The September 2024 revamp restructured the scheme’s architecture in a way that creates high-yield Prelims traps. Originally launched with three components—PSS, PDPS, and a pilot **Private Procurement & Stockist Scheme (PPSS)**—the integrated PM-AASHA converged the **Price Stabilization Fund (PSF)** and **Market Intervention Scheme (MIS)** into a unified **four-pillar framework (PSS, PDPS, MIS, and PSF)**, quietly dropping the dormant PPSS pilot. Administratively, **PSS, PDPS, and MIS** remain with the **Department of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare (DA&FW)**, whereas **PSF** continues to be operated by the **Department of Consumer Affairs (DoCA)**.

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

### The Empirical Verdict on PM-AASHA

Between 2018–19 and late 2024, cumulative procurement of pulses, oilseeds, and copra under PSS reached **195.39 LMT** valued at **₹1.07 lakh crore**, benefiting nearly **99.3 lakh farmers**—including a record Kharif 2024–25 soybean intervention (5.62 LMT) and Bihar’s first organized NCCF masoor procurement in 2026. Yet PM-AASHA’s structural bottleneck remains **institutional asymmetry**: because procurement hinges on state requisition, mandi-fee waivers, and functional state cooperative federations, execution remains heavily skewed toward Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Karnataka, leaving eastern pulse and oilseed growers largely exposed to spot-market volatility.

## 3. Sugarcane & Ethanol (E20) Economics - From Cane Arrears to Energy Policy

### Statutory FRP vs. Political SAP - Legal Federalism & Mill Insolvency

Sugarcane stands completely outside the non-statutory MSP architecture. It is the **only crop in India with a legally binding minimum price**, governed by Clause 3(1) of the **Sugarcane (Control) Order, 1966**, issued under Section 3 of the **Essential Commodities Act, 1955**. Replaced in 2009 from the older *Statutory Minimum Price (SMP)*, the **Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP)** is recommended by CACP and approved by CCEA before every sugar season (October–September).

Under Clause 3(2)–3(3A) of the Control Order, **no sugar mill can purchase cane below FRP**, and mills must clear farmer payments within **14 days of cane delivery**, failing which they owe **15% annual interest**. For **Sugar Season 2026–27** (approved 5 May 2026), CCEA fixed FRP at **₹365/quintal** linked to a **10.25% basic sucrose recovery rate**, with a premium/deduction of **₹3.56/qtl** for every 0.1% variance above or below 10.25%. To insulate farmers supplying low-recovery mills, deductions stop at 9.5%, establishing an absolute floor of **₹338.30/qtl**. Against CACP’s A2 + FL cost of **₹182/qtl**, the 2026–27 FRP provides a massive **100.5% margin** (up from ₹355/qtl in 2025–26 and ₹340/qtl in 2024–25).

This central statutory floor collides with India’s federal sugar politics through **State Advised Prices (SAP)** announced unilaterally by Uttar Pradesh (e.g., **₹400/qtl** in 2025–26), Punjab, Haryana, and Uttarakhand. In **West Uttar Pradesh Sugar Mills Association v. State of U.P. (2020)**, a five-judge Supreme Court Constitution Bench reaffirmed *U.P. Cooperative Cane Unions Federations* (2004): because trade and commerce in sugar fall under **Entry 33 of List III (Concurrent List)**, states possess independent statutory authority to fix a price **higher than central FRP**, though never lower. Separately, in the **2025 *Raju Shetti* verdict**, the Bombay High Court quashed Maharashtra’s 2022 resolution allowing mills to split FRP into two instalments, reinforcing the strict 14-day lump-sum mandate.

### The C. Rangarajan Formula (2012) & the 2026 Ramesh Chand Committee

When sugar prices drop during cyclical global gluts, high statutory FRP/SAP forces mills to pay elevated input prices while selling sugar below cost—producing massive **cane arrears**. To break this boom-bust cycle, the **C. Rangarajan Committee (2012)** recommended partial decontrol:

- **Implemented Reforms (2013):** Abolition of the **10% Levy Sugar obligation** (where mills sold subsidized sugar to PDS below cost) and removal of the central **regulated release mechanism** (though monthly sugar-sale quotas were later reintroduced in 2018 to arrest price crashes).
- **Unresolved Core - Revenue Sharing Formula (RSF):** Rangarajan proposed linking cane prices to actual market realization—allocating farmers **70% of the ex-mill value of sugar plus primary by-products (molasses, bagasse, press-mud)** or **75% of sugar value alone**, with FRP serving as the minimum floor and a dedicated **Price Stabilization Fund** bridging deficits. Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu adopted statutory hybrid versions of RSF, whereas **Uttar Pradesh refused to abandon political SAP**.
- **Retained Spatial Monopsony:** Crucially, states retained **Cane Area Reservation** (binding farmers in a designated zone to sell exclusively to one assigned mill) and the **15 km minimum radial distance** between mills—depriving farmers of competitive price discovery while saddling mills with mandatory purchase obligations.

By 2026, a fresh **structural margin squeeze** forced policy intervention: while sugarcane FRP climbed from ₹275/qtl (2018–19) to **₹365/qtl (2026–27)**, the Centre’s ex-mill **Minimum Selling Price (MSP) of white sugar remained frozen at ₹31/kg since February 2019**. In response, the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution constituted a **high-level committee chaired by Prof. Ramesh Chand (2026)** to examine remunerative cane returns, revision of the frozen ₹31/kg white sugar MSP, independent third-party verification of mill sucrose recovery rates, weighbridge calibration, and weight deductions imposed on mechanically harvested cane.

### Ethanol Blended Petrol (E20) - Achieving the Target & the Great Feedstock Pivot

India’s structural cure for chronic sugar surpluses and cane arrears proved to be energy policy. Under the **Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) Programme** (piloted 2001, launched 2003 at E5), the **National Policy on Biofuels, 2018** originally targeted 20% ethanol blending by 2030; its **2022 Amendment** advanced the **E20 target by five years to Ethanol Supply Year (ESY) 2025–26** (November–October).

Backed by administered ex-mill ethanol prices, a 5% GST rate, and Ethanol Interest Subvention Schemes (EISS), India’s blending trajectory vaulted from **1.53% (2013–14)** to **10% (June 2022)**, **12.06% (2022–23)**, **14.60% (2023–24)**, **~19% (late 2024–25)**, and **19.99% in ESY 2025–26** (blending **1,134.36 crore litres**), effectively **achieving the 20% E20 milestone five years ahead of the original 2030 schedule**. Distillation capacity surged nearly fivefold from **421 crore litres (2014) to ~2,000 crore litres (2026)**.

Cumulatively from ESY 2014–15 through May 2026 (PIB), EBP generated **>₹1.97 lakh crore in foreign exchange savings**, substituted **~316 LMT of imported crude oil** (vital for a nation importing **~88.5%** of its crude), cut **~952 LMT of CO₂ emissions**, and channelled **>₹1.66 lakh crore to Indian farmers**. For sugar mills, ethanol revenue transformed cash flows: **99.5% of SS 2024–25 cane dues** (~₹1.02 lakh crore) and **~97% of SS 2025–26 dues** were cleared by August 2026. Having locked in E20 as base fuel, MoPNG has initiated technical consultations for a phased move toward **E25, E27, E30, and flex-fuel (E85/E100)** platforms.

Yet the most transformative shift inside India’s ethanol economy is its **rapid pivot away from sugarcane toward grains**. When erratic 2023–24 monsoons temporarily forced the Centre to restrict sugarcane juice diversion to protect domestic sugar stocks, grain distilleries surged:

- **The ESY 2025–26 Feedstock Reversal (AIDA, Nov 2026):** Grain-based feedstocks supplied **73.52% (800.53 crore litres)** of total ethanol supplies—up from just 17% in 2021–22—while sugar-based feedstocks (sugarcane juice/syrup, B-heavy molasses, C-heavy molasses) fell to **26.48% (288.38 crore litres)**, diverting **32.45 LMT of sugar**.
- **Maize as Anchor Feedstock:** **Maize alone delivered 489.71 crore litres** (~45% of supply, the single largest feedstock), incentivized by the highest administered OMC procurement price of **₹71.86/litre** in ESY 2025–26—compared to **₹65.61/L** for sugarcane juice/syrup, **₹64.00/L** for damaged foodgrains, and **₹57.97/L** for C-heavy molasses.
- **Surplus FCI Rice Allocation:** After a brief 2023–24 halt, the Centre allocated **52 LMT of surplus FCI rice per year** for both ESY 2024–25 and ESY 2025–26, raising FCI rice’s ethanol feedstock share to nearly **24.6%** in ESY 2025–26.

### The Four-Fold *Food vs. Fuel*, Fiscal & Groundwater Paradox

Despite undeniable energy-security and cane-arrear gains, India’s E20 success exposes four deep economic and ecological trade-offs that UPSC Mains routinely tests:

1. **The Groundwater & Virtual-Water Paradox:** Sugarcane is a 12–18 month crop requiring **1,500–2,500 mm of water**; producing **1 litre of sugarcane ethanol consumes ~2,500–3,000 litres of water**. India’s sugarcane area expanded from 49.27 lakh ha (2015–16) to **58.87 lakh ha (2025–26)**, outputting **500 MMT**—heavily concentrated in water-stressed Maharashtra (Marathwada/Western Maharashtra) and western UP. While pivoting to rainfed/kharif maize slashes water intensity per litre of ethanol, diverting FCI rice into fuel effectively converts Punjab–Haryana’s mined groundwater into automotive petrol.
2. **The Hidden Fiscal Subsidy in FCI-Rice Ethanol:** During ESY 2024–25, FCI sold surplus rice to ethanol distilleries at **₹22.50/kg**, whereas FCI’s **economic cost of rice stood at ₹40.42/kg (FY25 RE) and ~₹41.73/kg (FY26)**. Every kilogram of FCI rice burned in petrol tanks thus embedded an **implicit ₹18–19/kg taxpayer subsidy** inside the food subsidy bill—partially offsetting the headline foreign-exchange savings from crude substitution.
3. **The Food–Feed Inflation Squeeze:** Surging ethanol demand transformed India’s maize economy almost overnight. Because maize is the primary energy source for India’s poultry and dairy feed industries (consuming ~60% of domestic output), ethanol’s claim on ~10–12 million tonnes of maize pushed spot prices above MSP, raised poultry/egg feed inflation, and briefly turned India from a net maize exporter into an importer. Mitigating this demands scaling **ICAR–IIMR’s high-yielding maize catchment clusters** and accelerating **2G (second-generation) cellulosic ethanol** from rice straw and crop residues under the **Pradhan Mantri JI-VAN Yojana**.
4. **Vehicular Efficiency & Distillery Overcapacity:** Because ethanol holds ~33% lower energy density than petrol, pre-2023 E10-calibrated vehicles experience a **3–7% drop in fuel economy** on E20 (though ARAI/MoPNG confirmed in July 2026 that no widespread engine breakdowns occurred). Simultaneously, installed distillation capacity (~2,000 crore litres) already far exceeds E20 blending demand (~1,050–1,200 crore litres), creating debt-servicing stress for leveraged grain distilleries whenever feedstock rules fluctuate.

## 4. FCI Procurement, Buffer Stock Economics & Public Distribution Revamp

### The FCI Architecture, Regional Skew & Decentralized Procurement (DCP)

Established as a **statutory corporation under the Food Corporations Act, 1964** (operational January 1965), FCI executes three mandates: price support operations at MSP, nationwide grain distribution for PDS/welfare schemes, and maintenance of operational and strategic buffer reserves.

In practice, FCI’s **open-ended procurement** (buying every quintal of FAQ paddy and wheat offered at procurement centres) produces acute geographic concentration. Per **PRS India’s 2026–27 analysis**, **92% of India’s Central Pool wheat** comes from just **three states** (Madhya Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana), and **65% of paddy** comes from **five states** (Punjab, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha/UP). The **Shanta Kumar Committee (2015)** famously calculated from NSSO 70th-Round data that **only ~6% of Indian farming households** directly sold produce to public procurement agencies. While digitized procurement expanded beneficiary reach to **1.15 crore paddy farmers** (813.88 LMT paddy in KMS 2024–25) and **25.13 lakh wheat farmers** (300.35 LMT wheat in RMS 2025–26), dense procurement infrastructure remains largely absent across Bihar, Assam, Jharkhand, and Eastern UP—depressing farm-gate realizations in India’s poorest belt.

To correct this centralized bottleneck, the Centre introduced the **Decentralized Procurement (DCP) Scheme in 1997**. Under DCP, state governments directly procure, store, and distribute foodgrains against their NFSA/Other Welfare Scheme (OWS) allocations, handing over only the net surplus to FCI for the Central Pool (or drawing shortfalls from FCI), while the Department of Food & Public Distribution (DFPD) reimburses the state’s incurred **Economic Cost**.

DCP expanded significantly from 10 rice and 5 wheat states in 2014 to **17 States for rice and 9 States for wheat** by 2024–26. It slashed two-way inter-state railway haulage, lowered transit losses, and enabled local procurement of preferred varieties and coarse grains (e.g., Chhattisgarh’s paddy model and Odisha’s *Shree Anna* procurement). However, DCP suffers from a serious fiscal friction: states like Madhya Pradesh and Telangana must borrow tens of thousands of crores upfront as working capital, and any **delay or disallowance in central Economic Cost reimbursements** triggers mounting state interest burdens.

### FCI Economic Cost, Buffer Norms & OMSS(D) Dynamics

The **Economic Cost of Foodgrains** to FCI determines India’s fiscal food-subsidy liability and was directly interrogated in **UPSC Prelims 2019**. It consists of exactly three additive heads:

$$\text{Economic Cost} = \text{Pooled Cost of Grain (MSP + Bonus)} + \text{Procurement Incidentals} + \text{Distribution Cost}$$

- **Pooled Cost of Grain (~78–80% of total):** Weighted average MSP (plus state bonus, if any) paid to farmers.
- **Procurement Incidentals (~10–13%):** Statutory state mandi fees, rural development cess, *Arhtiya* commissions (historically reaching 8.5% in Punjab), gunny bags (mandatory jute packaging quotas), custom milling charges, and mandi-to-depot handling.
- **Distribution Cost (~7–10%):** Inter-state railway/road freight, godown storage rentals, bank interest on working capital, FCI administrative overheads, and transit/storage losses.

By FY 2024–25 (RE) and FY 2025–26, FCI’s Economic Cost climbed to **₹40.42–₹41.73/kg for rice** and **₹28.50–₹29.80/kg for wheat**. Because the Union Government merged pandemic relief into NFSA and converted **Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY)** into **100% free foodgrains** (effective 1 January 2023 and extended for five years from **1 January 2024 to December 2028** at an estimated **₹11.80 lakh crore** outlay), the **Central Issue Price (CIP) dropped to ₹0/kg** (down from NFSA Schedule I statutory ceilings of ₹3 rice / ₹2 wheat / ₹1 coarse grains).

Consequently, the consumer subsidy now equals **100% of FCI’s Economic Cost**. In **Union Budget 2026–27**, total food subsidy under PMGKAY stood at **₹2,27,429 crore** (97% of DFPD’s ₹2,35,047 crore allocation, roughly **0.6% of GDP**—accounting, along with fertilizer subsidy, for ~87% of total central subsidies). Note a crucial fiscal-accounting milestone: prior to **2020–21**, the Centre suppressed its headline fiscal deficit by pushing FCI into off-budget borrowing from the **National Small Savings Fund (NSSF)**; clearing those NSSF dues on-budget in 2020–21 caused food subsidy to spike briefly to 3.6% of GDP before restoring genuine fiscal transparency.

To guarantee uninterrupted PDS supply and drought insurance, CCEA prescribes quarterly **Foodgrain Stocking Norms for the Central Pool** (effective January 2015), combining **Operational Stocks** with a fixed **50 LMT Strategic Reserve (30 LMT Wheat + 20 LMT Rice)**:

- **1 April:** 135.80 LMT Rice + 74.60 LMT Wheat = **210.40 LMT**
- **1 July:** 135.40 LMT Rice + 275.80 LMT Wheat = **411.20 LMT**
- **1 October:** 102.50 LMT Rice + 205.20 LMT Wheat = **307.70 LMT**
- **1 January:** 76.10 LMT Rice + 138.00 LMT Wheat = **214.10 LMT**

Because procurement is open-ended while annual NFSA/OWS allocation hovers near **600 LMT**, actual Central Pool inventories routinely swell far above stocking norms—reaching **736.61 LMT on 1 July 2025** (377.83 LMT rice + 358.78 LMT wheat against the 411.20 LMT norm). Excess stocks inflate **buffer carrying costs** and deteriorate in quality, compelling FCI to liquidate grain via the **Open Market Sale Scheme – Domestic [OMSS(D)]** through weekly e-auctions to flour millers, processors, and state governments (in 2026–27, the Centre doubled the OMSS(D) rice disposal target from **25 LMT to 50 LMT**), alongside subsidized retail sales like **Bharat Atta and Bharat Rice (₹29/kg)**. While OMSS(D) cools off-season CPI spikes, selling grain below FCI’s ₹41/kg Economic Cost directly widens the exchequer’s food subsidy loss and crowds out private warehousing arbitrage.

### Shanta Kumar High-Level Committee (2015) - Restructuring Audit

Tasked with re-engineering FCI from a bloated monopoly into an agile food-security agency, the **Shanta Kumar Committee (2015)** issued six landmark recommendations—whose uneven decade-long implementation illustrates the political economy of Indian food reform:

- **1. Geographic Realignment of Procurement:** Hand over routine procurement in surplus, capacity-rich states (Punjab, Haryana, MP, Chhattisgarh, AP, Odisha) to state agencies, redirecting FCI toward **Eastern India** (Eastern UP, Bihar, West Bengal, Assam) where smallholders suffer distress sales. *Status:* Partially achieved via DCP expansion (17 rice / 9 wheat states), yet Bihar and Assam still lack robust procurement ecosystems.
- **2. Direct MSP Payments & Negotiable Warehouse Receipts (NWR):** Route MSP directly into farmer bank accounts (ending *Arhtiya* commission capture) and promote **e-NWR pledge loans** so farmers hold stocks rather than panic-selling at harvest. *Status:* Direct Bank Transfer (DBT) of MSP is now near-universal across Punjab, Haryana, and major procuring states; e-NWR scaling remains modest.
- **3. Rationalizing NFSA Coverage & Reforming Issue Prices:** Prune NFSA population coverage from **67% down to 40%** and index Central Issue Prices to MSP rather than freezing them at ₹1–₹3/kg. *Status:* **Politically reversed**—coverage remains ~80 crore (~67% of 2011 Census), and PMGKAY drove CIP all the way down to **₹0** through December 2028.
- **4. Gradual Shift to Cash Transfers (DBT in PDS):** Replace physical grain distribution with cash transfers starting in **cities >1 million population** and grain-surplus states, indexing transfers to food inflation. *Status:* Confined only to three Union Territories (**Chandigarh, Puducherry, and urban Dadra & Nagar Haveli**); states resisted over inflation-erosion risks and intra-household spending leakage.
- **5. Storage Modernization & Phasing Out CAP:** Outsource stocking to **CWC, SWCs, and private players** via the **Private Entrepreneurs Guarantee (PEG)** scheme and PPP **hub-and-spoke steel silos**, while abolishing unscientific **Covered and Plinth (CAP)** open storage. *Status:* **High success**—CAP storage has been largely phased out; total covered Central Pool storage exceeds **761 LMT**, with railway-linked steel silos scaling rapidly.
- **6. Capping Statutory State Levies & Bonuses:** Cap heterogeneous state mandi taxes/cesses at a uniform **~3%** (down from 8.5–14.5%) and discontinue distortionary state MSP bonuses that crowd out private grain buyers. *Status:* Central norms curbed state bonuses from Central Pool reimbursement, though state-level political bonuses (e.g., Chhattisgarh, MP) periodically resurface via state treasuries.

### SMART-PDS, AI Logistics & the February 2026 Rice Fortification Pause

Between 2020 and 2026, DFPD transformed PDS from a paper-ledger network plagued by ~28–40% historical leakage into one of the world’s largest real-time digital logistics stacks:

- **ONORC & SMART-PDS (2023–26):** **One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC)** achieved 100% nationwide portability across all **36 States/UTs**, logging over **107 crore portability transactions by March 2026**, supported by the **Mera Ration 2.0** app. Approved for 2023–26, **SMART-PDS** replaced fragmented state NIC software with a unified cloud-native national digital backbone integrating ration-card lifecycle management, e-PoS biometric authentication, allocation, FPS dealer margins, and grievance redressal.
- **AI Supply-Chain & Smart Warehousing Stack (2024–26):** Built with the **World Food Programme (WFP)** and **IIT-Delhi (FITT)**, **Anna Chakra** deploys linear-programming and AI route optimization across PDS depots and Fair Price Shops—already saving **~₹250 crore** in public freight expenditure and reducing transport emissions. Parallel platforms include **SCAN** (*Subsidy Claim Application for NFSA*, a single-window portal streamlining DCP state subsidy settlements), **Depot Darpan** (digital warehouse quality grading), **ASHA** (AI beneficiary-feedback analytics), and the **Smart Warehousing System (launched June 2026)**—integrating FASTag/ANPR gates, AI bag-counting cameras, geo-tagged smart locks, and IoT temperature/moisture sensors across FCI and CWC godowns.

**Rice Fortification & the Crucial February 2026 Strategic Pause:** Following a 2019 15-district pilot, the Centre scaled up mandatory rice fortification in three phases—blending custom-milled rice at a **1:100 ratio** with extruded **Fortified Rice Kernels (FRK)** enriched with **Iron, Folic Acid, and Vitamin B12** per FSSAI standards—achieving 100% replacement across TPDS, ICDS, and PM-POSHAN by March 2024. On **9 October 2024**, the Union Cabinet extended universal free fortified rice supply until **December 2028** as a 100% Central Sector Initiative (**₹17,082 crore** outlay within PMGKAY).

However, on **27 February 2026**, DFPD announced a **temporary discontinuation/pause on new fortified rice procurement under PMGKAY and allied schemes** pending redesign of the nutrient-delivery mechanism. The pivot rested on hard operational evidence:

- **IIT Kharagpur Shelf-Life Study:** Real-world testing revealed that heat, ambient humidity, and **FCI’s 2–3 year public-storage cycle** caused severe micronutrient degradation inside blended bags—including **50–75% loss of Vitamin B12**—exposing a fundamental mismatch between FCI’s slow-moving buffer architecture and labile micronutrient stability.
- **Clinical & Testing-Capacity Constraints:** Earlier white papers by **NITI Aayog and ICMR–NIN (2023)** had flagged modest standalone impacts on anaemia, while **PRS India (Dec 2025)** noted that **13 states and 8 UTs** (including Bihar, Andhra Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh, hosting 13.4 crore beneficiaries) lacked a single FSSAI-notified batch-testing laboratory. Medical groups also flagged iron-overload risks for **thalassemia and sickle-cell anaemia** patients concentrated in tribal belts, reinforcing the shift toward targeted nutrition, millets (*Shree Anna*), and dietary diversification rather than blanket staple fortification.

### Welfare Convergence - NFSA, PMGKAY & Rural Wage Floors

India’s grain-pricing and procurement complex does not operate in isolation from rural labour and social-protection markets. Free universalized grain provisioning under PMGKAY/NFSA effectively lowers the cash subsistence floor for rural households, reducing distress-induced land alienation while keeping rural real wages from collapsing during crop failures.

Simultaneously, India’s statutory rural wage-employment guarantee—overhauled from MGNREGA into the **Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) (VB-G RAM G) Act, 2025** (in force from 1 July 2026)—directly shapes agricultural cost of production (`Cost A2 + FL`) by lifting rural reservation wages during lean seasons while incorporating seasonal no-work calibrations to avoid acute sowing/harvesting labour shortages on farms. For complete statutory provisions, entitlement design, and social-security governance architecture, jump directly to [[8. Social Protection I - Poverty, Food Security & VB-G RAM G#VB-G RAM G Act 2025 and Rural Livelihoods|VB-G RAM G Act 2025 & NFSA]].

## 5. Direct Income Transfers & the Doubling Farmers' Income (DFI) Roadmap

### Decoupled Direct Transfers - PM-KISAN & State Variants

Because MSP procurement favours large-surplus wheat and paddy growers in a handful of states—and creates trade-distorting **Amber Box** exposure at the WTO—Indian policymakers increasingly turned toward **decoupled Direct Income Support** after 2018.

Launched on **24 February 2019** (retrospectively effective from **1 December 2018**), the **Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN)** is a **100% Central Sector Scheme** administered by MoA&FW (while States/UTs verify beneficiary land records). Eligible landholding farmer families (defined strictly as husband, wife, and minor children) receive **₹6,000 per year** in **three equal ₹2,000 DBT instalments** into Aadhaar-seeded bank accounts, subject to exclusion criteria (institutional landholders, income-tax payers, serving/retired government employees and pensioners drawing ≥₹10,000/month excluding Class IV/MTS staff, constitutional post holders, and registered professionals).

On **31 July 2026**, the Union Cabinet approved the **continuation of PM-KISAN from 2026–27 to 2030–31** with a **₹3.15 lakh crore** outlay (Union Budget 2026–27 allocation: **₹63,500 crore**). Following the **24th instalment released on 30 October 2026** (reaching ~9.34 crore farmers), cumulative transfers surpassed **₹4.64 lakh crore**, with women accounting for roughly **1 in 4 beneficiaries** (>₹1.06 lakh crore).

Economically, PM-KISAN excels on three counts: it is **crop-neutral** (does not incentivize rice–wheat monocropping), **price-non-distorting** (leaves market price discovery intact), and **WTO Green Box compliant** (Annex 2 decoupled income support). Yet it suffers from two glaring structural flaws:

- **The Tenant & Landless Exclusion Trap:** Because eligibility rests on recorded land ownership (*pattas* / Record of Rights), India’s estimated 15–30% **informal oral sharecroppers, tenant cultivators, and landless agricultural labourers** receive zero coverage.
- **Inflation Erosion:** Frozen at a nominal ₹6,000/year since December 2018, its real purchasing power for seeds and fertilizers has eroded by roughly a third by late 2026.

State-level direct transfer architectures offer instructive design contrasts:

- **Telangana (*Rythu Bandhu* → *Rythu Bharosa*):** Pioneered per-acre pre-season investment support (₹10,000/acre under *Rythu Bandhu*, recast as *Rythu Bharosa* targeting ₹12,000/acre for cultivable land). Per-acre transfers scale proportionally with holding size, making them **regressive toward large landowners** unless capped, though highly effective at easing Kharif/Rabi input credit crunches.
- **Andhra Pradesh (*YSR Rythu Bharosa* → *Annadata Sukhibhava*, 2025):** Distinguished itself by integrating **tenant farmers** via formal Crop Cultivator Rights Cards (CCRCs) and permitting nominee payments upon landholder death.
- **Odisha (*KALIA* → *CM-KISAN*):** Pioneered **equity-centric targeting** by allocating a dedicated cash transfer stream exclusively to **landless agricultural households** for allied livelihoods (goatery, poultry, mushroom units) alongside small/marginal cultivators.
- **West Bengal (*Krishak Bandhu*):** Combines seasonal per-acre assistance with a built-in **₹2 lakh life-insurance death benefit** for cultivators aged 18–60.

### The Ashok Dalwai Committee (DFI) & Empirical Farm-Income Reality

In April 2016, the Union Government constituted the **Ashok Dalwai Inter-Ministerial Committee** to craft a blueprint for **Doubling Farmers' Real Income (DFI) by 2022–23** over a **2015–16 base** (requiring ~10.4% annual real farm-income growth). Its 14-volume report fundamentally reframed Indian agricultural policy—shifting focus from **gross output ("production-centric")** to **net household surplus ("income-centric")** across **seven engines of income growth** (six farm-and-market engines plus one structural macroeconomic exit):

1. **Improvement in crop productivity** (closing yield gaps via HYVs, soil health, and micro-irrigation).
2. **Improvement in livestock productivity** (dairy, poultry, fisheries—high-income-elasticity growth drivers).
3. **Resource-use efficiency and reduction in cost of production** (precision inputs, balanced NPK, solarization).
4. **Increase in cropping intensity** (utilizing rice fallows in Eastern India for short-duration pulses/oilseeds).
5. **Diversification toward high-value agriculture** (horticulture, floriculture, agro-forestry, spices, sericulture).
6. **Improvement in real prices received by farmers** (e-NAM, FPOs, food processing, warehouse pledge finance—improving farmer-to-consumer terms of trade).
7. **Shift of surplus workforce from farm to non-farm occupations** (recognizing that ~46% of India’s workforce cannot sustainably divide ~16–18% of national GVA).

How did ground-level farm incomes actually evolve? Evaluating this requires **Dual-Standard Empirical Hygiene**, because India’s two primary national surveys use distinct methodologies and sampling frames:

- **NSO Situation Assessment Survey (SAS) 77th Round (2018–19):** The last comparable official all-India NSO benchmark recorded average monthly agricultural household income at **₹10,218** on a paid-out cost basis—falling to **₹8,337** once imputed family labour and own-farm inputs are deducted. Crucially, **wages (39.8%) surpassed net crop cultivation (37.2%)** as the single largest income source, followed by **animal farming (15.5%)** and non-farm business (6.3%); **50.2% of agricultural households were indebted** (average loan ₹74,121). Between SAS 2012–13 (₹6,426) and 2018–19, nominal income rose ~59%, yet real inflation-adjusted income grew just **~16%** (~2.5% CAGR), with massive spatial inequality: **Meghalaya (₹29,348), Punjab (₹26,701), and Haryana (₹22,841)** towered over **Jharkhand (₹4,895), Bihar (₹7,542), and Uttar Pradesh (₹8,061)**.
- **NABARD All India Rural Financial Inclusion Survey (NAFIS, 2021–22):** Estimated average monthly agricultural household income at **₹13,661** (up from ₹8,931 in NAFIS 2016–17). Because NAFIS definitions differ from NSO SAS—and **no new NSO SAS round has been released since 2018–19**—no official statistical verification exists confirming whether the 2022–23 real-doubling target was met. The empirical consensus is clear: **price policy (MSP) alone cannot double smallholder incomes** when average landholdings sit below 1.08 ha; durable prosperity requires combining **allied livestock/horticulture diversification, post-harvest value capture, and non-farm rural industrialization**.
