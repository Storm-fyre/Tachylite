# Chapter 9 - Information Warfare, Cognitive Operations, IT Rules 2026 (Deepfakes) & Online Radicalization

Modern conflicts no longer begin with troop mobilization across borders; they begin inside the human cognitive layer. When hostile actors weaponize recommendation algorithms, generative AI deepfakes, and end-to-end encrypted messaging apps, they can fracture social cohesion, delegitimize constitutional institutions, and trigger public-order breakdowns without firing a single artillery shell.

For India—a linguistically diverse democracy with over 800 million social media users—information security presents an acute constitutional paradox. Under-regulating digital platforms exposes internal security to cross-border disinformation, communal riots, and virtual radicalization; over-regulating them chills political dissent, investigative journalism, and democratic deliberation protected under **Article 19(1)(a)**. Navigating that razor's edge defines India's 2021–2026 regulatory overhaul and its landmark High Court and Supreme Court battles.

## Cognitive Operations, Information Warfare and Generative AI Disruption

### Fifth-Generation Warfare, Cognitive Operations and FIMI

Strategic doctrine classifies modern perception targeting under **Fifth-Generation Warfare (5GW)** and **Cognitive Domain Operations (CDO)**. Whereas classical wars targeted territory and armed forces, and fourth-generation insurgencies targeted state stamina, cognitive operations directly target *how citizens and decision-makers perceive reality*—seeking victory through epistemic confusion, institutional distrust, and societal paralysis rather than physical occupation.

Hostile states institutionalize this discipline through distinct strategic frameworks:
- **PLA "Three Warfares" Doctrine:** China operationalizes cognitive coercion through the synchronized deployment of **Public Opinion (Media) Warfare** (shaping global and domestic narratives before crises), **Psychological Warfare** (deterring adversary elites and eroding public morale), and **Legal Warfare (Lawfare)** (manufacturing favourable interpretations of international norms and territorial claims). Integrated with AI-driven open-source intelligence synthesis, this doctrine treats peacetime information shaping as decisive combat preparation.
- **Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference (FIMI):** Popularized by the European Union's External Action Service (and operationalized via the November 2025 European Democracy Shield), FIMI deliberately shifts regulatory focus away from policing *speech truth* toward disrupting *behavioral manipulation*—identifying coordinated, covert, non-illegal patterns of foreign state-backed inauthentic amplification, botnets, and proxy media networks.
- **The MDM Taxonomy:** Analytical precision separates **Misinformation** (false content shared without malicious intent), **Disinformation** (deliberately fabricated content engineered to deceive or destabilize), and **Malinformation** (genuine private information—such as leaked personal data or out-of-context historical clips—weaponized strategically to inflict communal or reputational harm).

India confronted full-spectrum wartime information warfare during **Operation Sindoor (May 2025)** following the April 22, 2025 Pahalgam terror attack. Alongside kinetic drone and missile exchanges, coordinated external propaganda networks flooded X, YouTube, Instagram, and WhatsApp with recycled video-game clips, AI-altered airstrike footage, fabricated casualty claims, and communal provocations aimed at fracturing domestic unity. India's counter-architecture combined a **24×7 Centralized Inter-Ministerial Control Room** (uniting Army, Navy, Air Force, and Press Information Bureau officers), transparent operational briefings backed by satellite imagery, rapid **PIB Fact Check Unit** rebuttals, and the blocking of **over 1,400 anti-India propaganda URLs**.

### Algorithmic Fault-Line Polarization and Generative AI Deepfakes

External adversaries rarely invent domestic cleavages from scratch; they exploit the commercial architecture of Web 2.0. Social media ranking algorithms optimize for dwell time and engagement—metrics empirically maximized by moral outrage, tribal binaries, and communal fear rather than nuance. Automated botnets and coordinated troll networks cheaply game these engagement signals to push fringe provocations into mainstream feeds, trapping citizens inside self-reinforcing **echo chambers** and **filter bubbles**.

**Generative Artificial Intelligence** has dramatically collapsed the cost and technical threshold of persuasive fabrication. Powered by **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)**, diffusion architectures, and neural text-to-speech cloning, **deepfakes** (hyper-realistic synthetic audio, image, and video) create four severe security and governance failure modes:

1. **Public-Order and Communal Flashpoints:** Synthetic videos depicting fabricated religious desecration, altered hate speeches, or staged atrocities can ignite localized mob violence far faster than police verification mechanisms can debunk them.
2. **Electoral Subversion:** Deployed across India's 2024 general elections and 2025–2026 state polls, AI-cloned candidate voices and doctored withdrawal announcements distort voter autonomy during the final 48-hour silence period before polling.
3. **Economic Destabilization and Executive Fraud:** Hyper-realistic synthetic videos of regulators, stock-exchange chiefs (such as fabricated market-advisory videos targeting financial leadership in 2026), or corporate CEOs trigger panic trading and high-value wire-transfer fraud.
4. **The "Liar's Dividend":** Perhaps the deepest systemic corrosion—once citizens know any audio or video *can* be faked, malicious actors can effortlessly dismiss authentic investigative footage, leaked evidence, or genuine official recordings as "AI deepfakes," destroying shared civic truth.

### Personal Data Fueling Cognitive Manipulation

Micro-targeted perception operations and non-consensual synthetic impersonation rely fundamentally on large-scale harvesting of personal, behavioral, and biometric datasets. Scraping voiceprints, facial biometrics, and psychographic preference graphs enables hostile actors to tailor disinformation to hyper-local linguistic and caste-community anxieties, while compliance workflows under synthetic-media verification rules themselves process sensitive user identity and content-review records.

Within India's statutory ecosystem, the lawful processing boundaries, consent mechanics, fiduciary duties, and sovereign national-security exemptions governing those underlying citizen datasets reside in the statutory personal-data regime rather than intermediary content rules—explored comprehensively under [[5. Digital Governance, DPI & DPDP Architecture#DPDP Act 2023 and 2025 Rules|DPDP Act 2023 & 2025 Rules]].

## Online Radicalization, Encrypted Ecosystems and Counter-Extremism

### Virtual Caliphates and Decentralized Terror Recruitment

The territorial collapse of the physical Islamic State "Caliphate" in Iraq and Syria did not extinguish transnational jihadist recruitment; it accelerated its migration into borderless **virtual-caliphate ecosystems**. Extremist outfits now radicalize, train, and task Indian youth remotely without requiring physical travel to conflict zones or vulnerable cross-border training camps.

Two transnational networks dominate India's online counter-radicalization threat matrix:
- **Islamic State–Khorasan Province (ISIS-K):** Operates a sophisticated multilingual propaganda apparatus anchored by the ***Al-Azaim Media Foundation*** and its digital flagship magazine ***Voice of Khurasan***. Tailoring grievance narratives to Indian socio-political flashpoints in English, Hindi, Urdu, Tamil, and Malayalam, ISIS-K explicitly promotes decentralized attacks and self-directed violence. In January 2025, Issue 43 of *Voice of Khurasan* underscored the operational centrality of encrypted broadcast apps to sustaining clandestine networks.
- **Al-Qaeda in the Indian Subcontinent (AQIS):** Combines ideological literature produced by regional clerics with tightly compartmentalized domestic sleeper modules communicating over encrypted channels such as **Telegram** and **Rocket.Chat**. Persistent domestic subversion is reflected in the National Investigation Agency's (NIA) **January 2026 chargesheet** in the Gujarat AQIS online radicalization conspiracy and pan-India searches across 20 locations in 10 states in **July 2026** targeting online caliphate networks.

### The Three-App Funnel, Lone Wolves, Gaming Chatrooms and Crypto Funding

NIA forensic reconstructions reveal that modern handlers execute a **Multiplatform Communication Paradigm (MCP)**—often termed the **"Three-App Model"**—to evade single-platform moderation and intelligence surveillance:

1. **Algorithmic Scouting (Open Platforms):** Handlers monitor public social media (Instagram, Facebook, YouTube) to identify ideologically curious, socially alienated, or aggrieved youth engaging with polarizing religious or political reels.
2. **Closed Grooming and Tactical Instruction (Encrypted Messengers):** Recruits are migrated to **Telegram** (which blends broadcast channels, bots, and encrypted chats), **Signal**, or niche decentralized platforms, where handlers operate behind VPNs or overseas proxies (from Syria, Afghanistan, or Sub-Saharan Africa) to deliver ideological indoctrination and DIY improvised explosive device (IED) manuals.
3. **Micro-Financing (Cryptocurrency Wallets):** Rather than exposing cells via traceable bank transfers or physical *hawala* couriers, handlers disburse small operational grants via **cryptocurrency wallets** and privacy-enhanced virtual digital assets to procure chemicals, electronics, or local hideouts.

Two auxiliary digital vectors intensify detection difficulty:
- **Online Gaming Chatrooms:** Extremist recruiters increasingly infiltrate **multiplayer gaming lobbies and VoIP/chat ecosystems** (such as Discord servers and in-game voice channels). Ephemeral voice audio leaves sparse forensic logs, demographic skew captures impressionable adolescents, and first-person shooter environments enable the **gamification of violence**—blurring ideological grooming into peer camaraderie.
- **Self-Directed Lone-Wolf Actors:** Bypassing formal terror-group induction drastically shrinks intelligence footprints. Incidents such as the **October 2022 Coimbatore car-bomb blast** and the **March 2024 Bengaluru Rameshwaram Café IED explosion**—alongside the busted Pune ISIS bomb-making module—illustrate how self-radicalized operatives or micro-cells move from digital indoctrination to kinetic strikes with minimal external logistical trails.

### India's Counter-Radicalization and De-Radicalization Response

Countering online extremism requires synchronizing coercive legal disruption with preventive psychological diversion:
- **Statutory Disruption:** The **Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA), 1967** (as amended in 2019 to designate *individuals* as terrorists) and the **NIA Act, 2008** (expanded in 2019 to cover **cyber-terrorism** and **extraterritorial offences** against Indian citizens/interests) anchor prosecution of remote handlers. Under the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023**, **Section 113** (terrorist acts), **Section 152** (acts endangering sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India via electronic communication), **Section 196** (promoting enmity between groups), and **Section 353** (circulating false statements conducing to public mischief) modernize domestic criminal deterrence.
- **State De-Radicalization Models:** Recognizing that incarcerating impressionable youth radicalized online often transforms prisons into jihadist networking hubs, state police units—notably **Maharashtra ATS**, **Telangana Police**, and **Kerala Police**—deploy quiet, family-centric de-radicalization programmes combining clinical psychologists, mainstream community clerics, vocational rehabilitation, and non-stigmatizing mentorship for early-stage online recruits.

## Statutory Speech Boundaries, Interception and Intermediary Safe Harbour

### Constitutional Baseline - Article 19(2), Shreya Singhal and Anuradha Bhasin

All digital speech regulation in India must survive constitutional scrutiny under **Article 19(1)(a)** (freedom of speech and expression), which can be restricted *only* by law on the **eight exhaustive grounds** enumerated in **Article 19(2)**: *(1) sovereignty and integrity of India, (2) security of the State, (3) friendly relations with foreign States, (4) public order, (5) decency or morality, (6) contempt of court, (7) defamation, and (8) incitement to an offence*. Notably, **"fake news," "annoyance," or "general public interest"** are *not* standalone Article 19(2) grounds.

Two landmark Supreme Court verdicts define India's digital free-speech doctrine:
- ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015):** A Division Bench (Justices J. Chelameswar and Rohinton F. Nariman) struck down **Section 66A of the IT Act, 2000** in its entirety for violating Article 19(1)(a). The Court articulated the foundational constitutional triad separating **Discussion** and **Advocacy** (fully protected, however unpopular or offensive) from **Incitement** (solely restrictable when proximately linked to Article 19(2) harms). Undefined terms like *"grossly offensive," "menacing,"* and *"annoyance"* suffered from fatal **vagueness**, **overbreadth**, and an unconstitutional **chilling effect**. *(When state police continued filing "zombie" FIRs under Section 66A, the Supreme Court in 2021–2022 PUCL directives ordered immediate cessation of all prosecutions under the invalidated section).*
- ***Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020):** Addressing prolonged communication shutdowns in Jammu and Kashmir, the Supreme Court held that freedom of speech and expression and the right to practice any profession over the **medium of the internet** enjoy constitutional protection under Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g). Any restriction must satisfy the **proportionality standard**—legality, legitimate goal, suitability, **least-restrictive means**, periodic review, and mandatory public publication of suspension orders.

### Section 69A Blocking, Section 69 Interception and Section 79 Safe Harbour

Three distinct provisions of the **Information Technology Act, 2000** (inserted/amended in 2008) govern content blocking, lawful surveillance, and platform immunity—frequently confused in examinations:

**1. Section 69A (Power to Block Public Access to Information):** Empowers strictly the **Central Government** (not State Governments) to direct intermediaries or government agencies to block public access to specific URLs, apps, or accounts on **six narrow grounds** aligned with core Article 19(2) interests: *sovereignty and integrity of India, defence of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, or preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to the above*. Notice that defamation, contempt of court, and decency/morality are absent from Section 69A.

In ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)**, the Supreme Court **upheld Section 69A** specifically because the accompanying **IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Blocking for Access of Information by Public) Rules, 2009** contained structured safeguards: written reasoned orders by a Designated Officer (MeitY), scrutiny by an Inter-Departmental Examination Committee, pre-decisional hearing to the originator/intermediary where identifiable, and bi-monthly oversight by a Cabinet Secretary-led Review Committee. Nonetheless, **Rule 16's strict confidentiality clause**—barring disclosure of blocking requests and orders—attracts persistent constitutional criticism for frustrating judicial review by affected creators and journalists.

**2. Section 69 (Interception, Monitoring and Decryption vs. E2EE):** Unlike Section 69A (which blocks public viewing), **Section 69** empowers *both* the **Central and State Governments** to intercept, monitor, or decrypt any information generated, transmitted, received, or stored in any computer resource. Crucially, Section 69 applies on the six Section 69A grounds **plus a seventh, much wider ground: *"for investigation of any offence"***—which extends beyond Article 19(2)—with non-cooperation punishable by up to **7 years' imprisonment**.

Under the **IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Interception, Monitoring and Decryption) Rules, 2009**, every order requires prior written approval of the **Union or State Home Secretary** (following *PUCL v. Union of India*, 1997 safeguards) and bi-monthly review by a Cabinet Secretary/Chief Secretary committee. Two critical executive milestones shape its operation:
- **MHA Notification (December 20, 2018):** Specified **10 central agencies** authorized to seek Section 69 interception—**IB, NCB, ED, CBDT, DRI, CBI, NIA, R&AW, Directorate of Signal Intelligence** *(restricted to J&K, North-East, and Assam)*, and **Delhi Police Commissioner** (constitutional challenges remain pending before the Supreme Court).
- **February 2024 MeitY Amendment to Rule 23(1):** Extended mandatory **6-month destruction of interception records** (unless required for ongoing functional needs) from only security agencies to **both the competent authority (Union/State/UT Home Secretaries) and security agencies**.

Because **End-to-End Encryption (E2EE)** ensures only the sender and recipient hold decryption keys, intermediaries cannot hand over plaintext under Section 69—triggering an enduring legal and architectural collision with the ***Justice K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)** fourfold privacy test (*legality, legitimate aim, necessity, proportionality*).

**3. Section 79 (Conditional Intermediary Safe Harbour):** Protects neutral digital intermediaries (social media platforms, ISPs, search engines, marketplaces) from civil or criminal liability for third-party user-generated content, provided they act strictly as passive conduits, observe statutory due diligence (**Sec 79(2)(c)**), and expeditiously remove unlawful content upon acquiring **"actual knowledge"** (**Sec 79(3)(b)**). In ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)**, the Supreme Court **read down Section 79(3)(b)**, ruling that "actual knowledge" cannot mean private citizen complaints (which would coerce platforms into arbitrating millions of private disputes via mass censorship); it strictly means receiving a **court order** or a **notification from the appropriate government or its agency** tied to Article 19(2) grounds.

## IT Rules 2021 to 2026 Synthetic Media Overhaul

India's **Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021**—framed under Sections 69A, 79, and 87 of the IT Act—transformed online governance from light-touch safe harbour into proactive statutory accountability. Administratively, the Rules bifurcate authority across two ministries: **Part II** (Due Diligence for Intermediaries and Social Media) is administered by **MeitY**, whereas **Part III** (Code of Ethics and Three-Tier Self-Regulatory Grievance Redressal for Digital News Publishers and OTT/Online Curated Content Platforms) is administered by the **Ministry of Information and Broadcasting (MIB)**.

### Foundational 2021–2025 Architecture - SSMIs, Traceability, GACs and Rule 3(1)(d)

**1. Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs — Rule 4):** Platforms crossing the government-notified threshold of **50 lakh (5 million) registered users in India** face heightened compliance duties:
- **Tri-Officer India-Resident Architecture:** Mandatory appointment of three Indian-resident officers—a **Chief Compliance Officer (CCO)** (senior employee/key managerial personnel personally liable for due-diligence failures), a **Nodal Contact Person** (24×7 coordination with law-enforcement agencies), and a **Resident Grievance Officer (RGO)**—backed by published monthly compliance reports and voluntary user-verification mechanisms.
- **Rule 4(2) "First Originator" Traceability:** Mandates messaging SSMIs (such as WhatsApp) to enable identification of the **first originator** of an unlawful message within India solely upon a **court order** or a **Section 69 competent-authority order**, strictly restricted to grave offences punishable with **≥5 years' imprisonment** (sovereignty/integrity, security of the State, public order, rape, sexually explicit material, or CSAM), invoked only when **less intrusive means fail**, and barring disclosure of message contents.

In ***WhatsApp LLC v. Union of India*** (filed before the Delhi High Court in May 2021 and still undecided as of late 2026), Meta argued—telling the Court in April 2024 that *"if we are told to break encryption, then WhatsApp goes"*—that tracing originators across forwarded encrypted chats requires embedding permanent, platform-wide user fingerprints on billions of private messages, destroying E2EE guarantees and failing *Puttaswamy* necessity and proportionality. Conversely, the Union contends that unbridled encryption shields lynching-rumour instigators and terror recruiters behind complete impunity, demanding technological innovation rather than backdoors.

**2. Grievance Appellate Committees (GACs — 2022 Amendment, Rule 3A):** Introduced via the **October 28, 2022 Amendment** (with **three GACs** notified on January 27, 2023 and the fully digital portal `gac.gov.in` operational from March 1, 2023), Rule 3A created a statutory online appeal layer for "Digital Nagriks" dissatisfied with an intermediary Grievance Officer's decision. Each GAC comprises a **Chairperson and two whole-time members** appointed by the Central Government (**1 ex-officio + 2 independent members**). Users may appeal within **30 days**, and GACs endeavour online disposal within **30 calendar days**, issuing binding compliance orders to intermediaries. While GACs curb arbitrary Big Tech account suspensions, civil-society critics question executive appointment of adjudicators reviewing user speech.

**3. October 22, 2025 Amendment — Rule 3(1)(d) Senior-Officer Safeguards:** Effective **November 15, 2025**, MeitY tightened Section 79(3)(b) takedown notices under Rule 3(1)(d) to curb inconsistent directives by junior functionaries:
- **Senior-Level Authorization:** Takedown intimations may now be issued *only* by an officer not below the rank of **Joint Secretary** (or **Director** where no JS is appointed) in ministries/departments, and strictly by a specially authorized officer not below **Deputy Inspector General of Police (DIG)** for police authorities.
- **Reasoned Intimation:** Replaced broad "notifications" with mandatory **"reasoned intimations"** specifying the exact statutory provision violated, the nature of the unlawful act, and the precise URL or electronic identifier.
- **Periodic Accountability:** All Rule 3(1)(d) intimations undergo mandatory **monthly review** by an officer not below the rank of **Secretary** in the Appropriate Government.

### The February 2026 Synthetic Media Overhaul (G.S.R. 120(E))

Responding to explosive deepfake proliferation across elections, financial scams, and non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII), MeitY notified the **Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2026** vide Gazette Notification **G.S.R. 120(E) on February 10, 2026**, entering into force on **February 20, 2026**. The overhaul shifts India's intermediary regime from reactive post-harm moderation to proactive synthetic-media governance:

**1. First Statutory Definition of Synthetically Generated Information (SGI) — Rule 2(1)(wa) & Rule 2(1)(ca):** Rather than using the colloquial term "deepfake," Rule 2(1)(wa) anchors regulation around **viewer perception and realism** across four cumulative limbs:
- Content must be **audio, visual, or audio-visual information** (images, videos, sound recordings)—crucially, per MeitY's official FAQs, **pure text outputs are excluded**;
- Artificially or algorithmically created, generated, modified, or altered using a computer resource;
- Engineered so that it **appears to be real, authentic, or true**; and
- Depicts any individual or event in a manner likely to be perceived as **indistinguishable from a natural person or real-world event** (encompassing AI deepfakes, face swaps, and voice cloning).
- **Rule 2(1A)** explicitly clarifies that all statutory references to "information" used to commit unlawful acts across Rules 3(1)(b), 3(1)(d), 4(2), and 4(4) encompass unlawful SGI.

**2. Good-Faith Exclusions vs. The Satire Dilemma:** To prevent choking legitimate creative and enterprise workflows, Rule 2(1)(wa) carves out **good-faith workflow exclusions**—routine editing, formatting, colour correction, noise reduction, compression, transcription, accessibility enhancements, and bona fide educational/training materials—provided they do not materially alter or misrepresent underlying reality. Crucially, however, the Rules contain **no blanket exemption for satire, parody, art, or evident fiction**: lawful political satire or parody resembling real individuals remains permissible *only* if properly declared and labeled as SGI.

**3. Mandatory Proactive Prevention, Prominent Labeling and Permanent Provenance (Rule 3(3)):** Intermediaries offering computer resources enabling creation, generation, or modification of SGI must:
- Deploy **reasonable and appropriate technical measures** (including automated tools) to prevent users from generating or disseminating unlawful SGI—explicitly targeting CSAM, non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII), impersonation, and illegal fabrications;
- Ensure permissible SGI carries a **prominent visual label or prefixed audio disclosure** enabling immediate identification *(notably, the final Rules dropped the October 2025 draft's rigid mandate covering 10% of screen area or initial 10% of audio duration in favour of a flexible prominence standard)*;
- Embed **permanent metadata or technical provenance mechanisms** (such as cryptographic watermarking or C2PA-aligned provenance) carrying a **unique identifier** identifying the intermediary's computer resource, *"to the extent technically feasible"*; and
- Technically prohibit users from **modifying, suppressing, or removing** the AI label, permanent metadata, or unique identifier.

**4. Heightened SSMI Pre-Publication Verification (Rule 4(1A)) & User Warnings:** Significant Social Media Intermediaries allowing users to upload or publish media must, *before publication*: (a) require users to **declare** whether content is SGI, (b) deploy **automated/technical verification tools** calibrated to content format and source to verify declarations, and (c) prominently display an SGI label wherever confirmed. All intermediaries must additionally notify users at least **once every three months** in plain language about legal consequences under the IT Act and BNS/BNSS for generating or sharing unlawful SGI.

**5. Hyper-Compressed Takedown and Grievance Timelines (Applicable Across ALL Unlawful Content):** The February 2026 Rules drastically compressed compliance clocks across *all* online content, not merely AI-generated media—with non-compliance triggering **loss of Section 79 safe harbour**:
- **Court Orders / Senior-Officer Reasoned Intimations (Rule 3(1)(d)):** Slashed from **36 hours → 3 hours**.
- **NCII, Nudity, Sexual Acts & Impersonation/Morphed Complaints (Rule 3(2)(b)):** Slashed from **24 hours → 2 hours** (operationalizing MeitY's November 11, 2025 NCII SOP for rapid victim protection).
- **Sensitive Rule 3(1)(b) User Removal Grievances (Proviso to Rule 3(2)(a)(i)):** Slashed from **72 hours → 36 hours**.
- **General Grievance Disposal (Rule 3(2)(a)(i)):** Slashed from **15 days → 7 days** (while retaining 24-hour acknowledgment).

**360-Degree Multi-Lens Critique (GS3 × GS2 × Governance × GS4 Ethics):**
- **National Security & Victim Protection Lens (GS3):** Compressing NCII and unlawful-intimation windows to 2–3 hours directly neutralizes the initial viral-cascade window during communal crises, border conflicts (such as Operation Sindoor), and intimate deepfake extortion—shifting regulatory posture from post-damage litigation to real-time containment.
- **Constitutional Free-Speech & Proportionality Lens (GS2):** Compelling platforms to adjudicate complex legal notices within 3 hours on pain of losing Section 79 immunity incentivizes risk-averse **automated over-removal ("collateral censorship")**, eroding due process and chilling lawful journalism, whistleblowing, and satire. Further, extending mandatory labeling to *lawful* political satire lacking deceptive intent raises compelled-speech concerns under Article 19(1)(a).
- **Technical Feasibility & Market Equity Lens (Governance):** Cryptographic provenance metadata remains easily stripped via analog screen-recording, re-encoding, or compression across platforms—making the *"to the extent technically feasible"* qualifier a compliance grey zone. Meanwhile, 24×7 automated detection and 2–3 hour legal-escalation engineering disproportionately burden domestic Indian startups relative to US Big Tech incumbents.
- **AI & Media Ethics Lens (GS4):** Mandatory provenance advances citizen autonomy and **epistemic integrity** (the ethical right to distinguish authentic human testimony from synthetic simulation) while protecting human dignity against non-consensual likeness appropriation—yet ethical legitimacy demands transparent, non-discriminatory enforcement free from partisan weaponization.

### Judicial Counterbalance - Kunal Kamra (2024) vs. X Corp. Sahyog (2025)

Two landmark High Court verdicts—both now before the Supreme Court in 2026—expose the central constitutional tension inside India's intermediary regime: **where does legitimate state regulation of digital harm cross into unconstitutional executive censorship?**

**1. Bombay High Court — *Kunal Kamra v. Union of India* (September 26, 2024) [PIB Fact-Check Unit Struck Down]:**
The **April 6, 2023 Amendment** inserted an obligation into **Rule 3(1)(b)(v)** requiring intermediaries to make reasonable efforts not to host information concerning *"any business of the Central Government"* identified as **"fake, false, or misleading"** by a Central Government-notified **Fact Check Unit (FCU)** (notified as PIB FCU in March 2024), risking loss of Section 79 safe harbour upon non-removal.

Challenged by satirist Kunal Kamra, the Editors Guild of India, and media associations, a Bombay High Court Division Bench delivered a **split verdict on January 31, 2024**—**Justice G.S. Patel** invalidating the rule as unconstitutional censorship, and **Justice Neela Gokhale** upholding it. Serving as tie-breaker judge, **Justice A.S. Chandurkar** (opinion September 20, 2024; formal 2:1 striking down on **September 26, 2024**) struck down the FCU amendment as violating **Articles 14, 19(1)(a), and 19(1)(g)** and exceeding statutory authority under the IT Act:
- **Outside Article 19(2) & Vagueness:** Neither "fake," "false," nor "misleading" was defined, nor did mere falsity without proximity to Article 19(2) grounds permit speech restriction.
- **Judge in Its Own Cause (*Nemo Judex in Causa Sua*):** Empowering the Central Government to unilaterally declare criticism regarding *its own business* false violated Article 14 natural justice.
- **No State-Curated "Right to Truth" & Chilling Effect:** Rejecting the argument that citizens possess a fundamental right to state-certified truth, the Court held that threatening safe-harbour loss created an impermissible **chilling effect** barred by *Shreya Singhal*.
- **2026 Supreme Court Status:** Following procedural dismissal in June 2025, the Supreme Court restored the Union's Special Leave Petition in February 2026; in **March 2026**, a bench led by **CJI Surya Kant** admitted the appeal as an issue of *"paramount importance"* while explicitly **refusing to stay** the Bombay High Court's invalidation.

**2. Karnataka High Court — *X Corp. v. Union of India* (September 24, 2025) [`Sahyog` Portal Upheld]:**
In March 2025, X Corp (formerly Twitter) challenged MHA/I4C's centralized ***Sahyog* Portal**, which automates and routes **Section 79(3)(b)** / **Rule 3(1)(d)** unlawful-content takedown intimations from authorized central and state nodal officers directly to intermediaries. X argued that *Sahyog* erected a parallel censorship machinery circumventing **Section 69A**'s statutory hearing and committee-review safeguards affirmed in *Shreya Singhal*.

On **September 24, 2025**, a Single-Judge Bench (**Justice M. Nagaprasanna**) dismissed X Corp's writ petition in a 351-page ruling, upholding both *Sahyog* and Rule 3(1)(d):
- **Instrument of "Public Good":** Held that *Sahyog* does not invent new blocking powers; it merely streamlines administrative compliance with existing statutory duties under Section 79(3)(b) to remove content already unlawful under Indian statutes.
- **Citizenship Bar on Article 19:** Reaffirmed that **foreign corporate entities cannot claim Article 19 fundamental rights**, which belong exclusively to Indian citizens, cautioning against uncritical transplantation of US First Amendment jurisprudence into India.
- **Reconciling *Shreya Singhal*:** Distinguished blocking orders (Sec 69A) from safe-harbour due-diligence intimations (Sec 79(3)(b)), noting the regulatory landscape evolved post-2021.
- **2026 Supreme Court Status:** X Corp appealed before a Karnataka HC Division Bench (November 2025), while Indian citizens (including Kunal Kamra and Haresh Jagtiani) filed parallel writ petitions in the Bombay High Court (February 2026) curing the foreign-corporation standing barrier and challenging *Sahyog* alongside the October 2025 Rule 3(1)(d) amendment. On **July 22, 2026**, the **Supreme Court stayed High Court proceedings** while considering the Union's transfer petitions to authoritatively resolve the **Section 69A vs. Section 79(3)(b)** constitutional boundary.

> [!warning] Prelims Traps & Atomic Distinctions
> - **SGI Scope Trap (Feb 2026 Rules):** Under **Rule 2(1)(wa)** (`G.S.R. 120(E)`, notified Feb 10, 2026; effective **Feb 20, 2026**), **Synthetically Generated Information (SGI)** covers strictly **audio, visual, or audio-visual** realistic content—**pure AI-generated text is EXCLUDED**. Likewise, routine good-faith editing/colour correction/accessibility enhancements are excluded, **whereas satire/parody is NOT exempt** from labeling.
> - **Takedown Clock Scope Trap:** The February 2026 compressed takedown windows (**3 hours** for court/senior-officer orders under Rule 3(1)(d); **2 hours** for NCII/nudity/impersonation under Rule 3(2)(b); **36 hours** for specified Rule 3(1)(b) grievances; **7 days** for general grievances) apply to **ALL unlawful online content**, NOT exclusively to AI-generated deepfakes.
> - **Section 69 vs. Section 69A Authority & Grounds Inversion:** **Section 69A** (blocking public access) can be exercised **ONLY by the Central Government** on **6 grounds**. **Section 69** (interception/monitoring/decryption) can be exercised by **BOTH Central and State Governments** on those 6 grounds **PLUS "investigation of any offence"**. Neither provision includes defamation or contempt of court.
> - **Ministry Split Under IT Rules 2021:** **Part II** (Social Media Intermediaries, SSMIs, GACs, SGI) is administered by **MeitY**; **Part III** (Digital News Publishers & OTT platforms' Code of Ethics) is administered by the **Ministry of Information and Broadcasting (MIB)**. Meanwhile, the ***Sahyog* Portal** is operated by **I4C under MHA**, not MeitY.
> - **Senior-Officer Takedown Threshold (Oct 2025 Amendment):** Under amended **Rule 3(1)(d)** (effective Nov 15, 2025), takedown intimations cannot be issued by Sub-Inspectors or SPs—only by **Joint Secretary (or Director where JS is absent)** for ministries/departments and **DIG-rank or above** for police, with **monthly review by a Secretary-rank officer**.
> - **Judicial Status Precision (Late 2026):** In ***Kunal Kamra***, the Bombay HC's Sept 2024 invalidation of the PIB Fact-Check Unit remains **in force** (SC admitted appeal in March 2026 but **refused a stay**). In ***X Corp.* (`Sahyog`)**, the Karnataka HC upheld *Sahyog* in Sept 2025, and the **Supreme Court stayed HC appeal proceedings in July 2026** pending consolidation. **WhatsApp's Rule 4(2) challenge** in Delhi HC remains **undecided**.
> - **Article 19 Corporate Standing Trap:** Under Indian constitutional jurisprudence (reaffirmed in *X Corp.* 2025), **Article 19 freedoms belong strictly to Indian citizens**, NOT to foreign corporate intermediaries—though foreign entities can still invoke **Article 14** against manifestly arbitrary state action.

> [!info] Mains Arsenal
> - **High-Impact Primary Data & Case Anchors (2024–2026):**
>   - **Operation Sindoor Counter-Disinformation Model (May 2025):** Over **1,400 anti-India propaganda URLs blocked**, backed by a 24×7 Tri-Service–PIB Control Room and real-time satellite-imagery briefings countering external FIMI.
>   - **Regulatory Milestones:** Gazette **G.S.R. 120(E) (Feb 10/20, 2026)** defining SGI (Rule 2(1)(wa)) and slashing takedowns (36h→3h, 24h→2h); **Oct 22, 2025 Rule 3(1)(d) Amendment** (JS/DIG authorization + monthly Secretary review); **Nov 11, 2025 MeitY NCII SOP**; **Feb 2024 Interception Rule 23(1) Amendment** (6-month record destruction by Home Secretaries).
>   - **Radicalization Pattern:** NIA's **Three-App Funnel** (Instagram discovery → Telegram/Rocket.Chat grooming → Crypto wallet micro-funding), gaming-lobby infiltration, and lone-wolf cases (Coimbatore 2022, Bengaluru Rameshwaram Café 2024, Jan 2026 Gujarat AQIS chargesheet).
>   - **Constitutional Dialectic:** Pair ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)** (*Advocacy vs. Incitement*), ***Puttaswamy* (2017)** (*Proportionality*), ***Anuradha Bhasin* (2020)** (*Least-restrictive means*), ***Kunal Kamra* (2024)** (*No executive monopoly over truth*), and ***X Corp.* (2025)** (*Digital sovereignty vs. Sec 69A procedural guardrails*).
> - **Structural Way Forward (Balanced Policy Prescriptions):**
>   1. **Harmonize Sec 69A and Sec 79(3)(b) via Primary Legislation (Proposed Digital India Act):** Replace fragmented executive rule-making with parliamentary statutory guardrails—introducing independent judicial/parliamentary oversight for blocking orders, reforming Rule 16 total secrecy, and codifying proportionality for the *Sahyog* portal.
>   2. **Calibrate SGI Compliance with Risk-Tiered Timelines & Satire Safe Harbours:** Maintain 2-hour emergency takedowns for NCII, CSAM, and verifiable communal-riot hoaxes, while building reasonable appellate buffers and clear parody/artistic-expression safe harbours so platforms do not default to algorithmic over-censorship.
>   3. **Adopt Privacy-Preserving Attribution Instead of Breaking E2EE:** Rather than weakening end-to-end encryption under Rule 4(2), scale **metadata analytics, device-level forensic interception under judicial warrant**, viral forward-friction caps, and global cryptographic provenance standards (**C2PA / hardware-level signing**).
>   4. **Institutionalize Whole-of-Society Cognitive Resilience:** Build an independent **multi-stakeholder Counter-FIMI Centre** (modeled on Finland's media-literacy ecosystem and EU Democracy Shield), embed critical digital literacy in school curricula, and scale state-level family-centric de-radicalization programmes nationally.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Statutory Text vs. Popular Buzzword Distortion:** UPSC exploits imprecise media shorthand—expect traps claiming **SGI under the Feb 2026 Rules** covers *all AI-generated text, audio, and video* (false: pure text is excluded) or mandates a fixed *10% screen-area watermark* (false: that was in the Oct 2025 draft, dropped in the final Feb 2026 Gazette).
- **Executive Authority & Jurisdiction Swaps:** Watch for statements empowering *State Governments* to block websites under **Section 69A** (false: Central Government only), restricting **Section 69** interception solely to *Article 19(2) grounds* (false: includes "investigation of any offence"), or placing **Part III (OTT/Digital News)** under MeitY (false: MIB) and ***Sahyog*** under MeitY (false: MHA/I4C).
- **Pending vs. Settled Judicial Outcomes:** Traps frequently state that the Supreme Court has *stayed* the Bombay HC's *Kunal Kamra* FCU judgment (false: stay explicitly refused in March 2026), *upheld* WhatsApp's Rule 4(2) exit or struck down Rule 4(2) (false: still pending in Delhi HC), or *finalized* the *Sahyog* portal verdict (false: HC appeals stayed by SC in July 2026).
- **Officer Rank & Review Hierarchy Inversion:** Under the October 2025 Rule 3(1)(d) amendment, expect traps substituting *Superintendent of Police (SP)* or *District Magistrate* for **DIG** and **Joint Secretary/Director**, or omitting the mandatory **monthly review by a Secretary-rank officer**.
