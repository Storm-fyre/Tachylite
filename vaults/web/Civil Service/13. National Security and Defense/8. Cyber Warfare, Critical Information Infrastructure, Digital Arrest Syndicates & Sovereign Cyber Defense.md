# Chapter 8 - Cyber Warfare, Critical Information Infrastructure, Digital Arrest Syndicates & Sovereign Cyber Defense

Cyberspace has dissolved the classical geography of national security. Unlike land, sea, air, or outer space, the **fifth domain of warfare** is entirely man-made, globally interconnected, and structurally offense-dominant: a single unpatched vulnerability or a compromised third-party vendor can bypass thousands of kilometres of physical border fortifications in milliseconds.

For India, this structural exposure scales linearly with its developmental triumph. Rapid digitalization through **Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)**—with over 1.22 billion telephone subscribers, 969 million internet users, and UPI processing roughly 81% of retail payment volume across 691 live banks—has compressed the distance between state resilience, economic continuity, and citizen vulnerability.

As reported by the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In), tracked cybersecurity incidents surged from **15,92,917 in 2023** to **20,41,360 in 2024** and **29,44,248 in 2025**—an 85% expansion in two years. Defending sovereign cyberspace therefore demands operating simultaneously across four distinct theatres: state-sponsored sabotage of Critical Information Infrastructure (CII), statutory compliance and incident response, transnational financial-scam syndicates, and tri-service military cyber deterrence.

## Cyber Threat Spectrum and Critical Information Infrastructure Vulnerabilities

### State-Sponsored Advanced Persistent Threats and Gray-Zone Coercion

Modern interstate cyber competition operates predominantly below the threshold of armed conflict, exploiting **attribution latency** and **plausible deniability**. Adversaries deploy **Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)**—well-resourced state or state-proxied units that establish stealthy, long-term footholds inside target networks for intelligence exfiltration, pre-positioning of disruptive payloads, or wartime paralysis.

China remains India's most formidable state cyber adversary, guided by the doctrine of **"system destruction warfare"**—paralyzing an opponent's command, logistics, and economic grids before kinetic forces even engage. On **April 19, 2024**, Beijing dissolved the People's Liberation Army Strategic Support Force (PLASSF) and reorganized its capabilities into three specialized arms reporting directly to the Central Military Commission: the **PLA Cyberspace Force** (offensive cyber, electronic warfare, and five theater-aligned Technical Reconnaissance Bases), the **PLA Aerospace Force**, and the **PLA Information Support Force** (network architecture and cyber defense).

China pairs uniformed PLA units with intelligence organs (Ministry of State Security) and private contractor ecosystems in a **"whole-of-nation" model**. Intrusion sets linked to Chinese operators—such as *RedEcho* (deploying *ShadowPad* malware against Indian regional load despatch centres during the 2020–2021 Ladakh standoff) and *Tag-38*—demonstrate calibrated pre-positioning inside India's power and telecom arteries designed for strategic signaling rather than immediate kinetic effect.

On the western front, Pakistan-linked **APT36 (*Transparent Tribe*)** executes persistent cyber-espionage campaigns tailored specifically against Indian defense personnel, diplomatic missions, military academies, and public-sector undertakings. Relying on credential harvesting, weaponized PDF/office lures, honeytrap social engineering, and mobile spyware (*CapraRAT* / *CrimsonRAT*), APT36 prioritizes human-layer penetration over exotic exploits. During **Operation Sindoor (May 2025)**, state-aligned Pakistani hacktivist collectives launched over 1.5 million probing, website defacement, and DDoS attempts against Indian portals, though layered national hardening prevented systemic operational disruption.

### Commercialized Attack Vectors and Systemic Indian Breaches

Beneath pure state espionage lies an industrialized cybercrime economy lowering technical barriers to catastrophic disruption:

- **Zero-Day Exploits:** Undisclosed software vulnerabilities weaponized before vendors issue patches, traded on gray/black markets and hoarded by intelligence agencies for high-value penetration.
- **Supply-Chain Compromises:** Attackers breach trusted third-party software vendors, cloud integrators, or open-source libraries to cascade malware simultaneously across hundreds of downstream institutional clients.
- **Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS):** Botnets—networks of hijacked IoT devices, routers, and endpoints—flood target servers with volumetric traffic to sever public services or mask concurrent data exfiltration.
- **Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS):** Criminal syndicates lease encryption malware and negotiation portals to affiliates on a profit-sharing basis, increasingly deploying **double extortion** (encrypting operational files while threatening public release of exfiltrated sensitive data).

Three high-impact Indian incidents expose distinct structural fractures across public healthcare and financial infrastructure:

1. **AIIMS New Delhi Ransomware Attack (November 2022):** Malicious encryption paralyzed the hospital's e-Hospital servers, reverting India's premier tertiary medical institution to manual paper registers for over two weeks and underscoring the lethal public-health spillover of legacy healthcare IT.
2. **ICMR Data Breach (October 2023):** Personally identifiable COVID-19 testing records of roughly 81.5 crore Indian citizens surfaced for sale on dark-web forums, highlighting poor data-minimization hygiene and inadequate segmentation of legacy public repositories.
3. **C-Edge Technologies Ransomware Incident (July 2024):** The Eastern European **RansomEXX** syndicate exploited an unpatched Jenkins Command Line Interface vulnerability (**CVE-2024-23897**) via a collaborator linked to C-Edge Technologies—a TCS–SBI joint venture supplying core banking software to small banks. To prevent lateral contagion across India's national payment backbone, the **National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI)** temporarily isolated C-Edge from retail payment rails on July 31, 2024, halting UPI, IMPS, and AePS services across **200–300 cooperative and regional rural banks** for roughly 24 hours. The episode proved that **shared-vendor concentration risk** can convert a single unpatched server into a macroeconomic choke point.

### SCADA and Industrial Control Systems Across Operational Technology

The gravest cyber-warfare risk lies not in stealing corporate emails, but in crossing the bridge from **Information Technology (IT)** into **Operational Technology (OT)**—specifically **Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)** systems and **Industrial Control Systems (ICS)** that govern physical valves, turbines, transformers, and switches. Originally engineered for reliability rather than adversarial security, legacy OT hardware runs decades-old firmware, relies on unencrypted serial protocols, and cannot tolerate frequent reboots for security patching.

When organizations bridge previously air-gapped OT networks to corporate IT for remote telemetry, billing, or predictive maintenance, kinetic sabotage becomes remotely executable—as demonstrated globally by **Stuxnet (2010)** reprogramming Siemens programmable logic controllers (PLCs) at Iran's Natanz centrifuge facility. Across India's critical sectors, OT exposure manifests sharply:

- **Power Grids:** Synchronized national electricity transmission relies on real-time frequency balancing via Regional Load Despatch Centres (RLDCs) and GPS-timed phasor measurement units. Compromise of substation switches or SCADA telemetry—probed during the October 2020 Mumbai grid outage and Ladakh power-grid intrusions—risks cascading regional blackouts.
- **Nuclear Facilities:** In September 2019, North Korea-linked *Dtrack* espionage malware infected the **administrative IT network** of the **Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant (KKNPP)**. Crucially, Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL) confirmed that the safety-critical reactor control network remained strictly air-gapped and uncompromised—yet the incident shattered complacency regarding administrative reconnaissance inside strategic enclaves.
- **UPI and NPCI Payment Rails:** Real-time settlement architecture concentrates Systemic Importance across core switches, clearing houses, and third-party application providers. While NPCI's core network remained intact during the 2024 C-Edge breach, perimeter compromise at peripheral banks or API gateways threatens public trust in cashless transactions.
- **Civil Aviation and Telecom:** Modern aviation depends on satellite navigation (exposed to cross-border GNSS/GPS spoofing), air traffic management networks, and biometric passenger-processing interfaces; meanwhile, 5G transitions telecom cores from proprietary hardware to cloud-native, software-defined architectures, expanding the attack surface across base stations and signaling protocols (SS7/Diameter).

### Physical CII Protection and Aviation Bomb-Hoax Deterrence

Cyber resilience fails if physical perimeters remain porous—USB drop attacks, rogue insider hardware implants, and hybrid coercion rely on bridging physical and digital access. India secures its strategic physical CII primarily through the **Central Industrial Security Force (CISF)** under the Ministry of Home Affairs, guarding nuclear plants, space installations, seaports, metro networks, power stations, Parliament, and over 68 civil airports via its specialized **Airport Security Group (ASG)**.

Regulatory oversight of civil aviation rests with the **Bureau of Civil Aviation Security (BCAS)** under the Ministry of Civil Aviation (MoCA)—an independent statutory regulator implementing the **National Civil Aviation Security Programme (NCASP)** aligned with **ICAO Annex 17** standards, including Biometric Access Control Systems (BACS) and airport-level **Bomb Threat Assessment Committees (BTACs)**.

In 2024, India confronted a novel **cyber-physical hybrid disruption**: an unprecedented wave of **1,019 hoax bomb threats** targeting Indian domestic and international flights—dispatched anonymously via VPN-masked social media handles and encrypted email services. Even when assessed as low-credibility, mandatory aviation safety protocols forced mid-air diversions, fighter-jet escorts, emergency evacuations, and tarmac isolation, costing airlines an estimated **₹13–17 lakh per flight hour** and severely straining CISF/BTAC bandwidth.

India's counter-deterrence architecture combined digital accountability with statutory tightening:
- **Digital Traceability and Platform Liability:** MeitY issued strict advisories reminding social media intermediaries of their due-diligence duties under the IT Rules to swiftly remove threat posts, preserve subscriber metadata, and assist law enforcement in unmasking VPN-masked perpetrators within statutory timelines.
- **Administrative Blacklisting:** Enforcing the **Aircraft (Security) Rules, 2023**, authorities moved to place confirmed hoax callers onto the national **No-Fly List** while building a centralized CISF/Police/AAI hoax-caller database.
- **Statutory Overhaul:** Because the **Suppression of Unlawful Acts against Safety of Civil Aviation (SUASCA) Act, 1982** historically focused on *in-flight* unlawful interference, MoCA initiated amendments broadening its remit to cover bomb threats made while an aircraft is *on the ground*—converting such acts into **cognizable, non-bailable offences** carrying steep fines and imprisonment up to life in aggravated scenarios.

## Statutory and Institutional Cyber Defense Grid

### Core Statutory Architecture - IT Act 2000 and Telecommunications Act 2023

India lacks a single omnibus "Cyber Security Act"; instead, sovereign cyber governance rests on two primary statutory pillars complemented by sectoral regulations (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI) and the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 (with DPDP Rules notified in November 2025).

**Information Technology Act, 2000 (as amended in 2008)** anchors both criminal deterrence and institutional mandates:
- **Civil Liability and Computer Offences:** **Section 43** imposes civil compensation for unauthorized access, data extraction, or system disruption; **Section 66** criminalizes dishonest or fraudulent acts under Sec 43 (imprisonment up to 3 years and/or ₹5 lakh fine). **Section 66C** (identity theft) and **Section 66D** (cheating by personation using computer resources) serve as primary domestic criminal hooks against phishing, spoofing, and digital-arrest impersonation.
- **Section 66F (Cyber Terrorism):** Punishes acts committed with intent to threaten India's unity, integrity, security, or sovereignty—or to strike terror—by denying access, penetrating protected computer resources, or introducing contaminants likely to cause death, injury, or disruption of essential supplies/CII. Carries imprisonment extending **up to life**.
- **Section 70 (Protected Systems and CII):** Statutorily defines **Critical Information Infrastructure** as *"computer resource, the incapacitation or destruction of which, shall have debilitating impact on national security, economy, public health or safety"*. Empowers the appropriate government to declare any CII facility a **Protected System** via Official Gazette notification, making unauthorized access punishable with up to 10 years' imprisonment.
- **Sections 70A and 70B:** Provide the statutory birth certificates for **NCIIPC** and **CERT-In**, respectively.

**Telecommunications Act, 2023** replaced the colonial Indian Telegraph Act, 1885, elevating network security into primary law. Acting under **Sections 22(1) and 22(2)**, the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) notified two landmark subordinate frameworks in **November 2024**, refined via the **Telecommunications (Telecom Cyber Security) Amendment Rules, 2025**:

1. **Telecommunications (Telecom Cyber Security) Rules, 2024 (amended 2025):** Mandates every telecommunication entity to maintain a formal cybersecurity policy, appoint a **Chief Telecommunication Security Officer (CTSO)**, conduct periodic security audits, and report telecom security incidents within **six hours**. Under **Section 42(3)(c), (e), (f)** and **Section 42(7)** of the Act, tampering with **IMEI numbers**, obtaining SIMs via impersonation, or knowingly possessing unauthorized **SIM Boxes** are **cognizable and non-bailable offences** punishable by up to **three years' imprisonment and/or ₹50 lakh fine**. On **November 28, 2025**, DoT invoked these amended rules to direct OTT messaging apps using Indian mobile numbers (WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal, Snapchat, etc.) to enforce **continuous active-SIM binding** and **6-hour web-session logouts**, severing offshore scam compounds from dormant or removed Indian SIMs.
2. **Telecommunications (Critical Telecommunication Infrastructure) Rules, 2024:** Empowers designation of core telecom assets as **Critical Telecommunication Infrastructure (CTI)**, subjecting them to mandatory government audits and prohibiting remote maintenance from outside India without prior authorization.

### NCIIPC, CERT-In, NCSC and Cyber Swachhta Kendra

India intentionally separates *strategic CII protection* from *national incident response* and *law enforcement*, though functional overlaps demand continuous apex coordination.

**National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC):** Notified on **January 16, 2014** under **Section 70A of the IT Act**, NCIIPC functions as a specialized unit of the **National Technical Research Organisation (NTRO)** under the **Prime Minister's Office (PMO)**—not under MeitY. It is the sole national nodal agency tasked with protecting CII across **six notified Critical Sectors**: (1) Power & Energy, (2) Banking, Financial Services & Insurance (BFSI), (3) Telecom, (4) Transport, (5) Government, and (6) Strategic & Public Enterprises. NCIIPC enforces the *Information Security Practices and Procedures for Protected System Rules, 2018*, issues baseline security architectures, runs vulnerability assessments, and works through Sectoral CERTs (such as **CSIRT-Power** and **CERT-Fin**).

**Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In):** Designated under **Section 70B of the IT Act** under the **Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY)**, CERT-In serves as the 24×7 national operational responder for cyber incidents across government, private, and citizen domains. Its regulatory teeth crystallized through the landmark **CERT-In Cyber Security Directions of April 28, 2022** (issued under **Sec 70B(6)**), imposing four binding compliance pillars:

- **6-Hour Mandatory Incident Reporting:** Service providers, intermediaries, data centres, bodies corporate, and government organisations must report **20 specified categories of cyber incidents** (including targeted scanning, ransomware, data breaches, unauthorized access, and SCADA/OT compromise) within **6 hours** of noticing or being brought to notice.
- **180-Day Local Log Retention:** All ICT system logs must be maintained securely for a **rolling period of 180 days within Indian jurisdiction** to preserve forensic trails.
- **NTP Time Synchronization:** All covered entities must synchronize ICT system clocks with the Network Time Protocol (NTP) servers of the National Informatics Centre (NIC) or National Physical Laboratory (NPL) to ensure legally coherent cross-network timelines.
- **5-Year KYC Retention for VPNs, Cloud and Crypto:** Data centres, Virtual Private Server (VPS) providers, Cloud Service Providers, **Virtual Private Network (VPN) service providers**, and Virtual Asset Service Providers must validate and store customer names, IP addresses, usage patterns, and contact records for **five years** (even post-cancellation). While this strengthened forensic attribution, it triggered friction with privacy-focused global "no-log" VPN providers (such as ExpressVPN and NordVPN), several of which pulled physical servers out of India while serving Indian users via virtual servers abroad.

Beyond incident triage, CERT-In operates two vital operational wings:
1. **National Cyber Coordination Centre (NCCC):** Functions as a real-time cyberspace situational-awareness control room scanning internet traffic metadata to detect nascent botnets, malware campaigns, and reconnaissance probes, sharing tailored alerts with states and organizations.
2. **Cyber Swachhta Kendra (CSK):** Launched in 2017 under Digital India as CERT-In's **Botnet Cleaning and Malware Analysis Centre**, extending citizen-level cyber hygiene through free diagnostic and removal tools. Collaborating with the **Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C-DAC)** and domestic antivirus vendors (Quick Heal, eScan, K7), CSK distributes four flagship indigenous C-DAC endpoint tools:
   - ***M-Kavach 2*:** Comprehensive Android mobile security solution auditing misconfigurations, detecting banned/hidden applications, and neutralizing spyware.
   - ***USB Pratirodh*:** Desktop security controller authenticating and restricting unauthorized portable USB mass-storage media (pen drives, external drives).
   - ***AppSamvid*:** Desktop application whitelisting solution for Windows permitting only pre-approved executables to run, thwarting zero-day and ransomware payloads.
   - ***Browser JSGuard*:** Heuristic browser extension for Chrome and Firefox detecting and neutralizing malicious HTML and JavaScript web attacks in real time.

CERT-In also maintains the **Cyber Crisis Management Plan (CCMP)**, has empanelled **200 security auditing organizations**, and has conducted over **109 national cyber mock drills** involving more than 1,438 organizations across central and state tiers.

**National Cyber Security Coordinator (NCSC):** Positioned within the **National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS)** under the PMO/NSA (headed in 2026 by **Navin Kumar Singh**), the NCSC serves as the strategic bridge unifying civilian (MeitY), policing (MHA), technical-intelligence (NTRO), telecom (DoT), and military (MoD) cyber agencies. A **2024 amendment to the Allocation of Business Rules (AoBR)** formally designated NSCS as the nodal coordinating body for national cybersecurity strategy and overall interagency direction. However, a glaring policy gap persists: although India achieved **Tier-1 ("Role Modelling") status** in the **ITU Global Cybersecurity Index (GCI) 2024** across legal, technical, capacity, and cooperation pillars, the long-drafted **National Cyber Security Strategy (NCSS)** prepared by NCSC to replace the outdated **National Cyber Security Policy (NCSP), 2013** still awaited formal public notification as of late 2026.

## Cybercrime Ecosystem, Financial Fraud Rails and Transnational Scam Syndicates

### The Jurisdictional Fracture and I4C's Federal Architecture

While cyber warfare targets state power, high-volume cybercrime erodes household wealth and institutional trust in India's digital economy. Data cited in an April 2026 RBI discussion paper revealed that complaints on the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal skyrocketed from **~2.6 lakh in 2021 to ~28 lakh in 2025**, with reported fraud losses climbing from ₹551 crore to **₹22,931 crore** in 2025 alone—part of more than **₹52,000 crore** siphoned from Indian citizens over five years.

Combating this wave runs straight into a constitutional bottleneck: under the **Seventh Schedule**, *Police* and *Public Order* belong to the **State List**. When a victim in Pune transfers money via UPI to a mule account in Kolkata operated remotely from Cambodia using a SIM card issued in Mewat, fragmented state FIRs collapse under jurisdictional latency.

To bridge this federal gap without usurping state policing powers, the Ministry of Home Affairs established the **Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C)** (approved 2018, inaugurated 2020, and elevated into an **Attached Office of MHA on July 1, 2024**). I4C operationalizes a multi-layered national policing grid:

- **NCRP, Helpline 1930 and CFCFRMS:** The National Cybercrime Reporting Portal (`cybercrime.gov.in`) and toll-free **1930 helpline** feed directly into the **Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System (CFCFRMS)**. Exploiting the **"golden hour"** immediately following a fraudulent transfer, CFCFRMS transmits real-time electronic freeze alerts across banks, wallets, and payment aggregators—saving over **₹4,386 crore** by early 2025 and expanding further via e-Zero FIR pilots and the July 2026 **Money Restoration Module (MRM)**.
- **Cyber Fraud Mitigation Centre (CFMC):** Launched on **September 10, 2024**, CFMC physically co-locates representatives from major banks, payment aggregators, telecom operators, IT intermediaries, and central/state police under one roof in New Delhi for instant operational action—moving from asynchronous email tickets to live war-room disruption.
- **Samanvaya Platform and *Pratibimb* Module:** *Samanvaya* serves as the unified Management Information System (MIS) correlating interstate cybercrime linkages, while its geospatial module ***Pratibimb*** maps active criminal mobile infrastructure onto live GIS maps for jurisdictional police, enabling **29,837+ arrests** and over 2.33 lakh cyber-investigation assistance requests by mid-2026.
- **Suspect Registry and Seven JCCTs:** The **Suspect Registry** (launched September 2024) maintains a shared national repository of fraudulent bank accounts, IMEIs, and UPI IDs across financial ecosystems. Simultaneously, **Seven Joint Cyber Coordination Teams (JCCTs)** anchor interstate crackdowns around India's primary domestic cybercrime hotspots: **Mewat, Jamtara, Ahmedabad, Hyderabad, Chandigarh, Visakhapatnam, and Guwahati**.
- **Capacity Building and Forensics:** The **Cyber Commando Programme** (launched September 10, 2024) trains a specialized police cadre at IITs, IIITs, Rashtriya Raksha University (RRU), and National Forensic Sciences University (NFSU) (281 trained by mid-2026 toward a multi-year 5,000-commando target). Complementing it are the ***CyTrain*** MOOC platform (over 1.63 lakh police and judicial officers registered by June 2026) and the **National-Digital Investigation Support Centre (N-DISC)**—formerly NCFL-Investigation—operating in New Delhi (2019) and Assam (August 2025). I4C also administers the ***Sahyog* Portal** to streamline takedown notices to IT intermediaries under Section 79(3)(b) of the IT Act (analyzed alongside the September 2025 Karnataka High Court ruling in Chapter 9).

### Telecom and Financial Defenses - Sanchar Saathi, Chakshu and RBI MuleHunter.AI

Because digital scams require two indispensable infrastructures—**spoofed telecom channels** to deceive victims and **layered bank accounts** to launder proceeds—India hardened both rails between 2024 and 2026.

**Telecom Defense Layer (*Sanchar Saathi* and *Chakshu*):** Operated by the Department of Telecommunications (recording 23 crore+ portal visits and 1.7 crore+ app downloads by May 2026), **Sanchar Saathi** democratizes telecom hygiene via four core modules:
- **CEIR (Central Equipment Identity Register):** Blocks and traces lost or stolen mobile handsets nationwide across all telecom networks using IMEI numbers (nearly 10 lakh handsets blocked/traced by May 2026).
- **TAFCOP (Telecom Analytics for Fraud Management and Consumer Protection):** Enables citizens to view all SIMs registered against their identity and disconnect fraudulent connections—contributing to the disconnection of **3.4 crore suspicious mobile numbers** by May 2026.
- **KYM (Know Your Mobile):** Verifies IMEI genuineness before purchasing handsets, combating gray-market tampered devices.
- ***Chakshu* and Spoofed-Call Prevention:** *Chakshu* ("Eye") allows citizens to report suspected fraud calls, SMS, or WhatsApp lures *before* financial loss occurs. Crowdsourced *Chakshu* intelligence powered the **International Incoming Spoofed Calls Prevention System** (launched October 2024), which filters overseas calls spoofing Indian `+91` numbers directly at International Long-Distance (ILD) gateways and blocked over **309 rogue international carriers/aggregators**.

Crucially, *Sanchar Saathi* also became a constitutional test case for **Article 21 proportionality**. On November 28, 2025, DoT invoked the Telecom Cyber Security Rules to mandate **pre-installation of the *Sanchar Saathi* app** on all smartphones sold or imported in India, with features barred from restriction. Following intense scrutiny over OS-level root privileges and the *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy* proportionality standard, the Centre withdrew the pre-installation mandate on **December 3, 2025**, keeping *Sanchar Saathi* strictly **voluntary and removable**.

**Financial Defense Layer (RBI *MuleHunter.AI* and IDPIC):** Cyber syndicates evade traditional rule-based bank alerts by renting thousands of legitimate low-balance KYC accounts—**"money mules"**—to fragment and funnel stolen cash through five to ten hops into cryptocurrency or offshore merchants within minutes.

To break this velocity advantage, the **Reserve Bank Innovation Hub (RBIH)** (a wholly owned RBI subsidiary) launched ***MuleHunter.AI*** in **December 2024**. Unlike static threshold rules, *MuleHunter.AI* deploys machine-learning graph and behavioral analytics to spot anomalous pass-through velocity, dormant-account reactivation spikes, and network-level mule clusters in real time. By **March 2026**, *MuleHunter.AI* was live across **26 banks**.

Two institutional upgrades closed the loop between banking and policing:
1. **Indian Digital Payment Intelligence Corporation (IDPIC):** Incorporated on **October 16, 2025** as a non-profit **Section 8 company** to operate the sector-wide **Digital Payments Intelligence Platform (DPIP)**—correlating real-time fraud signals *across* banks rather than leaving each bank blind outside its own ledger.
2. **I4C–RBIH Intelligence Fusion (May 12, 2026):** An operational MoU directly feeds I4C's **Suspect Registry** and NCRP mule identifiers into RBIH's training datasets for *MuleHunter.AI*, while RBI initiated public consultation in March 2026 to update its 2017 customer-liability guidelines with a structured compensation mechanism for small-value electronic fraud.

### Countering "Digital Arrest" Transnational Cyber-Slavery Scam Hubs

Between 2023 and 2025, **"Digital Arrest"** emerged as India's most psychologically devastating cyber-extortion vector, targeting retired bureaucrats, senior citizens, academics, and corporate leaders (including a 2024 ₹7 crore extortion involving a staged Skype "Supreme Court hearing").

**Operational Anatomy:** Perpetrators initiate contact via automated calls alleging an intercepted FedEx/DHL courier containing narcotics, forged passports, or money-laundering links to the victim's Aadhaar. The call is escalated onto WhatsApp or Skype video featuring impersonators in police/CBI/ED uniforms seated inside fabricated police stations or courtrooms, flashing forged arrest warrants bearing Supreme Court or RBI seals. By weaponizing fear, social stigma, and continuous video surveillance—ordering victims not to hang up or contact relatives—syndicates coerce multi-crore transfers into "RBI verification accounts". As I4C repeatedly clarified, **Indian criminal law (BNSS) recognizes no concept of virtual or digital arrest**.

**Geopolitical Ecosystem — Southeast Asian Cyber-Slavery Compounds:** Investigations revealed that roughly half of India's high-value digital-arrest and investment scams originate not from domestic gangs, but from industrialized, militarized scam compounds operating inside weakly governed enclaves of **Myanmar (Myawaddy / KK Park)**, **Cambodia (Sihanoukville / Poipet)**, and **Laos (Golden Triangle Special Economic Zone)**. Run by transnational organized crime syndicates (frequently linked to Chinese triad capital and local armed militias), these compounds execute **dual victimization**: trafficking educated Indian and South Asian youth via fraudulent IT/data-entry job ads, confiscating their passports, and forcing them under physical coercion to script and execute scams against their own compatriots.

**Whole-of-Government Counter-Offensive and Supreme Court Intervention:**
- **Executive and Diplomatic Disruption:** An MHA High-Level Inter-Ministerial Committee (constituted May 2024) integrated I4C, MEA, DoT, ED, and CBI. MEA coordinated consular rescues and repatriations of trafficked Indian nationals alongside Myanmar, Cambodian, and Lao authorities; I4C blocked over **83,668 WhatsApp accounts and 3,962 Skype IDs** used for digital-arrest extortion; and DoT's spoofed-call gateway filters and November 2025 SIM-binding directions choked cross-border impersonation rails.
- **Supreme Court Judicial Oversight:** In **October 2025**, the Supreme Court took suo motu cognizance in ***Suo Motu Writ Petition (Criminal) No. 3 of 2025 (In Re: Victims of Digital Arrest Related to Forged Documents)*** after a senior-citizen couple in Haryana lost ₹1.05 crore to fake Supreme Court warrants. Treating the forgery of judicial orders as a direct assault on public faith in the rule of law, the Court on **December 1, 2025** entrusted a **pan-India CBI investigation** covering both transnational organizers and negligent/collusive bank officials, nudged states to grant general consent, pressed for Interpol cooperation, and demanded RBI deploy AI/ML against mule accounts. Following the Court's February 2026 directions calling large-scale digital siphoning "robbery and dacoity," I4C circulated a unified **SOP for NCRP–CFCFRMS** operationalizing the **Grievance Redressal Module (GRM)** and **Money Restoration Module (MRM)** across subordinate courts in **July 2026**.
- **Measurable Contraction:** Combined awareness campaigns (including national caller-tune warnings), technical blocking, and scam-compound raids drove a sharp decline in reported digital-arrest complaints on NCRP—from **1,23,672 in 2024** to **58,249 in 2025**, and **16,377 in the first half of 2026**—though syndicates continue mutating toward AI voice-cloning and decentralized crypto laundering.

## Military Cyber Warfare, Sovereign Tech Hardening and Global Cyber Norms

### Passive Hardening vs. Active Cyber Deterrence

Strategic theory distinguishes two fundamentally different postures for defending sovereign cyberspace:

1. **Passive Hardening (Deterrence by Denial):** Aims to raise the technical cost and reduce the operational payoff of an intrusion so drastically that adversaries conclude an attack will fail. Instruments include **Zero-Trust Architecture** ("never trust, always verify"), strict IT–OT air-gapping, network micro-segmentation, indigenous operating systems, hardware root-of-trust, post-quantum cryptography, and rapid offline recovery backups. Passive defense carries zero escalatory risk—but in an offense-dominant domain where defenders must guard every node while attackers need only one flaw, pure passivity invites relentless cost-free probing.
2. **Active Cyber Deterrence (Defend Forward and Deterrence by Punishment):** Shifts from waiting behind firewalls to actively hunting adversary infrastructure outside friendly perimeters (*persistent engagement*), preemptively disrupting command-and-control servers, imposing technical/economic retaliation, or signaling **cross-domain deterrence** (linking catastrophic cyber strikes on CII to kinetic, financial, or diplomatic reprisals).

Yet classical nuclear deterrence does not map cleanly onto cyberspace due to three structural paradoxes:
- **The Attribution Dilemma:** Forensic attribution takes days or months and relies on classified intelligence that states hesitate to expose publicly, dulling swift punitive credibility.
- **Exploit Fragility ("Use-It-or-Lose-It"):** Unlike an ICBM whose capability can be demonstrated without invalidating it, displaying a zero-day cyber weapon immediately reveals the flaw to the target, who patches it—eroding deterrent stockpiles upon demonstration.
- **Escalation Ambiguity:** International law lacks a settled quantitative threshold defining when a non-kinetic cyber intrusion crosses **Article 51 of the UN Charter** ("armed attack") to justify kinetic self-defense, creating dangerous miscalculation risks during crises.

### Tri-Service Defence Cyber Agency and Joint Cyberspace Doctrine

Recognizing cyberspace as a contested military theater integral to multi-domain warfare, India approved a tri-service institutional framework in 2018 and operationalized the **Defence Cyber Agency (DCyA)** in **2019** under **Headquarters Integrated Defence Staff (HQ IDS)**, reporting to the **Chief of Defence Staff (CDS)**. Headed by a two-star officer (Major General / Rear Admiral / Air Vice Marshal) drawing personnel from the Army, Navy, and Air Force, DCyA works in tandem with dedicated Service Cyber Groups/CERTs and the Indian Army's **Command Cyber Operations and Support Wings (CCOSWs)** (approved in 2023 to embed dedicated cyber-warfare capacity directly into operational Army commands).

A watershed doctrinal milestone arrived when CDS General Anil Chauhan released India's first **Joint Doctrine for Cyberspace Operations (JP 2.01)** on **June 18, 2024**, followed by its **declassified public version on August 7, 2025**. The doctrine formally establishes three strategic shifts:
- **Offensive–Defensive Integration:** Moves India's military posture beyond purely defensive network hygiene toward **synchronized offensive and defensive cyberspace operations** integrated across land, sea, air, and space domains.
- **Shared-Sovereignty Reality:** Explicitly acknowledges that unlike territorial borders, cyberspace constitutes a global common requiring dovetailing military cyber "Ends, Ways, and Means" into the broader civil-national security fabric.
- **Joint Readiness Exercises:** DCyA operationalizes this doctrine through **Exercise *Cyber Suraksha*** (held in May 2024, June 2025, and expanded into multi-domain cyber and electronic-warfare drills in October 2026), uniting over 100 military, NTRO, DRDO, CERT-In, and I4C practitioners alongside CISO leadership conclaves to stress-test GPS spoofing, DDoS, and defense-network resilience.

Structurally, however, DCyA faces two acute constraints compared to China's CMC-level PLA Cyberspace Force or US Cyber Command: **manpower scalability** (calls to elevate DCyA into a full-fledged **Tri-Service Cyber Command** collide with military "teeth-to-tail" streamlining) and **civilian talent retention** (rigid military pay scales struggle to attract elite vulnerability researchers, strengthening proposals for a **National Cyber Reserve Force** drawn from India's private IT/cybersecurity industry).

### Sovereign Tech Hardening - Indigenous Maya OS and Chakravyuh

Software monoculture is a strategic vulnerability: running defense and government endpoints on proprietary foreign operating systems exposes sovereign networks to foreign zero-day monopolies, telemetry leakage, and vendor supply-chain coercion.

To break this dependency, a joint team from **DRDO, C-DAC, and NIC** engineered ***Maya OS*** (initiated in 2021, with Ministry of Defence rollout commencing **August 15, 2023** across South Block internet-facing systems and expanding across the Navy, Army, and Air Force). Built atop open-source **Ubuntu Linux**, *Maya OS* solves the adoption barrier that stalled C-DAC's earlier **BOSS (Bharat Operating System Solutions)** Linux (2007) by replicating a familiar Windows-like graphical interface while hardening the underlying kernel with secure boot, full-disk encryption, and application sandboxing.

Paired directly with *Maya OS* is ***Chakravyuh***—an indigenous endpoint detection, anti-malware, and deception layer that creates a virtualized barrier isolating endpoints from external internet threats. Yet operating-system sovereignty represents only the middle layer of a true **Sovereign Tech Stack**. Long-term defense independence requires closing deeper hardware and cryptographic dependencies—specifically indigenous microprocessors (e.g., *Shakti*, *Vega*, *DHRUV64*), trusted telecom/network routers, and transitioning national encryption standards to **Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC)** before adversary quantum computing enables *"harvest now, decrypt later"* compromises of classified state data.

### Global Cyber Norms - UN Convention Against Cybercrime vs. Budapest Convention

Borderless cybercrime renders unilateral domestic policing futile when suspect servers, cloud data, and operators reside abroad—where traditional **Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs)** routinely take 12 to 36 months to produce electronic evidence. Two competing international legal regimes attempt to solve this evidentiary bottleneck:

**Council of Europe Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001):** The world's first multilateral cybercrime treaty (augmented by its 2022 Second Additional Protocol on cross-border e-evidence) harmonizes criminal laws, mandates a 24×7 cooperation network, and is championed by Western democracies. **India has consistently declined to join the Budapest Convention** on three principled grounds:
1. **Exclusionary Drafting:** Negotiated purely within the Council of Europe without India's or the Global South's participation, leaving non-European states as rule-takers.
2. **Sovereignty Erosion (Article 32(b)):** Permits foreign law enforcement agencies to access stored computer data located in another country without the host state's authorization whenever the entity possessing the data gives lawful consent—an arrangement India views as infringing digital sovereignty.
3. **Asymmetric Data Reciprocity:** New Delhi judged that accession would not guarantee faster, equitable access to electronic evidence held by US/European Big Tech platforms relative to unilateral compliance burdens.

**United Nations Convention against Cybercrime (2024 — "Hanoi Convention"):** Championed by India alongside BRICS and Global South nations as an inclusive UN-led alternative, the treaty was **adopted by consensus via UNGA Resolution 79/243 on December 24, 2024**. It opened for signature in **Hanoi, Vietnam on October 25–26, 2025** (remaining open at UNHQ New York until December 31, 2026) and enters into force **90 days after the 40th state deposits its instrument of ratification**. Following endorsement in the September 12, 2026 **BRICS New Delhi Declaration**, **India formally signed the Convention on September 25, 2026** at UNGA 81 (as of October 2026, 95–96 states had signed, with Qatar, Azerbaijan, and Vietnam depositing early ratifications).

**Core Provisions and Structural Trade-Offs of the UN Convention:**
- **Strengths for India:** Establishes the first universal, legally binding global framework for preserving and sharing **electronic evidence** across borders—covering not only cyber-dependent offences (illegal access/interference) but also cyber-enabled **online financial fraud**, child sexual exploitation/grooming, **non-consensual dissemination of intimate images**, and electronic evidence for any **"serious crime"** (punishable by ≥4 years' imprisonment)—anchored by a sovereign-consent **global 24×7 network** and technical capacity-building for developing states.
- **Human-Rights and Governance Critiques:** Civil-society coalitions and technology firms warn that mandating cross-border electronic surveillance and evidence-sharing for non-cyber "serious crimes" lacks ironclad treaty-wide human-rights vetoes, creating risks that authoritarian regimes could misuse mutual assistance against journalists, dissidents, whistleblowers, or ethical security researchers. Balancing sovereign investigative efficiency with constitutional privacy safeguards during domestic ratification will define India's implementation credibility.

**State-on-State Cyber Warfare Norms:** Crucially, both the Budapest and Hanoi Conventions address *cybercrime*, not *state-sponsored cyber warfare*. Norms restraining state cyber operations remain voluntary and non-binding: the **UN Group of Governmental Experts (UN GGE)** affirmed in **2013** that existing international law (including the UN Charter) applies to cyberspace and established **11 voluntary norms of responsible state behavior in 2015** (notably barring peacetime attacks on civilian CII and national CERTs). Following the **UN Open-Ended Working Group (OEWG, 2021–2025)**, states operationalized a **permanent global UN mechanism for cybersecurity dialogue in July 2026**, though geopolitical polarization between Western multi-stakeholder models and Sino-Russian "cyber sovereignty" doctrines prevents any binding "Digital Geneva Convention"—leaving expert frameworks like the **Tallinn Manual 2.0** purely persuasive.

### Prelims Trap Signature

- **Statutory vs. Executive & Ministry Mapping:** UPSC frequently tests whether a cyber institution is statutory (NCIIPC under Sec 70A, CERT-In under Sec 70B) versus executive (I4C as an MHA Attached Office, NCSC under NSCS, DCyA under HQ IDS), and swaps their parent ministries (NTRO/PMO vs. MeitY vs. MHA vs. MoD).
- **Treaty Accession vs. Entry-into-Force Conflation:** Expect deceptive statements claiming India is a party to the *Budapest Convention* (false) or that the *UN Convention against Cybercrime (Hanoi Convention)* entered into force upon India's September 2026 signature (false—signature is non-binding and global entry into force requires 40 ratifications).
