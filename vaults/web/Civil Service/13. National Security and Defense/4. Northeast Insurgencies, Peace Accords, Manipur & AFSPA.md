# Northeast Insurgencies, Peace Accords, Manipur (2023–2026) & AFSPA

India’s Northeast—connected to the mainland only through the 22-km **Siliguri Corridor ("Chicken’s Neck")** while sharing over 5,180 km of international frontiers with China, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Bhutan, and Nepal—has historically posed India’s most complex ethnic-insurgency puzzle. Unlike Left-Wing Extremism’s pan-regional ideological uniformity or J&K’s single-sponsor proxy war, Northeastern militancy fragmented into dozens of ethno-nationalist, sub-regional, and identity-driven movements rooted in colonial cartography, post-1947 integration trauma, demographic anxieties, and cross-border sanctuaries in Myanmar’s Sagaing region.

Over the last decade, New Delhi engineered an unprecedented wave of **territorial peace accords** across Assam, Tripura, and eastern Nagaland—slashing overall regional insurgency fatalities by over 70%—yet the **Manipur ethnic–security crisis (2023–2026)** starkly exposed how unresolved land-tenure asymmetries, Myanmar’s civil-war spillover, narco-insurgency networks, and fractured state capacity can ignite severe internal instability overnight.

## Colonial Frontier Legacies - ILP (1873) & Excluded Areas

To comprehend why hill–valley cleavage defines almost every contemporary Northeast conflict, one must trace two colonial legal instruments that deliberately segregated the Brahmaputra and Imphal plains from surrounding hill societies.

- **Bengal Eastern Frontier Regulation (BEFR), 1873 & Inner Line Permit (ILP):** Enacted by the British Raj ostensibly to protect indigenous hill tribes from exploitation—and practically to secure colonial monopolies over tea gardens, oilfields, and elephant-hunting tracts in Assam—BEFR 1873 drew an administrative **"Inner Line"** across frontier foothills [2.1.1]. Non-indigenous British subjects (today, Indian citizens from outside the notified state) were prohibited from crossing this line or acquiring land without an official **Inner Line Permit (ILP)** [2.1.1]. Post-Independence, ILP continued in **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, and Mizoram**, and was extended to **Manipur on December 11, 2019** (effective January 1, 2020) via a Presidential Adaptation Order—exempting all four ILP states from the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 [2.1.1, 2.1.3].
- **Excluded & Partially Excluded Areas (Government of India Act, 1935):** Building upon the Scheduled Districts Act (1874) and the Montagu–Chelmsford "Backward Tracts" (1919), Sections 91–92 of the **Government of India Act, 1935** classified frontier hills into **Excluded Areas** (Naga Hills, Lushai Hills/Mizoram, North Cachar Hills, Balipara and Sadiya Frontier Tracts—placed under the **direct discretionary rule of the Provincial Governor**, completely outside elected provincial ministries) and **Partially Excluded Areas** (Garo, Khasi–Jaintia, and Mikir Hills—where provincial laws applied only subject to Governor’s modifications) [2.1.6].

When the Constituent Assembly’s **Gopinath Bordoloi Sub-Committee** designed independent India’s asymmetric federalism, these colonial buffers evolved into the **Sixth Schedule** (Autonomous District Councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram) and **Article 371** special provisions (notably Art 371A for Nagaland, Art 371C for Manipur, and Art 371G for Mizoram) [2.1.6]. While these safeguards protected indigenous land and customary laws, colonial non-integration left hill communities detached from India’s anti-colonial freedom struggle—seeding deep post-1947 anxieties over political subordination to plains majorities.

## The Naga Insurgency - From Phizo’s NNC to the 2026 FNTA Agreement

Often termed the **"Mother of All Insurgencies"** in the Northeast, the Naga conflict predates independent India itself and trained subsequent insurgent cadres across Manipur, Assam, and Mizoram.

### Historical Evolution - NNC, Shillong Accord & the NSCN Split

- **NNC & Early Militarization (1946–1975):** Emerging from the Naga Club (1918), which had famously submitted a 1929 memorandum to the Simon Commission requesting exclusion from British-Indian reforms, the **Naga National Council (NNC)** under **Angami Zapu Phizo** rejected union with India, declared unilateral Naga independence on **August 14, 1947**, organized a 1951 "plebiscite," and launched armed insurrection by 1955–56. While New Delhi negotiated the **Sixteen-Point Agreement (1960)** with moderate Nagas—creating **Nagaland as India’s 16th state on December 1, 1963** protected by **Article 371A**—underground resistance persisted.
- **Shillong Accord (November 11, 1975) & Birth of NSCN (1980):** A section of NNC and its Federal Government of Nagaland signed the **Shillong Accord**, unconditionally accepting the Indian Constitution and agreeing to deposit arms. China-trained hardliners **Isak Chishi Swu**, **Thuingaleng Muivah** (a Tangkhul Naga from Manipur’s Ukhrul), and **S.S. Khaplang** (a Hemi Naga from Myanmar) denounced the pact as betrayal, founding the **National Socialist Council of Nagaland (NSCN)** in **January 1980** across the Myanmar border.
- **1988 Fratricidal Split:** Fierce tribal and tactical rivalries shattered the movement in **April 1988** into **NSCN (IM)** (Isak–Muivah, dominant in Manipur’s Naga hills and southern Nagaland; entered a **ceasefire with GoI in August 1997**) and **NSCN (K)** (Khaplang, strong in eastern Nagaland and Myanmar’s Sagaing region; entered ceasefire in 2001, unilaterally abrogated it in **March 2015** leading to the deadly Chandel ambush and India’s June 2015 cross-border raid). After Khaplang’s death (2017), NSCN (K) fractured into the anti-talks Myanmar-based **NSCN (K–Yung Aung)** and the pro-talks **NSCN (K–Niki)** (which signed a ceasefire in September 2021).

### Greater Nagalim, Dual-Track Talks & the Flag/*Yehzabo* Deadlock

For over two decades, final settlement negotiations have pivoted on two competing tracks and an intractable symbolic impasse:

- **Greater Nagalim Redline:** NSCN (IM) demanded the political integration of all contiguous Naga-inhabited territories across **Nagaland, Manipur (four hill districts: Ukhrul, Senapati, Tamenglong, Chandel), Assam, Arunachal Pradesh (Tirap, Changlang, Longding), and northern Myanmar** (~1.2 lakh sq km). When New Delhi briefly extended the NSCN (IM) ceasefire "without territorial limits" in June 2001, violent protests erupted in Imphal (June 18 Uprising), forcing the Union to recognize that redrawing state boundaries is politically unviable.
- **August 3, 2015 Framework Agreement (NSCN-IM):** Signed in the presence of PM Modi, the pact acknowledged the *"unique history and position of the Nagas"* and proposed an inclusive enduring relationship built around *"shared sovereignty"* and peaceful coexistence. Yet finalization stalled over NSCN (IM)’s rigid insistence on a **separate Naga National Flag** and a **separate Constitution (*Yehzabo*)**—demands New Delhi flatly rejected as incompatible with constitutional unity after abrogating J&K’s separate flag and constitution in 2019.
- **November 17, 2017 Agreed Position (WC-NNPGs):** Expanding talks beyond NSCN (IM), the Union engaged the **Working Committee of Naga National Political Groups (WC-NNPGs)**—an umbrella of seven Nagaland-based factions representing indigenous Nagaland tribes resentful of Tangkhul-led NSCN (IM) dominance and rampant "parallel taxation" (extortion). WC-NNPGs pragmatically accepted a constitutional settlement without insisting on Greater Nagalim or immediate flag/constitution separation, leaving New Delhi balancing two rival Naga blocs.

### 2024–2026 Breakthrough - ENPO & Frontier Nagaland Territorial Authority (FNTA)

Cutting across the macro-insurgency, the **Eastern Nagaland Peoples’ Organisation (ENPO)**—representing **eight backward Naga tribes** (Chang, Khiamniungan, Konyak, Phom, Sangtam, Tikhir, Yimkhiung, and Sumi of Kiphire) across **six neglected eastern districts** (**Mon, Tuensang, Longleng, Kiphire, Noklak, and Shamator**)—mounted an intense political agitation demanding a separate "Frontier Nagaland" state, culminating in a total **zero-turnout boycott of the April 2024 Lok Sabha elections** [1.3.2, 1.3.8].

Following intensive tripartite dialogue across 2024–2025, the **Union Government, Government of Nagaland, and ENPO signed a landmark Tripartite Agreement on February 5, 2026**, establishing the **Frontier Nagaland Territorial Authority (FNTA)** [1.3.1, 1.3.3]. Without altering Nagaland’s territorial integrity or **Article 371A**, the FNTA creates a dedicated mini-secretariat headed by an Additional Chief Secretary, devolves executive and legislative-consultative powers across **46 subjects**, and guarantees ring-fenced financial allocations proportional to eastern Nagaland’s population and area [1.3.1, 1.3.5]—neutralizing a simmering sub-regional secessionist flashpoint through asymmetric developmental federalism.

## Northeast Peace Accords Landscape (1986–2024)

Rather than treating insurgency solely as a kinetic policing challenge, the Indian state perfected an asymmetric **Accord Diplomacy** architecture—combining military fatigue, leadership splits, targeted financial packages, cultural protections, and democratic co-optation without yielding on sovereignty or state dismemberment:

- **1986 Mizo Peace Accord (June 30, 1986 — The Gold Standard):** Ended the 20-year **Mizo National Front (MNF)** insurgency under **Laldenga** (born out of administrative neglect during the 1959 *Mautam* bamboo-flowering famine). The accord upgraded Mizoram to India’s **23rd full state (February 20, 1987)**, inserted **Article 371G** protecting Mizo customary law and land ownership, and peacefully transitioned the MNF from jungle guerrillas into a mainstream constitutional ruling party—transforming Mizoram into one of India’s most peaceful states.
- **2020 BTR Bodo Accord (January 27, 2020 — 3rd Bodo Accord):** Signed between GoI, Assam, **all four factions of the National Democratic Front of Bodoland (NDFB)**, ABSU, and UBPO (succeeding the 1993 BAC and 2003 BTC accords). It renamed the Sixth Schedule **BTAD** as the **Bodoland Territorial Region (BTR)**, expanded Assembly/Council seats from 40 to 60, committed a **₹1,500 crore** development package, recognized Bodo as an associate official language of Assam, established a Bodo-Kachari Welfare Council outside BTR, and secured the surrender of **1,615 NDFB cadres** [1.4.4].
- **Karbi Anglong Agreement (September 4, 2021):** Tripartite accord with **five Karbi militant outfits** (PDCK, KPLT, KLNLF, KLF, UPLA), securing the surrender of over 1,000 cadres, a **₹1,000 crore** five-year package, and enhanced autonomy for the Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council (KAAC) within Assam [1.4.5].
- **Assam Adivasi Peace Accord (September 15, 2022):** Signed with **eight Adivasi/tea-tribe armed groups** (Birsa Commando Force, AANLA, ACMA, STF, and allies); 1,182 cadres laid down arms alongside a **₹1,000 crore** package and creation of an Adivasi Welfare and Development Council [1.4.5].
- **Dimasa Peace Accord (April 27, 2023):** Tripartite agreement with the **Dimasa National Liberation Army (DNLA)** ending Dima Hasao militancy via 168+ surrenders and a **₹1,000 crore** joint Centre–State development package [1.4.5].
- **Pro-Talk ULFA Tripartite Accord (December 29, 2023):** Concluded 44 years of militancy by the **Pro-Talk faction of the United Liberation Front of Asom** (founded in 1979; led by Arabinda Rajkhowa and Anup Chetia), which formally disbanded following the pact [1.4.3]. Its centerpiece secured an unprecedented **₹1.5 lakh crore** phased investment/infrastructure commitment, constitutional land-right safeguards for indigenous Assamese, and reservation of **97 of Assam’s 126 Assembly constituencies** for indigenous communities post-delimitation [1.4.3]—though Paresh Baruah’s Myanmar-sheltered **ULFA (Independent)** faction remains outside the settlement.
- **Bru–Reang Quadripartite Agreement (January 16, 2020):** Resolved a 23-year humanitarian crisis by permanently resettling approximately **37,000 displaced Bru (Reang) refugees**—who had fled ethnic clashes in Mizoram in 1997—across **12 model resettlement villages in Tripura** backed by a **₹600+ crore** package (housing, land plots, ₹4 lakh fixed deposit per family, and monthly aid), substantially completed by 2024–25 [1.4.6].
- **Tripura Peace Accord (September 4, 2024):** Tripartite Memorandum of Settlement between GoI, Tripura, the **National Liberation Front of Tripura (NLFT)**, and the **All Tripura Tiger Force (ATTF)**, resulting in **328+ armed cadres** laying down weapons and a **₹250 crore** tribal-area development package [1.4.2]—closing Tripura’s 35-year armed insurgency (complementing the March 2024 political *Tiprasa Accord* with Tipra Motha).

## Manipur Crisis and AFSPA 1958

Nowhere in India do internal ethnic fault lines, cross-border geopolitical fragility, and the constitutional paradox of extraordinary security laws collide more starkly than in **Manipur (2023–2026)** and the operational debate over the **Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958**.

### Manipur Ethnic–Security Crisis (May 2023 – Late 2026)

#### 1. Structural Geographic, Land-Tenure & Constitutional Fault Lines
Manipur’s internal security architecture rests atop an acute spatial and institutional asymmetry:

- **The 10:90 Valley–Hill Imbalance:** The fertile, oval **Imphal Valley** comprises barely **~10% of Manipur’s geographical area** yet houses roughly **~53% of the state’s population**—predominantly **Meiteis** (Vaishnavite Hindus, indigenous Sanamahi followers, and Meitei Pangal Muslims) holding 40 of the 60 Assembly seats. The surrounding **90% mountainous periphery** is inhabited by recognized Scheduled Tribes—principally **Nagas (~24%)** in the north and east and **Kuki-Zo communities (~16%)** across the southern and western districts (Churachandpur, Kangpokpi, Tengnoupal, Pherzawl, Chandel) [2.4.1].
- **MLR&LR Act, 1960 & Article 371C Friction:** Under **Section 158 of the Manipur Land Revenue and Land Reforms (MLR&LR) Act, 1960**, non-tribals (including Meiteis) are **barred from purchasing or settling on tribal land in hill areas** without Deputy Commissioner and District Council consent, whereas tribals face no reciprocal restriction buying property inside the Imphal Valley [2.4.1]. Politically, **Article 371C** establishes a **Hill Areas Committee (HAC)** of hill MLAs inside the Manipur Assembly to safeguard hill interests; yet Kuki-Zo and Naga bodies argue Manipur’s six Autonomous District Councils—created under a 1971 Parliamentary Act rather than the **Sixth Schedule**—lack real fiscal autonomy, while valley Meiteis perceive themselves as constitutionally boxed into 10% of their ancestral state [2.4.1].

#### 2. Immediate Triggers, Poppy Narco-Insurgency & Myanmar Spillover
Violence erupted across Manipur on **May 3, 2023** after three flashpoints converged:

- **The ST Status Legal Trigger:** A controversial **March 27, 2023 single-judge Manipur High Court order** directing the state government to consider recommending **Scheduled Tribe (ST) status for Meiteis** (subsequently expunged by a Division Bench on **February 21, 2024** after sharp Supreme Court disapproval) alarmed hill tribes that Meitei ST inclusion would dissolve Section 158 land protections and dominate reserved jobs [2.4.4]. Clashes broke out during the May 3 All Tribal Students’ Union Manipur (ATSUM) Solidarity March in Churachandpur and Torbung, rapidly spiraling into statewide ethnic cleansing [2.4.4].
- **Poppy Narco-Insurgency Nexus & Reserved Forest Evictions:** Prior to May 2023, the state government’s aggressive **"War on Drugs"** targeting illicit **poppy cultivation** across southern Kuki-inhabited hill ranges—tied directly into the **Golden Triangle** heroin and *Yaba* (methamphetamine) trafficking economy—alongside eviction drives in Reserved/Protected Forests alienated hill communities who viewed the drives as selectively targeting Kuki-Zo villages.
- **Post-2021 Myanmar Civil-War Spillover:** Following Myanmar’s February 2021 military coup, intense fighting between the junta and ethnic Chin/PDF rebels pushed thousands of Chin–Kuki refugees across the unfenced, Free Movement Regime (FMR) frontier into Manipur—sharpening Meitei fears of demographic alteration, illegal arms inflow, and strident demands for a state National Register of Citizens (NRC).

#### 3. Armed Actors, Looted Armories & Ethno-Spatial Partition
Within weeks, communal rioting hardened into a militarized ethno-territorial standoff paralyzing state neutrality:

- **Valley Armed Formations:** Radicalized Meitei socio-armed militias—prominently **Arambai Tenggol** and Meitei Leepun—mobilized heavily alongside resurgent valley-based insurgent outfits (PLA, UNLF, PREPAK, KYKL—collectively *CorCom*; though the **UNLF-Pambei** faction signed a landmark peace accord with GoI and Manipur in **November 2023**) [2.4.6].
- **Kuki-Zo SoO Militant Outfits:** Roughly 25 Kuki-Zo armed groups grouped under the **Kuki National Organisation (KNO)** and **United People’s Front (UPF)** had operated under a tripartite **2008 Suspension of Operations (SoO)** agreement with designated camps [2.4.5]. Accused by the state government of breaking camp rules and joining frontline firefights (prompting Manipur to withdraw support from SoO), the Union subsequently renegotiated and signed a **stricter revised SoO extension on September 4, 2025**, relocating designated camps away from vulnerable zones, tightening biometric/weapon audits, and binding groups to respect Manipur’s territorial integrity [2.4.5].
- **Armory Looting & Buffer Zones:** Between May and September 2023, mobs systematically looted **~5,600–6,000 automatic weapons** (INSAS, AK-47s, SLRs, light machine guns, mortars) and lakhs of rounds from Manipur Police armories [2.4.6]. Equipped with state weaponry—and by September 2024 even improvised **weaponized quadcopter drones and locally modified RPG rockets**—armed "village volunteers" enforced a rigid physical partition between valley and hill districts, patrolled by Army, Assam Rifles, and CAPF **buffer zones** along foothills (Bishnupur–Churachandpur, Imphal West–Kangpokpi). Over 260 lives were lost and ~60,000 citizens remained internally displaced across relief camps.

#### 4. Constitutional & Security Arc (2025–2026) - President’s Rule Revocation & AFSPA Extension
Following persistent deadlock, fractured coalition unity, and Chief Minister N. Biren Singh’s resignation on **February 9, 2025**, the Union invoked **Article 356**, imposing **President’s Rule on February 13, 2025** while placing the Assembly under **suspended animation** (extended for a second six-month term in August 2025) [1.1.2, 1.1.3]. Under central oversight led by Governor Ajay Kumar Bhalla, security forces mounted intensive weapon-surrender campaigns (recovering thousands of looted arms), hardened national highway escorts (NH-2 and NH-37), tightened border fencing, and brokered the revised September 2025 SoO pact.

On **February 4, 2026**, nearly a year later, the Union **revoked President’s Rule**, swearing in a restructured NDA coalition ministry headed by Chief Minister **Yumnam Khemchand Singh** alongside a deliberate multi-ethnic power-sharing pairing of two Deputy Chief Ministers—**Nemcha Kipgen (Kuki-Zo)** and **Losii Dikho (Naga)** [1.1.1, 1.1.5]. Yet restoring elected governance did not eliminate underlying distrust: Kuki-Zo civil bodies continued pressing for a Separate Administration / Union Territory with Legislature, displaced families struggled to return across buffer zones, and security fragility compelled the MHA to maintain **AFSPA across Manipur’s hill districts and foothill/fringe zones** (having re-imposed it in six volatile foothill/Jiribam stations in November 2024), formally **extending AFSPA for another six months effective October 1, 2026** (valid through March 31, 2027), excluding only **13 core Imphal Valley police stations** [1.2.1, 1.2.3].

### Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act (AFSPA), 1958 - Statutory Mechanics & Constitutional Guardrails

Modeled on the colonial *Armed Forces (Special Powers) Ordinance, 1942* (promulgated to suppress the Quit India movement), Parliament enacted **AFSPA in September 1958** specifically to empower military deployment against the Naga insurgency in Assam and Manipur (extended via parallel legislation to **Jammu & Kashmir in 1990**, and formerly Punjab/Chandigarh from 1983 until its 1997 repeal) [2.2.1].

#### Core Statutory Architecture
- **Section 3 (Declaration of "Disturbed Area"):** Empowers the **Governor of the State**, Administrator of the UT, or—following a pivotal **1972 Amendment**—the **Central Government** to declare the whole or any part of a state/UT a **"Disturbed Area"** by Gazette notification if it considers conditions so dangerous that using armed forces **in aid of civil power** is necessary [2.2.1]. In ***Naga People’s Movement of Human Rights (NPMHR) v. Union of India* (1997)**, a five-judge Supreme Court Constitution Bench upheld AFSPA’s constitutional validity—holding that Parliament is competent under Entry 2A of the Union List—while laying down strict **Do’s and Don’ts** and mandating that Disturbed Area notifications **cannot be indefinite and must undergo periodic review every six months** [2.2.2].
- **Section 4 (Extraordinary Operational Powers):** Authorizes any commissioned officer, warrant officer, non-commissioned officer, or person of equivalent rank in a Disturbed Area—after giving such due warning as deemed necessary—to:
  1. **Fire upon or use force even to the causing of death** against any person acting in contravention of prohibitory orders against assemblies of five or more persons or carrying weapons, firearms, or explosives [2.2.1];
  2. **Destroy** any arms dump, fortified position, or shelter from which armed attacks are made or trained [2.2.1];
  3. **Arrest without warrant** any person who has committed or is reasonably suspected of committing a cognizable offense [2.2.1]; and
  4. **Enter and search without warrant** any premises to make arrests or recover abducted persons, arms, or explosives [2.2.1]. *(Section 5 mandates handing arrested persons to the nearest police station with the "least possible delay.")*
- **Section 6 (Legal Protection / Immunity Clause):** Prohibits any prosecution, suit, or other legal proceeding against armed forces personnel for acts done or purported to be done under AFSPA **except with the previous sanction of the Central Government** [2.2.1]—a provision critics contend has historically resulted in near-zero prosecution sanctions and perceived impunity.

#### Reform Committees & Supreme Court Human-Rights Balancing
- **Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy Committee (2004–2005):** Appointed in November 2004 following widespread public outrage over the custodial death of **Thangjam Manorama** in Manipur, the five-member committee submitted its landmark report in **June 2005** [2.2.4]. Concluding that AFSPA had become *"a symbol of oppression, an object of hate and an instrument of discrimination and high-handedness,"* the committee unanimously recommended **repealing AFSPA entirely**—proposing instead to insert narrowly tailored military-protection clauses within the **UAPA** and establish independent **Grievance Cells** in every disturbed district [2.2.4]. The **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (5th Report on Public Order, 2007)** endorsed repeal, while the Supreme Court–appointed **Justice Santosh Hegde Commission (2013)** examined six test cases in Manipur, declaring all six to be **fake encounters** and warning that AFSPA had been widely abused.
- ***Extra Judicial Execution Victim Families Association (EEVFAM) v. Union of India* (July 8, 2016):** Deciding a monumental petition documenting **1,528 alleged fake encounters** in Manipur (1979–2012), a Supreme Court bench of Justices Madan B. Lokur and U.U. Lalit fundamentally recalibrated AFSPA jurisprudence [2.2.5, 2.2.8]:
  1. **End of Absolute Immunity:** Ruled that security forces enjoy **no blanket or absolute immunity** from criminal prosecution—even in a Disturbed Area, using **excessive or retaliatory force** is strictly illegal and must be investigated independently (triggering CBI Special Investigation Team FIRs) [2.2.5, 2.2.8].
  2. **Citizens Are Not "Enemies":** Rejected the Union’s contention that domestic insurgents can be treated as war-zone "enemies" exempt from criminal-law scrutiny, affirming constitutional protection under **Article 21** [2.2.5].
  3. **Indefinite Deployment Violates Constitutional Governance:** Observed starkly that keeping the Armed Forces deployed across Manipur for decades renders *"in aid of civil power"* a hollow fiction—reflecting failure of both civil administration and security leadership to restore normal policing [2.2.8].

#### Spatial Contraction & the Re-Imposition Dilemma (2015–2026)
Military leadership maintains that troops operating under extreme counter-insurgency ambiguity cannot function without statutory legal indemnity against malicious litigation. New Delhi’s pragmatic compromise over the past decade has been **progressive spatial contraction** rather than outright statutory repeal—shrinking AFSPA’s Northeast footprint dramatically as violence ebbed:

- **Completely Revoked:** **Tripura (2015)** and **Meghalaya (2018)** (Mizoram and Sikkim remain AFSPA-free) [1.2.7].
- **Substantially Reduced (October 2026 Status):**
  - **Assam:** Reduced by over 70% to just **3 districts** (**Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, and Charaideo**—down from four after removing Sivasagar in October 2025) [1.2.2, 1.2.6].
  - **Arunachal Pradesh:** Restricted strictly to **3 eastern districts** (**Tirap, Changlang, Longding**) plus **3 police stations in Namsai** district [1.2.6].
  - **Nagaland:** Confined to **8 districts and 21 police stations across 5 other districts** [1.2.6].
  - **Manipur Exception:** Reversing pre-2023 withdrawals (which had freed 19 valley stations), the 2023–2026 crisis forced MHA to re-impose AFSPA in six volatile foothill/Jiribam stations (November 2024) and extend it across all hill and buffer areas effective **October 1, 2026** (excluding only **13 core Imphal Valley police stations**) [1.2.1, 1.2.3].

The Manipur reversal crystallizes the ultimate public-policy tension: repealing AFSPA prematurely before building neutral, modernized State Police capacity invites armed-militia chaos, yet retaining it indefinitely without strict *EEVFAM* accountability, transparent court-martial disclosures, and district grievance cells erodes democratic consent across India’s sensitive eastern frontier.

### Prelims Trap Signature

1. **ILP States vs. Sixth Schedule States Overlap Trap:** **ILP (BEFR 1873)** operates in **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Manipur** (added Dec 2019)—NOT in Meghalaya, Tripura, or Assam. Conversely, the **Sixth Schedule** applies to **Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram**—meaning **Mizoram** is the ONLY state possessing *both* ILP and Sixth Schedule councils, while **Manipur’s hill Autonomous District Councils are statutory (1971 Act), NOT Sixth Schedule bodies** [2.1.1, 2.4.1].
2. **AFSPA Declaration & Prosecution Sanction Authorities:** Under **Section 3 of AFSPA (as amended in 1972)**, a Disturbed Area can be declared by **either the State Governor/UT Administrator OR the Central Government** (NOT solely the Governor or solely the Centre). However, under **Section 6**, prior sanction to prosecute armed forces personnel can be granted **ONLY by the Central Government** (NOT the State Government) [2.2.1].
3. **ENPO FNTA (Feb 2026) Constitutional Status Trap:** The **February 5, 2026 Frontier Nagaland Territorial Authority (FNTA)** agreement does **NOT** carve out a separate state or Union Territory from Nagaland and does **NOT** dilute **Article 371A**; it creates an autonomous mini-secretariat with **46 devolved subjects** across **6 eastern districts** within the existing territorial integrity of Nagaland [1.3.1, 1.3.5].
4. **Manipur President’s Rule & AFSPA 2026 Synchronization Trap:** Although **President’s Rule in Manipur was revoked on February 4, 2026** and an elected government returned to office, **AFSPA was NOT revoked across Manipur**—it remains extended across all hill and buffer/fringe zones (effective October 1, 2026 for six months), exempting only **13 core Imphal Valley police stations** [1.1.1, 1.2.1].
